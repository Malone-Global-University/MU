## 5. Substantive historical and documentary corrections

**§70.2 — Venezuelan boundary dispute.** The original misleadingly excluded Venezuelan participation from the settlement. The final edition distinguishes U.S.–British diplomatic influence from Venezuela's involvement in the subsequent arbitration and the 1899 award; separate strategic concerns remained.

**§70.6 — Clayton–Bulwer and Hay–Pauncefote.** The original stated inaccurately that the earlier agreement *required joint canal control*. The final edition correctly characterizes it as restricting exclusive unilateral control and explains the 1901 superseding agreement, Colombia's constitutional treaty ratification, the 1903 Senate rejection, Panamanian separatist agency, and American naval involvement. Source Window II identifies Colombian Foreign Minister Luis Carlos Rico's notes and John Hay's warning, rather than using a generic composite document.

**§70.7 — Panama sovereignty and treaty ratification.** The edition distinguishes the guarantee of Panamanian independence in Article I from perpetual use/control in Article II and sovereign-like jurisdiction in Article III. Panamanian provisional approval and expressions of local endorsement are distinguished from claims of universal consent. The 1914 signature and **1922 exchange of ratifications** of the Thomson–Urrutia settlement are separated.

**§70.8 — Canal labor and construction.** Expanded lock design, Gatun Lake, Chagres River, Culebra Cut, mosquito control, construction hazards, and West Indian labor. The Gold Roll–Silver Roll system is explained as an employment and welfare hierarchy increasingly racially classified, supported by National Archives documentation.

**§70.9 — Drago Doctrine.** The original source paraphrase overstated a claim that sovereign states could not be sued. The corrected edition distinguishes judicial adjudication of claims and acknowledgment of debts from **armed enforcement and territorial occupation**, with the authentic December 29, 1902 Argentine note and February 17, 1903 U.S. response linked.

**§70.10 — Roosevelt Corollary.** The 1904 annual message is linked, and the doctrine is distinguished from Monroe's 1823 noncolonization warning. American definitions of 'wrongdoing' and the limits of the purported police power are treated as contested claims, not as self-evident international law.

**§70.11 — Dominican receivership.** The original conflated 1905 proportional revenue allocation with the 1907 convention. The final text distinguishes the **provisional 1905 arrangement** from the **1907 bilateral debt settlement, debt-service priorities, and General Receiver** and identifies Dominican legislative approval. The 1916 military occupation is a subsequent and distinct escalation.

**§70.12 — Bryan–Chamorro.** The amended 1916 treaty text clarifies **perpetual canal option rights**, **99-year Corn Islands leases**, and **99-year Gulf of Fonseca naval-base rights**, renewable at U.S. option, for $3 million. Regional objections and Central American Court proceedings are identified.

**§70.13 — Cuban administration.** Corrected the Taft–Magoon sequence: Taft became provisional governor **September 29, 1906** and transferred authority to Magoon **October 13, 1906**. Cuba's 1912 racial/political crisis is identified as principally Cuban military repression, not misattributed solely to Americans; numerical death totals are not invented.

**§70.14 — Haitian treaty and protest.** Source Window VIII now pairs the **September 16, 1915 convention** with the **September 4, 1915 formal protest by Haiti's minister in Washington** against U.S. control of customs and governmental offices. This provides a genuine Haitian diplomatic perspective rather than an unspecified future petition. The section expands constitutional conflict, the 1918 referendum, Caco resistance, coercive road labor, public health, and limits of official administrative claims.

**§§70.15–70.16 — Dominican and regional resistance.** Distinguishes 1907 financial control from 1916–1924 direct occupation and includes Dominican civic organization and rural gavilleros resistance, the Central American Court's challenges to treaty concessions, and Panamanian and Cuban strategies.

**§§70.17–70.19 — Pan-Americanism and wartime diplomacy.** Identifies the 1889–1890 conference, April 14, 1890 bureau resolution, 1910 Pan American Union, 1902 health cooperation, and the OAS creation only in **1948**. Clarifies differences among countries that entered the First World War in 1917 or 1918, states that broke diplomatic ties only, and those remaining neutral. Wilson's Mexican interventions are presented as background rather than displacing the separate Mexican Revolution treatment.

### Primary-source editorial rules retained

- The instructional windows use **labeled editorial paraphrases**, not invented quotations or purported verbatim translations.
- An identified official document's perspective is clearly distinguished from the motives and experiences of ordinary Cubans, Dominicans, Haitians, Panamanians, Nicaraguans, and canal workers.
- Certain archival facsimiles and alternate translations were **not exhaustively page-collated**; a historian requiring precise textual quotations should consult the linked original edition or facsimile directly.
- The three Map Studies are text-only exercises, **not embedded historical map plates**; the edition does not claim otherwise.
- Exact quantitative series and casualty totals require stated year/definition/source, and contested claims are not represented as exact censuses.
