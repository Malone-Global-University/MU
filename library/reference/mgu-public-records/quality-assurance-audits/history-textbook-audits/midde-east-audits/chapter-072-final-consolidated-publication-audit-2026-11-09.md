## 4. Consolidated historical accuracy assessment

The twenty sections were examined as an integrated narrative with the original approved plan. The following historical safeguards have been incorporated, and no publication-critical factual contradiction was identified in the targeted verification performed:

1. **Succession and accession (1520):** Süleyman's succession after Selim and the Janbirdi al-Ghazali rebellion are separated from the already completed Mamluk conquest. The House of Osman is treated as dynastically continuous but not governed by an automatic eldest-son succession rule.
2. **Palace and central government:** The birun, enderun, imperial council, grand vizierate, chancery, treasury, and learned offices are not retroactively presented as modern bureaucracies or unchanging across centuries. Ibrahim Pasha's grand vizierate (1523–1536) and the career of Celâlzâde Mustafa are contextualized.
3. **Law and legal officers:** Kanun and sharia are described as overlapping rather than as a secular/religious binary. Ebussuud's chief muftiship (1545–1574), the ilmiye, and the roles of judges and fatwas are distinguished.
4. **Land and finance:** A timar is a claim to specified revenue in return for service, not unrestricted ownership of peasant land; tahrir is explained as a fiscal record rather than a comprehensive modern census. Miri rights, provincial variation, and the limitations of state registration are emphasized.
5. **Military organization:** Janissaries, devshirme, palace troops, provincial sipahis, engineers, artillery, and transport are distinguished. Recruitment coercion and career opportunity are both acknowledged. Military achievements are explained through logistics and institutions, not firearm determinism.
6. **Europe and Mediterranean:** Belgrade 1521, Rhodes 1522, Mohács 1526, Vienna 1529, Baghdad 1534, Preveza 1538, Buda 1541, operations around Nice 1543, and Malta 1565 are properly contextualized. Mohács is not equated with immediate direct control of all Hungary.
7. **Iran and diplomacy:** The Peace of Amasya is dated to 29 May 1555 as a conventional reference date, with the safeguard that it was not a precise modern fixed border. Shah Tahmasp's avoidance of unfavorable battle, difficult Ottoman logistics, and territorial ambiguity are addressed. Traditional claims surrounding a definitive 1536 French capitulations treaty are treated cautiously.
8. **Legitimacy:** Islamic and dynastic sovereignty coexist with Persianate and Roman imperial claims; the unsupported story of a ceremonially completed Abbasid caliphate transfer in 1517 is not treated as factual.
9. **Architecture:** The Süleymaniye mosque was completed in 1557 while buildings in the larger complex continued to be completed afterward (including 1559). The chronological distinction is explicit.
10. **Dynastic women and waqfs:** Hürrem's charitable and political institutions are analyzed through identifiable documentary evidence rather than sensational intrigue; Mihrimah's patronage is recognized.
11. **Succession crises:** Ibrahim's execution in 1536, Mustafa's in 1553, and Bayezid's in 1561 are correctly situated, while disputed allegations and private motives are not presented as established facts.
12. **Szigetvár:** Süleyman died in September 1566 while the fortress was under siege; its fall is correctly placed after his death, with the succession to Selim II and the involvement of Sokollu Mehmed Pasha explained.

**Editorial conclusion:** The manuscript has a coherent argument organized around dynastic, administrative, legal, fiscal, military, diplomatic, and provincial institutions. It avoids both an ahistorical perfect golden age and an inevitable post-1566 decline narrative. Chapters 73–75 remain responsible for the detailed regional and social expansions.
