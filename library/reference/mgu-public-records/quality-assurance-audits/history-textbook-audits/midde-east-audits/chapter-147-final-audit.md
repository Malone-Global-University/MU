## 1. Scope and governing requirements

This audit records the completed revision of the submitted condensed manuscript, not a plan for future expansion. The governing materials are the authoritative Middle East multi-volume chapter outline, the Middle East Master Instructions, the approved four-division Chapter 147 plan, and the historical and editorial audit of the original draft. The submitted HTML was the source for the design shell, section sequence and existing study apparatus. The revision retains these foundations while replacing the abbreviated content with historically developed prose and complete educational materials.

The outline assigns Chapter 147 the Lebanese Civil War, the Israeli invasion, PLO evacuation, Sabra and Shatila, Syrian influence, Iranian involvement, Hezbollah's development, resistance, sectarian politics and the conclusion of the civil war. The chapter now covers all of these subjects in chronological and thematic relationship. It treats the institutional breakdown of 1975 as the starting point, concentrates on 1982–1990, and closes with the postwar arrangements of 1991. It does not attempt to absorb the subsequent history of Lebanon into the assigned period.

The original draft presented one short paragraph per historical section, placeholder definitions and limited source discussion. The completed manuscript addresses those substantive deficiencies through a single integrated expansion. The agreed chapter-specific narrative objective of 15,000–20,000 words is met; the general Master Instructions benchmark is not treated as a reason to truncate a complex history.

### Chapter boundaries

- Chapter 138 remains responsible for the origins of the confessional political order and descent into civil war. The opening sections of Chapter 147 provide continuity rather than a second comprehensive prewar history.
- Chapters 136, 144 and 145 supply background on Palestinian armed politics, the Iranian Revolution and the Islamic Republic. Their subjects are treated here only where needed to explain Lebanese developments.
- Chapter 148 can proceed to comparative political Islam and transnational Islamist networks without having to reconstruct Hezbollah's Lebanese origins.
- Later chapters retain responsibility for post-1991 political developments, the Israeli withdrawal of 2000, the Syrian withdrawal of 2005, the 2006 war and later regional confrontations. Subsequent evidence is sometimes used retrospectively to assess events within Chapter 147; it is labeled accordingly.

## 4. Historical verification and corrections incorporated

The following findings resolve the substantive concerns of the initial audit. Bracketed reference numbers correspond to the numbered source list inside the completed HTML, rather than to an independent numbering system in this audit.

### 4.1 Arafat's evacuation and the continuing Palestinian presence

**Correction:** The revised Section 147.8 distinguishes Arafat's August 30, 1982 departure aboard a Greek vessel bound for Greece from the subsequent establishment of PLO headquarters in Tunis. It explains that the evacuation dispersed fighters among several destinations and did not remove all Palestinian armed organizations or Palestinian civilians from Lebanon. Arafat's return to northern Lebanon and the December 20, 1983 evacuation from Tripoli are included as separate events.

**Evidence:** The contemporary August 31 report in [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/archives/article/1982/08/31/m-yasser-arafat-a-quitte-beyrouth_3144052_1819218.html) and the [JTA contemporary report](https://www.jta.org/archive/arafat-leaves-beirut-aboard-greek-cruise-ship-bound-for-athens-before-going-to-tunis) support the route; the [1982 departure plan](https://www.palquest.org/en/historictext/9658/plan-departure-lebanon-plo-leadership-offices-and-combatants-beirut) and [1983 UN Yearbook account](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-207972/) support the distinction between the Beirut and Tripoli episodes. [15–17, 40]

### 4.2 The 1981 ceasefire and the decision to invade

**Correction:** The narrative and causal feature no longer describe the July 1981 ceasefire as ineffective throughout its existence. Relative quiet along the frontier is distinguished from the broader conflict and competing interpretations of the ceasefire's scope. The Argov assassination attempt is attributed to the Abu Nidal Organization, a rival of Arafat's PLO. The stated forty-kilometer aim, wider strategic planning, government authorization and the actual expansion toward Beirut are examined separately.

**Evidence:** The [U.S. State Department historical account](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1981-1988/lebanon), [IDF First Lebanon War history](https://www.idf.il/en/mini-sites/wars-and-operations/first-lebanon-war/) and [IDF retrospective discussion of the operation](https://www.idf.il/en/articles/2022/operation-peace-for-the-galilee-the-first-lebanon-war/) inform the chronology and stated or acknowledged aims. Official military narratives are institutional accounts, not independent proof of every disputed motive. [10–12]

### 4.3 The separate international forces

**Correction:** Syrian intervention and the Arab Deterrent Force are distinguished from the UN peacekeeping mission created in March 1978 and the multinational deployment associated with the 1982 evacuation and subsequent intervention. A separate comparative reference table summarizes their origins, authority, composition and roles. UNIFIL is not characterized as a force created to conquer or govern Lebanon, and its mandate is distinguished from its limited enforcement capabilities.

**Evidence:** [UNIFIL historical background](https://unifil.unmissions.org/en/background), [Resolution 425](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-189331/), [Resolution 426](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/71623), and the State Department historical account. [4, 6–8, 10]

### 4.4 Sabra and Shatila: perpetrators, responsibility and estimates

**Correction:** Section 147.9 reconstructs the events of September 1982, identifies the Lebanese militiamen as direct perpetrators and separately examines Israeli military control, authorization of militia entry, warnings, failures of prevention and the Kahan Commission's findings. The commission's finding of indirect Israeli responsibility and personal responsibility on Ariel Sharon's part is not conflated with a final accounting of all possible legal responsibility. The perpetrators are not treated as representatives of an entire religious or national community.

The chapter provides attributed rather than unexplained victim figures. The Israeli military estimate of approximately 700–800 victims is distinguished from Bayan al-Hout's documentation of 1,390 cases, including 906 recorded dead and 484 missing. The latter combined count is not described as 1,390 independently confirmed deaths, and neither estimate is declared definitive.

**Evidence:** [Kahan Commission report](https://www.palquest.org/en/historictext/9664/kahan-commission-inquiry-report-events-refugee-camps-beirut), the [archival report reproduction](https://syriaaccountability.org/report-of-the-commission-of-inquiry-into-the-events-at-the-refugee-camps-in-beirut-8-february-1983/), and [Sciences Po's comparative account of the massacre](https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/sabra-and-chatila.html). [18–20]

### 4.5 May 17, 1983 agreement and Western intervention

**Correction:** The text distinguishes the agreement's bilateral provisions from the Israeli government position linking full implementation to the departure of other foreign forces, particularly Syrian forces. The agreement's failure is explained through Syrian opposition, Lebanese domestic conflict and the changing military and diplomatic situation. Its March 1984 abrogation is placed after the February uprising and collapse of the government's immediate military position.

**Evidence:** [English text of the May 17 Agreement](https://content.ecf.org.il/files/M00242_AgreementbetweenIsraelandLebanon-EnglishText_0.pdf), [historical context and abrogation record](https://www.ecf.org.il/issues/issue/183), and [U.S. Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1981-1988/lebanon). The agreement text and contemporary setting are treated as distinct evidence. [10, 21, 23]

### 4.6 The 1983 bombings, hostage-taking and organizational attribution

**Correction:** The established figures of 241 American service members and 58 French military personnel killed in the October 23 barracks bombings are retained. The chapter describes the Iranian-backed networks associated with early Hezbollah and the Islamic Jihad name, distinguishes claims of responsibility and subsequent governmental and judicial attribution, and does not equate all clandestine operations with a fully consolidated organization under an independently established command structure. Hostage-taking and attacks on civilians are explicitly distinguished from attacks on military personnel.

The retrospective U.S. foreign terrorist organization designation is dated to 1997 rather than presented as a legal designation already in existence in 1982–1991. The chapter distinguishes the designating government's legal classification from analysis of specific events and methods.

**Evidence:** [U.S. historical account](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1981-1988/lebanon), [subsequent federal judicial summary concerning Peterson v. Iran](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_01-cv-02094/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_01-cv-02094-10.pdf), [CRS Hezbollah historical account](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20101008_R41446_74080e475b543f8ec66c3a60d2e821ecacedd810.html), [FBI TWA 847 case record](https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/hijacking-of-twa-flight-847), and [1997 U.S. designation list](https://1997-2001.state.gov/global/terrorism/terrorist_orgs_list.html). The federal judicial summary is evidence of later findings; no claim is made that all original case exhibits or intelligence materials were independently examined. [10, 22, 27, 29, 38]

### 4.7 Indigenous Shia mobilization and Hezbollah's emergence

**Correction:** Sections 147.12–147.13 expand the social and political foundations of Amal, the role of Musa al-Sadr and the Supreme Islamic Shia Council, Hussein al-Musawi's Islamic Amal, the Iranian Revolution, the arrival of IRGC personnel with Syrian permission, and the organizational development of Hezbollah. Hezbollah's emergence beginning around 1982 is distinguished from its February 1985 public manifesto and later leadership arrangements. Mohammad Hussein Fadlallah's influence is not equated with formal command.

The Open Letter study identifies allegiance to Khomeini, opposition to occupation and Western intervention, rejection of Israel's legitimacy, its advocated Islamic order and its stated position on popular choice. These are attributed political claims, not endorsement or proof of actual behavior.

**Evidence:** Sabrina Mervin, [“Shiites in Lebanon,” Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/shiites-in-lebanon/), the [identified translation of the 1985 Open Letter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/harvard.9780674067523.c8/html), and the [CRS historical and ideological analysis](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20101008_R41446_74080e475b543f8ec66c3a60d2e821ecacedd810.html). The analysis uses paraphrases; it does not invent quotations or claim independent examination of the complete archive of internal Hezbollah records. [9, 25–27]

### 4.8 Military fragmentation, occupation and intracommunal conflicts

**Correction:** The Lebanese Army is treated as a damaged but surviving institution rather than an entity that vanished in 1975. Its earlier fragmentation is distinguished from the renewed breakdown of 1984. The occupation zone is distinguished from the much wider Israeli presence before the 1985 partial withdrawal. The Khiam detention facility, the SLA and Israeli involvement are examined with qualified attribution. The War of the Camps, Mountain War, intra-Shia fighting and intra-Christian war illustrate the instability of identitarian and external alliances.

**Evidence:** AUB and PalQuest chronological materials, UNIFIL historical background and [Human Rights Watch's 1999 retrospective report](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1999/lebanon/Isrlb997-05.htm) on the southern occupation and detention. The later HRW report is not treated as evidence that every later practice occurred identically during the 1980s. [4–6, 28]

### 4.9 Taif and the implementation of the postwar settlement

**Correction:** The revised -19 and -20 sections distinguish adoption of the Document of National Accord on October 22, 1989, constitutional implementation, the October 13, 1990 defeat of Aoun, and the 1991 demobilization and amnesty arrangements. Parliamentary parity, cabinet authority, the goal of abolishing political sectarianism, militia disarmament and Syrian redeployment are explained as separate provisions.

The six-month disarmament framework is linked to specified institutional milestones. The Syrian two-year framework and later agreement on redeployment are not described as an unconditional two-year deadline for total Syrian withdrawal. Taif's call for dissolution of all Lebanese and non-Lebanese militias is clearly distinguished from the later political treatment of Hezbollah as a resistance force against continued Israeli occupation. There was no explicit Hezbollah exemption named in the original accord.

**Evidence:** [UN Peacemaker, Taif Document of National Accord](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9412), [UN Secretary-General's report pursuant to Resolution 1559](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-180901/), and [CRS Lebanon historical account](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20050610_IB89118_59a9fa6891c6508e428d98e622b35fba8698e4ec.html). The source window presents a structured paraphrase of the English text and identifies its provisions. [32, 34–35]

### 4.10 Casualty estimates, war economy and postwar accountability

**Correction:** The unsupported single death-toll figure is replaced by identifiable, differently constituted estimates. The chapter distinguishes recorded fatalities and broader estimates, including Fawwaz Traboulsi's reported recorded figure of 71,328, World Bank historical estimates of approximately 150,000 deaths, and ICTJ's account of more than 100,000 civilian deaths and approximately 17,000 disappearances. It does not combine these figures into a synthetic total or treat missing people as confirmed dead. The coverage of currency depreciation, militia revenues, employment, displacement, education, humanitarian services and family survival is substantially expanded.

The 1991 amnesty and the May 22, 1991 Syrian-Lebanese Treaty of Brotherhood, Cooperation and Coordination are separately documented. The implications of amnesty for accountability and the limits of postwar sovereignty remain part of the concluding analysis.

**Evidence:** [Makdisi and Sadaka](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/373981468308066282/pdf/344110v20PAPER101Official0use0only1.pdf), Elisabeth Picard's work on the militia economy [3], [Traboulsi's author-provided chapter outline](https://www.fawwaztraboulsi.com/en/books/a-history-of-modern-lebanon-first-arabic-edition/), [ICTJ Lebanon](https://www.ictj.org/where-we-work/lebanon), [World Bank 2016 country diagnostic](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/951911467995104328/pdf/103201-REPLACEMENT-PUBLIC-Lebanon-SCD-Le-Borgne-and-Jacobs-2016.pdf), [UN treaty registration 28932](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/showDetails.aspx?objid=08000002800b9fe2), and [Amnesty International's 1997 report on Lebanon](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/mde180191997en.pdf). The Traboulsi figure is drawn from the author's published outline; its underlying enumeration was not independently reconstructed. [1, 3, 30–31, 33, 36–37, 39]

## 5. Documentary and methodological apparatus

The primary-source window now examines Taif in its constitutional, military and diplomatic dimensions. It identifies the document, contextualizes the negotiations, differentiates stated obligations from actual implementation and supplies five analysis questions. The supplemental document study addresses six documents: Resolution 425, Resolution 509, the Kahan Commission report, the May 17 Agreement, the Open Letter and Taif. The supplementary feature contains five comparative source questions; the end-of-chapter source questions are a separate set of six.

The bibliography contains 41 numbered entries, including contemporary reporting, treaty and agreement texts, United Nations records, official institutional histories, scholarly studies, human-rights documentation and further reading. The main sections and analytical features provide numbered references. The revision distinguishes contemporary documents from retrospective investigations and political self-description from verified conduct. It avoids invented quotations, invented eyewitness testimony and unsupported numerical precision.

The presence of a scholarly reference does not imply that every page of a book or archival collection has been independently examined. Some entries are explicitly identified as further reading. This is a targeted verification of material claims and source studies, not an exhaustive archival or independent peer review. The Taif English text was available in extracted form, supplemented by an official subsequent UN account; a PDF screenshot request was blocked. The May 17 agreement PDF was inspected using its rendered page as well as its text. Availability of external sources can change, and an exhaustive live HTTP check of every reference was not performed.

## 8. Remaining limitations and editorial disposition

The remaining historical uncertainty concerns the nature of the evidence itself: incompleteness of wartime casualty inventories, conflicting estimates of individual controversies, clandestine organizational command relationships, and the distinction between stated intentions and actual political decisions. These issues are now identified and qualified in the chapter rather than resolved through unsupported claims. Later political and legal evidence is dated and contextualized. No additional routine narrative expansion or structural pass is recommended.

**Final scholarly disposition:** The approved architecture has been fulfilled, the audited historical corrections incorporated, the required educational apparatus completed, and the chapter-specific depth objective met. The manuscript is ready for first-edition textual publication and editorial lock. The remaining operational gate is deployment through MGU's normal publication workflow; the audit does not assert that the chapter is already live.
