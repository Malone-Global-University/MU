## C. Historical accuracy: principal controls and corrections

1. **Ismaili origins and genealogies.** §54.1 distinguishes the Fatimid dynasty's claim to descent from Fatima and Ali from independently demonstrable genealogical proof; it does not flatten all early Ismaili and Qarmatian factions into one movement.
2. **Kutama political agency.** §§54.2–54.4 treat Kutama groups as political/military founders and explain the missionary movement, the Aghlabid displacement in 909, Mahdiyya and al-Mansuriyya, Egypt's failed early expeditions, and Abu Yazid's dangerous revolt. The story is not a retrospective inevitability narrative.
3. **Egyptian conquest and urban chronology.** §§54.5–54.8 distinguish Jawhar's conquest and Cairo's foundation in **969** from al-Muizz's arrival and transfer of court in **973**; Fustat's continuing commercial role remains explicit. The al-Azhar narrative distinguishes founding in 970, first khutba in 972, and developed teaching associated with 988.
4. **Jawhar's guarantee.** The `aman` window analyzes a political promise transmitted in later narratives. It is not offered as conclusive proof of policy implementation or presented as a fabricated verbatim quotation.
5. **Religious complexity.** §§54.9–54.12 explain the Ismaili daʿwa, jurisprudence, administrative pragmatism, al-Aziz's policies, al-Hakim's 1009 destruction of the Holy Sepulchre, Dar al-Ilm in 1005, and the emergence of the Druze movement without psychologizing al-Hakim as a factual conclusion.
6. **Egypt's social and economic history.** §§54.13–54.16 distinguish Nile agricultural revenue, Fustat's markets, Mediterranean and Red Sea commerce, civic work, scholarship, and material culture. Geniza documents are specifically dated and not generalized across centuries or communities.
7. **1097 original document.** Geniza window identifies **CUL Add.3418 + CUL Add.3421**, dated **9 November 1097**, in Judaeo-Arabic, concerning the India trader Yosef ha-Lebdi and merchant representative Yequtiʾel ibn Moshe. This is an identifiable Fustat court record in the Princeton Geniza Project (PGPID 5388), not a hypothetical letter.
8. **Rival caliphates.** §§54.17–54.18 distinguish Fatimid religious recognition, provincial allegiance, commerce, and direct control. Al-Basasiri's occupation of Baghdad in **1058–1059** is described as temporary rather than permanent conquest of Iraq.
9. **Mid-eleventh-century crisis.** §54.19 treats the interaction of Nile failures, military factionalism, transport disruption, food shortage, and fiscal weakness; speculative catastrophic statistics are avoided. Badr al-Jamali's summons in the early 1070s is distinguished from his **1074** intervention and consolidation.
10. **Endpoint.** §54.20 distinguishes the **1094** succession dispute and Nizari–Mustaʿli division from the dynasty's formal termination in **1171**, which is an epilogue reserved for fuller treatment in Chapter 61.

## G. Editorial boundaries and remaining limitations

The chapter consciously avoids duplicating Chapter 53's account of Buyid administration, Chapter 51's full maritime history, and the future Chapter 61 treatment of Saladin's establishment of Ayyubid rule. It remains focused on Fatimid state formation, Cairo, Egyptian institutions, religious policy, and the competing caliphal order. The 1171 dissolution receives a clearly marked concluding epilogue.

**Limitations disclosed:** (1) medieval source windows are editorial paraphrases, not independently original-page-collated quotations; (2) geography studies require a historical atlas and have no embedded map plates; (3) the report records historical verification against selected reliable specialist publications and source records, not exhaustive manuscript, numismatic or archaeological cataloguing; (4) the book is a scholarly textual first edition, not an illustrated critical source edition.
