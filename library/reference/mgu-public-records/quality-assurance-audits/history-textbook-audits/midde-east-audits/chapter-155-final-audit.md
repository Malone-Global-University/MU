## III. Principal factual corrections implemented

### A. June 1989 succession versus July constitutional revision

The new chapter clearly separates Khomeini's death on **3 June 1989**, Khamenei's selection by the Assembly of Experts on **4 June**, and the **28 July** ratification of revised constitutional provisions. It distinguishes Khamenei's initially problematic clerical qualification under the earlier rules from the subsequent elimination of the *marja* requirement, and explains the abolition of the prime minister's office and redistribution of powers. The revision also contextualizes Montazeri's removal as prospective successor, including his objections to political repression. The original draft alluded to these issues but did not sufficiently develop the constitutional timing or institutional significance.

**Principal evidence:** [1] revised constitutional text and [2] *Encyclopaedia Iranica* account of the constitution. The text distinguishes constitutional law from reconstruction of elite political bargaining and does not invent private dialogue.

### B. Khatami's elections and limits on reform

The chapter uses the documented **23 May 1997** election and Khatami's reported **69.1%** share, and identifies his second election in **June 2001**, with approximately **77.1%**. These are grounded in the Iran Data Portal election records [3]–[5]. The prose expands the 1998 political murders and 1999 student protests, distinguishing the authorities' explanation of unauthorized Intelligence Ministry actors from independently established institutional responsibility. It explains Guardian Council vetoes and the importance of candidate disqualifications before the 2004 parliamentary election without treating all abstention as automatic support for reformists.

**Assessment:** Historical chronology and institutional interpretation are defensible. Exact casualty figures for the 1999 events were deliberately not inserted where available source series and classifications are variable.

### C. Ahmadinejad, the 2009 election and the Green Movement

The chapter differentiates the 2005 runoff, Ahmadinejad's economic-populist appeal, the wider conservative movement and the continuing constitutional powers of unelected institutions. It corrects the earlier presentation of **62.6%** in the 2009 election as though it were the only official series: contemporary official announcements commonly gave that approximate percentage, while a tabulation reproduced by the Iran Data Portal reports **63.1%** under a different published series or denominator. The prose attributes both and does **not** represent the reported victory as independently validated. It separately treats allegations of irregularities, restricted verification, the Green Movement's varied aims, and documented security-force and detention abuses. The 2011 confinement of Mousavi, Rahnavard and Karroubi is correctly placed after the 2009 protest cycle.

**Principal evidence:** Iran Data Portal [7]–[8], Human Rights Watch's contemporary World Report 2010 [9], and USIP chronology [10]. The text does not assert a precise national death toll that the documentary basis here cannot establish reliably.

### D. Economic policy and subsidy reform

The reconstructed narrative distinguishes Rafsanjani's reconstruction, foreign-debt pressures and the rise of foundation or IRGC-linked commerce from the later policies of Ahmadinejad. It gives the **December 2010** energy/commodity price increases and offsetting direct household transfers their own treatment rather than collapsing them into a claim of simple austerity. It explains why households and firms faced different consequences and how contemporaneous IMF evaluations differ in scope from later retrospective assessments.

**Principal evidence:** IMF Country Report 11/241 [11] and Working Paper 11/167 [12]; *Encyclopaedia Iranica* and Stanford research [13]–[14]. No unsupported headline figure has been assigned to individual groups or provinces.

### E. Safeguards failures, voluntary diplomacy and legal instruments

The nuclear sections distinguish the 2002 public disclosures from the IAEA's **2003** safeguards findings concerning material and activity reporting. They explain that disclosure of an unfinished facility is not in every case a completed safeguards violation, and that enrichment, conversion, heavy-water work and civilian power generation serve different technical purposes. The **21 October 2003 Tehran statement** and **14 November 2004 Paris Agreement** are differentiated from the December 2003 signature (without ratification) of the Additional Protocol. The chapter identifies the Paris suspension as a **voluntary confidence-building measure**, not an ordinary standing safeguards obligation. This distinction is documented in the source text reproduced in UK parliamentary materials [15] and contemporaneous records reviewed by CRS [16].

### F. UN resolutions and November 2011 IAEA report

The narrative differentiates **Resolution 1696 (July 2006)**, which demanded suspension, from **1737 (December 2006)**, which began sanctions; it then explains the incremental roles of **1747 (2007)**, **1803 (2008)** and **1929 (June 2010)**. It clearly separates UN measures from additional U.S. and European restrictions. The chapter does not claim that the November 2011 IAEA report proved Iran possessed an operational weapon. Nor does it understate the Agency's findings as vague speculation: GOV/2011/65 described credible information about activities relevant to a nuclear explosive device, indications of a structured program prior to late 2003, and indications that some relevant work continued thereafter, with unresolved questions as of the report date. Iranian objections are attributed as positions, not automatically validated or dismissed.

**Principal evidence:** actual reproduced GOV/2011/65 [17] and annex [18]; Security Council institutional resolution descriptions [19] and independent chronology [20]–[21]. This is the chapter's most important technical and evidentiary correction.

## IV. Social and interpretive completeness

The completed narrative avoids treating political change as an uninterrupted struggle of undifferentiated reformists against undifferentiated conservatives. It distinguishes clerical from elected and military institutions, explains disagreement within factions, and evaluates the effects of economic policy on households, workers, students and women. Separate attention is given to Baha'is and to the distinct experiences of Azeri, Kurdish, Arab, Baluch and Sunni communities. The discussion of minority status is descriptive and sourced at an appropriate level; it avoids claiming identical interests for every community.

The regional division recognizes Iranian influence without reducing Hezbollah or Iraqi political parties to instruments lacking independent constituencies. It discusses Afghanistan and Iraq after 2001 only to the extent needed to explain **Iranian** policy, preserving the fuller Iraq-war narrative for Chapter 156. The chapter ends in **2011**, avoiding anachronistic coverage of the 2013 Iranian election, the 2015 JCPOA and subsequent regional wars. The post-2011 scholarship in the bibliography is identified as retrospective interpretation, not evidence available to contemporaries.
