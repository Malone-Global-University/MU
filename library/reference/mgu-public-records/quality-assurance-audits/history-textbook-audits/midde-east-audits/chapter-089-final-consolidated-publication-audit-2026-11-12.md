## 4. Publication-critical historical corrections

1. **Azov chronology / 1711–1739:** The early-century context now explicitly recognizes Peter I's 1696 capture of Azov, its surrender after the 1711 Pruth campaign, and severe fortification and naval restrictions in the 1739 peace. It does not claim Russia started the century without a southern position, or that the Great Northern War had already ended by 1711. Specific 1739 ownership formulations vary in secondary summaries; the chapter emphasizes defensible treaty restrictions pending original-clause collation.
2. **Küçük Kaynarca (1774):** Article 3 political independence is distinguished from annexation in 1783. Article 7 and Article 14 interpretation is described as disputed rather than asserting an unconditional Russian protectorate over every Ottoman Orthodox Christian. Merchant navigation is distinguished from unrestricted movement of Russian warships through the Turkish Straits. Ottoman religious role in Crimea is context-specific, not assumed a timeless uniform caliphal jurisdiction.
3. **Crimea (1774–1783):** Şahin Giray, the 1778 government-directed relocation of Crimean Christians, the 1779 Aynalıkavak Convention, Russian intervention, annexation, and uneven administrative incorporation are separately developed. Forced movement is not conflated with later nineteenth-century Caucasian expulsions.
4. **Black Sea operations:** The strategic difference between Kherson, Sevastopol, Ochakov, and later Odessa is maintained. The second war and January 1792 Jassy peace are placed accurately without implying Russian control of the Straits or disappearance of Ottoman political capacity.
5. **Georgia:** The 1783 Treaty of Georgievsk created a protectorate; the destruction of Tbilisi in 1795 showed the failure of protection; 1801 incorporation is confined to epilogue/reference context and is **not** silently dated to 1783.
6. **Sheikh Mansur:** The unsupportable assertion of proven formal Naqshbandi membership was removed. The chapter explains that his exact affiliation remains disputed (Bennigsen), identifies his capture at Anapa in 1791 and death in Russian captivity in 1794, and avoids projecting Imam Shamil's nineteenth-century organization backward.
7. **Captives and treaties:** Article XXV of Küçük Kaynarca is identified by actual article number; release rights and conversion-related exceptions are discussed without inventing a prisoner or declaring all slavery abolished. Smiley's analysis of the emergent state prisoner-of-war system is differentiated from treaty language.
