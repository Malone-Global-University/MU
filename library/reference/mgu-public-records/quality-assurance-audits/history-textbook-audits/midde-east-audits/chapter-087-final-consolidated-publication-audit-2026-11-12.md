## 4. Historical accuracy and specialist source verification

**First Saudi State and founding chronology.** The manuscript differentiates Muhammad ibn Saud's early authority and modern **1727** commemoration from the traditional **1744** religious–political pact. Cole Bunzel's analysis of the modern Founding Day revision: “Refounding the Kingdom,” *Middle Eastern Studies* 61/6 (2025): 927–941, https://doi.org/10.1080/00263206.2025.2482632. Religious–political state formation is treated with reference to Bunzel, *Wahhabism* (Princeton, 2023), https://academic.oup.com/princeton-scholarship-online/book/56937; David Commins (2006); Michael Crawford (2014); and Michael Cook, “On the Origins of Wahhābism,” *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* 2/2 (1992): 191–202, https://doi.org/10.1017/S1356186300002376. No single source is taken as a verbatim account of an alleged 1744 pact.

**Religious doctrine and controversy.** The chapter explains *tawhid*, *shirk*, and *takfir* distinctly and addresses disputes among Muslim scholars rather than treating reform as unanimously accepted. Modern interpretive comparisons draw on Ahmad Dallal, *Islam without Europe* (UNC Press, 2018), https://uncpress.org/9781469641409/islam-without-europe/; Tarik K. Firro, “The Political Context of Early Wahhabi Discourse of Takfir,” *Middle Eastern Studies* 49/5 (2013): 770–789, https://doi.org/10.1080/00263206.2013.811648. *Kitab al-Tawhid* is named but no uncollated critical edition or fabricated quotations are inserted.

**Najd, al-Ahsa, and the Hijaz.** The struggle with Banu Khalid is represented as an extended conflict with major successes in the **1790s** rather than a single permanent victory in 1795. Najdi expansion, control of oases, and changes in allegiance are distinguished. The chapter also keeps **Mecca's 1803 seizure** outside its main chronological period. The Ottoman–sharifian Hajj framework is grounded in Nir Shafir, “In an Ottoman Holy Land: The Hajj and the Road from Damascus, 1500–1800,” *History of Religions* 60/1 (2020), https://doi.org/10.1086/709169. Suraiya Faroqhi, *Pilgrims and Sultans* (1994) covers **1517–1683** and is correctly marked earlier-period institutional background.

**Oman.** The Ya‘ruba anti-Portuguese maritime background includes **1650** (Muscat) and **1698** (Fort Jesus). Ahmad bin Said's **1749 election** is distinguished from preceding consolidation during the Ya‘ruba civil war and Iranian intervention, following Jeremy Jones and Nicholas Ridout, *A History of Modern Oman* (Cambridge, 2015), chapter 1, https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/history-of-modern-oman/oman-and-the-al-bu-said/883ADF91B35D6E7ABD74B6F5C0F2F2AA. Omani *aflaj* and Ibadi local governance are supported by John C. Wilkinson and Soumyen Bandyopadhyay with Birgit Mershen, “Falaj Communities in Oman” (2022), https://doi.org/10.1163/26666286-12340028. Nineteenth-century Zanzibar-centered institutions are not projected backward onto Ahmad's rule.

**Yemen.** The Qasimi imamate is situated after **1635–1636**, with territorial differentiation between Zaydi highlands and Shafi‘i and other regions. Hadramawt is NOT depicted as continuously governed from Sana'a in the eighteenth century. The reigns of **al-Mahdi Abbas, 1748–1775**, and **al-Mansur Ali, 1775–1809** (only to 1800 in the main narrative), are distinguished; **Muhammad al-Shawkani's appointment in 1795** is documented in specialist research on Yemen, including Bernard Haykel, *Revival and Reform in Islam* (2003), and the article “Al-Shawkâni and the Jurisprudential Unity of Yemen,” https://www.persee.fr/doc/remmm_0997-1327_1993_num_67_1_1587. Nancy Um, *The Merchant Houses of Mocha* (University of Washington Press, 2009), https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295989105/the-merchant-houses-of-mocha/, supports the port economy. The manuscript does not invent a sudden one-year collapse of Yemeni coffee commerce.

**Niebuhr bibliography and source status.** Carsten Niebuhr's *Beschreibung von Arabien* (Copenhagen: Nicolaus Möller, **1772**) is cataloged at Heidelberg University Library, https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/niebuhr1772, with **printed pp. 181–283** identified as the Yemen section. The chapter notes Yemen travel in 1762–1763 and does not invent a German quotation or translation. The catalog and section locator were checked; the individual original pages were NOT collated for text or claims in this edition.

**Epilogue boundary.** The 1802 Karbala attack, 1803 seizure of Mecca, Ottoman–Egyptian intervention in the 1810s, and 1818 destruction of Diriyah are identified as subsequent developments, not falsely presented within 1700–1800.
