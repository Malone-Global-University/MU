## 1. Executive assessment

The submitted Chapter 146 draft contained all twenty approved headings, but most of its sections consisted of a single paragraph. It established a sound historical framework without explaining the war at the depth required for a university-level textbook. The final revision retains the four-division architecture, the original chapter design, and the assigned historical boundaries. It replaces the condensed historical narrative with fully developed treatments of the war's origins, changing military balance, international dimensions, social costs, and termination. It also completes the reference and instructional apparatus and incorporates the historical corrections identified in the initial review.

The completed narrative explains why Iraq initiated the full-scale invasion in September 1980, why its original strategy failed, why Iran continued the war after recovering most occupied territory, and why the conflict ended in 1988 without an immediate comprehensive settlement. It integrates the international arms trade, Gulf finance, chemical warfare, urban bombardment, maritime attacks, direct U.S.–Iranian confrontation, civilian experiences, and the unequal political and economic consequences for Iran and Iraq. The analysis does not reduce the war to an ancient sectarian rivalry or to a direct Cold War proxy conflict. It does not treat a comparative study of the belligerents as an equivalence of responsibility for aggression or for specific violations of international law.

The chapter is approved for first-edition textual publication on the basis of the consolidated revision and the targeted source checks described below. This is an editorial assessment, not a claim that every statement has been independently verified against original military archives or that a live website deployment has been tested.

## 2. Governing documents and approved scope

The review used the original Chapter 146 HTML draft, the approved twenty-section chapter plan, the authoritative Middle East chapter outline, and the Middle East Master Instructions. The outline assigns Chapter 146 responsibility for Saddam Hussein's invasion, Iranian resistance, trench warfare, chemical weapons, attacks on cities and shipping, Gulf-state involvement, superpower policy, casualties, and long-term political and social consequences.

The approved chronological focus is 1980–1988, with necessary background on the 1975 frontier settlement and the 1979 Iranian Revolution. Limited postwar discussion explains implementation of the ceasefire, repatriation, the 1991 UN assessment of responsibility, and the emergence of subsequent regional problems. These extensions are necessary to understand the war's termination and consequences; they do not convert Chapter 146 into a general history of the 1990s.

The general length guidance is not a fixed ceiling. The final main narrative exceeds fifteen thousand words through substantive development of all twenty sections. Including front matter, features, exercises, references, and navigation, the complete manuscript exceeds the original 15,000–20,000-word overall production objective. The additional material serves the assigned subject and required pedagogical apparatus rather than simply extending the manuscript to an arbitrary file size.

## 3. Final historical corrections and documentary verification

### 3.1. The Algiers settlement — Section 146.1

**Original issue:** The draft treated the March 1975 political agreement as a single completed river-boundary settlement and omitted the land-frontier and security provisions.

**Final correction:** The chapter distinguishes the March 6, 1975, communiqué negotiated in Algiers from the Treaty concerning the State Frontier and Neighbourly Relations signed in Baghdad on June 13, 1975, with its accompanying protocols. It identifies the river-boundary, land-demarcation, and frontier-security components, and explains the cessation of Iranian support for Iraqi Kurdish insurgents. It also distinguishes the thalweg from an arbitrary geometric midpoint and the Iraqi-Iranian settlement from the unrelated 1981 Algiers Declarations between Iran and the United States.

**Verification:** [UN Treaty Collection, registration no. 14903](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/showDetails.aspx?objid=080000028010131f); [FRUS, 1969–1976, vol. XXVII, document 273](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d273). The treaty record confirms the dates and registration of the treaty and associated instruments. The contemporary U.S. assessment identifies the three principal aspects of the March communiqué.

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.2. Origins of the invasion and responsibility — Sections 146.2–146.3

**Original issue:** The initial draft gave a concise account of Iraqi strategic calculations without distinguishing the preceding border incidents, Saddam's stated justifications, and the decision to launch a full-scale invasion.

**Final correction:** The narrative now separates the political and security background, the Iraqi government's repudiation of the 1975 agreement on September 17, 1980, and the air and ground invasion beginning on September 22. It explains the Iranian government's revolutionary challenges, the Iraqi government's domestic repression, and the limited evidentiary value of official claims about private motives. Earlier hostilities are not presented as establishing an Iraqi right to invade.

**Verification:** [Saskia M. Gieling, Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Iraq vii. Iran-Iraq War”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iraq-vii-iran-iraq-war/); [UN Secretary-General, report S/23273, December 9, 1991](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/133224). The latter is a subsequent assessment of responsibility and must not be confused with the original text of Resolution 598.

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.3. Iranian military recovery and the 1982 decision — Sections 146.4–146.7

**Final development:** The chapter distinguishes the fall of Khorramshahr in October 1980, the defense and relief of Abadan in September 1981, and the recapture of Khorramshahr on May 24, 1982. It develops the intervening offensives and the strategic consequences of Iraq's retreat. The July 1982 Operation Ramadan is treated as a new phase of the war, rather than another operation to recover the same occupied Iranian territory.

Iranian security concerns, demands for responsibility and reparations, political objectives, and the effect of previous military successes receive separate treatment. The counterfactual possibility of an earlier settlement is discussed without asserting that the proposed 1982 terms guaranteed a comprehensive and enforceable peace. The account also avoids reducing all Iranian operations to mass infantry assaults.

**Verification:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Iran-Iraq War](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iraq-vii-iran-iraq-war/); [Security Council Resolution 514 (1982)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/31564/); [Annie Tracy Samuel, “Pursuing the Aggressor,” in The Unfinished History of the Iran-Iraq War (2021)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/unfinished-history-of-the-iraniraq-war/pursuing-the-aggressor/3D667096452ACAA231423B416CE71BA9). Samuel's published chapter summary supports the discussion of how Revolutionary Guards histories interpreted security considerations. This is not presented as direct verification of every confidential deliberation.

**Disposition:** Substantially developed; interpretive uncertainty appropriately retained.

### 3.4. Chemical warfare — Section 146.8

**Original issue:** The draft identified Iraqi chemical attacks and the Geneva Protocol but gave little documentary explanation and minimal treatment of victims and enforcement.

**Final correction:** The final chapter distinguishes the historical evidence of Iraqi chemical warfare, the effects of different agents, the 1984 UN specialist investigation, subsequent international condemnation, and the specific chronology of Resolutions 612 and 620. It identifies the 1925 Geneva Protocol as the applicable historical instrument rather than retrospectively applying the later Chemical Weapons Convention. It acknowledges differences in the evidence available concerning the belligerents, while making the extensive documentation of repeated Iraqi use unambiguous.

Halabja is placed within the wartime use of chemical weapons, while the full account of the Anfal genocide is reserved for Chapter 149. Victims of state-directed persecution are not automatically incorporated into a single interstate military death count. The chapter distinguishes documented injuries and long-term consequences from uncertain aggregate estimates.

**Verification:** [UN specialist report S/16433, March 26, 1984](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/63325); [Resolution 612, May 9, 1988](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/161154); [Resolution 620, August 26, 1988](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/44865); [Human Rights Watch, Genocide in Iraq (1993)](https://www.hrw.org/report/1993/07/01/genocide-iraq/anfal-campaign-against-kurds).

**Disposition:** Resolved. The chapter provides a direct supplementary comparison of the investigations and resolutions.

### 3.5. War of the Cities — Section 146.9

**Original issue:** The draft concentrated on the 1985 and 1987–1988 campaigns and provided insufficient coverage of earlier escalation.

**Final correction:** The narrative acknowledges attacks on border settlements from the beginning of the war and identifies the 1984 escalation and June UN-mediated arrangements concerning civilian centers. It traces the subsequent campaigns through 1988 and describes displacement, urban disruption and humanitarian-law issues. It does not imply that every aerial attack belonged to an identically defined named campaign.

An attributed 2010 study is used to illustrate the consequences of recorded ballistic-missile impacts on Iranian cities: 2,312 civilian deaths and 11,625 injuries. The text expressly limits these figures to the study's subject and does not identify them as an aggregate urban-war or bilateral casualty total.

**Verification:** [UNIIMOG historical background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/uniimogbackgr.html); [Civilian casualties of Iranian cities by ballistic missile attacks during the Iraq-Iran war, PubMed PMID 20356443](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20356443/).

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.6. Gulf governments, arms suppliers and wartime finance — Sections 146.10–146.15

**Final development:** The manuscript differentiates Iraq's attack on Iranian petroleum infrastructure and Iran's escalation against shipping linked to Iraqi supporters. It examines Saudi and Kuwaiti finance, the GCC's establishment on May 25, 1981, Oman's distinct diplomatic posture, and differences in commercial interests within the UAE. It distinguishes American economic, diplomatic and intelligence assistance from Soviet, French, Chinese and other military supply networks; the relationships are not represented as two fixed blocs.

The Iran-Contra affair is identified as a separate episode from the Tehran embassy hostage crisis. The revised economic analysis distinguishes foreign-exchange reserves, external debt, particular creditor groups, military expenditure and broader economic loss. It includes attributed historical figures for Iraq's approximately $35 billion in reserves in 1980 and its roughly $37 billion in obligations to Gulf creditors by 1990; these different measures and dates are intentionally not combined into a single war-cost figure.

**Verification:** [GCC Secretariat, founding history](https://www.gcc-sg.org/en/AboutUs/Pages/FoundingDay.aspx); [SIPRI, The Iraq–Iran War and the Arms Trade (1984)](https://www.sipri.org/publications/1984/iraq-iran-war-and-arms-trade); [CRS, Iran: U.S. Policy and Options](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/97-231.html); [CRS, Iraq's Economy: Past, Present, Future](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL31944.html); [Simon Hinrichsen, “The rise of Iraqi indebtedness, 1979–2003” (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00263206.2022.2081553); [Office of the Historian, The Gulf War, 1991](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1989-1992/gulf-war).

**Disposition:** Substantially developed and documentarily grounded.

### 3.7. Karbala-4 and Karbala-5 — Section 146.16

**Original error:** The draft placed both operations in early 1987.

**Final correction:** Operation Karbala-4 began on December 24, 1986; Operation Karbala-5 began on January 9, 1987. Their sequence, distinct objectives, losses, and failure to secure a decisive breakthrough toward Basra are developed separately. The chronology and review exercises preserve the corrected dates.

**Verification:** U.S. Marine Corps, FMFRP 3-203, *Lessons Learned: The Iran-Iraq War*, volume 1, Karbala campaign chronology; [Pesach Malovany, Wars of Modern Babylon (2017)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1pwtdcv). Precise unit strengths and casualty claims are not repeated as settled facts where the evidence is incomplete.

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.8. USS Stark and the international naval confrontation — Section 146.17

**Original error:** The draft reported a single missile striking USS Stark.

**Final correction:** On May 17, 1987, an Iraqi aircraft launched two Exocet missiles that struck the American frigate, killing 37 sailors. The chapter explains Iraq's explanation and the continued overall orientation of U.S. Gulf policy. It distinguishes Earnest Will, Nimble Archer, the mining of USS Samuel B. Roberts, and Praying Mantis, and relates these events to the growing pressure on Iran.

The destruction of Iran Air Flight 655 on July 3, 1988 is accurately identified as the loss of a scheduled civilian airliner with all 290 passengers and crew. American and Iranian interpretations are distinguished without creating uncertainty about the aircraft's civilian character or the documented deaths. The later legal dispute is not represented as having produced a merits judgment establishing the legality of the shooting.

**Verification:** [Naval History and Heritage Command, USS Stark](https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/museums/nmusn/explore/photography/ships-us/ships-us-s/uss-stark-ffg-31.html); [NHHC, Carlisle A. H. Trost](https://www.history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/people/chiefs-of-naval-operations/admiral-carlisle-a--h--trost.html); [Global Affairs Canada, historical summary of Flight 655, Annex A](https://international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/reports/flight-ps752/report-2022); [International Court of Justice, Aerial Incident of 3 July 1988](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/79).

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.9. Iran's acceptance of Resolution 598 and the final offensives — Sections 146.18–146.19

**Original issue:** The compressed chronology risked presenting the final Mojahedin offensive and Operation Mersad as causes of the prior Iranian decision to accept Resolution 598.

**Final correction:** Iraq's 1988 counteroffensives, including Fao in April and Majnoon in June, are established before the acceptance of the ceasefire framework. Iran formally notified the Secretary-General on July 17; public announcements followed, and Khomeini publicly endorsed acceptance on July 20. The Mojahedin attack and Operation Mersad occurred later in July, during the interval before the actual ceasefire. The battlefield confrontation is kept distinct from the executions of political prisoners discussed in Chapter 145.

**Verification:** [UNIIMOG historical background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/uniimogbackgr.html); [Khomeini, “On Accepting UN Security Council Resolution 598” (July 1988), Iran Data Portal](https://irandataportal.syr.edu/on-accepting-un-security-council-resolution-598-july-1988); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Iran-Iraq War](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iraq-vii-iran-iraq-war/). Differences between the formal diplomatic notification and public announcement explain the July 17/18 variation in historical accounts.

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.10. Resolution 598, the ceasefire and subsequent implementation — Section 146.19

**Original issue:** The draft compressed the resolution's provisions, acceptance, establishment of UNIIMOG, ceasefire, and eventual findings concerning responsibility.

**Final correction:** The text now distinguishes the resolution's adoption on July 20, 1987; the July 1988 acceptance; agreement on implementation in August; Resolution 619 establishing UNIIMOG on August 9; and the commencement of the ceasefire on August 20, 1988. The primary-source window examines the operative paragraphs individually, including the demand for cessation and withdrawal, provisions for observers and prisoners, and paragraph 6's requested consideration of an impartial inquiry.

The resolution did not itself identify an aggressor or create a final reparations award. The subsequent findings of the Secretary-General on December 9, 1991 are discussed as a distinct document. The return of prisoners and the supervised withdrawal are similarly distinguished from the cessation of major hostilities. The observer mission concluded in February 1991; prisoner repatriations continued much longer.

**Verification:** [Resolution 598 (1987)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/137345); [Resolution 619 (1988)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/44162); [UNIIMOG historical background](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/uniimogbackgr.html); [Secretary-General S/23273 (1991)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/133224); [ICRC, historical perspectives on neutral intermediation and repatriation](https://blogs.icrc.org/cross-files/the-icrc-as-a-neutral-intermediary-historical-and-contemporary-perspectives/).

**Disposition:** Resolved.

### 3.11. Human losses and casualty methodology — Section 146.20

**Original issue:** The condensed chapter appropriately avoided a false precision but lacked sufficient attributed quantitative evidence.

**Final correction:** The chapter presents selected, differently scoped demographic and epidemiological estimates. A 2020 study reports 183,623 Iranian lost lives, 554,990 injured people and 40,240 captured. A separate study of registered Iranian war-related deaths spans 1980–2005 and accounts for the possibility of later deaths associated with the conflict. A 2016 Iraqi sibling-history study estimates nearly 200,000 adult deaths from the Iran-Iraq War. The narrative explains why the Iranian registration data and Iraqi survey estimate cannot be mechanically summed into a definitive bilateral total.

Earlier strategic estimates and broader casualty figures are acknowledged as differently constructed. Deaths, total casualties, injuries, prisoners, missing persons, displaced populations, and deaths from internal persecution are not treated as interchangeable categories. The ICRC's account of the last prisoner exchange in 2003 illustrates the continuation of human consequences after 1988.

**Verification:** [Human Casualties and War, PubMed PMID 32349506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32349506/); [Years of life lost, PMID 24344985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24344985/); [Iraqi war mortality, PMID 27768730](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27768730/); [ICRC, frequently asked questions about its work in Iraq](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/faq-icrc-work-iraq-information-people-affected).

**Disposition:** Resolved. Uncertainty about the aggregate total is deliberately preserved as an evidentiary limitation.

## 6. Chapter boundaries and volume integration

- **Chapter 144:** The revolution and overthrow of the monarchy remain background, not a repeated narrative.
- **Chapter 145:** The formation and consolidation of the Islamic Republic, factional conflict and political prisoner executions remain the responsibility of Chapter 145. Chapter 146 examines the military and wartime effects of those institutions and distinguishes Operation Mersad from the prison executions. No revision of Chapter 145 was undertaken.
- **Chapter 149:** Kurdish resistance, Halabja and the Anfal genocide appear where necessary to explain warfare and civilian persecution. The full regional and genocidal history remains assigned to Chapter 149.
- **Chapter 150:** The debt burden, regional militarization and residual diplomatic issues prepare the account of the Kuwait crisis; the 1990 invasion and 1991 coalition campaign are not retold.
- **Chapter 155:** Iranian postwar reconstruction and institutional developments are introduced only to establish the consequences of the conflict.

**Finding:** The four-division sequence and division of historical responsibility are preserved. No additional main section or structural reorganization is necessary.

## 8. Residual scholarly limitations

Several aspects of the war remain subject to legitimate historical disagreement or incomplete evidence: private deliberations in Baghdad and Tehran, the relative weight of Iranian leaders' motives in 1982, exact unit-level losses, the attribution and timing of particular incidents, the aggregate fiscal and demographic cost of the war, and the diversity of individual wartime experiences. The chapter addresses these limitations through attribution, source distinction and cautious language rather than artificial numerical precision or assertions of universal scholarly consensus.

The review confirms the central chronology and checks the publication-critical facts against the identified documentary and scholarly sources. It does not claim an exhaustive archival reconstruction of every operation, inspection of every source in full, or a certified specialist peer review. The chapter's linked reference records provide a basis for future source-focused research. The underlying narrative and instructional structure do not require another expansion pass solely because these legitimate historical uncertainties persist.
