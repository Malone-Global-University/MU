## 3. Scholarly content and historical qualifications

**Family and lifecycle (§§85.1–85.4):** Distinguishes residence, household membership, kinship, property rights, guardianship, marriage, deferred dower, and court-registered divorce from generalized household stereotypes. Leslie Peirce's Aintab scholarship supplies the period-specific documentary setting. The narrative does not falsely treat a sixteenth-century Aintab family as a universal model for Iran, Egypt, or rural Anatolia.

**Gender and labor (§§85.5–85.8):** Treats enforceable claims and legal standing separately from practical equality, with estate and endowment evidence and historical differences in class, legal status, and geography. Royal women's authority is situated within palace and dynastic politics rather than generalized to all women. The Kösem/Murad IV regency chronology is carefully distinguished from the earlier death of Ahmed I.

**Slavery (§§85.9–85.12):** Explains violence of enslavement, coerced labor, gendered vulnerability, elite Ottoman kul and Safavid ghulam institutions, manumission, freedom litigation, and continuing dependencies. Rejects the inference that elite promotion was typical of enslaved people. Distinguishes legal categories and regional variation, including the danger of flattening *umm walad* and contractual manumission rules; later Ottoman slavery scholarship is explicitly identified as contextual, not direct evidence for 1600.

**Education (§§85.13–85.16):** Covers home teaching, literacy distinctions, apprenticeship, religious schooling and advanced learning, Muslim and non-Muslim educational institutions, oral culture, correspondence, manuscript collections, and the Urdubadi manuscript project in Safavid Isfahan. No unverifiable population-wide literacy percentages are asserted.

**Everyday life (§§85.17–85.20):** Compares housing, food, water, health, clothing, religious practice, leisure, coffeehouses, neighborhood sociability, class distinctions, and source bias; explicitly closes Part XIX and explains its bridge to Chapter 86 without narrating Chapter 86 itself.

No direct source quotation, invented medieval/early-modern archival shelfmark, numerically exact enslaved population estimate, or universal household size has been introduced as an authenticated fact. The literature's different chronological coverage is discussed where relevant.

## 4. Four documentary-study source windows and integrity limits

1. **§85.4 — Aintab family litigation:** Leslie Peirce, *Morality Tales* (University of California Press, 2003), including “İne’s Story: A Child Marriage in Trouble” and a published description of the 1540–1541 court records. **Status:** edition-guided interpretation; not a freshly collated Ottoman-register translation.
2. **§85.6 — Women's property and inheritance:** Ottoman court records through Peirce's study, particularly “Women, Property, and the Court.” **Status:** methodology and genre analysis; no fabricated, supposedly specific dated probate transcription.
3. **§85.12 — Freedom suits:** Joshua M. White, “Slavery, Freedom Suits, and Legal Praxis in the Ottoman Empire, ca. 1590–1710,” *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 65, no. 3 (2023): 526–556, including identifiable litigation involving Mehmed bin Abdülcelil. **Status:** identified published case study; original Ottoman legal document not independently collated.
4. **§85.16 — Safavid manuscript anthology:** Nozhat Ahmadi and Kathryn Babayan, “The Isfahan Anthology Project” (2024), including discussion of the Urdubadi household anthology, Tehran University MS 2591 (preface dated 1697). **Status:** documented secondary analysis and manuscript identification; no original-language facsimile collation.

All four windows clearly disclose their editorial paraphrase/source-study status. A future facsimile/edition-collation research phase would be required to reclassify them as primary-text transcriptions. **That phase has not been represented as complete.** This limitation does not negate first-edition publication of the present transparently labelled textual studies.
