### Historical verification: significant results

1. **Multiple campaign stages:** Encyclopaedia Iranica’s *Mongols* and *Khwarazmshahs* entries support the distinction among the early 1219–1221 Khwarazmian invasions, later consolidation in western Iran and the Caucasus, and Hülegü’s expedition. The chapter does not falsely treat these as one continuous invasion.  
   https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mongols/  
   https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khwarazmshah/iii-the-line-of-anustigin/

2. **Armenian and Georgian evidence:** Lorenzo Pubblici’s Cambridge analysis supports the distinction between an early reconnaissance movement in the Caucasus and the campaigns of 1235–1236, as well as the importance of indirect administration. Kirakos’s personal 1236 captivity is supported in published modern scholarship and available translations; Armenian, Georgian, and Syriac sources receive separate treatment rather than being collapsed into “Christian sources.”  
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-the-mongol-empire/georgia-and-the-caucasus/8E6330B3AB980BE701D164C1979E92F0  
   https://www.attalus.org/armenian/kg1.htm

3. **Alamut:** Research from the Institute of Ismaili Studies supports the Maymundiz surrender on 19 November 1256 and the broader decline of the Nizari fortress state; the revised text distinguishes the end of that political system from extinction of Nizari populations or immediate capture of every stronghold.  
   https://www.iis.ac.uk/scholarly-contributions/the-mediaeval-ismailis-of-the-iranian-lands/

4. **Juvayni’s actual position:** Encyclopaedia Iranica confirms that Ata-Malik Juvayni accompanied Hülegü and was appointed administrator of Baghdad in 1259. The chapter distinguishes his direct access to the later campaigns from the information he transmitted concerning earlier episodes, and all source windows are explicitly paraphrases, not invented translations.  
   https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jovayni-ala-al-din/

5. **Baghdad chronology:** The Mongol siege is conventionally dated from late January 1258 with al-Musta’sim’s surrender on or around 10 February and execution usually dated 20 February; the chapter distinguishes surrender, entry, sack, and execution. Near-contemporary reports are not treated as unanimous on every detail.  
   https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/baghdad/baghdad-iranian-connection-ii/  
   https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/tusi-nasir-al-din-bio/

6. **Casualties and libraries:** The direct violence and institutional destruction are stated clearly, while unsupported exact population totals and the later literary motif of an ink-black Tigris are not presented as verified numerical or physical facts. Assertions of universal destruction are rejected without treating the sack as minor.

7. **Plague hypothesis:** Fancy and Green (2021) proposed a plague interpretation; Brack, Biran, and Amitai (2024) reexamined Persian, Syriac, and Arabic evidence and found it insufficient to substantiate a plague outbreak in 1258. Both sides are identified. The chapter does not deny general risks of disease or sanitation-related death.  
   https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2021.3  
   https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2023.38

8. **1260 boundary:** The capture of Aleppo and Damascus, withdrawal of substantial Mongol forces, and Ayn Jalut are contextual endpoints. Detailed Mamluk political and military institutions are reserved to Chapter 65.  
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mongols-and-mamluks/878493FCE48BAA1AD2532273408FD886
