### Narrative coherence and chronological boundary

The two principal storylines are retained as independent historical developments: the First Intifada begins in December 1987 and remains unresolved at the chapter's endpoint; Iraqi aggression begins on August 2, 1990, and the coalition's offensive culminates in Kuwait's liberation in February 1991. Their intersection is established through regional diplomacy, the PLO's position during the Kuwait crisis, changing financial relationships, and the postwar possibilities of the Madrid process. The chapter does **not** say the Intifada caused the invasion. It does **not** say the Gulf War ended the First Intifada. The March 1991 Iraqi uprisings, sanctions, Kurdish developments, and Madrid negotiations are introduced only insofar as needed to establish the transition into Chapters 151 and 152.

## 3. Specific substantive historical corrections and verifications

### 3.1 First-Intifada fatality categories — corrected

The draft's phrase describing Israeli civilian and security-force deaths as being in the “low hundreds” was imprecise. B’Tselem's retrospective tables for the interval from December 9, 1987, to December 8, 1993, identify **1,095 Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces; 58 Palestinians killed by Israeli civilians; 57 Israeli civilians killed by Palestinians; and 44 Israeli security personnel killed by Palestinians**. The latter two figures total **101**. B’Tselem's earlier report covering December 9, 1987, to October 31, 1991, reports **802 Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces**. The completed text distinguishes end dates and separate classifications. It also retains, as a separately attributed and uncertain issue, deaths of Palestinians caused by other Palestinians. No broad causal claim is inferred from a single count.

**Principal documentary sources:**
- B’Tselem, *Human Rights Violations in the Occupied Territories, 1990–1991*: https://www.btselem.org/sites/default/files2/violations__of_human_rights_in_the_occupied_territories_1990_1991.pdf
- B’Tselem, *Human Rights Violations in the Occupied Territories, 1992–1993*: https://www.btselem.org/sites/default/files2/human_rights_violations_in_the_occupied_territories_1992_1993.pdf

### 3.2 Adopted UN resolutions versus vetoed drafts — corrected

The draft treated Security Council resolutions and vetoed proposals ambiguously. The completed text distinguishes formally adopted resolutions from defeated drafts. The UN record includes an **April 15, 1988 vetoed draft** concerning deportations in the occupied territories and **Resolution 672 (October 12, 1990)**, which the Council adopted following fatal violence in Jerusalem. These are different diplomatic instruments and cannot be presented as if both were adopted resolutions.

**Sources:** https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-181797/ and https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/99236

### 3.3 Iraq's external debt — quantified with uncertainty retained

Contemporary accounts based on international financial analysts and U.S. export-finance sources put Iraq's foreign debt near **$79–80 billion** around the time of the 1990 crisis. These figures are not an undisputed audited debt schedule: Gulf governments and Baghdad disagreed about the treatment of wartime assistance, and estimates varied in scope. The final narrative makes this ambiguity explicit, separates Gulf governmental lenders from commercial and other foreign creditors, and explains the connection between debt service and petroleum-revenue expectations without suggesting that hardship legitimized invading Kuwait.

**Sources:**
- *Washington Post*, August 9, 1990: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/business/1990/08/09/iraq-owed-up-to-79-billion-to-foreigners/107a73d4-5ef0-4da1-905d-04dbd2210662/
- *Washington Post*, September 16, 1990: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1990/09/16/at-war-iraq-courted-us-into-economic-embrace/b47ffece-d081-44a5-8163-c00b8a52eabd/

### 3.4 Kuwait's population — quantified and delimited

The manuscript now specifies the Public Authority for Civil Information series as reproduced by the Gulf Labour Markets, Migration and Population project: **2,151,680 residents in 1990, consisting of 578,511 Kuwaitis and 1,573,169 non-Kuwaitis**. The Kuwaiti-citizen share is approximately **26.9%**. This is a reference-year administrative series rather than a verified census on the morning of August 2. The revised narrative uses it as a quantitative context for discussing professional migrants, Palestinian residents, stateless Bidun, labor and citizenship, and the diverse effects of war on residents.

**Source:** https://gulfmigration.grc.net/kuwait-population-by-nationality-kuwaiti-non-kuwaiti-1990-dec-2024/

### 3.5 Resolution 678 — primary-source paraphrase verified

The official UN Digital Library record identifies **Security Council Resolution 678, adopted November 29, 1990** by **12 votes in favor, 2 against (Cuba, Yemen), and 1 abstention (China)**. It set a January 15, 1991 compliance deadline and authorized states cooperating with the government of Kuwait to use **“all necessary means”** to implement Resolution 660 and the subsequent relevant resolutions and to restore peace and security. The chapter's source window explicitly labels its description a **paraphrase** and limits the exact quotation to the brief verified phrase. It distinguishes authority delegated to cooperating states from a UN-controlled combat force, and emphasizes that overthrowing Iraq's government was not an express requirement of the resolution.

**Sources:** https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/102245 and https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/282100

### 3.6 Resolution 687 and the formal ceasefire — verified

Resolution 687 was adopted **April 3, 1991**; Iraq formally accepted the terms, and the formal ceasefire took effect **April 11**, a development distinct from the coalition's **February 28** suspension of offensive operations. The chapter correctly describes chemical and biological weapons destruction, ballistic missiles over the specified range, nuclear inspections, boundary arrangements, financial liability, and the continuing postwar sanctions framework. It does not suggest that the military campaign or Security Council arrangement automatically removed Saddam Hussein.

**Sources:** https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/110709; https://peacemaker.un.org/en/node/9415; https://unispal.un.org/pdfs/s23484.pdf

### 3.7 Schwarzkopf's role — corrected

The original actor reference identified General Norman Schwarzkopf too narrowly as commander of coalition ground forces. The completed manuscript identifies him as **commander in chief, U.S. Central Command**, and principal coalition theater commander. It distinguishes Lieutenant General **John Yeosock's** responsibility for the U.S. Army ground component, with further subordinate and allied commands. This correction matters because the joint and multinational character of the operation was central to its operational success.

**Supplementary military source:** *Joint Operations in the Gulf War*: https://media.defense.gov/2017/Dec/28/2001861717/-1/-1/0/T_CARPENTER_JOINT_OPERATIONS.PDF

### 3.8 War fatalities and the Amiriyah shelter — estimates attributed and categorized

A Project on Defense Alternatives reconstruction, building on Human Rights Watch and demographic investigator Beth Osborne Daponte, concluded that **3,664 Iraqi civilians** were directly killed in the 1991 war and estimated **20,000–26,000 Iraqi military fatalities**. Human Rights Watch's earlier civilian estimate of roughly **2,500–3,000** drew on eyewitness evidence and was not claimed to be exhaustive. These remain contested analytical estimates rather than a universally established official count. The completed manuscript distinguishes military from civilian deaths, Iraqi civilians from Kuwaiti occupation victims, war deaths from March 1991 uprising deaths, and direct deaths from subsequent excess mortality associated with infrastructure collapse and sanctions.

The **February 13, 1991 Amiriyah shelter** bombing is treated as a documented case of mass civilian casualties in which the United States asserted a command-facility rationale. The revised text does not claim that all publicly disputed facts about prior military use have been conclusively resolved. Analysis addresses distinction, feasible precautions, military advantage, and foreseeable harm without inventing a definitive legal judgment for every individual strike.

**Sources:** https://www.hrw.org/reports/1991/gulfwar/ and https://www.comw.org/pda/0310rm8ap2.html

### 3.9 Kuwaiti occupation victims, missing persons, and civilian treatment — distinguished

Middle East Watch's contemporary 1990 account gave an attributed estimate of at least **600 killed in the first three months of occupation**; later UN documentation identifies **over 600 Kuwaitis and third-country nationals arrested and disappeared** during the occupation. These figures concern different categories, periods, and investigations and must not be added or treated as identical. The manuscript clarifies that their human consequences were distributed among Kuwaiti citizens, Palestinians, other foreign residents, and people in vulnerable legal positions.

**Sources:** https://www.refworld.org/reference/annualreport/hrw/1991/en/43631 and https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4032525

### 3.10 Military campaign, oil fires, and postwar boundaries — retained with qualifications

The air offensive began **January 17, 1991**, and the coalition ground offensive began **February 24**, ending in the February 28 suspension. The history continues to distinguish Desert Shield from Desert Storm and the approximate hundred-hour ground offensive from the total duration of the campaign. The manuscript discusses Scud attacks on Israel and Saudi Arabia, Israel's nonintervention, changing assessments of Patriot interception performance, the Battle of Khafji, and the use of different coalition components. Iraqi destruction of Kuwaiti oil infrastructure remains treated as deliberate wartime environmental devastation, while the magnitude of oil spills and associated long-term harm is identified as variable across studies.

### 3.11 Additional editorial safeguards

The revised chapter further preserves the following historical distinctions: opposition to occupation is not equivalent to endorsement of attacks on civilians; civil disobedience was important but did not describe every act within the First Intifada; Palestinian residents of Kuwait cannot be equated with policy positions of the PLO leadership; Iraq's economic grievances are explanatory circumstances, not justification for territorial conquest; UN authorization for collective enforcement is not the same as a command structure administered by the UN; and the Kuwait war's limited objective of expelling Iraqi forces is not an attempt that failed to depose Saddam Hussein.

The core resolution metadata and the distinction among these decisions were verified against official UN Digital Library records. Resolution 678's short quoted language and November 29 date and vote were confirmed. ## 7. Remaining scholarly limitations

- The statistical literature on the Intifada and 1991 Gulf War contains multiple period definitions and classification systems. The narrative now clearly attributes the most important figures but should not be regarded as an exhaustive casualty database or a forensic reconstruction of every incident.
- Estimates of direct Gulf War fatalities and the full costs of the subsequent humanitarian crisis remain debated. The chapter differentiates these categories rather than inventing a consensus.
- Kuwait's 1990 population series is not a person-by-person August 2 count; the text accurately presents it as the reference-year series.
- The details of the Amiriyah facility's prior military usage remain contested in publicly accessible information. The civilian deaths and legal questions are not dismissed because of this uncertainty.
- The precise extent of damage to Kuwaiti petroleum infrastructure and secondary ecological outcomes differ by source and definition; the narrative does not present one unqualified global figure for all consequences.
- No proprietary scholarly book has been examined line by line; the bibliography retains works for reference and further verification as appropriate.
- Not every external reference was opened to test live URL status, and no live-production website access, server URL checks, formal accessibility audit, or publisher-level copyediting proof was completed.
- Maps are deliberately reserved for later insertion by the publisher; their absence is **not** a content or publication blocker in the MGU workflow.

None of these limitations demands additional routine narrative passes before the planned editorial lock. They should be retained as ordinary first-edition source and deployment notes.
