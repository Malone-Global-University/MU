### 3. Historical audit — targeted claims and corrections

**3.1 Timur's political legitimacy.** The chapter consistently distinguishes non-Chinggisid male-line descent, the title *Gürgän/Küregen* acquired through marriage, figurehead Chinggisid sovereignty, and actual military command. It avoids the false assertion that Timur was a ruling khan by direct descent. The distinction follows specialist discussion in Beatrice Forbes Manz, *The Rise and Rule of Tamerlane* (1989), and her subsequent study *Power, Politics and Religion in Timurid Iran* (2007). Sources: https://as.tufts.edu/history/people/faculty/beatrice-manz ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/power-politics-and-religion-in-timurid-iran/76E586BC8BF57708CA9163184B9F9740 .

**3.2 The battle of Ankara.** July **1402** is used without assigning a falsely certain day. The textual discussion notes the common July 20 and July 28 variants; different historical traditions and scholarly works preserve or prefer different dates. Several published accounts prefer July 28. Sources: https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/9789047422471/Bej.9789004158368.i-250_002.xml ; https://makale.isam.org.tr/bitstreams/22dec909-50cc-4d94-bbf4-027b928bf3eb/download . The exact day is not essential to the chapter's causal explanation and is deliberately left open.

**3.3 Two Timurid assaults on Baghdad.** The rewrite separates Timur's occupation in **1393** from the extensive devastation of **1401**, and both from the Mongol destruction of **1258** covered in Chapter 63. Casualty totals are not asserted as verified. Targeted source: Tia Wheeler, “Baghdad during the Early Timurid Era,” https://isamveri.org/pdfdrg/D195119/2008/2008_WHEELERT.pdf .

**3.4 The 1405–1409 succession.** The chapter names **Pir Muhammad** as Timur's designated heir, **Khalil Sultan** as ruler of Samarkand after Timur's death, and **Shah Rukh** as the dynast who established his supremacy there in **1409** and appointed Ulugh Beg. Pir Muhammad's death in 1407 and the competing regional bases are included. Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Khalil Sultan,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kalil-soltan/ .

**3.5 Ulugh Beg and dynastic instability.** The 1447 succession crisis and Ulugh Beg's death in 1449 after his conflict with Abd al-Latif are included alongside his scientific work, avoiding the misleading idea that his intellectual distinction ensured political success. See Manz (2007); UNESCO, *History of Civilizations of Central Asia*, https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/vol_IVa%20silk%20road_the%20timurid%20states%20in%20the%20fifteenth%20and%20sixteenth%20centuries.pdf .

**3.6 Husayn Bayqara.** The account distinguishes the contested accession in **1469** from the consolidation in **1470**, notes the role of Yadgar Muhammad and competing Aq Qoyunlu interests, and avoids claiming that every year of his reign was politically uninterrupted. Sources: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hosayn-bayqara/ ; https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herat-iii/ .

**3.7 Herat in 1507.** The death of Husayn Bayqara in **1506**, the contested position of **Badi al-Zaman** and **Muzaffar Husayn**, and the conquest of Herat by **Muhammad Shaybani** in **1507** are included. The chapter distinguishes a change in sovereign power from the continued employment of some administrators and survival of intellectual institutions. Sources: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herat-iii/ ; https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khorasan-x-history-in-the-safavid-and-afsharid-periods/ .

**3.8 Reconstructions and their limits.** Khorasan and Herat's fifteenth-century market, urban, and agricultural developments are distinguished from the economic effects of Timur's warfare; the chapter does not imply that the restoration of a bazaar proves universal prosperity. Sources: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herat-iv/ ; https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/economy-vi-in-the-timurid-period/ ; Maria E. Subtelny, *Timurids in Transition* (2007), https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9789047421603/html .

**3.9 Timurid art and architecture.** Samarkand, Herat, Bibi-Khanym, Gur-e Amir, Gawhar Shad foundations, Baysunghur's manuscript patronage, and Timurid influence on later traditions receive developed coverage. The narrative explicitly recognizes coerced artisan transfer and disputed individual artwork attributions. Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/the-art-of-the-timurid-period-ca-1370-1507 ; https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/603 ; https://www.archnet.org/collections/1496 .

**3.10 Primary-source authorship and chronology.** Four source-study units discuss Nizam al-Din Shami's official *Zafarnama*, Ibn Arabshah's hostile account (completed around **1435**), Ibn Khaldun's autobiography concerning Damascus in **1401**, and Clavijo's diplomatic visit to Samarkand in **1404**. The source windows are interpretive editorial paraphrases, **not verbatim quotations or independently manuscript-collated translations**. For source dates and available English texts: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ajaeb-al-maqdur/ ; https://books.google.com/books/about/Ibn_Khaldun_and_Tamerlane.html?id=4bTzvgEACAAJ ; https://catalog.folger.edu/record/46848 .

### 4. Scope discipline and historical interpretation

- **Chapter 63:** Mongol expansion and Baghdad's Abbasid crisis are referred to only when needed for orientation; the chapter does not retell the Mongol conquests as its principal subject.
- **Chapter 64:** The Ilkhanate's administrative and cultural traditions are summarized as inherited context, not duplicated in full.
- **Chapter 65:** Mamluk government is not retold; Syria in 1400–1401 appears insofar as Timur's invasion transformed Mamluk territories.
- **Chapters 67–70:** Later Ottoman, Qara Qoyunlu, Aq Qoyunlu, local Caucasian, and Safavid histories are referenced to explain regional connections but not treated as substitutes for their future chapters.
- **Indian history:** The Delhi campaign is discussed for its impact on Timurid imperial resources rather than as a complete replacement for the Delhi Sultanate's history.

The chapter avoids projecting modern national identities onto fourteenth-century dynasties, distinguishing Turco-Mongol political structures from Persianate literary and administrative culture. It avoids unverified precise casualty estimates and discloses the limitations of retrospective and court-centered sources. The historical argument integrates atrocity, coercive migration, provincial political institutions, uneven economic recovery, social history, religious networks, and scientific and artistic accomplishments.
