## 4. Historical accuracy, chronology, and interpretive safeguards

### 4.1 Political and geographic chronology

- **After 1335:** The death of Abu Sa'id and dissolution of Ilkhanid supremacy created political opportunities; the chapter carefully distinguishes prior Mongol influence from later fragmentation.
- **c. 1300–1520:** The focus remains on late-medieval and initial early-modern local communities; evidence from the sixteenth century is explicitly labeled retrospective when applied to earlier periods.
- **Hasankeyf, 1462:** The locally established Ayyubid princely house was overtaken during Uzun Hasan's fifteenth-century expansion; the chapter does not imply that the wider medieval Ayyubid empire still existed. Its documented local chronology is distinguished from the larger former state.
- **Armenian Cilicia, 1375:** The loss of the Cilician kingdom is separated from survival of Armenian communal, ecclesiastical and economic institutions. Avoids the false implication that Armenian life ceased with political kingship.
- **Etchmiadzin, 1441:** Restoration/election of Kirakos I Virapetsi is explicitly distinguished from the *continuing* Catholicosate at Sis under Gregory IX Mousabegian and separate ecclesiastical center at Akhtamar.
- **Chaldiran, 1514:** Treated as context for Kurdish local negotiations rather than duplicated military narration from Chapter 70. Negotiations associated with Idris Bidlisi are explained as variable local processes, not a single universal treaty.
- **Ottoman administrative document, 1525:** TSMA D.5246 is **outside** the chapter's main 1520 boundary; used openly as an immediately subsequent document illustrating Ottoman classification after transition, not evidence of an unchanged fourteenth-century institution.
- **Sharafnama, c. 1596–1599:** Treated as a retrospectively composed Kurdish dynastic source. Genealogies and early-emirate claims are not silently promoted to contemporary evidence.

### 4.2 Local political history

- §§69.2–69.8 distinguish tribal confederations, local dynastic households, frontier bargaining, tribute, military obligations and variable territorial jurisdiction; no uniform model is imposed on every Kurdish community.
- §§69.5–69.6 are strengthened by **contemporary Bitlis letter TSMA E.3145** (Sharaf b. Shah-Muhammad to Mehmed II; edited Dehqan and Genç, 2019) and the independently documented political transformation of Hasankeyf. The letter's precise dating is not fabricated.
- §69.7 expressly warns that Hakkari, Bahdinan and Ardalan are documented unevenly: mature Ottoman-era arrangements are not mechanically backdated into the 1300s.
- The conclusion differentiates negotiated or delegated local autonomy from independent sovereignty, and observes that local ruling houses could also be overthrown.

### 4.3 Armenian and Syriac institutions

- §§69.9–69.12 treat Armenian churches, Catholicosates, monasteries, manuscripts, colophons, merchant households and rural society as independent topics, not only as responses to conquerors.
- **Sanjian 1449.1 (pp. 209–214)** and **Sanjian 1469.3 (p. 296)** are identified research targets rather than invented passages. The latter's biblical allusions are discussed as scriptural intertextuality, not neutral census evidence.
- §§69.13–69.14 distinguish **Syriac Orthodox** and **Church of the East** histories; the two communities are not conflated, and the English terms Syriac/Assyrian are treated sensitively with reference to period and context.
- **Harvard Houghton Syr. 108, Folder 18** is identified and linked to Philip M. Forness's 2021 study of the late-fifteenth/early-sixteenth-century Tur Abdin manuscript; composition and survival are not extrapolated into unsupported population estimates.

### 4.4 Religion, economy and society

- §69.18 distinguishes historical Lalish pilgrimage and Shaykh Adi's legacy from the later demonstrable organization of Yazidi traditions; modern religious categories are not retrojected without discussion.
- §69.15 includes agriculture, irrigation, pastoral access, seasonal migration, land use, local taxation and gendered labor; §69.16 explains tolls, market networks and the continued operation of trade across rival dynastic territories.
- §69.17 notes violence, displacement and local reconstruction without asserting unverifiable aggregate casualty figures or permanent abandonment in every district.
- §§69.19–69.20 describe the emergent Ottoman–Safavid frontier as a negotiated and contested political process with Kurdish emirs, Armenian and Syriac institutions, farmers and traders as actors rather than passive recipients.
- Community terminology is contextualized; no claim that modern ethnic or national categories had unchanged political meanings in the fourteenth century.

## 8. Residual first-edition limits and disposition

1. **No exhaustive original-language manuscript or archival facsimile collation** was undertaken. Direct quotations are intentionally not invented; the four source windows remain labeled source-critical editorial paraphrases.
2. **Maps are text-based exercises** requiring a historical atlas. No original embedded geographic plate or fully georeferenced administrative map is promised.
3. **Source availability and historiographical limits:** Kurdish principalities have uneven documentary survival, and a substantial portion of later evidence is retrospective. Dates that are not securely established are presented cautiously. The audit supports a responsible first-edition historical-text product, not a claim that every historical proposition has been exhaustively checked against all manuscripts.
4. **Scope boundary:** The chapter emphasizes local worlds c. 1300–1520; it leaves broader Ottoman–Safavid campaigns to Chapter 70 and mature Ottoman provincial administration to Chapter 74. Later sources (1525, c. 1597) are used openly as comparative witnesses, not in-period narrative dates.
