## 4. Historical verification and chronology corrections

The following consequential issues identified during the original audit were incorporated directly into the new narrative and subsequently checked against institutional or specialist materials.

1. **Confederate origins and the meaning of the names.** The chapter does not interpret Black Sheep/White Sheep as proof of a single ancient totemic ancestry. *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Aq Qoyunlu,” explicitly describes alternative speculative meanings and the Bayandur leadership: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aq-qoyunlu-confederation/.
2. **Qara Yusuf's separate successes.** The narrative distinguishes his recovery of Tabriz in 1406 from the 1408 defeat of Timurid opponents, the Jalayirid defeat of 1410, and the subsequent Baghdad transition around 1412. It avoids presenting all four as one campaign or one date. The Baghdad sequence is discussed in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Iraq iii”: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iraq-iii-mongols-to-safavids/.
3. **Shah Rukh's campaigns.** The three major interventions in 1420–1421, 1429 and 1434–1435 are differentiated; Qara Yuluq Osman's death in 1435 is not omitted. *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Aq Qoyunlu”: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aq-qoyunlu-confederation/.
4. **Uzun Hasan's contested rise.** The capture of Amid in 1452 is distinguished from the ending of his conflict with Jahangir around 1457, rather than treated as immediate general recognition. Same specialist reference.
5. **Jahan Shah's fall and Abu Sa'id.** Jahan Shah's defeat and death in November 1467, the spread of Aq Qoyunlu authority during 1468 and Abu Sa'id's capture and execution in 1469 are separate turning points. Same specialist reference; see also Woods, *The Aqquyunlu* (revised edition, 1999), University of Utah Press: https://nes.princeton.edu/publications/aqquyunlu-clan-confederation-empire.
6. **Otlukbeli and military technology.** August 1473 is treated as a significant Ottoman success limiting western Aq Qoyunlu ambitions, not the immediate end of the Iranian state. The chapter rejects a purely gun-versus-cavalry explanation and addresses military organization and alliance logistics. See the Aq Qoyunlu reference above and H. R. Roemer, “The Türkmen Dynasties,” *The Cambridge History of Iran*, vol. 6 (1986): https://doi.org/10.1017/CHOL9780521200943.005.
7. **Soyurghal, fiscal reform and Yaqub.** The chapter differentiates revenue assignments, exemptions, judicial/administrative latitude, and measures affecting the tamgha; it does not describe soyurghal as straightforward modern fee-simple land title. It distinguishes the intentions of Qazi Isa's reforms from their enforcement. See V. Minorsky, “The Aq-qoyunlu and Land Reforms,” *BSOAS* 17:3 (1955), 449–462: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X00112376 and *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Aq Qoyunlu.”
8. **Blue Mosque and women's endowment.** Its portal inscription is dated 4 Rabiʿ I 870/25 October 1465, not as an unsupported construction date invented from a modern photograph. Khatun Jan Begum is identified as the endower; the association with the wider Mozaffariya complex, nearby commercial property, and later modifications is explained. Sandra Aube, “Tabriz x. Monuments (1): The Blue Mosque,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/tabriz-x-monuments/.
9. **Safaviyya and confessional complexities.** The manuscript avoids the untenable proposition that the Qara Qoyunlu were uniformly Shia and the Aq Qoyunlu uniformly Sunni. Religious practice, court policy, devotion to Ali, spiritual lineages, marriage, military following, and later political mobilization are distinguished. Consult Quiring-Zoche, the Cambridge Turkmen dynasties chapter, and Kathryn Babayan, *Mystics, Monarchs, and Messiahs* (2002): https://nes.princeton.edu/publications/mystics-monarchs-and-messiahs-cultural-landscapes-early-modern-iran.
10. **The 1490–1508 transition.** The fall of Tabriz to Ismail in 1501 does not imply simultaneous conquest of every Aq Qoyunlu territory. The narrative carries the sequence through major territorial losses in 1503 and 1507–1508, while explicitly noting that Murad survived beyond 1508 and was killed in 1514. *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Aq Qoyunlu,” and “Iraq iii” give the necessary distinctions.

### Historical restraint applied

Political and religious identities are not reduced to modern ethnic or confessional categories. No unverifiable medieval troop counts, annual revenue figures, or massacre totals are introduced. The differences between a claim in a royal chronicle, direct documentary testimony, a contemporary foreign observer and modern scholarly inference are maintained. The treatment of ordinary rural and mobile populations explicitly acknowledges the uneven survival of sources.

## 8. Chapter boundaries and series continuity

The draft takes the post-Mongol/Timurid background from Chapter 66 but does not retell Timur's full campaign history. It relates Ottoman expansion to the Aq Qoyunlu without duplicating Chapter 67 or preempting the complete later Ottoman-Safavid story of Chapter 70. §68.16 provides essential local social history while preserving Chapter 69's separate responsibility for Kurdish, Armenian and other communities. The narrative closes at the effective loss of Aq Qoyunlu sovereignty through approximately 1508, without attempting the full account of Shah Ismail's rule reserved to Chapters 70 and 76.
