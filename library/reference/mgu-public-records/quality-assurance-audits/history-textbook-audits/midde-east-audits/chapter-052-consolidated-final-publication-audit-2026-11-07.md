## 3. Independent historical and editorial findings

### Division I — Byzantine–Islamic frontiers (52.1–52.4)
- Preserves Taurus, Anti-Taurus, Cilician and Upper Euphrates geography and distinguishes frontier societies from surveyed boundaries.
- Clarifies Byzantine *theme* institutions without assuming a timeless universal class of soldier-landholders; distinguishes <em>thughūr</em> and <em>ʿawāṣim</em> by their changing historical usage.
- Explains Amorium in 838 as a major campaign, not durable Abbasid conquest of Anatolia.
- Develops Melitene in 934 as part of cumulative Byzantine campaigning, logistics and the decentralization of neighboring authorities, without claiming historical inevitability.

### Division II — Armenian polities and society (52.5–52.8)
- Distinguishes Bagratid restoration around 884–885, Artsruni kingship in Vaspurakan from 908, and Ani's emergence as royal center in 961.
- Expands Armenian estates, church structures, patronage, manuscript culture, architecture, peasantry and urban exchange in their own right.
- Distinguishes the negotiated Vaspurakan transfer c. 1021–1022 from the differently contested incorporation of Ani in 1045; pressure and dynastic politics are both treated, rather than reducing incorporation to one cause.
- T. W. Greenwood's scholarship is used to guard against assuming one geographically fixed medieval 'Armenia'.

### Division III — Kurdish communities and dynasties (52.9–52.12)
- Provides independent treatment of pastoral and agrarian communities, highland trade, kinship and town–countryside relationships, as opposed to reducing Kurdish history to military auxiliaries.
- Distinguishes Hasanwayhid / Annazid centers in the Zagros, Shaddadid Dvin/Ganja, and Marwanid Diyar Bakr.
- **Important chronology correction:** the Shaddadid family's later authority at Ani belongs principally after the chapter's main endpoint; it is no longer presented as an established tenth-century possession.
- Makes explicit that some Kurdish-origin traditions are retrospective; coinage, later dynastic histories and modern syntheses cannot establish uniform identity or political institutions.

### Division IV — Hamdanids and Byzantine expansion (52.13–52.16)
- Distinguishes Sayf al-Dawla's Aleppine rule and court panegyric from independently assessable military results.
- Separates Byzantine raid/sack at Aleppo in 962 from territorial annexation at Tarsus in 965 and Antioch in 969; explains administrative incorporation and negotiated regional relationships.
- Includes the social consequences of warfare, captivity, resettlement, fiscal change and religious diversity without constructing fabricated first-person testimony.

### Division V — Turkic recruitment, conversion and mobility (52.17–52.20)
- Differentiates enslaved, dependent and free military service, patronage careers, Central Asian Islamization, Qarakhanid politics, Oghuz household migration, and retrospective early Seljuk traditions.
- Does not equate all Turkic-speaking groups or project the later Anatolian Seljuk transformation into the tenth century.
- Keeps Manzikert (1071) only as a later forward reference, preserving the Chapter 52 chronological boundary.

**Historical audit disposition:** the principal chronology and interpretive boundaries were checked against accessible specialist historical reference and publisher sources. These checks support the claims above. They do **not** establish exhaustive collation of all Byzantine, Armenian, Arabic or Persian medieval texts, nor guarantee that every interpretive detail is uncontested; the manuscript is transparent about these limits.
