## 2. Consolidated historical accuracy audit

### June 1967: escalation, war, geography, and law

- **Crisis:** May 13 Soviet warning to Egypt and Syria was not borne out by the reported Israeli concentration; Egypt moved troops to Sinai and requested UNEF withdrawal; Nasser closed the Straits of Tiran around May 22–23; King Hussein signed the defense pact with Egypt on May 30. Israel launched the opening air offensive on June 5. Distinguish the sequence from a verdict that war was inevitable or that all actors possessed identical intentions.
- **Campaign:** Israel's Egyptian air operation; Sinai battle and retreat; Israel's capture of the West Bank and East Jerusalem; and the June 9–10 offensive on the Golan are separately narrated with terrain, command failures, and civilian consequences. Israeli victory is not treated as conferring recognized sovereign title to newly occupied areas.
- **Territorial distinctions:** Sinai was Egyptian sovereign territory; Gaza was administered by Egypt; the West Bank was administered and annexed by Jordan with limited recognition; East Jerusalem had been under Jordanian control; and the Golan was Syrian. Military administration elsewhere is differentiated from Israel's postwar legal and municipal measures in East Jerusalem.
- **Jordan/Jerusalem correction:** The lean manuscript's suggestion that Jordan lost its *claim to the holy places* was incorrect. The final §66.3 states that Jordan lost **territorial control of East Jerusalem** but that its Hashemite custodial role and the Jordanian waqf continued. The later acknowledgement in Article 9 of the **1994 Jordan–Israel peace treaty** is presented as subsequent corroboration, not as a settlement existing in 1967.
- **Displacement:** A/6713 (1967) states that more than **300,000** people became homeless or left homes, **including roughly 120,000 UNRWA-registered refugees**. The text identifies the count as a contemporary agency estimate rather than an exhaustive census of everyone affected and avoids double-counting individuals already displaced in 1948.

**Documentary checks:** [U.S. State Department 1967 history](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1961-1968/arab-israeli-war-1967); [UNRWA A/6713 1967 report](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-198325/); [U.S. Congressional Research Service discussion of Jordanian custodianship](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2023-06-23_RL33546_26ed9ceb8573d6771229927aa5af3d0fde9bac19.html).

### Khartoum, 242, Palestinian politics, and 1967–1973

- **Khartoum summit:** **August 29–September 1, 1967**; its September 1 resolution includes the famous rejections of peace, recognition, and direct negotiation, while also committing to collective diplomatic coordination and assistance. The final analysis does not equate a public summit formula with every private government position.
- **Resolution 242:** **November 22, 1967**. The final section and its source study explain preambular and operative language, the withdrawal/peace/secure-borders linkage, freedom of navigation, a just refugee settlement, the English/French drafting debate, and the absence of explicit Palestinian national self-determination. It does not invent the word “all” in a purported authenticated quote.
- **War of Attrition:** Escalation in 1968–1969, major 1969–1970 fighting, **August 7, 1970 ceasefire**, Soviet air-defense assistance, and civilian evacuations. The 1970 Rogers initiative is distinct from the earlier failed 1969 plan.
- **PLO/Black September:** Karameh **March 21, 1968**; Arafat PLO chairmanship in **1969**; Jordan–PLO armed conflict in **September 1970** with further conflict in **1971**. Palestinian factions, civilian populations, and the Jordanian state are not conflated.
- **Sadat:** Nasser died **September 28, 1970**; Sadat's July **1972** announcement about Soviet advisers is not described as a complete end to Soviet arms and military relations.

**Documentary checks:** [Khartoum resolution](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-193039/); [Resolution 242 original text through FRUS](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v19/d542); [Rogers initiative and State Department documents](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v12/d170); [U.S. State Department 1973 overview](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/arab-israeli-war-1973).

### October 1973 military and diplomacy chronology

- **October 6:** Egypt and Syria launched coordinated attacks; Egypt crossed under a surface-to-air and antitank-defense plan; Syria advanced on the Golan. Objectives were not identical: Egyptian canal bridgeheads and Syrian recovery aspirations are distinguished.
- **October 14:** Egypt's armored advance in Sinai failed and weakened forces available to defend the canal sector.
- **Night of October 15–16:** Israeli forces crossed to the western bank near Deversoir, with fighting around the Chinese Farm and the potential encirclement of Egypt's Third Army. Final battlefield position is not equated with comprehensive political victory.
- **October 22, 23, 25:** **UN Security Council Resolutions 338, 339, and 340**, respectively. Resolution 338 called for a ceasefire and negotiations implementing 242; 339 reaffirmed the ceasefire and demanded restoration of positions; 340 demanded compliance and established **UNEF II**.
- **October 1973 oil response:** The final text briefly mentions Arab production restrictions/embargoes and the distinct petroleum-pricing issue; Chapter 65 remains responsible for OPEC/OAPEC economics.
- **December 1973 Geneva**, **January 18 1974 Egypt–Israel disengagement**, **May 31 1974 Syria–Israel disengagement and UNDOF**, **September 1975 Sinai II** are separated from the 1979 comprehensive bilateral treaty.
- **Rabat October 28 1974:** Recognition of the PLO as sole legitimate representative is explained as a change in political representation, not establishment of a Palestinian state.
- **Camp David September 17 1978:** Two separate *frameworks* are distinguished from the **March 26 1979 Egypt–Israel peace treaty**, including the unresolved Palestinian political framework and the phased Sinai withdrawal commitments.

**Documentary checks:** [UNEF II 338–340 resolution register](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/unef2res.html); [UNEF II chronology and disengagement history](https://peacekeeping.un.org/sites/default/files/past/unef2backgr2.html); [United Nations report on January/May 1974 disengagement](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-180616/); [Rabat 1974 resolution](https://www.un.org/unispal/document/auto-insert-194621/); [Camp David 1978 documents](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/camp-david-meeting-the-middle-east-documents-agreed-camp-david); [Egypt–Israel treaty and related documents](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/egyptian-israeli-peace-treaty-treaty-and-related-documents).

**Limits of historical audit:** The audit checked critical dates, document content, geography, categories of legal status and displacement, and important interpretive claims, but is not exhaustive collation of every secondary-source assertion or all Arabic/Hebrew/English archival materials. No unsupported single absolute casualty figure was imposed on contested military and civilian accounts.

## 5. Editorial first-edition limitations and decision

1. All eight source-study passages are **editorial paraphrases**, not verified verbatim quotations or manuscript-page-collated translations; the original documents are identified and linked but not exhaustively compared across archival languages/editions.
2. The three map exercises require external historical atlases and **do not** include finished embedded map plates.
3. Interpreted casualty figures, responsibility for war escalation, legal readings of Resolution 242, and assessments of strategic victory are treated with evidentiary qualification rather than unwarranted certainty.
4. The main historical scope ends in **1979**; references to the 1994 Jordan–Israel treaty are narrowly contextual corroboration of the holy-site custodial role, not a retrospective rewrite of the 1967 chronology.

These limits are compatible with the established **MGU scholarly textual HTML first-edition standard** and are transparently disclosed to readers. They are not grounds for repeated generic expansion passes. Any later illustrated/archival edition may add authenticated facsimiles and custom historical maps without reopening the textual first edition for routine writing.
