## IV. Historical accuracy checks and corrections

The complete historical analysis was not copied from the lean draft. The rewrite incorporates the following high-priority corrections and conceptual guardrails:

- **1975:** April 13 Ain al-Rummaneh is explained as a trigger, not sufficient or singular cause. Militias and political coalitions are named; 'Christian versus Muslim' and 'religious war' are rejected as comprehensive explanations of the shifting conflict. The first-war massacres are discussed without assigning collective guilt to religious populations.
- **Palestinian distinction:** civilian refugees, camp institutions, PLO leadership, and armed factions remain separate categories. The 1969 Cairo agreement does not become an invented grant of Palestinian statehood, and the unofficial translation limitation is disclosed.
- **1976 Syria:** the Syrian entry, its initial tactical alignment against leftist/PLO forces, subsequent shifting alliances, and the qualified authority of the Arab Deterrent Force are distinguished. The intervention is not described as a fixed, unconditional alliance with one community.
- **1978:** Operation Litani, UNSC 425/426, UNIFIL, Israeli support for border-area militias, and the difference between peacekeeping objectives and enforceable outcomes are treated separately.
- **1982 invasion:** the assassination attempt against Israeli ambassador Shlomo Argov is attributed to the **Abu Nidal Organization**, hostile to Arafat's PLO leadership; the wider military and political purposes and Beirut siege are explained. The PLO's evacuation does not imply that civilian refugees left with its leadership.
- **Sabra and Shatila:** direct massacre perpetration by Lebanese militiamen and Israeli indirect responsibility (Kahan Commission) are distinguished; no unsupported single definitive death toll is inserted. The commission's institutional limitations and survivors' perspective are disclosed.
- **October 1983 vs February 1984:** the bombings are not said to instantly end the multinational presence; U.S. offshore redeployment in February **1984** is accurately placed in the wider political-military failure. Verified with U.S. State Department Office of the Historian: <https://history.state.gov/milestones/1981-1988/lebanon>.
- **Amal and Hezbollah:** distinct organizational genealogies, changing alliances and conflicts, Shi'a social diversity, the effects of occupation, and Iranian support are differentiated. Religious identity is not equated with militia membership.
- **1988–1990 and Taif:** rival governments, Aoun's campaign, intra-Christian fighting, parliamentary agreement in 1989, later constitutional reforms, and October 1990 termination of principal war fighting remain separate developments.
- **Postwar:** the Syrian role, selective militia disarmament, Taif institutions, economic reconstruction under Hariri and Solidere, debt, displacement and property controversies are analyzed without claiming that commercial reconstruction resolved all institutional problems.
- **2000 withdrawal:** Israel's withdrawal and UN Blue Line verification are described without representing the Blue Line as a final boundary. Shebaa Farms remains described as disputed.
- **2005:** Hariri was assassinated **14 February**; Syrian troop withdrawal completed **26 April 2005**. Troop withdrawal is distinguished from the question of continuing political influence.
- **2006:** the July fighting, civilian effects, Resolution 1701's **11 August** adoption, and **14 August** cessation are differentiated. Civilian casualties are discussed qualitatively without inventing exact totals or legal conclusions.

**Scope preservation:** Chapter 61 supplies the 1948/Nakba foundation; Chapter 66 the 1967/1973 wars and Palestinian organizational shifts; Chapter 67 the general ideological history of political Islam; Chapter 69 the Iran–Iraq/Gulf conflicts; Chapter 70 the peace process; Chapters 71–72 later Arab upheavals and contemporary synthesis.
