## 2. Historical review and corrections

### A. Corrected previous publication blockers

1. **National Pension expansion — §91.5:** Corrected 1995 coverage to **farmers and fishermen in rural areas**, followed by extension to much of the **urban population in April 1999**. The distinction between legal coverage and actual benefit receipt is made explicit. The National Pension Service's institutional history is linked in the documentary register.
2. **1987 fertility — Continuity and Change:** Corrected an erroneous reference to fertility 'around replacement level' to approximately **1.53 children per woman**, already materially below replacement. The historical fertility series is linked.
3. **Non-regular employment — §91.3:** Updated vague '37–38 percent' to **38.2% of wage employees in August 2025** (approximately 8.56 million) and **38.5% in August 2026** (approximately 8.67 million), with an explicit wage-employee denominator and survey classification caution. The 2026 result was verified through reporting on the national statistical release; the official 2025 release is linked as well.
4. **Jeonse — Chapter Opening and §91.13:** Clarified that the deposit is a legally refundable tenancy deposit which may resemble financing economically, not an identical loan contract. Expanded the distinction among default, property-price declines, landlord insolvency, and intentional fraud.
5. **2025 versus 2026 OECD education evidence — §91.9 and source window:** Distinguished **71% tertiary attainment among 25–34-year-olds** in the 2025 country note from **58% among adults aged 25–64** in the 2026 note. Added the 2025 comparative parental-education outcomes (85% versus 45% for differently defined parental educational groups), with cohort definitions.
6. **Private-education survey — §91.8 and source window:** Distinguished total expenditure (₩29.2 trillion in 2024 to ₩27.5 trillion in 2025) from average monthly spending (**₩458,000 all pupils**; **₩604,000 participating pupils** in 2025). Declining pupil counts and participation must be taken into account.
7. **2025 census — source window:** Identified **1 November 2025** as the reference date and **28 July 2026** as the release date; specified **26.38 million** capital-region inhabitants from a national census population of **51.82 million** (50.9%), and a separate 66.1% share of one- and two-person general households.
8. **2025 final birth statistics — source window:** Identified the **26 August 2026** final data release and the final **254,341 births** (rounded elsewhere to 254,300), compared with the provisional estimates published earlier. Retained annual total fertility rates **0.72 (2023), 0.75 (2024), and 0.80 (2025)** as period measures, not completed lifetime fertility.
9. **2025 pension reform — source window:** Added the precise schedule: **9% contributions in 2025, 9.5% in 2026**, annual increases of 0.5 percentage points to **13% in 2033**, and the **43% nominal replacement-rate parameter from 2026**, applicable to the legally relevant contribution period rather than retroactively to all earlier contributions or pensions already being paid. Distinguished reform rules from conditional fiscal projections.
10. **Foreign-population definitions — §91.19:** Clarified that a 2025 count of approximately 2.78 million foreign nationals staying in the country included approximately 620,000 short-term stays, and cannot be represented simply as a count of settled immigrants.

### B. Scholarly and editorial development

Rather than replace sound prose, added contextual historical discussions to the shortest sections, particularly **§§91.6, 91.7, 91.9–91.11, 91.13–91.14, and 91.18–91.20**. These additions address education's changing geography, admissions fairness, alternatives to university, housing tenure and redevelopment, repayment protections, intergenerational housing differences, old-age care, categories of foreign presence, and measurement of policy impact.

The revised chapter preserves nuanced distinctions among economic growth and distribution, income and wealth, relative poverty and material deprivation, examinations and genuine mobility, housing ownership and tenancy, crime and political disagreement, fertility rates and annual birth counts, and observed results and long-term demographic projections.
