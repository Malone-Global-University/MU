## 1. Review scope and editorial decision

The supplied HTML manuscript was examined directly, compared with the approved 20-section production plan and the chapter's seven subjects in the governing textbook outline (tribal enterprises, natural resources, tourism, agriculture, finance, energy, and community development). The review concentrated on the author's stated uncertainties and publication-critical factual, legal, financial and institutional claims. The final manuscript incorporates one consolidated correction and expansion pass; the original historical framework, narrative sequence, instructional features, and visual shell remain intact. No generalized rewrite was required.

The submitted manuscript already contained approximately 10,361 words of direct section-level narrative, despite the author's estimate of 9,000–10,000. The final count is 11,890 words (whitespace-separated text in the direct paragraphs of the 20 historical sections, excluding feature boxes and documentary-reference lines). It therefore falls within the proposed 10,000–12,000-word planning range. Total visible text, including navigation, duplicated mobile navigation, exercises, references, and features, is 21,962 whitespace-separated words; this is not a measure of substantive narrative. Counts do not imply scholarly quality by themselves.

## 3. Substantive corrections, historical verification, and expansion

### A. Historical foundations and land tenure (§§121.1–121.7)

- **Allotment estimate:** the commonly cited decline from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million in 1934 is supported by the BIA historical policy overview and Senate Report 106-361. It is retained as an approximate aggregate historical estimate rather than a parcel inventory. Some historical accounts use different baselines or estimates; the manuscript identifies its basis.
- **Indian Reorganization Act, Section 17:** the draft conflated the original 1934 provisions with current law. The original required a petition by at least one-third of adult Indians and ratification at a special election by a majority of adult Indians living on the reservation. Public Law 101-301 (1990) substituted a petition by the tribe and ratification by its governing body. The lease-term provision changed from ten years for reservation land to twenty-five years for specified trust or restricted lands. The final narrative and Source Window 1 distinguish original law from amended law and clarify that the charter-specific restrictions do not displace separate leasing statutes. Source: 25 U.S.C. §5124 and amendment notes.
- **Government vs corporation:** retained the distinction between political authority and the legal form used to conduct business, avoiding universal conclusions about sovereign immunity or taxation. Qualified an overbroad claim about federal assistance and outside decision-making; self-determination arrangements can allow Indigenous governments to administer transferred funds.
- **Entrepreneurship:** expanded the discussion of local purchasing, supplier development, contracting experience, private business finance, and market capacity (§121.6). The new construction example is explicitly illustrative, not a report of an actual firm or contract.
- **HEARTH Act:** clarified that approved tribal surface-leasing regulations do not themselves authorize mineral extraction or individually owned trust-allotment leases. BIA's 2026 procedural guidance and dated approval register ground the discussion in contemporary practice. Clarified Section 248 mortgage insurance rather than conflating it with the Section 184 guarantee.

### B. Agricultural enterprise, finance, energy and tourism (§§121.8–121.12)

- **Indian Financing Act:** verified the original statute (Public Law 93-262, April 12, 1974, 88 Stat. 77) and distinguished legislative authority from the BIA's program description. The federal guarantee or insurance can cover up to 90% of eligible loans; this is lender-risk support, not a 90% grant to borrowers. Source Window 2 now links both records.
- **CDFIs:** the Native Initiatives page and Treasury's FY 2024 Annual Report support the institutional narrative. Retained the 2001 study and 2002 Native initiative context. Treasury reported 14 certified Native CDFIs in 2001 and 64 at the end of FY 2024 (p. 51); the final chapter identifies this dated count and its inclusion of Native Hawaiian communities. A precise November 2026 count was not established through an official current certification-list audit and is not asserted. The number of institutions is not a measure of loans outstanding or household prosperity.
- **NAPI:** the enterprise's institutional history records April 16, 1970 as its founding and Congress's 1962 authorization of the Navajo Indian Irrigation Project. Its 110,630-acre objective is a planned irrigable area, not actual acreage planted in a given year; the latter remains described without unsupported precision. The original sector narrative is retained.
- **Agricultural census:** USDA's June 2024 highlights of the 2022 Census report 78,316 American Indian/Alaska Native producers (alone or in combination) making decisions on 58,336 operations, and approximately 63 million operated acres. These are dated survey categories, not the number of Indigenous nations, tribally owned enterprises, or acres owned by tribes. The source is identified and linked.
- **Energy tax law:** the text now identifies the July 4, 2025 statutory change (Public Law 119-21), and the applicable wind and solar clean electricity credit deadlines—construction begun before July 5, 2026, or facilities placed in service before January 1, 2028, subject to further eligibility conditions. IRS Notice 2025-42 and IRS tribal-government guidance document the distinction between underlying credit eligibility and elective payment. No assertion is made that a particular project qualifies or that every federal energy funding program remains fully funded.
- **Tourism:** the NATIVE Act was approved September 23, 2016 (Public Law 114-221). The Hualapai Skywalk case is expanded using the March 19, 2012 federal district court order: the tribal council's condemnation of a contractual interest prompted questions of jurisdiction and exhaustion of tribal remedies. The order is not represented as resolving the ultimate commercial dispute. Added an assessment of project control, liability, employment, visitor spending and cultural authority.

### C. Corporate histories and federal contracting (§§121.13–121.16)

- **Cherokee Nation Industries:** Cherokee Nation Businesses' institutional timeline confirms the June 1969 manufacturing venture in Stilwell, the Principal Chiefs Act in 1970, W. W. Keeler's August 1971 election, and the second plant's 1974 production of IBM parts. The narrative no longer implies the tribal government's historical existence had been extinguished. The appointed and elected phases of Keeler's leadership are distinguished.
- **Cherokee financial reporting:** the FY 2023 company report describes allocating 37% of profit to the Nation and retaining/reinvesting the remaining 63%. This is included as a reported allocation for the stated period, not a verified perpetual legal distribution rate. The Nation's FY 2025 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report listing, published in 2026, is linked as a separate audited-government evidence source; its detailed financial statements were not independently re-audited here. Improved distinctions among operating revenue, operating profit, net income, distributable cash, governmental distributions and community economic benefits.
- **Ho-Chunk, Inc.:** the corporation's own history verifies its 1994 establishment by the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska and its explicit diversification response to gaming competition. The tribe's 1992 casino preceded the company; the text no longer implies the corporation's formation preceded tribal gaming. The FY 2025 annual report announcement, dated June 9, 2026, establishes availability, not independent verification of all reported outcomes. The housing materials point to a 1990–2022 socioeconomic study. The narrative maintains distinction between institutional achievement and evidence of household/community outcomes.
- **8(a) rules:** the original draft's blanket treatment of tribal enterprise disadvantage was corrected. The January 2026 edition of 13 CFR §124.109 recognizes tribal social disadvantage but requires the tribe to demonstrate economic disadvantage, whereas qualifying Alaska Native corporations have a statutory deeming provision. Each applicant entity must satisfy applicable business, ownership, control and size requirements. The SBA guidance dated August 28, 2026 identifies September 10, 2026 as the effective date of a new individual social-disadvantage rule and specifically excludes entity-owned firms from that new individual test. This does not remove other applicable eligibility, approval or compliance rules.
- **Public finance:** the chapter now treats own-source revenue as a broader category than enterprise distributions alone; taxes, fees, lease payments, royalties and investment income may contribute. The chapter retains the difference between corporate performance and government revenue and between federal transfers and revenue generated by the nation.

### D. Comparative economic institutions (§§121.17–121.20)

- **Canada's fiscal framework:** the chapter retains both the original 2005 First Nations Fiscal and Statistical Management Act and its subsequent development. The 2023 amendments adding the First Nations Infrastructure Institute are identified. The current consolidated act is linked in Source Window 3. The FNFA announced its C$90 million inaugural debenture in June 2014; the narrative uses that dated issuance as evidence of pooled borrowing and does not equate it with current outstanding debt.
- **Reserve property and lending:** qualified the original generalized description of Indian Act section 89 creditor protections to account for the location and ownership of property, creditor status, and statutory exceptions.
- **Sen̓áḵw:** corrected the draft's 2020 construction date to September 6, 2022 for the documented groundbreaking. Squamish Nation's project record confirms the May 2022 services agreement and C$1.4 billion CMHC loan announcement. The 2026 project updates confirm continuing construction; the project is not described as completed.
- **Indigenous Loan Guarantee Program:** Department of Finance Canada's 2026 financial statements establish the C$10 billion guarantee envelope and the administering corporation's December 2024 incorporation. The first C$400 million guarantee in July 2025 supported a consortium representing 38 Indigenous communities in acquiring a 12.5% interest in the Westcoast pipeline system. The guarantee is credit support, not a cash grant or the total valuation of the investment.
- **Canadian North:** a material anachronism was corrected. Exchange Income Corporation reports completing acquisition in July 2025; Canadian North is no longer jointly owned by the Inuit corporations named in the original draft. Nunavut's July 15, 2026 announcement confirms its decision not to exercise its option to acquire a minority stake. The distinction between equity ownership and continued arrangements for public air service is preserved.
- **Métis Settlements:** the 1989 Alberta–Métis Settlements Accord was distinguished from implementing legislation in 1990. Avoided implying all Métis communities operate under the Alberta land-based settlement model.
- **Procurement:** the 5% mandatory minimum contract-value target is distinct from actual departmental results. Official FY 2024–25 reporting records PSPC at 3.16% and Shared Services Canada at 9.8%; Finance Canada's FY 2025–26 results report records 12.77%. All are department-specific, dated measures; not government-wide figures.
- **Mexico and Ixtlán de Juárez:** the IPSI case study supports the community forestry enterprise, assembly-based decision-making, forest management plans and distributions to enterprise and community purposes. These are historically attributed institutional observations, not a current audited income statement. The discussion distinguishes landholding membership from municipal residence, and legally differentiated ejido parcels from communal rights. The September 30, 2024 Article 2 constitutional amendment confirms Indigenous peoples' and communities' public-law status with legal personality and their own patrimony. The June 2026 call and consultation protocol for proposed implementing legislation demonstrate an ongoing process, not proof of enactment or completion.
- **Synthesis:** the concluding distinction among growth, enterprise ownership, community welfare, and the practical capacity of self-government is preserved. The geographical introduction no longer promises a separate Greenland case study not actually provided.

## 4. Source windows and documentary integrity

All three source windows are explicitly labeled as paraphrases. No unverified quotation was inserted. The first is now historically versioned (1934 and 1990), the second links to the originating statute and the agency program, and the third distinguishes the original Canadian fiscal statute from later amendments. Student questions, context, perspective and limitations remain present. The public manuscript includes a linked documentary register and section-specific reference notes rather than production commentary or a separate embedded technical audit.

The source review prioritized original statutes, regulatory text, court orders, official government reporting and Indigenous institutional histories. Corporate sources are identified as self-reported evidence; the chapter distinguishes them from independent assessments. Links point to the available source records, but the review does not claim an exhaustive audit of every statement, every external link's continued future availability, every legacy cross-reference number, every corporate financial statement, or the outcome of every individual development project. Precise contemporary quantities without sufficient documentary support were avoided rather than estimated. The 2026 cutoff records verified developments available within that period, not an assurance of comprehensive coverage of every federal funding adjustment.

Future changes should be limited to new evidence, material corrections, or a subsequent edition rather than continued minor editorial passes.
