## 3. Historical corrections applied directly

1. **Indigenous sovereignty in the opening:** Changed an exclusive, overgeneralized claim about authority over 'most' territory to acknowledge substantial Indigenous control and regional variation.
2. **Mexico in 1821:** Distinguished the independent Mexican state from the federal republic established after the First Mexican Empire.
3. **Geographic terminology:** Removed duplication of *Llano Estacado* and *Staked Plains*.
4. **Treaty of Córdoba, 1821:** Identified Juan O'Donojú's local signature; the Spanish government repudiated the agreement and formally recognized Mexican independence only in **1836**.
5. **Texas demographics:** Clarified commonly cited 1836 estimates of about 30,000 Anglo-Americans, 3,500 Tejanos, and 5,000 enslaved Black people, and noted the uncertainty of contemporary categories and totals.
6. **Apache scalp bounties:** Removed uncorroborated specific attribution of a 1837 Chihuahua act and distinguished documented Sonoran mid-1830s laws from Chihuahua's policies.
7. **John J. Johnson attack:** Corrected the personal name, placed the April 22, 1837 attack in the Animas Mountains region, clarified use of a concealed swivel gun, named Juan José Compá, and distinguished scholarly counts from divergent older narratives.
8. **Texas–Cherokee 1836 treaty:** Restored the sequence: February 23 signing, **December 29, 1836 tabling**, **December 16, 1837 nullification**; recognized Sam Houston's legal disagreement.
9. **Council House Fight (1840):** Added alternate rendering of Muguara/Muk-wah-ruh, acknowledged Mexican children among the captives brought to council, and adjusted the approximate number of Comanche prisoners to thirty (account-dependent).
10. **Tehuacana Creek (1844):** Distinguished the October 9 signed treaty from proposed fixed-boundary articles **struck during council negotiations before signature**, according to the published *Texas Indian Papers*; the Texas Senate subsequently ratified the treaty January 24, 1845, and President Anson Jones signed it February 5.
11. **Primary-source transparency:** Linked the edited Mier y Terán diary, Texas State Library Cherokee treaty text, TSHA Council House entry with identifiable McLeod report, and treaty transcription hosted by the Lipan Apache Tribe. Corrected documentary prompts and legal inferences.
12. **Map catalog accuracy:** Clarified that Texas State Archives map no. 02026 is a photostat record of an 1834 Arrowsmith map; the consulted catalog provides metadata, **not a currently viewable digital image**.
13. **Bibliography:** Added the published Mier y Terán documentary edition with editor, translator, press and date; retained verified scholarly works.
14. **HTML accessibility/responsiveness:** Made the chronology table independently horizontally scrollable on small screens, with a keyboard-focusable labeled container.

The final reconciliation also corrected Chapter 68’s opening: **Part XII begins at Chapter 67**, while Chapter 68 continues it. The summary and glossary now consistently state that the 1844 proposed boundary articles were omitted, rather than describing treaty boundary provisions as unenforceable. The four source windows remain documented editorial paraphrases, not original transcriptions.

No corrections required to the established chronology of Texas's 1836 de facto independence, the Neches battle in July 1839, the Council House Fight on March 19, 1840, the August 1840 Victoria–Linnville raid, or the annexation process culminating December 29, 1845.

### Documentary methodology

Four primary-source windows remain **editorial paraphrases or document syntheses**. They are not manuscript-collated quotations. In particular, the Council House Fight source window combines reportage and later memoirs, and the exact original McLeod wording was not independently collated against a manuscript; the Mier y Terán study identifies a published edited edition but does not assert that each claim was checked against a specific diary folio. Treaty text, official interpretation, and subsequent ratification must be distinguished in teaching. This is a limitation of an identified first-edition instructional adaptation, not a claim of verbatim source publication.

The chapter uses the academic concept of Comanchería critically: it does not equate Comanche regional influence with a centralized sovereign state or fixed boundaries. It distinguishes local Apache communities, the place of subordinate/migrant nations, Mexican frontier districts, and different Texan governing coalitions, and does not make the U.S.–Mexico War itself part of this chapter.

After the direct historical and depth expansion, a further cross-reading of the completed chapter revealed two remaining editorial inconsistencies. Both were corrected directly in the canonical HTML, not left as recommendations:
