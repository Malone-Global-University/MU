## Independent source and history checks

### 1. Treaty framework (§§64.2–64.4)

The 1791 Treaty of Holston and the July 8, 1817 and February 27, 1819 Cherokee treaties are distinguishable in date and provisions. The 1817 agreement explicitly named Cherokees living east of the Mississippi and on the Arkansas, contemplated land exchange on an acre-for-acre basis, and set census and other administrative terms. The 1819 agreement made specific eastern cessions, addressed 640-acre individual reservations under specified conditions, fee-simple grants, education funds, annuity division, and a renewed obligation to remove intruders. The final narrative distinguishes national land, migration, and household grants; it does not treat every reservation provision as identical.

Primary transcriptions:  
- https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-cherokee-1791-0029  
- https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-cherokee-1817-0140  
- https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-cherokee-1819-0177

### 2. Cherokee national legal development (§§64.5–64.8)

The early laws printed in *Cherokee Phoenix* include the September 11, 1808 measures and April 1810 enactments concerning interclan responsibility and the formation of a Standing Committee. The October 20, 1820 resolution laid out eight districts, judicial officers, marshals and circuit administration. The final text does not equate statutory centralization with immediate disappearance of older kin and town authority. It treats written-law objectives separately from questions of enforcement.

Primary transcriptions:  
- https://www.wcu.edu/library/DigitalCollections/CherokeePhoenix/Vol1/no04/cherokee-laws-page-1-column-1a.html  
- https://www.wcu.edu/library/DigitalCollections/CherokeePhoenix/Vol1/no06/cherokee-laws-page-1-column-1a.html

### 3. Language, gender and slavery (§§64.9–64.12)

The syllabary gained broad recognition around 1821 and was used for Cherokee-language correspondence and later publication. The text distinguishes its development from later typographic standardization, and English-language mission instruction from Cherokee-language learning. Women's matrilineal roles, their exclusion from the formal electorate, and the varied conditions of enslaved African Americans are treated as distinct historical matters rather than aggregated into a single marginal category. The scholarly reference register includes McLoughlin, Perdue, Miles and Reed.

### 4. New Echota and constitutional convention (§§64.13–64.14)

New Echota became the Cherokee national capital in 1825. The council authorization of October 13, 1826 arranged for a convention scheduled to meet July 4, 1827, while the completed constitution is dated July 26, 1827. The final text distinguishes the convention's framework and its legal process from unsupported accounts of private deliberation.

Primary collection: https://www.loc.gov/item/28014183/

### 5. Constitution, territory and rights (§§64.15–64.16)

Article I, §2 declares Cherokee lands common national property while protecting citizens' improvements and restricting their alienation to outsiders. Article II separates government powers. Article III establishes the Committee and Council as legislative branches and states specific voting, officeholding and racial qualifications. Article IV assigns the election of the principal chief to the General Council. Article VI contains provisions relating to clergy, belief qualifications for civil office, and protected religious worship. The prose and paired documentary studies distinguish those clauses and do not falsely imply all restrictions share the same scope. The final text acknowledges the existence of enslavement and gender inequality within the nation.

Primary serial publication:

### 6. Cherokee Phoenix and national diplomacy (§§64.17–64.20)

The first issue of *Cherokee Phoenix* appeared February 21, 1828. Its bilingual purpose and publication of laws and political material are independently identifiable. John Ross was selected principal chief in 1828 under the constitutional procedure, not via a modern direct presidential vote. The diplomatic source study uses identifiable 1827 correspondence printed in the newspaper May 14, 1828, rather than an invented speech. The narrative remains bounded by 1828, leaving the removal era and forced migration for later chapters.

They cover: 1817/1819 treaties; early national laws; 1826 convention authorization; 1827 constitutional territory; 1827 voting, officeholding and religion; the 1828 first *Phoenix* issue; and national diplomatic correspondence. Where full original wording is not collated, the window explicitly labels its summary as an editorial paraphrase. One short excerpt from Article I §2 is verified against the published transcription. No invented direct quotations, fabricated speeches, or ungrounded facsimile claims were introduced.

The source program is sufficient for **first-edition historical-text publication** but is not a claim of exhaustive archival or original-language facsimile collation. Scholarship is identified and cross-checked for bibliographic identity, but complete page-by-page review of every modern secondary work is outside this edition's evidentiary scope. These limitations should accompany deployment if higher documentary standards or illustrated map plates are requested later.
