## 1. Original 1885 Major Crimes Act — corrected

**Location:** §118.3 main narrative; primary-source window “Ex parte Crow Dog (1883) and the Major Crimes Act (1885).”

The original window incorrectly implied that the March 3, 1885 Act applied in the territories and that later amendments first extended coverage to reservations inside states. The actual §9, 23 Stat. 385, provided **two branches from the outset**:

- For seven enumerated crimes committed by Indians **within U.S. territories, whether inside or outside an Indian reservation**, territorial criminal law and courts applied.
- For those crimes committed by Indians **within an Indian reservation situated inside a state**, the act required trial under laws and in courts applicable to offenses within the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States.

The revised narrative and source window explain both clauses and avoid treating the modern definition of Indian country as identical to the original statute's geographic terminology. The existing broader distinction between tribal and federal authority is preserved.

**Evidence:** [March 3, 1885 statute, original text](https://teachinglegalhistory.unl.edu/s/oer/item/955); [*United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375 (1886)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/118/375/); [*Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556 (1883)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/109/556).

## 2. VAWA 2013/2022 changes — corrected

**Locations:** §118.9 jurisdiction matrix; §118.15 substantive narrative; primary-source window “Special Tribal Criminal Jurisdiction: 25 U.S.C. §1304.”

The revised material distinguishes the 2013 scheme—covering specified domestic/dating violence and protection-order offenses and requiring non-Indian defendants to have qualifying ties through residence, employment, or intimate/dating relationships—from the 2022 reforms, which **removed the old ties-to-the-tribe requirement**. It retains the important limitations: only specified covered crimes, other statutory conditions including geography and procedural safeguards, and the general rule that special Tribal criminal jurisdiction cannot reach cases with **both a non-Indian defendant and a non-Indian victim** other than qualifying obstruction-of-justice and assault-of-tribal-justice-personnel cases. It does not imply a general reversal of *Oliphant* or blanket tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians.

The text now separates the ordinary Indian-country framework of **25 U.S.C. §1304** from the separate designated Alaska Native village pathway established by **25 U.S.C. §1305**, rather than suggesting that the latter automatically converts all village lands into Indian country. The October 1, 2022 effective date appears in the narrative. The pre-2022 statute is linked separately so that students can make a historical comparison instead of reading only the later codification.

**Evidence:** [25 U.S.C. §1304 (current statutory text)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t25/s1304/); [2021 statutory text (before the 2022 changes)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title25/html/USCODE-2021-title25-chap15.htm); [25 U.S.C. §1305](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/25/1305); [U.S. Department of Justice, VAWA 2013/2022 explanation](https://www.justice.gov/tribal/violence-against-women-act-vawa-reauthorization-2013-0).

## 3. Public Law 280, GAO evidence, and methodology — corrected

**Locations:** §§118.12 and 118.20.

The text now distinguishes the **five states originally designated in 1953** from the subsequent inclusion of Alaska in 1958. It clarifies that federal acceptance of the Omaha Reservation retrocession occurred in **October 1970** and federal acceptance of the **partial** Yakama Nation retrocession in **October 2015**. These are dates of federal acceptance, not an invented assertion that all arrangements took effect immediately in identical ways.

The November 2026 GAO report's estimate—about **65% of the 575 federally recognized tribes located in the six mandatory Public Law 280 states**—is retained with its critical qualification: **the geographic figure is not the percentage of tribes actually subject to the state's criminal jurisdiction under the law**. The report explains that certain tribal reservations were exempted and that some states returned jurisdiction through retrocession. The chapter also distinguishes GAO's November **lower-48** report from its February 2026 **Alaska** study and states the principal recommendation concerning tribal initiation and consent in retrocession processes.

**Evidence:** [U.S. GAO-27-108087 (November 16, 2026), including figure 1's methodological note](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-27-108087/index.html); [U.S. GAO-26-107533, Alaska](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107533); [Bureau of Indian Affairs, Yakama retrocession](https://www.bia.gov/as-ia/opa/online-press-release/assistant-secretary-washburn-accepts-washington-states-retrocession).

## 4. Additional precision improvements

- **§118.5:** Date of the Navajo Nation's own court system is now **April 1, 1959**, consistent with the [Navajo Nation Judicial Branch history](https://courts.navajo-nsn.gov/history.htm).
- **§118.16:** The establishment of the Navajo Peacemaking Court is dated **1982** and attributed to the **Navajo Judicial Conference**, consistent with the [judiciary's own public guide](https://courts.navajo-nsn.gov/publicguide.htm).
- **§118.14:** Enhanced sentencing authority under the Tribal Law and Order Act now distinguishes **offense/defendant eligibility in 25 U.S.C. §1302(b)**—a qualifying prior conviction or a comparable offense punishable by more than a year under federal or state law—from the separate procedural safeguards in **§1302(c)**. Existing 3-year-per-offense, 9-year aggregate, and $15,000 figures remain unchanged. See [25 U.S.C. §1302](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t25/s1302/).
- **§118.8:** The original quotation from *Montana v. United States* is retained. It is supported by the [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/450/544); the two historically consequential exceptions are explained in the prose.
- **Source windows:** All three now offer direct links to primary opinions, statutes, or official interpretive sources; original paraphrases continue to be labeled as such. No long quotations were manufactured.

## 5. Compliance and instructional depth

The chapter maintains the prescribed division of labor in the textbook. It makes only brief contextual references to Canadian First Nations constitutional politics (Chapter 117), the Alaska Native land settlement (Chapter 115), the broader criminal-justice system (Chapter 130), violence against Indigenous women (Chapter 129), and contemporary Indigenous governance (Chapter 139). A chronological account of U.S. tribal judicial sovereignty remains the central subject.

The updated jurisdiction matrix remains explicitly illustrative and qualified by Indian-country status, statutory exceptions, tribal/Indian status, and offense categories. The chapter does not collapse *Montana*'s civil-jurisdiction doctrine into *Oliphant*'s criminal-jurisdiction holding; does not confuse tribal sovereign immunity with adjudicative jurisdiction; and does not present the statutory expansions of 2010, 2013, or 2022 as wholesale elimination of federal and state limits. These are critical interpretive strengths that should remain unchanged.
