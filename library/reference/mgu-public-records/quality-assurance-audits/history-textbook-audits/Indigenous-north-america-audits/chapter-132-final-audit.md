## 1. Governing assignment and historical scope

The authoritative chapter outline assigns this chapter to investigate public history, museums, memorials, renamed places, contested monuments, and the struggle over how colonial history is remembered. The approved internal plan comprises five divisions and twenty numbered sections (132.1–132.20). The revised file retains that structure and covers the United States and Canada substantially, with discrete comparative material on Mexico City and Nuuk, Greenland.

**Central interpretation:** Public memory does not merely repeat the past. Institutions select evidence, designate sites, name places, sponsor commemorations, and define which accounts appear in civic life. Indigenous nations have maintained their own historical traditions and increasingly challenged or directed the institutions that represent their histories. The text distinguishes documented historical events, later acts of commemoration, and modern disputes over civic honor.

**Chapter boundaries:** The text uses previous chapters on boarding and residential schools, repatriation, and child welfare solely as contextual references. It does not replace Chapter 125's repatriation law, Chapter 127's school reckoning, Chapter 131's family-policy history, or Chapter 145's intended treatment of detailed technology and data sovereignty. It closes Part XXII and connects forward to Chapter 133.

## 2. Revision history and substantive work completed

The submitted first-pass manuscript was structurally complete but substantively lean (one or two narrative paragraphs per numbered section and a condensed closing apparatus). This revision retains its chapter architecture and educational features while adding evidence-based historical development throughout:

1. **Division I (132.1–132.4):** Expanded the evidentiary roles of oral tradition, winter counts, wampum, archival catalogues, translation, and community access protocols. Added specific Indigenous-authored interventions by William Apess, Sarah Winnemucca, George Copway, Zitkala-Sa, Vine Deloria Jr., Jennifer Nez Denetdale, and other scholars.
2. **Division II (132.5–132.8):** Distinguished the history of colonial figures from the later history of their monuments, addressed commissioning and dedication practices, and developed cases involving Columbus, Serra, Oñate, Cornwallis, Macdonald, Ryerson, Sand Creek, and Little Bighorn. Clarified that removal from public honor does not necessarily destroy the historical record, while destruction of an original artifact can eliminate material evidence.
3. **Division III (132.9–132.12):** Expanded the history of museum collecting, problematic ethnographic displays, provenance and cataloguing, the 1992 Canadian museum task-force report, the 1989 NMAI legislation and 2004 opening, Indigenous-operated museums, Mukurtu, and management of federal historic sites.
4. **Division IV (132.13–132.16):** Added the changing federal status of Denali/Mount McKinley through 2025; precise Columbus Day legal chronology; distinctions in Canadian commemorative observances; detailed TRC Calls to Action 79–83; the Mexico City sequence of 2020, 2021, and 2023; and the 2020 Greenland statue controversy and vote.
5. **Division V (132.17–132.20):** Expanded Indigenous memorial and veterans' commemoration, the operational choices for contested monuments, differences between consultation and shared authority, and a substantive five-paragraph conclusion integrating historical interpretation and Indigenous sovereignty.

The draft has been developed at textbook scale without compressing late divisions or relying on a superficial chronology alone.
