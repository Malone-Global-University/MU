## 3. Historical and source audit

### Verified / supportable in the draft

1. **Taylor et al., Science 2023:** *Early dispersal of domestic horses into the Great Plains and northern Rockies*, doi:10.1126/science.adc9691. The primary article describes pre-1680 domestic horses at Blacks Fork (Wyoming), Kaw River (Kansas), Paa’ko Pueblo (New Mexico), and American Falls (Idaho), and Iberian ancestry, transport/management and ceremonial evidence. It supports **early horse presence**, **not** all nations being fully mounted during the first half of the 1600s. Primary: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adc9691 ; PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36996225/ .
2. **Spanish law:** Philip II's 1568 limitation on Indigenous horseback riding, carried into the later *Recopilación*, is supported; **do not confuse** the decree's date (1568), the compilation's authorization (1680), and its first printed edition (1681). Legal / scholarly cross-check: https://www.loc.gov/item/31010935/ ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/feral-empire/indigenous-equestrianism/4BCD90FE74886000E1634EEF5F6EEC83 . The original feature was a vague composite instead of a source-specific window and has been corrected.
3. **Henday, 1754–1755:** Documented HBC journey and equestrian observations. Critically, his **original journal is lost**; four surviving versions diverge, including on whether Indigenous leaders agreed to travel to the Bay. DCB: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/1400 ; Great Plains Encyclopedia: https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.ea.015.html . This has been clarified in the Primary Source Window without inventing quotations.
4. **Saukamappee–Thompson:** Thompson heard Saukamappee's account in winter 1787–1788, recollecting earlier events often placed near the 1730s. Historical source print: *David Thompson's Narrative of His Explorations in Western America, 1784–1812* (Tyrrell ed., 1916), pp. 326–332; contextual guide https://assets.mission-us.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/16182109/psd-saukamappee_s_recollections-mission3.pdf . The testimony is mediated memory, not independently dated contemporaneous first arrival.
5. **Villasur, 1720:** Defeat of a Spanish column by Pawnee and Otoe forces near the Platte/Loup Rivers is well established. Accounts vary on the exact day; chapter wisely states the year only. Nebraska State Historical Society: https://history.nebraska.gov/marker-monday-the-villasur-expedition-1720/ . This is not proof that horses alone determined the battle.
6. **1780s smallpox:** The 1781 Missouri River epidemic affecting Mandan and other village societies is historically attested. NPS Mandan history: https://www.nps.gov/knri/learn/historyculture/mandan.htm . Avoid importing 1837 demographic figures into the 1780s.
7. **Lakota winter counts:** Specific, distinct records need not offer one universal 'first Lakota horse' date. Long Soldier's winter count records an 1801–1802 horse-related episode for that remembered group; other winter counts differ. Minneapolis Institute of Art: https://new.artsmia.org/learning-resources/teaching-the-arts/tta/objects-in-focus/lakota-winter-count . This example has been added to the Indigenous Source Study.

### Corrections incorporated into the working HTML

1. §49.6: scope horse ownership prohibition carefully; riding restrictions are better attested than a blanket universal ban on ownership.
2. Spanish regulation source window: identify **1568** prohibition and book VI, title I, law XXXIII in the later *Recopilación*; explain specific law vs separately granted exceptions and 1680/1681 compilation-publication dates.
3. Henday window: identify four divergent transmitted copies, lost original, and disagreement over Indigenous response to the Bay trade proposal.
4. Winter-count feature: add the source-specific Long Soldier 1801–1802 example, explicitly not a date for all Lakota nations.
5. §49.8: note Paa’ko Pueblo evidence may predate the permanent 1598 New Mexico colony. Avoid explaining all early circulation as a direct result of settled New Mexican ranching.
6. §49.8: remove an overstrong implication that genetic ancestry excludes all later British/French-associated contributions.
7. Archaeological feature: identify actual site evidence at Blacks Fork, Kaw River, Paa’ko, and American Falls.
8. Oral-history feature: identify Saukamappee's recorded recollection and its mediated textual context.

**No direct, unattributed historical quotations were added.** The updated instructional primary-source paraphrases remain labeled as paraphrases. There is no basis for a global historical rewrite at present, but any *new* claims in an expanded draft must be independently sourced and audited.

### Limitations to observe during expansion

- Nineteenth-century descriptions of horse tack, children's training, marriage exchanges, women's work, and ceremony must not automatically be projected back to 1600–1750. Specify nation, date, and the source's retrospective nature.
- Archaeological horse presence ≠ universal horse adoption; Indigenous exchange networks are historically plausible and favored by current evidence, but exact transmission routes and nation-by-nation dates are often unresolved.
- Avoid generic Indigenous statements ("all Plains people") where household, nation, and century distinctions are known.
- Avoid invented herd size, load capacities, raid force sizes, and precisely quantified environmental impacts.
- Protect Chapter 50 Comanche-specific political history, Chapter 51 Apache nation histories, and Chapter 53 captivity/slavery for their assigned scope; refer to them rather than narrate them in advance.
