## Historical verification and corrections

1. **HCR 108 (Aug. 1, 1953)** was checked against the original 67 Stat. B132. It declared a policy objective and requested legislation; it did not itself terminate all identified nations.
2. **Public Law 280 (Aug. 15, 1953)** was distinguished from tribe-specific termination statutes. It concerned specified criminal and civil adjudicatory jurisdiction; original mandatory-state and exception language was checked against Bureau of Indian Affairs / U.S. Code materials. Alaska was added later.
3. **Menominee:** 1954 act versus Apr. 30, 1961 effective date, 1968 treaty-rights ruling, 1970 policy shift, 1973 federal restoration, and later land/governmental rebuilding were separately described. Original statute and official Supreme Court opinion linked.
4. **Klamath:** 1954 act, 1958 statutory withdrawal election (1,660 withdrawing and 473 remaining), and later timberland transfers were distinguished, using the Klamath act and 1971 Court of Claims case.
5. **Western Oregon:** 1954 law distinguished from later federal implementation and the Siletz (1977) and Grand Ronde (1983) restoration statutes.
6. **California:** 1958 Rancheria Act linked to the original statute; incomplete or contested implementation and *Tillie Hardwick* litigation were distinguished from a universal restoration of all rancherias.
7. **Utah:** 1954 termination of the four Paiute bands was distinguished from the Cedar band's different position and the five-band 1980 restoration. The Ute Partition and Termination Act was treated as affecting statutory membership categories, not all Ute citizens uniformly.
8. **Federal policy in 1970:** Nixon's July 8, 1970 message was correctly described as executive policy, not a legislative repeal or automatic restoration.

## Documentary limitations

Core federal statutory, judicial, and institutional URLs were matched against public records; two links found unreliable during final checking were replaced with GovInfo and Smithsonian sources. The audit did not independently reproduce every federal archival item or authenticate every secondary historical proposition against an original transcript. The source windows contain labeled analytical paraphrases, not word-for-word transcriptions. Several documentary links lead to official law compilations or archival finding aids rather than a particular scan of a hearing transcript, and are clearly presented as such. The narrative differentiates contemporary documents, subsequent court interpretation, and tribal historical accounts.
