## 2. Historical verification

- **1675 religious-leader arrests:** 47 accused, three executed by hanging and one suicide in custody are supported by John L. Kessell's *Kiva, Cross, and Crown*, Chapter 5 (National Park Service reproduction), though other retellings vary in details. The chapter appropriately attributes the figures and notes record limitations.
- **1680 Spanish casualties:** More than 400 settlers/soldiers and **21 Franciscan friars** are conventional estimates, supported by National Park Service accounts and Smithsonian/NMAI presentation. The denominator for friars varies in historical summaries (32 or 33); the chapter says *about 33*, which is justified by this variance.
- **Leaders:** Luis Tupatú (Picuris), Alonso Catiti (Santo Domingo), a Taos associate named in early testimony as **El Saca** (rendered in some later narratives as *Jaca*), and **Domingo Naranjo** are grounded but not interchangeable. Scholarship and the New Mexico history education resource identify Domingo Naranjo's African and Indigenous parentage rather than a securely Pueblo genealogy; the corrected text avoids presenting every participant as Pueblo by descent. **Juan el Tano** is supported as the envoy who delivered the red/white cross ultimatum to Governor Otermín at Santa Fe.
- **1689 Zia:** A figure around 600 killed is recorded in historical Spanish-derived accounts; it is not an independently verified casualty census. The text properly labels it an attributed, potentially imprecise estimate.
- **December 1693 Santa Fe:** The frequently cited execution of around 70 captured Pueblo defenders after Vargas's recapture is supported by Santa Fe municipal history and modern PBS historical accounts. The corrected chapter preserves attribution to Spanish accounts.
- **July 1694 Astialakwa:** The often-cited figure of **84 Jemez killed** is supported by historical reconquest synthesis; not independently countable today. The chapter retains clear attribution.
- **1696 uprising:** Five Franciscans and about twenty-one additional Spaniards killed appear in Vargas-era historical literature, with exact attribution and variations in sources; the manuscript appropriately labels the figures as reported estimates.
- **Document windows:** Antonio de Otermín's letter to Fray Francisco de Ayeta is dated **8 September 1680**; Pedro Naranjo's deposition was **19 December 1681**, obtained when he was captured at Isleta; the journal of Diego de Vargas supports the violent recapture of Santa Fe in December 1693. All three HTML source features are explicitly labeled paraphrases without manufactured quotations. Otermín, Naranjo and Vargas are interested witnesses; the chapter analyzes those limitations.
- **Settlement archaeology:** Patokwa (est. c. 1681), Boletsakwa (c. 1683 on tree-ring evidence) and Kotyiti (early 1680s) are archaeologically attested. Jemez/Towa communities built the first two; Kotyiti in the Cochiti/Keres region included Tewa-speaking residents. All have relevant *dual-plaza* evidence, although the precise identification of individual kivas is debated. §44.20 was locally reinforced with this chronology and differentiated evidence.
- **Chapter 54 cross-reference:** The authoritative outline names Chapter 54 *Native Christianity and Religious Transformation* and assigns Christian adaptation, resistance and syncretism broadly. It does **not** expressly guarantee a detailed Pueblo-specific discussion. The cross-reference was reworded as a thematic connection rather than a promise that Chapter 54 covers the case in greater detail.
