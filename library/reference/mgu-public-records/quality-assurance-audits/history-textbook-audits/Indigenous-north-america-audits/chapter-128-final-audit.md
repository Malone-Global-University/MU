## 1. Scholarly determination and completion scope

The approved five-division, twenty-section Chapter 128 manuscript has been substantively revised, rather than replaced. The completed chapter preserves Indigenous survivors and their families as historical agents while explaining what the Interior Department's 2021–2024 investigation established, what the official counts do not establish, and why federal apology and proposed legislation are distinct from completed remedies. It maintains the textbook's conceptual boundaries with Chapters 87–88 (boarding-school institutions), 125 (repatriation), 127 (Canada), 131 (child welfare), and 132 (public memory).

The initial direct section narrative was **8,501 words**. The finished chapter contains **11,118 words of direct historical narrative**, an addition of **2,617 words**, within the approved 10,000–12,000-word target. The count includes only direct narrative `<p>` children of numbered sections 128.1–128.20 and **excludes** documentary-link notes, the chapter opening and historical context, nested feature and case-study content, the chapter summary, source register, questions and other apparatus. Its shortest section has 462 direct narrative words and its longest 644, consistent with the desired approximately 450–650-word working range.

The main scholarly interventions are focused on accurately distinguishing types of evidence and types of institutional action. The draft's substantial architecture and sound core findings were retained. The specific prepublication deficiencies—narrative shortfall, imprecise 2022 death tally, misleading Carlisle count, omitted Genoa test outcome, erroneous definition of appropriations, incomplete characterization of recommendation seven, and lack of source links—are corrected in the final HTML.

## 3. Publication-critical historical corrections

### 3.1 Volume I's preliminary death count — §128.6

**Original issue:** Volume I's preliminary record was described only as deaths “in the hundreds.” That was not wrong in a strict numerical sense, but was unnecessarily imprecise in a documentary chapter.

**Final correction:** The text now identifies the 2022 finding as **more than 500 documented child deaths at approximately 19 school sites**. This is explicitly identified as a **preliminary documented minimum** rather than a count of all deaths or evidence that deaths were limited to those 19 institutions. The comparison with Volume II's 973 documented deaths remains a comparison between investigative stages, not an assertion that hundreds of new deaths occurred between 2022 and 2024.

**Evidence:** [Interior Indian Affairs — Volume I (May 2022)](https://www.bia.gov/sites/default/files/dup/inline-files/bsi_investigative_report_may_2022_508.pdf); [National Native American Boarding School Healing Coalition, report history](https://boardingschoolhealing.org/federal-indian-boarding-school-initiative/).

### 3.2 Appropriate interpretation of federal appropriations — §128.11

**Original issue:** The draft incorrectly defined appropriations as sums Congress “authorized.” A statutory authorization, an appropriation, and an actual expenditure are distinct fiscal concepts.

**Final correction:** The paragraph describes appropriations as budget authority made available for designated purposes, distinct from authorizations and confirmed expenditures. It retains the report's **more than $23.3 billion in FY2023 inflation-adjusted dollars made available during 1871–1969** and explains that the estimate covers related institutions and assimilation policies, not operating expenses at the 417 identified schools alone. A new explanation rejects dividing that entire estimate by the number of schools to infer a typical cost per campus. Volume II specifically states it did not research actual amounts spent at each institution.

**Evidence:** [Volume II, federal support and appropriations](https://www.bia.gov/sites/default/files/media_document/doi_federal_indian_boarding_school_initiative_investigative_report_vii_final_508_compliant.pdf), especially the funding analysis and methodological caveats.

### 3.3 Carlisle cemetery population and 1927 relocation — §128.13

**Original issue:** The statement that approximately **186 children** were associated with the Carlisle cemetery overstated the evidence. The published historical figure refers to **at least 186 individuals interred in the original cemetery between 1880 and 1918**, including students, Indigenous prisoners of war, and a teacher's child. Treating all 186 as children enrolled in the school would be misleading.

**Final correction:** The section specifies the population and time period; names the Army's cemetery **relocation in July 1927**; and explains why the move and re-marking of graves introduce problems of identity matching. It preserves the specific regulatory framework under which the Army Office of Army Cemeteries carried out modern returns, rather than misattributing all returns to NAGPRA.

**Evidence:** [Dickinson College, Carlisle cemetery information](https://carlisleindian.dickinson.edu/node/32911); [Office of Army Cemeteries, Carlisle Barracks](https://armycemeteries.army.mil/Cemeteries/Carlisle-Barracks-Main-Post-Cemetery).

### 3.4 Genoa historical years and the negative excavation finding — §128.14

**Original issue:** The draft described geophysical and archaeological work but omitted the **result** of the July 2023 test. A case study about evidentiary uncertainty must state negative findings as well as preliminary leads.

**Final correction:** The school is dated **1884–1934**. The manuscript now explains that a selected suspected gravesite was excavated in July 2023 and that **no human remains or grave features were found in that tested area** when the excavation ended July 20. It makes clear that the test does not resolve all reports of student deaths, establish the locations of all burials, or disprove evidence concerning other areas of the former school grounds. The case uses the result to teach methodological restraint rather than implying an unverified grave discovery.

**Evidence:** [Nebraska Commission on Indian Affairs, July 28, 2023 report](https://indianaffairs.state.ne.us/no-remains-found-at-former-genoa-indian-school/); [Nebraska Public Media, Genoa investigation](https://nebraskapublicmedia.org/en/news/news-articles/a-haunting-mystery-in-a-nebraska-town-how-a-federal-native-american-boarding-school-lost-its-dead/).

### 3.5 Alaska examples and governance chronology — §128.15

The completed section identifies **Wrangell Institute (1932–1975)** and the Bureau of Indian Affairs' operation of **Mt. Edgecumbe (1947–1983)**, and clarifies that later operation or governance must not be assumed to be identical to historical federal assimilation policies. Native Hawaiian institutions are discussed within their distinct legal and colonial histories. The chapter avoids assuming every school serving Indigenous students met the Interior investigation's classification criteria.

**Reference:** [Indian Affairs federal initiative overview](https://www.bia.gov/service/federal-indian-boarding-school-initiative), in conjunction with the educational-institution histories named in the draft and prepublication audit.

### 3.6 Correct account of the eight Volume II recommendations — §128.18

**Original issue:** Recommendation seven was reduced to archival access. That distorts its principal emphasis.

**Final correction:** The eight themes are presented in order: (1) acknowledgment/apology/repudiation/affirmation, (2) remedies for present-day impacts, (3) national memorial, (4) identify and return children, (5) former federal school sites, (6) public education and telling the story, (7) **further research into present-day health, economic, familial and intergenerational outcomes and culturally relevant interventions**, and (8) international relationships. The text recognizes that access to historic records remains important without mistaking it for the substance of number seven. It further distinguishes recommended investment commensurate with historic federal commitments from spending already authorized, appropriated, or disbursed.

**Evidence:** [Interior Volume II, recommendations, especially pp. 98–104](https://www.bia.gov/sites/default/files/media_document/doi_federal_indian_boarding_school_initiative_investigative_report_vii_final_508_compliant.pdf).

### 3.7 Congressional legislative status and presidential apology — §§128.17, 128.19

The October 25, 2024 presidential apology at the Gila River Indian Community remains accurately dated, described as a public executive acknowledgment, and distinguished from lawmaking, compensation, or an independent commission. The chapter uses the official published presidential remarks instead of an unattributed paraphrase.

The proposed Truth and Healing Commission on Indian Boarding School Policies Act is accurately described **as of the November 20, 2026 congressional calendar**. At that point S. 761 had been reported by the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs on July 31, 2025, without becoming law, while H.R. 7325 was a separately introduced House bill. The historical account does not transform committee action into passage by both chambers or into an operating commission.

**Evidence:** [GPO presidential remarks, October 25, 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/DCPD-202400946); [GPO congressional calendar, November 20, 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CCAL-119hcal-2026-11-20/html/CCAL-119hcal-2026-11-20-pt13.htm); [H.R. 7325 introduced text](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr7325ih).

## 4. Historical safeguards and interpretation

The chapter maintains distinctions necessary for sound historical writing:

- **417** federal Indian boarding schools is a reported list defined by institutional criteria, not a count of every school that educated Indigenous children. Volume II's additional **1,025** other institutions are **not** silently added to the 417.
- **973** documented deaths is a federal **minimum**, not a universal final death toll. Documentary deaths, missing children, physical burial locations and named individual identifications remain separate evidence categories.
- **74** marked or unmarked burial sites were identified at **65** school locations. The numerical difference reflects locations/sites, not contradictory figures.
- **$23.3 billion** is an appropriations estimate over the cited period and broader policy scope; it is neither an audited expenditure ledger nor a 2024 federal appropriation.
- Ancestral returns at Carlisle are described under Army cemetery processes, whereas other sites may involve different federal, tribal or state laws; NAGPRA is not treated as an automatic universal framework.
- The federal investigation, survivor testimony, tribal research, local archaeology, presidential speech, legislative proposals and actual enacted remedies are distinguished as different kinds of sources and actions.
- The U.S. chapter is compared to Canada's Chapter 127 but does not invent a U.S. equivalent of Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

These safeguards are embedded in the narrative and are reinforced by the three source windows, vocabulary boxes, historical debate and review questions.
