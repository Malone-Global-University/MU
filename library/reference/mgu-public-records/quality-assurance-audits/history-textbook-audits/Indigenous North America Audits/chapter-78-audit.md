## 3. Historical verification and editorial decisions

### 3.1 Treaties and Wallowa land protection

The June 11, 1855 treaty and June 9, 1863 treaty are separate instruments. The latter reduced the reservation by approximately ninety percent, with **disputed consent among non-signing bands**. The original text was ratified in 1859 and the revised treaty ratified and proclaimed in 1867. The 1855 instrument retains specified accustomed-use rights. Grant's **June 16, 1873** Wallowa reservation executive order and **June 10, 1875** revocation are confirmed by historical orders and subsequent legal findings. The chapter avoids claiming that the 1873 reserve covered the entirety of the band’s wider homeland.

Evidence: NPS Treaty Period <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/the-treaty-era.htm>; NPS 1863 Treaty <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/treaty-of-1863.htm>; U.S. Forest Service original treaty transcriptions; Indian Claims Commission findings on the 1873 reserve (<https://cdm17279.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p17279coll10/id/2265/download>).

### 3.2 1877 removal, outbreak, and Idaho campaign

Howard's May 1877 Fort Lapwai council and detention of Toohoolhoolzote are supported by Greene's documented study. The May 31 Dug Bar crossing occurred **before** the outbreak of fighting. The mid-June settler attacks were by identifiable participants and should not be attributed to an undifferentiated national command; the broader NPS account mentions **seventeen** settler deaths. At White Bird on **June 17**, NPS reports six peace-party members approaching under a white flag, shooting by a civilian volunteer, **34 U.S. dead**, additional wounded, and no Nimiipuu deaths. These precise categories are distinguished from total campaign casualties.

At Looking Glass's camp on **July 1**, troops struck a band that had been attempting to remain outside the fighting; this *expanded* rather than prevented resistance. The Clearwater engagement occurred **July 11–12**; the movement from Weippe onto the Lolo Trail is dated **July 16** in NPS interpretation.

Evidence: Greene <https://www.npshistory.com/publications/nepe/greene/chap1b.htm>; NPS White Bird <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/white-bird-battlefield.htm>; NPS Looking Glass <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/looking-glass-camp.htm>; NPS Clearwater <https://home.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/battle-of-the-clearwater.htm>; NPS Lolo Trail <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/lolo-trail-and-pass-history.htm>.

### 3.3 Big Hole, Camas Meadows, and Yellowstone

**Big Hole** was fought **August 9–10, 1877**. An early-morning assault killed Nimiipuu women and children and led to a prolonged counterattack and withdrawal. NPS editions present differing figures of approximately **60–90** or **80–90** Nimiipuu killed; these are **estimates** and must not be substituted for a fully identified death roll. Some NPS publications differ in how they include later wound deaths and civilian volunteer fatalities on the U.S. side. The final narrative acknowledges these conflicts rather than manufacturing a single count. The howitzer action is grounded in battlefield archaeology. **Camas Meadows** occurred **August 20**, when more than 200 U.S. pack animals were taken. Yellowstone passage involved documented tourist encounters and uncertain local route reconstruction.

Evidence: NPS Big Hole <https://www.nps.gov/biho/learn/historyculture/index.htm>; NPS archaeology <https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/retracing-fighters-steps-battle-big-hole.htm>; Greene <https://www.npshistory.com/publications/nepe/greene/index.htm>; NPS 1877 site index <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/1877.htm>.

### 3.4 Bear Paw and divided surrender

The September 30, 1877 attack is separated from the **October 1–5 siege**. The NPS chronology covers Miles's interception with approximately 450 troops after a rapid approach, a disputed October 1 truce/detention episode, October 2 prisoner exchange and Looking Glass's reported death, October 4 arrival of Howard, and **October 5** surrender by Joseph with **White Bird refusing and escaping**. Joseph did **not** command or surrender every community; the famous English surrender phrase was mediated by Charles Erskine Scott Wood and interpreters, so the text should not be presented as unmediated original-language testimony.

Evidence: NPS Bear Paw <https://home.nps.gov/nepe/planyourvisit/bear-paw-battlefield.htm>; Greene, Chapter 13 <https://www.npshistory.com/publications/nepe/greene/chap13a.htm>.

### 3.5 Captivity, Canada, advocacy, and return

NPS reports **432 people taken into American captivity** after Bear Paw, subsequently held at Fort Leavenworth and then moved in **1878** to Indian Territory. Estimates of those reaching Canada vary because groups escaped at different times; the final narrative explicitly discloses this uncertainty. Joseph's April **1879** *North American Review* article is treated as an edited and mediated political text, not a verbatim unassisted composition. The **1885** return was partial: some went to the Nez Perce Reservation in Idaho, while Joseph's people were placed at Colville in Washington, not restored to Wallowa. The chapter ends with continuing Nimiipuu political and cultural life.

Evidence: NPS aftermath <https://www.nps.gov/nepe/learn/historyculture/1877-aftermath.htm>; 1879 text <https://www.digitalhistory.uh.edu/disp_textbook.cfm?psid=4054&smtID=3>.

## 4. Documentary source-register methodology

All **five** source-study windows are attributed **editorial paraphrases and source comparisons**, not original manuscript facsimile collations, contemporary tape transcriptions, or authenticated translations of verbatim oral statements. The revised HTML links each study to directly inspectable publicly available treaty texts, NPS material, or editions of Greene's scholarship. This is an **honest instructional source-study format**, not a claim that every original archival folio, handwritten order, or interview tape has been collated.

1. Window I after §78.3: 1855/1863 treaties, divided consent, and Grant's Wallowa orders.
2. Window II after §78.4: Howard's Lapwai council, executive coercion, interpretation, and land availability.
3. Window III after §78.8: White Bird, neutral Looking Glass, Cottonwood, Clearwater, contemporary U.S. reports, and Indigenous evidence.
4. Window IV after §78.10: Gibbon's reports, Yellow Wolf's retrospective account, and archaeological interpretation at Big Hole.
5. Window V after §78.16: the Bear Paw siege, mediated surrender words, White Bird, and Joseph's 1879 advocacy.

All five identify creator/source type, dates, intended audience or transmission, evidentiary strengths and limitations, and five questions. They provide linked reading sources and do not manufacture extended literal quotations.
