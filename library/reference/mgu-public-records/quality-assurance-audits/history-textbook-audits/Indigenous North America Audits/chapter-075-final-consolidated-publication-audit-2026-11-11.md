## III. Focused historical verification and corrections

### A. Fort Laramie Treaty (1851) and territorial claims

The treaty was concluded **September 17, 1851**, with commissioners David D. Mitchell and Thomas Fitzpatrick. It recorded territory, peace and road/post rights, but did not accomplish a general land cession. The Sioux, Cheyenne–Arapaho, and Crow geographic descriptions did not settle actual overlapping land use or later competition. The Senate reduced the annuity commitment from fifty years to ten, subject to possible extension and acceptance; not all parties accepted identically. The added §75.3 discussion distinguishes mapped boundaries from practical territorial authority.

**Document:** Oklahoma State University, *Treaty of Fort Laramie with Sioux, etc., 1851*, https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-of-fort-laramie-with-sioux-etc-1851-0594 .

### B. December 6, 1866 — Bingham and Bowers

The death of Lieutenant Horatio S. Bingham and Sergeant Gideon Bowers near Fort Phil Kearny is independently corroborated by the National Register history and the U.S. Army's *Atlas of the Sioux Wars*, second edition. The chapter retains the precise date and avoids assigning the complete December 21 ambush plan to one individual. Added background on the **December 19** encounter, for which contemporary accounts note Captain James W. Powell's refusal to be drawn into a pursuit.

**Evidence:** National Register of Historic Places, Fort Phil Kearny historical documentation, https://npgallery.nps.gov/pdfhost/docs/NRHP/Text/64500734.pdf ; Charles D. Collins Jr., *Atlas of the Sioux Wars*, second edition (2006), https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Portals/7/educational-services/staff-rides/atlas-of-the-sioux-wars-second-edition.pdf . The PDF textual source was available through search results; a remote PDF screenshot request was blocked by its server, so direct facsimile examination was not completed.

### C. December 21, 1866 — Fetterman Fight

The decisive count is **81 total dead: 79 military personnel, two armed civilians** (James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher). The commonly reported allocation, retained with an attribution qualification, is **49 enlisted infantry, 27 cavalry, three officers**, and **two civilians**. The three officers were Fetterman, Frederick H. Brown, and George W. Grummond. Some contemporary/retrospective descriptions of assignments differ. The chapter correctly distinguishes the military defeat from Sand Creek, where a village of noncombatants was massacred. The precise decoy leader, Crazy Horse's personal tactical role, force size, Carrington's oral orders, and Indigenous casualties remain disputed; no invented precision was introduced.

**Evidence:** Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site, https://www.fortphilkearny.org/fort-phil-kearny-history ; U.S. Army *Atlas*; historic post return reprinted in congressional record, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-03722_00_00-228-1648-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-03722_00_00-228-1648-0000.pdf .

### D. Hayfield Fight, August 1, 1867

National Park Service reports **19 soldiers and six civilians** in the defensive position, under Lieutenant Sternberg (whose first-name spelling varies in published references). Sternberg was killed; civilian **Don A. Colvin** participated in the ensuing defense. Soldier/civilian distinctions, the defensive enclosure, and the effect of breech-loading weapons are preserved. The manuscript now qualifies the varying attribution of attacking forces among Lakota and Cheyenne participants and makes no unsupported precise casualty claims.

**Evidence:** NPS, *Hayfield Fight*, https://home.nps.gov/bica/learn/historyculture/hayfield-fight.htm .

### E. Wagon Box Fight, August 2, 1867

Verified Captain James W. Powell's command and the killing of **Lieutenant John C. Jenness and two enlisted men** (Henry Haggerty and Thomas Boyle). The fight near Fort Phil Kearny is separate from Hayfield near Fort C. F. Smith; high reported Indigenous casualties were unreliable contemporary claims, not enumerated facts. Differences in defensive cover and breech-loading firepower help explain the tactical outcome without negating the larger Indigenous strategic victory.

**Evidence:** *WyoHistory*, *The Wagon Box Fight, 1867*, https://wyohistory.org/encyclopedia/wagon-box-fight-1867 ; U.S. Army *Atlas*.

### F. Indian Peace Commission of 1867

Congress authorized the commission in July 1867. Verified the full eight-member roster: civilian members **Nathaniel G. Taylor, John B. Henderson, Samuel F. Tappan, and John B. Sanborn**, with army representatives **William T. Sherman, William S. Harney, Alfred H. Terry, and C. C. Augur**. The manuscript now avoids implying all members attended each council and stresses that their mandate included railway security as well as peace.

**Evidence:** National Archives, *The Last Attempt: The Indian Peace Commission of 1867–1868*, https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2022/11/29/the-last-attempt-the-indian-peace-commission-of-1867-1868/ ; *Report to the President by the Indian Peace Commission*, January 7, 1868.

### G. 1868 treaties and the withdrawal chronology

The **April 29, 1868** Fort Laramie treaty had initial signatories but did **not** yet include Red Cloud; he signed **November 6, 1868** after the military withdrawal. The **May 10, 1868 Northern Cheyenne–Northern Arapaho treaty is a separate instrument**, not a different date for the Lakota agreement. Article XVI describes land north of the North Platte and east of the Bighorn Mountains as unceded Indian territory and promises closure of the Bozeman Trail and abandonment of forts. This chapter discusses these provisions only insofar as they explain the war's conclusion.

**Sources:** National Archives treaty catalog, https://www.archives.gov/research/native-americans/treaties/catalog-links ; Yale Avalon 1868 treaty text, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/nt001.asp ; OSU Northern Cheyenne–Arapaho treaty, https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-northern-cheyenne-and-northern-arapaho-1868-1012 .

The withdrawal was not synchronous. The **NPS dates Fort C. F. Smith's departure to July 29** and describes its personnel reaching Fort Phil Kearny August 2, with that fort abandoned shortly after. A post-return-based reconstruction instead dates Fort Phil Kearny's formal abandonment **July 31**. **Fort Reno's final post return dates evacuation August 18**. These incompatible descriptions of Phil Kearny's administrative versus troop departure are explicitly disclosed; the text does not fabricate a universal July 29/August 1 withdrawal date. It also avoids identifying the exact Indigenous individuals who burned all three forts.

**Sources:** NPS Fort C. F. Smith, https://home.nps.gov/bica/learn/historyculture/fort-cf-smith-part-3-abandonment.htm ; Fort Phil Kearny/Bozeman Trail Association, *Trail of Blood, Part IV*, https://www.fortphilkearny.org/partfour .
