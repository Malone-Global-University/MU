### Targeted accuracy findings and corrections

**1. Gold discovery and Nisenan presence.** Marshall's January 24, 1848 discovery at Sutter's Mill took place in Nisenan homeland, with Nisenan workers among the labor force. The National Museum of American History catalogs the 1848 gold artifact; the National Museum of the American Indian's Gold Rush education project explicitly includes the Nisenan workers in the discovery setting. §70.3 and Source I now distinguish an artifact's evidence from sovereignty and the lived history of the site.

**2. Burnett and statehood.** California entered the Union on September 9, 1850. The first governor Peter H. Burnett gave his extermination-language address **January 6, 1851**, not during the 1849 convention. The official California State Library transcription verifies the wording and date. The chapter distinguishes a speech forecasting exterminatory conflict from a specific written order to kill.

**3. 1850 Indian Act and amendments.** The *Act for the Government and Protection of Indians* was enacted **April 22, 1850**, and authorized court-controlled labor and other subordinating legal arrangements. Section 3 was broadened in **1860**. Its indenture-related provisions were repealed in **1863**, but **not** the whole 1850 act; complete repeal was **1937**. The California Research Bureau/State Library legislative-history account and Library of Congress original 1850 statute catalog support these distinctions. §70.6, Source II, vocabulary, chronology, and review consistently reflect them. Other state testimony restrictions are not incorrectly attributed solely to that one statute.

**4. Eighteen treaties.** The three federal commissioners negotiated **18** treaties in **1851–1852**. The U.S. Senate rejected all of them **July 8, 1852**, and imposed secrecy removed in **January 1905**. Their provisions therefore never became binding federal treaty land guarantees. National Archives, California NAHC, and the Oklahoma State University treaty database independently support the basic sequence. §70.8 and Source III use an actual 1851 Camp Fremont agreement and distinguish signature from ratification.

**5. Government financing of violence.** The California State Archives expenditure recapitulation reproduced by the Smithsonian (NMAI Source E) itemizes named militia expedition expenditures for **1850–1852**, totaling **$843,573.48**. The completed chapter correctly labels that as the archival table's total for the enumerated operations, **not** the total cost of all killing or all governmental campaigns through 1873. The broader scholarship also identifies spending and federal reimbursements beyond the schedule. Source IV links the archival educational transcription and the separate 1861 appropriation source.

**6. Pomo Clear Lake massacre.** Attack occurred in May 1850 and involved U.S. soldiers under Nathaniel Lyon, with the preceding exploitation by Kelsey and Stone treated as context rather than an excuse for attacking civilian communities. Mortality numbers remain qualified where reliable sources disagree. This is described as an attack on civilians, not a conventional two-army battle.

**7. Mariposa/Ahwahneechee.** State-authorized Mariposa Battalion operated in 1851 in Sierra/Yosemite country; Tenaya and Ahwahneechee political agency receive treatment. National Park Service material confirms village and food destruction and forced movement. §70.11 rejects the description of Yosemite as vacant wilderness found by invading soldiers.

**8. Tolowa and Wiyot.** Tolowa Dee-ni' historical resources support the destruction of ceremonial communities, including Yontocket, often dated to **1853**. Disputed casualty numbers are not presented as exact totals. Wiyot tribal records date Tuluwat and associated Humboldt Bay massacres to **February 26, 1860**; the chapter says the night of **February 25–26** to account for early-hour reporting. It distinguishes deaths on the island from losses in coordinated surrounding attacks, and documents the World Renewal Ceremony and continuing Wiyot presence.

**9. Round Valley march, 1863.** The forced Chico-to-Round Valley movement is presented as a separate California history commonly remembered as California's Trail of Tears. Records report **461 departure** and **277 arrival** on a September 1863 escorted march; the numerical difference includes those left behind, deaths, and escapes and must not be mechanically treated as a verified death toll of 184. The chapter explicitly safeguards that distinction.

**10. Demography and genocide.** Madley's reconstruction of perhaps **150,000 in 1846** to **around 30,000 by 1873** is labeled a reconstructed total decline, not 120,000 documented homicides. §70.16 differentiates killings, disease, starvation, labor abuse, and displacement. §70.19 explains the later **1948 Genocide Convention**, the historical criteria of intent and acts, and the reasons major scholars (Madley and Lindsay) identify the catastrophe as genocide. Alternative emphasis on ethnic cleansing/settler colonialism is discussed without implying an absence of documented killing or false equivalence.

**11. Survivor testimony and methods.** The Sally Bell (Sinkyone) account appears through Gladys Ayer Nomland's **1935** publication and the Smithsonian's editorial presentation. Source V explicitly identifies it as later remembered testimony, not an 1850 contemporaneous deposition or an exhaustively manuscript-collated quotation. Tribal accounts, official documents, and modern scholarship are presented as complementary rather than interchangeable records.

### Limits of certification

1. **Document collation:** Five source windows provide attributed, clearly labeled editorial paraphrases and source studies. They have not been exhaustively line-collated against every original page/image. For quotations in a critical scholarly edition, an original-page collation pass would still be required.
2. **Geography:** The interactive teaching questions are text-guided and include links to museum maps. The HTML does not embed a self-contained historical atlas plate or precise nation-boundary shapefile. Students must consult external historical maps.
3. **Template parity:** The standalone HTML structurally follows the approved MGU textbook format and Chapter 69 model CSS/sidebar conventions. Literal byte-for-byte parity with the original Chapter 1 source is not independently established because that original file was not supplied in this task.
4. **Deployment:** No live maloneuniversity.org integration, hosting, external asset availability, content-management placement, or real-user production browsing was tested.
5. **Historiographical uncertainty:** Individual massacre casualty estimates, 1863 forced-march survivorship, regional population estimates, and retrospective interpretation of genocide remain subject to explicit scholarly qualifications. The chapter does not claim exhaustive tribal consultation or total archival coverage.
