## 3. Historical and documentary findings

### 3.1 Fort Laramie (1851) and Fort Wise (1861), §§74.2 and 74.9

**Corrected:** The 1861 Fort Wise treaty identifies **Albert G. Boone and F. B. Culver** as U.S. commissioners, not A. B. Greenwood. The agreement was made **February 18, 1861**, at Fort Wise, then **Kansas Territory**; ratification **August 6** and proclamation **December 5, 1861**. The treaty's **Article I** sets the retained reserve through the **Sandy Fork (Sand Creek), Arkansas, Purgatoire**, and then northern New Mexico boundary; **Article II** concerns individual forty-acre assignments. Sand Creek was on or near the treaty-defined eastern boundary, not an exact cadastral point established by a modern survey. NPS accounts variously describe the village as within or on the reservation boundary. One NPS overview says inside and an archaeological interpretation says on the boundary, which makes *on or just within* a responsible statement. The disputed legitimacy of the extensive cession by non-signing bands remains explicit.

**Primary treaty:** https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-arapaho-and-cheyenne-1861-0807  
**NPS reserve geography:** https://www.nps.gov/places/wayside-fort-lyon-reservation.htm  
**National Archives historic mapping:** https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2001/summer/sand-creek-massacre-1

### 3.2 Territorial proclamations and military orders, §§74.6–74.8

The **June 27, 1864** proclamation invited people seeking peace to make themselves known; the **August 11** proclamation authorized action against groups declared hostile. These were distinct documents with dangerous implementation effects. The **September 28, 1864** Camp Weld conference was not itself an unambiguous treaty, and the text properly preserves the mismatch between Indigenous expectations and Evans/Chivington's later claims. Major General Samuel R. Curtis's September 28 instruction to Chivington expressed opposition to peace before further punishment; it is not evidence that Curtis expressly ordered the attack on Black Kettle's specific camp. The final chapter maintains this distinction and does **not** endorse inflammatory quotations attributed to Chivington without firm attribution.

**NPS sequence:** https://www.nps.gov/sand/learn/timeline.htm  
**Contemporary printed evidence, reprinted:** https://panhandlepbs.org/weta/thewest/resources/archives/four/sandcrk.htm

### 3.3 November 29 attack and casualty reports, §§74.10–74.13

The NPS currently describes **approximately 675 soldiers** attacking a camp of **approximately 750 Cheyenne and Arapaho people**, with **more than 230 deaths**, a majority women and children; older reports give lower or higher estimates. The chapter correctly attributes the preferred estimate and does not treat it as a recovered exact casualty roster. **Correction:** The NPS historical overview says military records document **at least 16 soldiers killed or dying of wounds and as many as 78 wounded**; it notes possible friendly-fire casualties. The previous nonnumerical sentence was corrected without claiming the resulting estimates prove a conventional battle. The chapter also distinguishes the attack, pursuit, improvised defenses in the creek bed, and post-attack mutilations.

**NPS site information:** https://www.nps.gov/places/sand-creek-massacre-national-historic-site.htm  
**NPS detailed review:** https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/sand_historicaloverview.htm

### 3.4 Silas Soule, Joseph Cramer, and subsequent killing, §74.14

Contemporary letters and testimony support officers' opposition and refusal to direct their companies into the massacre. **Correction and qualification:** Sources identify **Charles Squires**, also rendered **Squier** in accounts, as involved in Soule's assassination on **April 23, 1865**; reports describe his arrest, escape before trial, and lack of conviction. The evidence does **not** establish that Chivington ordered Soule's murder. The revised chapter explicitly separates a documented assassination, identified suspects, and the unproved allegations of a conspiracy. A new source-critical paragraph explains the evidentiary strengths and limitations of participating officers' testimony.

**Denver Public Library:** https://history.denverlibrary.org/colorado-biographies/silas-soule-1838-1865  
**History Colorado archives:** https://5008.sydneyplus.com/HistoryColorado_ArgusNet_Final/ViewRecord.aspx?record=c16843bd-b6bd-42a9-b37b-f54c79d9d2c7&template=Object

### 3.5 Congressional inquiries and John Evans, §74.16

**Correction:** The 2014 Northwestern University John Evans Study Committee explicitly found **no known evidence Evans planned or knew of the massacre in advance**, while faulting his failure as superintendent of Indian affairs and his role in policies creating its conditions. The final chapter does not misrepresent this finding as direct advance participation. It separately notes other scholarly/institutional interpretations, including University of Denver work. Chivington's military discharge and the lack of criminal conviction are differentiated from the congressional and military reports condemning the events. The roles of John Evans, Chivington, the Fort Lyon command, volunteers, and the separate investigatory bodies remain distinct.

**Northwestern report:** https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2014/05/john-evans-study-committee-issues-report  
**Committee materials:** https://www.northwestern.edu/native-american-and-indigenous-peoples/about/john-evans-study-committee.html

### 3.6 Julesburg, §74.18

**Corrected:** The first major attack and fight near Julesburg/Camp Rankin occurred on **January 7, 1865**, and the subsequent destruction of the old Julesburg station occurred on **February 2, 1865**. The chapter does not conflate these with continuing regional raids. Distinct Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota participation is preserved rather than generalized to every member of those nations.

**NPS timeline:** https://www.nps.gov/sand/learn/timeline.htm

### 3.7 Little Arkansas treaty of October 14, 1865, §74.19

**Major correction:** Read the **ratified treaty**, not just a scholarly summary. Its **Article V** provides distinct **640-acre grants** to specified persons related by blood to Cheyenne and Arapaho families. **Article VI**, the Sand Creek reparation provision, grants **320 acres each to four named chiefs** (Black Kettle, Seven Bulls, Little Robe, and Black White Man), **160-acre grants** to those made widows or who lost a parent in the massacre, and separate compensation for documented property lost. The draft erroneously reversed the relative size of widow/orphan grants. Article VI's restrictions on land alienation and federal discretion over compensation also matter. The treaty was **signed October 14, 1865; ratified May 22, 1866; proclaimed February 2, 1867**. The published Article II contains a proviso allowing a later reservation outside Kansas with tribal assent. The chapter now distinguishes treaty provisions from their nonfulfillment; NPS states Sand Creek reparations were not paid as promised.

**Ratified treaty, Articles II, V, VI:** https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-cheyenne-and-arapaho-1865-0887  
**NPS treaty legacy:** https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/sand_sandcreek_legacies.htm

### 3.8 Public apology and later memory, §74.20

**Verified and specified:** Colorado Governor **John Hickenlooper formally apologized on December 3, 2014**, on behalf of Colorado to Cheyenne and Arapaho communities. **Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site** was established and dedicated in **2007** following the 2000 authorizing legislation. Indigenous consultation and disagreement over exact site locations are treated as part of the historical method, not erased. The controlled main chapter title remains *Sand Creek*, which is appropriate to the user's outline; the full massacre title is retained in subtitle and metadata.

**NPS chronology:** https://www.nps.gov/sand/learn/timeline.htm  
**NARA site and location history:** https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2001/summer/sand-creek-massacre-1
