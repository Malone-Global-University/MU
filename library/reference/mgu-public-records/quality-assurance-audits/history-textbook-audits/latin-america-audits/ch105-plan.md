# MGU Latin American History — Chapter 105 Production Plan

**Authoritative title:** Venezuela's Oil State: Petroleum, Democracy, and the Caracazo  
**Volume V — Democracy, Cartels, Oil, and Contemporary Latin America**  
**Part XXIV — Oil, Chávez, the Pink Tide, and Venezuela's Collapse**  
**Principal chronology:** 1914–1989, with selective nineteenth-century background and a brief bridge to 1992  
**Historical sections:** 20 across five divisions; **direct narrative target:** 11,500 words.

## Governing argument

Petroleum remade Venezuela's economy, territorial settlement, labor system, and political institutions. The distribution of oil rents supported infrastructure, social mobility, and a durable elected party system after 1958, but reliance on an externally priced export commodity also left fiscal policy and social expectations vulnerable to price shocks, indebtedness, and unequal access to public benefits. The 1989 Caracazo exposed the social and coercive costs of this arrangement. Avoid a deterministic explanation of later Chavismo: the military rebellions and subsequent political projects belong to Chapter 106.

## Division I — Foundations of the Petroleum State, 1914–1948 (2,250 words)

**§105.1 — Before the Oil Boom: Land, Agriculture, and the First Commercial Wells.** Establish Venezuela's older agrarian and export economy; the geography of Lake Maracaibo and the eastern basins; the 1914 Zumaque No. 1 commercial well; and oil as a change in economic institutions rather than an instantaneous national transformation. Explain the significance of earlier foreign and domestic exploration.

**§105.2 — Gómez, Concessions, and the Arrival of Foreign Oil Companies.** Examine Juan Vicente Gómez's dictatorship, concession agreements, property and subsurface rights, public revenue, foreign firms, and the relationships among business, coercion, and administrative centralization. Differentiate companies from the Venezuelan state and identify how individual contracts varied.

**§105.3 — The 1922 Boom, Export Growth, Labor, and Urbanization.** Reconstruct the Los Barrosos No. 2 blowout and subsequent oil expansion; the rise of Maracaibo and nearby camps; migration, occupational divisions, housing and public health; and relations between foreign firms, employees, and unions. Treat the interwar labor story and uneven development as central, not subsidiary.

**§105.4 — Oil Sovereignty, the 1943 Hydrocarbons Law, and Fifty–Fifty Revenue Sharing.** Analyze the legal reforms under Isaías Medina Angarita, the royalty/concession framework, the distinction between the 1943 law and the later 1948 fifty–fifty profits arrangement, and the ideas of oil nationalism and 'sowing the oil.' Emphasize institutions and distributive choices.

## Division II — Regime Change and the Foundations of Punto Fijo Democracy, 1945–1963 (2,250 words)

**§105.5 — The 1945 October Revolution, Acción Democrática, and the Trienio.** Explain the 1945 civil-military change of government, the brief reform period, expansion of participation, debates over labor and oil revenues, the 1947 election, and the 1948 coup. Avoid presenting the interlude as either fully inclusive democracy or a mere prelude to dictatorship.

**§105.6 — Pérez Jiménez, Authoritarian Modernization, and the Oil Economy.** Examine the military dictatorship, public works, urban development, repression, political surveillance, labor conditions, and the contradiction between visible modernization and restricted participation. Connect investment to the petroleum economy without treating every infrastructure project as a public benefit equally distributed.

**§105.7 — January 1958, the Pact of Punto Fijo, and Democratic Compromise.** Reconstruct the fall of Pérez Jiménez, mass mobilization, the participation of parties, and the 1958 agreement among Acción Democrática, COPEI, and URD. Explain the pact's commitments, subsequent political changes, and the exclusion of the Communist Party from its signatory arrangement; avoid portraying it as a fixed constitutional system.

**§105.8 — Betancourt, Electoral Institutions, Military Opposition, and Reform.** Trace the 1958 election and Rómulo Betancourt's presidency, constitutional arrangements under the 1961 constitution, civil-military conflicts, labor and rural reform, and the tensions between democratic consolidation and repression of armed opposition. Briefly refer to Chapter 82 on revolutionary movements without repeating it.

## Division III — Petroleum, Party Democracy, and Social Development, 1960–1975 (2,350 words)

**§105.9 — AD, COPEI, Political Competition, and the First Peaceful Transfer of Power.** Examine party organization, union relationships, state employment, electoral bargaining, and Rafael Caldera's 1968 victory and 1969 accession. Explain why institutional stability coexisted with exclusion, patronage, and political dissent.

**§105.10 — Venezuela, Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, and OPEC.** Explain Venezuela's role as an OPEC founding member in September 1960, the politics of producer cooperation, international pricing, bargaining power, and the different interests of petroleum states. Keep Venezuela at the center rather than recounting all global OPEC history.

**§105.11 — Oil Rents, Cities, Education, Inequality, and the Rentier State.** Analyze Caracas and Maracaibo, state-funded services and infrastructure, labor and migration, access to housing, gendered economic experiences, marginalized barrios, countryside change, and the long debate over whether petroleum created shared prosperity or durable dependence.

**§105.12 — The 1973–1974 Oil-Price Boom and the Gran Venezuela Project.** Explain the oil-price shock, Carlos Andrés Pérez's first presidency, public investment, expanded ambitions, and the vulnerability of fiscal promises to a volatile export price. Distinguish membership in OPEC from participation in the Arab oil embargo, in which Venezuela was not an embargo participant.

## Division IV — Nationalization, PDVSA, and the Limits of the Oil Boom, 1975–1983 (2,350 words)

**§105.13 — The 1975 Nationalization Law and the January 1976 Transfer.** Treat the passage of legislation and formal nationalization on January 1, 1976; compensation, continuity of operations, foreign technical contracts, and political arguments for state ownership. Distinguish state ownership from total termination of foreign company relationships.

**§105.14 — PDVSA: The New State Petroleum Enterprise and Its Subsidiaries.** Explain the holding company, subsidiaries such as Lagoven and Maraven, technical managers, labor arrangements, and debates over business autonomy, national interest, and accountability. Do not confuse formal national ownership with the immediate disappearance of earlier company culture.

**§105.15 — Spending the Windfall: Industrial Policy, Public Works, and Unequal Benefits.** Analyze import substitution, public enterprises, debt-financed investment, education and health commitments, patronage accusations, and the diverse social consequences of oil revenue. Include gender, regional differences, and environmental impacts where sourced.

**§105.16 — Falling Oil Prices, Capital Flight, Debt, and Black Friday, 1983.** Trace the vulnerability created by public expenditure and foreign borrowing, international credit conditions, the February 1983 exchange-rate crisis and devaluation, and its consequences for savings, imports, employment, and public confidence. Distinguish global pressures from choices made by Venezuelan administrations.

## Division V — A Crisis of the Rentier Democracy: 1984–1989 (2,300 words)

**§105.17 — Jaime Lusinchi, Austerity, Exchange Controls, and Social Frustration.** Explain the 1984–1989 government, the challenge of debt service, exchange controls and distributional effects, wage pressures, patronage controversies, and the gradual erosion of confidence in party institutions. Include social actors who opposed or tried to mediate the adjustment.

**§105.18 — Carlos Andrés Pérez Returns: The 1988 Election and the 1989 Adjustment Package.** Examine Pérez's political comeback, public expectations, his February 1989 policy announcement, international financing and negotiations, and changes to transportation and prices. Explain differences between announced policy, immediate implementation, and later political interpretation.

**§105.19 — The Caracazo, February–March 1989: Protest, Repression, and Civilian Loss.** Reconstruct initial protest in Guarenas on February 27, the rapid spread to Caracas and elsewhere, looting and civil unrest, deployment of security forces, suspension of protections, deaths and injuries, and later judicial investigations. Cite the Inter-American Court's Caracazo proceedings and distinguish documented fatalities from higher disputed estimates.

**§105.20 — The Fracturing of Punto Fijo Legitimacy and the Unfinished Oil-State Dilemma.** Synthesize how the oil economy, democratic achievements, unequal redistribution, debt, and state violence interacted. Explain what 1989 revealed without claiming Chávez's rise or Venezuela's later collapse were inevitable. Close with a disciplined bridge to Chapter 106's 1992 coup attempt and the subsequent political reorganization.

## Integrated instructional apparatus

Required: nine linked primary-source studies (e.g. hydrocarbons legislation, labor documentation, Punto Fijo text, 1961 Constitution, OPEC founding records, 1975–1976 nationalization documents, PDVSA institutional records, 1983 financial statements, the Inter-American Caracazo judgment); three dated, text-guided historical map studies (oil basins, urban change, locations of February–March 1989 unrest); three quantitative studies (oil-production/export dependence, oil-price/revenue and debt trajectories, documented 1989 fatalities with methodological caveats); three historical comparisons; two debates; three vocabulary boxes; timeline; one documentary image study; six perspectives; everyday-life, cause-effect, and continuity-change features; 35–40 defined terms; 10–12 summary paragraphs; and 18–25 linked documentary and scholarly references.

**Documentary controls:** Separate the 1943 Hydrocarbons Law from the 1948 fifty–fifty profit-sharing policy; treat January 1976 as the effective nationalization; describe the Punto Fijo pact precisely; distinguish oil-price gains from actual social distribution; and attribute Caracazo casualty estimates rather than asserting a single uncontested total.
