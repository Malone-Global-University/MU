## I. Scope and governing standard

The governing twenty-section plan requires Chapter 92 to trace the Shah's departure, Khomeini's return, the collapse of the last royal government, Mehdi Bazargan's provisional cabinet, the Revolutionary Council, revolutionary committees, and the competing institutions, social movements, regional claims, and political projects present before the March 1979 referendum. The chronological endpoint is **29 March 1979**. Chapter 93 retains the actual referendum, constitution-making, and subsequent institutional settlement; Chapters 94–95 retain the November hostage crisis and later revolutionary consolidation and repression.

The final manuscript preserves the original HTML design and five-division scheme. It does not replace the submitted draft with a new historical narrative; the revision is confined to independently warranted clarifications, documentary corrections, a limited amount of additional analysis, and source-verifiable citations.

**Central analytical question preserved:** How did the revolutionary movement that overthrew the Pahlavi monarchy attempt to govern Iran, and why did its victory yield competing claims to legal, religious, political, administrative, and coercive authority?

## IV. Historical corrections and source verification

### A. Revolutionary Council and appointment of Bazargan

**Correction:** The narrative now identifies **12 January 1979** as the date Khomeini publicly authorized the Revolutionary Council. The manuscript continues to distinguish this declaration from the organization's initially concealed and later changing membership rather than inventing a definitive founding roster.

**Verification:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, "Constitution of the Islamic Republic," identifies Khomeini's 12 January announcement and the proposed elected constituent assembly. The appointment decree of **5 February 1979** is available in an English translation of the published Khomeini materials:

- <https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitution-of-the-islamic-republic/>
- <https://emam.com/posts/view/14892/Decree-%5BThe-Prime-ministry-of-Mr.-Bazargan%5D>

**Source Window 1:** Rewritten around that identifiable decree. The paraphrase now specifies its promised referendum, elected constitution-drafting representatives, and parliamentary elections rather than implying that a general summary is an authenticated quotation. The English rendering is explicitly distinguished from the Persian original and from later implementation.

### B. Bazargan's government and the limits of administrative control

**Correction:** The manuscript now gives **16 April 1979** for Foreign Minister Karim Sanjabi's resignation, while identifying it as an event *outside* the chapter's March endpoint. The reference is only contextual; the chapter does not narrate later consolidation.

**Verification:** Contemporary reporting dated 16–17 April 1979, notably *The Washington Post*, "Foreign Minister Resigns in Iran, Cites 'Disorders.'"

- <https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/04/17/foreign-minster-resigns-in-iran-cites-disorders/7a6c275f-9771-441f-afec-0bce813ee04e/>

**Correction:** The chapter specifies **5 March 1979** for the resumption of Iranian crude oil exports and identifies the first post-revolution export loading at Kharg Island, the tanker *World Ambassador*, and NIOC's Hassan Nazih. It differentiates a single resumed shipment from full production and export recovery.

**Verification:** Contemporary reporting and retrospective Iran–U.S. Claims Tribunal material:

- <https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/03/06/iran-resumes-oil-export-as-japan-pays-premium/fb399e67-814e-419e-9876-253b250a3c8e/>
- <https://www.italaw.com/sites/default/files/case-html-files/italaw18124178-Phillips-Petroleum-Iran-v-Iran-Award-June-29-1989.html>

### C. Khomeini in Qom and revolutionary committees

**Correction:** §92.6 now gives **1 March 1979** for Khomeini's relocation from Tehran to Qom. The source chronology supports early March; this statement is confined to the date and does not imply that he stopped intervening in national affairs.

**Correction:** §92.8 now describes Mahdavi-Kani's committee instructions as issued or described around **7–8 March**. The discrepancy concerns the dating of instructions and their publication, not whether a process of coordination occurred. Amnesty International's later investigation specifically documents their discussion in an *Ayandegan* interview published **8 March**.

- <https://www.iranrights.org/library/document/338>

### D. Bazargan's 28 February address — Primary Source Window 2

**Correction:** The passage distinguishes **28 February 1979**, the date of Bazargan's televised speech, from **3 March 1979**, the date of the U.S. Embassy's declassified report. It now identifies **U.S. Embassy Tehran Telegram 2648, 1979TEHRAN02648**, entitled "Bazargan Speech of 28 February."

**Documentary basis:** The cable records embassy reporting on Bazargan's criticism of committees issuing orders and making arrests, his concern about an attack on a foreign embassy, his opposition to army dissolution, the disruption of petroleum operations, and his call for restraint regarding Kurdish grievances.

- <https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1979TEHRAN02648_e.html>

**Source integrity:** The window explicitly labels its source a *diplomatic summary*, not a transcript of Bazargan's words. It separates the attributed speech from the embassy's own commentary. There are no fabricated direct quotations.

### E. March women's demonstrations — Primary Source Window 3

**Publication-critical documentary deficiency corrected.** The draft treated *Kayhan*, *The Washington Post*, and other news reporting as a generic composite. The finished source window uses one identifiable primary document:

**Jonathan C. Randal**, "Women Protest in Iran, Shout 'Down With Khomeini,'" *The Washington Post*, issue of **9 March 1979**, p. A1, covering events in Tehran on **8 March**. The web archive displays the Tehran dateline/dispatch date as 8 March; the issue reference is distinguished from that displayed dateline.

- <https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/03/09/women-protest-in-iran-shout-down-with-khomeini/84d3a780-738a-4dfd-a2a5-c7d4f546323c/>

The reconstructed window confines its paraphrase to the article's reported protest routes, demands, armed intimidation, hostile counter-demonstrations, and estimate of crowds. The analysis explicitly distinguishes contemporaneous observations from the correspondent's interpretive contrast between religious and secular groups. The limitation section notes that neither the report nor the demonstrations establish that a nationwide compulsory-dress statute had already been enacted.

### F. Embassy attack and international relations

**Correction:** §92.18 now identifies the association of the **14 February 1979** attack on the U.S. Embassy with armed **Fedai guerrillas**, based on contemporary reporting and *Encyclopaedia Iranica*. It retains uncertainty about the precise identities, command arrangements, and degree of institutional authorization of individual attackers.

**Verification:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, "Hostage Crisis"; contemporary reporting; the International Court of Justice's account of the incident. The February attack, which was ended through intervention by Khomeini-aligned authorities and Bazargan's representatives, is still kept separate from the November 1979 seizure assigned to Chapter 94.

- <https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hostage-crisis/>
- <https://law.justia.com/cases/foreign/international/1980-icj-rep-3.html>

### G. Sanandaj and Turkmen Sahra

**Correction:** §92.16 identifies the major Sanandaj fighting as beginning **18 March 1979**, with a ceasefire reported around **23 March**. It does not replace disputed casualty figures with a falsely precise count. Accounts vary over the initial circumstances and distribution of responsibility.

**Verification:** Contemporary U.S. reporting and an Associated Press account dated March 23, reproduced by the Iran Human Rights Documentation Center:

- <https://iranhrdc.org/cease-fire-brings-peace-to-kurdish-city-in-iran/>
- <https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/03/20/troubles-erupt-in-irans-border-areas/8e36b188-e49f-4c6b-9728-30cb6ff35b2e/>

**Correction:** The Turkmen Sahra discussion now places major fighting around Gonbad-e Kavus in the **final week of March**, with some scholarly histories identifying **26 March** as its beginning. It identifies disputes over land, local councils, and control of security as contested historical causes, avoiding unsupported casualty totals.

### H. Economic and social institutional plurality

Substantive additions in §§92.8 and 92.11 distinguish the functioning of practical state administration from the acquisition of revolutionary legitimacy. They also distinguish the demands and institutional forms of workers' councils rather than treating all *shoras* as a single party-directed or state-directed organization. The chapter retains independent agency for women, labor, religious-nationalist constitutionalists, leftist movements, and regional communities.

## V. Scholarly balance and interpretive standards

- The chapter **does not equate overthrowing the monarchy with establishing a settled Islamic Republic**.
- It differentiates **the provisional cabinet, Revolutionary Council, Khomeini's personal authority, neighborhood committees, armed formations, and regular ministries**.
- It distinguishes provisional state administration from demands for revolutionary punishment and from competing claims to the legitimate use of force.
- The first executions are discussed in terms of the demand for accountability **and** procedural deficiencies; the larger history of consolidation and repression remains with Chapter 95.
- Kurdish and Turkmen demands are not conflated with one another, and demands for regional autonomy are not automatically classified as secession.
- The March women's movement is treated as an early contest over revolutionary promises, without projecting the later legal regime into March 1979.
- The narrative **ends before the result of the 30–31 March referendum**. The creation of a constitution and the results of the popular vote belong to Chapter 93.
- The January–February chronology repeats indispensable facts from Chapter 91 but uses them to explain succession and institutional authority, not to reproduce the prior chapter's protest history.

No broad historical claim that the eventual political settlement was inevitable was added. Distinctions between contemporary testimony, later scholarship, and uncertain numerical claims remain explicit.

## VII. Remaining scholarly limitations

1. The source windows are **clearly labeled paraphrases**, not original-text reproductions. Classroom excerpts with page references may be added later using verified editions.
2. The degree of command responsibility for some armed groups and the precise daily circumstances of local committee enforcement remain historically disputed. The chapter retains appropriate qualifications.
3. March 1979 regional casualty totals and exact sizes of crowds or armed groups are not resolved by the sources checked and have not been invented.
4. There is no claim that the three selected primary documents are representative of every Iranian constituency; their differing provenance and limitations are explicit.
5. Some source webpages may change or require access permissions over time; stable citation metadata remain visible in the bibliography.
6. Maps remain a separate user-managed production step and are **not** a deficiency under the MGU history workflow.
