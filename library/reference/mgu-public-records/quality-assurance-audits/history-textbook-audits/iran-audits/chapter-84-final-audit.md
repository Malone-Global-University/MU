## 3. Substantive historical revision

### Division I — Neutrality, strategic geography, and diplomacy

Expanded the relationship among Iran's neutrality, the Saadabad Pact's limitations, the oil sector and railway's wartime strategic value, and the British and Soviet calculations after Operation Barbarossa. Clarified that German commercial/technical employment did not itself establish military collaboration with the Axis; distinguished alleged intelligence threats from proven imminent threats. The narrative treats the Soviet appeal to Article VI of the 1921 Iranian-Soviet treaty as a *claimed legal justification*, not an independently established legal entitlement to invade.

The first Primary Source Window now identifies a particular British note, a Soviet account invoking the treaty, and the Iranian reply rather than paraphrasing undated “Allied demands.” See [FRUS 1941, British note of 25 August](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v03/d420), [Soviet communication](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v03/d405), and [Iranian response to Soviet note](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v03/d413).

### Division II — The 1941 campaign and constitutional transfer

Developed the distinct northern Soviet and southern British/Indian operations: land approaches, Caspian operations, southwest refinery and port objectives, railway junctions, tactical resistance, and limitations of Iranian military organization. Included the sinking of the Iranian sloop *Babr* in the naval operations near Khorramshahr, corroborated by the [Naval Historical Society of Australia](https://navyhistory.au/25-august-1941/). The text does not confuse national military defeat with individual cowardice.

For the Tehran political crisis, distinguished a cabinet reshuffle on 27–28 August from **Foroughi's public announcement of the cease-resistance order on 28 August**; the latter is supported by [Dreyfus's contemporary FRUS telegram of 28 August 1941](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v03/d419). Retained 16 September for abdication and 17 September for Mohammad Reza Shah's parliamentary oath. Corrected the over-simple assertion that Britain uniformly favored Pahlavi succession: British officials examined a possible Qajar alternative before accepting continuity. Specialist source: Shaul Bakhash, “Britain and the Abdication of Reza Shah,” *Middle Eastern Studies* 52, no. 2 (2016), 318–334.

The second source window presents the constitutional sequence as a transparent documentary paraphrase, identifies Iranian parliamentary material and contemporary diplomatic observations, and expressly avoids claiming a verified literal English translation of the Persian abdication statement. The [British Library collection on the ex-Shah's movement and enforced exile](https://qdl.qa/en/archive/81055/vdc_100061220855.0x000094) confirms his departure through Bandar Abbas on 27 September 1941 and the subsequent British control of his residence abroad.

### Division III — Occupation, treaty, and supply corridor

Clarified that occupation zones evolved and that Tehran's military status was not static. Distinguished the continued operation of Iranian ministries and Majles from de facto foreign control over troops, transport, ports, and military logistics.

The **Tripartite Treaty of 29 January 1942** is now analyzed with article-specific accuracy:
- **Article I:** Allied respect for Iranian territorial integrity, sovereignty, and political independence.
- **Article III:** Iranian cooperation and Allied military use of communication and transport facilities.
- **Article IV:** presence and conduct of foreign troops and the treaty's characterization of their presence as not constituting military occupation; associated consultation and economic provisions.
- **Article V:** withdrawal under the treaty's postwar six-month formula.

Documentary edition: [United Nations Treaty Collection, registration no. 271, UNTS 93:279](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/showDetails.aspx?objid=0800000280155f52); related [FRUS 1942 discussion of revised Articles IV–V](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1942v04/d302).

Substantially expanded the Persian Corridor as a connected system of ports, railheads, workshops, locomotives, sidings, truck assembly, warehousing, and Soviet receiving points. Differentiated early American railway operation in 1943 from the **formal takeover of the southern railway sector on 1 April 1943**. Added specific, properly dated wartime throughput evidence without treating figures for one transport category as nationwide trade totals. Main reference: T. H. Vail Motter, *The Persian Corridor and Aid to Russia* (1952); see also the [U.S. Army Center of Military History](https://history.army.mil/Publications/Publications-Catalog/Persian-Corridor-and-Aid-to-Russia/).

Expanded local labor recruitment, unequal wartime wages, transport competition, and the differences between employment growth and declining purchasing power.

### Division IV — Bread, households, refugees, and political revival

Developed grain procurement and distribution, inflation linked to wartime rial spending, transport diversion, and the mixed causes of the December 1942 unrest. **8 December 1942** is retained. Documentary records distinguish the initial student demonstration from later attacks on shops and Qavam's residence and from uncertain allegations about organized political instigation. See [FRUS, Dreyfus, 9 December 1942](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1942v04/d224) and Stephen L. McFarland, “Anatomy of an Iranian Political Crowd,” *International Journal of Middle East Studies* 17, no. 1 (1985), 51–65.

Expanded women's household work, regional differences, black markets, relief networks, and the effects of rationing. Clarified illness and health-system constraints without asserting a national epidemic or mortality count for which comparable demographic evidence is lacking.

For Polish evacuees, identified the two major waves of 1942, distinguished military from civilian categories, and used the approximately 116,000 figure explicitly as a sourced estimate. Added Bandar Pahlavi, Tehran, Isfahan's Polish children's institutions, the role of Jewish refugees, sanitation, and relief. Sources: [Polish government historical record](https://www.gov.pl/web/iraq/wartime-diplomacy-part-iv) and [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, Polish Refugees in Iran](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/polish-refugees-in-iran-during-world-war-ii).

The Tudeh Party and other political movements are presented as part of the reemergence of public contestation after dictatorship, not as evidence of completed democracy.

### Division V — Allied status, international declarations, and petroleum law

Verified and contextualized **9 September 1943** as Iran's declaration of war against Germany, along with its adherence to the wartime Declaration by United Nations. Clarified that this was not yet membership in the later postwar United Nations organization. Contemporary confirmation: [FRUS 1943, Iranian royal declaration](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v04/d459).

Distinguished the general Tehran Conference communiqué from the **separate Declaration of the Three Powers Regarding Iran of 1 December 1943**. The latter acknowledged Iran's contribution and its wartime economic hardship, pledged possible economic assistance, and reaffirmed independence and territorial integrity. Documentary edition: [FRUS, Declaration Regarding Iran](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v04/d432).

**Corrected the December 1944 petroleum statute materially.** The original draft suggested only restricted negotiation with parliamentary approval. The revised text explains the actual three-article law as reproduced in a contemporary telegram:
- Article 1 prohibited specified senior executive officials from discussions or agreements concerning petroleum concessions.
- Article 2 permitted negotiation about petroleum sales and government exploitation/administration, with Majles notification.
- Article 3 specified **three to eight years' imprisonment** and permanent disqualification from government office for violations.

Primary evidence: [FRUS 1944, U.S. Ambassador Morris's telegram dated 3 December](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v05/d517). The chapter reserves the 1946–1947 Soviet oil dispute for Chapter 85 and the major 1951–1953 nationalization crisis for Chapters 86–87.
