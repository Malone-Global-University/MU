## 3. Historical corrections and completed narrative

### 3.1 October 1977 Ten Nights — Chapter Opening and §91.2

**Problem:** The original Chapter Opening placed the readings in the Goethe-Institut's hall and on a university campus, misleadingly combining the event with later university gatherings. The Goethe-Institut co-organized the event but its own premises were insufficient.

**Correction:** The final chapter identifies the venue as the **German-Iranian Cultural Association on Pahlavi Avenue in Tehran**, in a large garden accommodating the audiences, with the Goethe-Institut and Writers' Association of Iran as collaborators. Later university gatherings are distinguished from the original **10–19 October 1977** series. The correction is based on the published institutional history and peer-reviewed research.

- *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Goethe Institute”: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/goethe-institute/
- Olmo Gölz, “Drifting toward Revolution: Kurt Scharf and the *dah shab* in Tehran,” *Iranian Studies* 56 (2023): 181–190: https://doi.org/10.1017/irn.2022.64.

### 3.2 Former Prime Minister Hoveyda's arrest — §91.12

**Problem:** The uploaded draft reported that Hoveyda and SAVAK director Nematollah Nassiri were both detained on 7 November 1978.

**Correction:** The final chapter dates **Amir-Abbas Hoveyda's arrest to 8 November 1978** and distinguishes it from the detention of Nassiri in the preceding roundup. This was also checked against the second narrative paragraph added during revision, preventing inconsistent dates in different parts of the same section.

- Abdorrahman Boroumand Center, Hoveyda biographical record: https://www.iranrights.org/memorial/story/-3780/amir-abbas-hoveyda.
- *Washington Post*, contemporary reporting dated 8–9 November 1978: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1978/11/09/former-prime-minister-jailed-in-iranian-anticorruption-drive/663fcc64-cd7b-411d-822b-effde0591c87/.

### 3.3 Qom, Tabriz, and the mourning cycle — §§91.5–91.7

The final chapter retains the documented succession of the **7 January 1978** newspaper article, the **9 January** confrontations in Qom, and **18 February** demonstrations in Tabriz. It expands the account of provincial organization and rejects an automatic, perfectly synchronized “forty-day machine.” Local organizers, commercial networks, religious institutions, and government responses shaped the importance of each commemoration. This is consistent with Charles Kurzman's analysis of shifting revolutionary expectations and with the established chronology.

- Charles Kurzman, *The Unthinkable Revolution in Iran* (Harvard University Press, 2004): https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv136c5c7.
- *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Chronology of Iranian History, Part 4”: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chronology-of-iranian-history-part-4/.

### 3.4 Cinema Rex and the problems of retrospective attribution — §91.8

The draft appropriately separated contemporaneous allegations from later evidence, and the final chapter makes that separation more explicit. It identifies the **19 August 1978** fire as arson and discusses the **1980** post-revolutionary prosecution of Hossein Takbalizadeh without claiming that one legal proceeding resolved every question concerning instigation, official conduct, or rescue failure. It explains why public belief in 1978 had political effects independently of the findings of later investigations. Competing fatality totals are treated as contested, not a fixed unqualified number.

- Abdorrahman Boroumand Center, Hossein Takbalizadeh biographical and trial record: https://www.iranrights.org/memorial/story/-8094/hossein-takbalizadeh.
- Karim Nikunazar, retrospective documentary journalism, “Cinema Hell”: https://globisreview.com/atlas/cinema-hell/.

**Limitation:** This chapter does not claim independent access to the full 1980 trial archive, nor does it assert a final determination of wider political responsibility beyond the available published evidence.

### 3.5 Black Friday and casualty figures — §91.9

The chapter keeps **8 September 1978 (17 Shahrivar 1357)** as the correct date and clearly separates Jaleh Square casualties from wider Tehran deaths. The added paragraph explains how contemporary reports of vastly larger numbers could influence mobilization even when unsupported by later record-based estimates. No controversial exact nationwide casualty figure is passed off as an established count. Where the narrative uses approximately sixty at the square it explicitly identifies this as the approximate result of later reconstructions, not a precise contemporaneous census. Source limitations remain in view.

### 3.6 Oil workers' agency and the transition toward dual power — §§91.10–91.11

The final chapter develops the stages of labor action: workplace grievances and organization; politicization of demands; export disruption; negotiations about limited domestic fuel supply; and the emerging **Oil Strikes Coordinating Committee**. The account distinguishes local labor organization from Khomeini's later efforts to coordinate petroleum policy. It does not portray the entire oil workforce as a passive instrument of clerical leadership, and it recognizes tensions among engineers, staff, manual workers, and different workplaces.

- Peyman Jafari, “Reasons to Revolt: Iranian Oil Workers in the 1970s,” *International Labor and Working-Class History* 84 (2013): 195–217: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0147547913000331.
- Peyman Jafari, “Linkages of Oil and Politics: Oil Strikes and Dual Power in the Iranian Revolution,” *Labor History* (2019): https://doi.org/10.1080/0023656X.2019.1537018.
- Contemporary U.S. reporting on demands to limit output to domestic requirements, *Washington Post*, December 1978: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1978/12/18/iran-arrests-20-leaders-in-oil-strike/287ea58c-0d3b-45ad-8da0-66599e5f664b/.

### 3.7 Khomeini's communications and competing opposition programs — §§91.13–91.16

The revision further explains how cassettes interacted with telephones, transcriptions, leaflets, intermediary visitors, and local religious and political organizations. This prevents technological determinism: material was distributed through social networks rather than transmitted through an infallible central mechanism. The narrative also adds women's diverse participation and explicitly rejects treating all demonstrators as advocates of an agreed constitutional or religious future. The government, constitutional nationalists, clerics, leftists, workers, and foreign diplomats are distinguished rather than collapsed into a single revolutionary voice.

- *PBS Frontline/Tehran Bureau*, “Politics and the Press in Iran III”: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/tehranbureau/2010/12/politics-and-the-press-in-iran-iii.html.

### 3.8 Completed Division V — §§91.17–91.20

The final **3,927-word** division covers the missing transition from a weakened regime to the monarchy's actual collapse:

- **§91.17:** Bakhtiar's constitutional strategy, rejection by leading National Front colleagues, the Regency Council formed in January 1979, and the Shah's **16 January departure without formal abdication**. Foreign diplomatic and military involvement is treated as a contested influence rather than proof of an externally planned overthrow.
- **§91.18:** Khomeini's **1 February** return; his speech at Behesht-e Zahra; the difference between legal constitutional authority and revolutionary claims; the Revolutionary Council; Bazargan's appointment **publicly announced on 5 February** (with earlier preparation distinguished from the announcement); and the emerging but incomplete condition of rival governmental authority.
- **§91.19:** Homafaran support, fighting around Doshan Tappeh on **9–10 February**, civilian and guerrilla participation, competing curfew orders, military fragmentation, and the **11 February 1979** neutrality decision by the Supreme Military Council. The section distinguishes military neutrality from unanimous ideological support for the revolution and locates its significance in the collapse of Bakhtiar's capacity to govern.
- **§91.20:** A substantial causal synthesis comparing authoritarian political institutions, uneven economic expectations, religious and secular organization, mourning cycles, strikes, revolutionary leadership, varying government responses, international policy, and military cohesion. It rejects monocausal and predetermined narratives while preserving the endpoint of the approved chapter.
