## 3. Consolidated correction register

Thirteen small source-level edits, grouped here by substantive purpose:

### 3.1 Birthplace evidence (§39.2)

Claude asserted Radkan as the most commonly cited exact birth locality. Scholarly reference works differ: the **TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi** gives Radkan, while **Encyclopaedia Iranica** identifies Nawqan/Nawghan near Tus. Both agree on the broader Tus district and approximate birth date (1018). The publication text now states both localizations and their documentary limits rather than treating one as indisputably established.

### 3.2 Nishapuri manuscript and Ravandi (Primary Source Window I)

Claude described Zahir al-Din Nishapuri's earlier Seljuk history as chiefly known through later historical works. This wording concealed the discovery of an apparently unique surviving manuscript in the Royal Asiatic Society collection and **A. H. Morton's 2004 critical edition**. The source note now records that manuscript and publication and clarifies that Ravandi **dedicated** his account to a Seljuk ruler of Rum, without implying that his residence or employment at that ruler's court is established.

### 3.3 Titles (§39.7)

The honorific **Qiwam/Qawam al-Din** is directly supported by inscriptions and court poetry. The title **Atabeg** also appears in historical traditions about the minister's guidance of Malik Shah, but its grant, timing, and precise status are debated. The corrected wording states the differentiated evidentiary basis and does not assert an uncontested formal appointment.

### 3.4 Alamut and the Nizari designation (chronology, §39.31, reference material)

**Hasan-i Sabbah's Ismaili supporters took Alamut in 1090.** The **Nizari–Mustaʿli split** occurred after the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir's death in **1094**, two years after Nizam al-Mulk died. Thus calling the attackers of 1090 or 1092 *Nizari* without qualification is anachronistic. The timeline, introductory reference material, §39.31, chapter summary, and review place entry now clearly distinguish the early **Ismaili Alamut movement** from its later **Nizari** identity. References to later Nizari narratives as retrospective sources remain appropriate and are now explained in context.

This fixes mobile overflow while preserving desktop presentation and the chapter's markup and scripting.

## 4. Historical verification and source-quality findings

**Chronological checks:** The principal chronology is supported: Nizam al-Mulk's career under Alp Arslan and Malik Shah, al-Kunduri's execution in 1064, the Baghdad Nizamiyya's construction around 1065 and inauguration in 1067, al-Ghazali's appointment in 1091, Nizam al-Mulk's assassination on **14 October 1092** in the western Iranian journey tradition, and Malik Shah's death in November. Medieval accounts vary about the minister's last months and the specific location or circumstances of the killing; the chapter appropriately treats responsibility as unresolved rather than proved.

**The *Siyasatnama*:** The chapter accurately conveys the work's internal 39-chapter initial version plus 11 additional chapters, the stated commission around 1086, and the substantial modern scholarly debate about compilation, interpolation, and attribution. It distinguishes normative political prescriptions from actual Seljuk administration. No invented direct quotation was found in the four designated source windows.

**Education:** The madrasa predates Nizam al-Mulk; he substantially sponsored and expanded a notable network. The Baghdad Nizamiyya opened in 1067. The documentary evidence for Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi's initial reluctance at its opening is presented as later biographical tradition, not as an undisputed contemporary eyewitness report.

**Administrative history:** The narrative distinguishes inherited Abbasid, Samanid, Buyid and Ghaznavid institutions from their Seljuk adaptation. It differentiates fiscal assignments from landownership and explains the limits of administrative surveillance. It does not claim that Nizam al-Mulk invented the vizierate, the *iqtaʿ*, or the madrasa.

**Four source windows:** All are explicitly labeled as paraphrase or thematic source-based synthesis, not direct quotation. The narratives and text attribution are plausible against specialist references; however, **individual primary-source paragraphs were not collated word for word against full Arabic or Persian critical editions and translations**. Some windows synthesize an author's larger themes rather than a uniquely identifiable passage. This limit should remain disclosed if the edition advertises primary-source teaching materials.

**Six publication cautions from the approved plan:** Satisfied: avoiding founder myths; distinguishing ideal from practice; scrutinizing *Siyasatnama* authorship; nuanced religious policies; rejecting the Khayyam–Hasan-i Sabbah schoolmate legend; and leaving the assassination's ultimate sponsorship unresolved.
