## 1. Scope, editorial independence, and method

This audit evaluates the actual completed HTML, not the original author's claims about its readiness. The starting point was the uploaded Claude manuscript, `Pasted text(20261125-024001).txt`, and the governing approved Chapter 90 plan. The original chapter was revised in place rather than replaced with a different structure or a new visual system. The revision addressed the defects identified in the preliminary audit: insufficient direct narrative, confusion between Isfahan and Ahvaz steel developments, provisional primary-source identities, incomplete Further Reading, imprecise urban-land and industrial-ownership chronology, and horizontal overflow from the chronology tables on narrow viewports.

**Verification method:** Direct prose in §§90.1–90.20 was extracted from immediate paragraph children of each numbered section. Sections and feature boxes were checked by their HTML identifiers. External documentary claims were checked principally against the United States Department of State's *Foreign Relations of the United States* (FRUS), *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, published legal history and tribunal summaries, and bibliographic records. The revised HTML was reparsed, its internal anchors validated, and its rendered layout tested in headless Chromium at desktop, tablet, mobile, and narrow-mobile widths. The browser test loaded the actual HTML markup as document content; it was **not** a test of a deployed MGU webpage.

The revised chapter ends in early to mid-1977. It does not narrate the revolutionary protest and strike sequence reserved for Chapter 91. It distinguishes the earlier White Revolution (Chapter 88) and political-opposition history (Chapter 89) from the oil-boom economy addressed here.

## 3. Historical corrections completed

### 3.1 The Isfahan and Ahvaz steel developments — §90.5

**Original problem:** The draft described an “Aryamehr steelworks near Ahvaz,” conflating the Aryamehr Steel Mill of Isfahan with a separate project.

**Correction:** The completed chapter identifies the **Aryamehr Steel Mill at Isfahan**, where Soviet-assisted construction resulted in the cast-iron department entering operation in **1971**. It distinguishes pre-existing rolling activity around **Ahvaz** from the separate **Ahvaz Steel Complex**, whose construction began in **1974**. The discussion explicitly distinguishes construction plans, designed capacity, commissioning, and production actually available during the Pahlavi era. Planned Ahvaz output is not presented as realized output in the early 1970s.

**Verified reference:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Steel Industry in Iran,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/steel-industry-in-iran/.

### 3.2 The precise 1973 oil agreement and its operating arrangements — §90.2 and Primary Source Window 1

The signing date is now **19 July 1973**, with contractual effect backdated to **21 March 1973**. The narrative and source window explain the replacement of the earlier consortium operating structure, NIOC's enlarged control, and the new **Oil Service Company of Iran (OSCO)**. They distinguish a service agreement and oil-purchasing relationships from the foreign companies' former right to operate the fields on their own behalf. The initial expansion-financing formulation—**60 percent NIOC funding and 40 percent purchasing-company advances**—is drawn from the documented agreement provisions, and is described as an arrangement for the initial expansion period, not a permanent ownership split.

**References:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Oil Agreements in Iran,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/oil-agreements-in-iran/; Iran–U.S. Claims Tribunal, *Mobil Oil Iran, Inc. et al. v. Iran*, https://www.trans-lex.org/232000/.

**Limit:** The source window is explicitly an **editorial paraphrase**. The contractual structure was compared with published summaries and judicial discussion; the audit does not claim a line-by-line edition or transcription of every article in the full original agreement. The source window links readers to verifiable legal and historical records.

### 3.3 The corrected U.S. intelligence assessment — §90.14 and Primary Source Window 2

**Original problem:** The draft treated the government assessment as a **1974** document and left its FRUS identifier unverified.

**Correction:** The feature now identifies **National Intelligence Estimate NIE 34–1–75, “Iran,” dated 9 May 1975**, officially published as **FRUS, 1969–1976, Volume XXVII, Document 121**. The chapter distinguishes the **date of composition in 1975** from its assessment of **Iran's economic performance and oil receipts in 1974**. The approximately **$20 billion** revenue figure is explicitly an American contemporary estimate, not an assertion that every accounting series or Iranian fiscal year yields the same total.

**Primary document:** https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d121.

The source window's paraphrase and perspective analysis were rewritten to align with the identified estimate. It makes no fabricated verbatim quotation.

### 3.4 The 1975 antiprofiteering sources — §90.15 and Primary Source Window 3

**Original problem:** The source was described only as unspecified “U.S. embassy reporting” and the FRUS document number was provisional.

**Correction:** The feature distinguishes **original embassy telegrams dated August–October 1975** from **FRUS Volume XXVII, Document 141**, which is a **later editorial note** presenting excerpts and summaries of those telegrams. It identifies Tehran telegram 8447, **29 August 1975**, and other dated communications. The narrative describes claims that price controls deterred import orders and shook merchant confidence. The figure of about **25,000 alleged violators detained or sentenced** is qualified as an **embassy-reported contemporary claim**, not a verified census of unique detainees or everyone imprisoned during the campaign.

**Official source:** https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d141.

The educational value here is the distinction between a diplomatic source, an editorial compilation, and the claims made by an observer with selected commercial contacts.

### 3.5 Housing and urban-land policy — §90.7

The vague passage about mid-1970s land laws was replaced. A **May 1975 Land Transactions Law** restricting repeated transfers of certain undeveloped urban land is identified as a specific pre-revolutionary measure against speculation. The discussion makes clear that this was not a blanket abolition of private ownership and must not be conflated with the much more far-reaching **1979 Urban Wastelands Act** enacted after the revolution.

**Reference:** Research on urban land law, including the University College London-hosted thesis discussing the May 1975 law, https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1317518/1/243840.pdf; *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Housing in Iran,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/housing-in-iran/.

**Limit:** The academic law-history account was used to identify the May 1975 provision; this audit does not claim inspection of a certified Persian-language copy of the original 1975 statute.

### 3.6 Industrial share ownership versus worker profit sharing — §90.8

The chapter now distinguishes the earlier White Revolution's **industrial profit-sharing arrangements** from the **1975 program widening share ownership**. The completed paragraph explains that designated private enterprises were to offer up to **49 percent of shares**, with differing provisions for many state enterprises. It carefully differentiates a legal right or opportunity to purchase equity from actually completed purchases or independent worker control of enterprise management. No generalized claim is made that all industrial workers became shareholders.

**References:** Historical discussions of the 1975 Law for the Expansion of Public Ownership of Productive Units and Iran–U.S. Claims Tribunal cases concerning compulsory share offerings, including https://jusmundi.com/fr/document/decision/en-aram-sabet-karim-sabet-reja-sabets-v-the-islamic-republic-of-iran-bonyad-e-mostazafan-and-bank-markazi-partial-award-award-no-593-815-816-817-2-wednesday-30th-june-1999; *International Commission of Jurists*, 1976 report for contemporary government descriptions.

### 3.7 Literacy, automobile production, inflation, and oil-market chronology — §§90.5, 90.10, 90.13, 90.16

The manuscript now identifies the **1976 literacy rate as approximately 47.5 percent** of the population aged six and over, rather than merely “about half.” It explains why this adult-and-child population stock differs from school-enrollment statistics, and why sex and rural–urban differences matter. A dated **1977 Paykan passenger-car production figure of 90,866** is used explicitly as an endpoint illustration, not assigned retroactively to 1974–1976 production. The inflation section distinguishes the **roughly 2.6 percent annual average in 1963–1972** from the **approximately 15.7 percent annual average during the Fifth Plan**, while warning against comparison with differently defined single-year contemporary estimates. The **December 1976 Doha OPEC split** is retained and quantified as a **5-percent increase for Saudi Arabia and the UAE versus a 10-percent increase for most other producers, including Iran**, with care not to treat an announced price change as a guaranteed fiscal receipt.

**References:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Iran National Company,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iran-national-company/; “Economy ix: In the Pahlavi Period,” https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/economy-ix/; U.S. Department of State FRUS, vol. XXVII, doc. 195, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d195; census-based literacy series cited in scholarly research.

### 3.8 Military-purchase projections — §90.19

The military section now examines operational training, spare parts, maintenance, supplier dependence, and competition for skilled labor. It refers to the May 1975 U.S. estimate's anticipation of roughly **$5 billion annually in Iranian foreign military purchases over five years** solely as a **forecast of future commitments**, not verified actual spending or deliveries. Its discussion of opportunity costs is comparative, not an assertion that every defense outlay was economically useless.

**Primary document:** https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d121.

## 4. Substantive narrative expansion and balance

The additions were made across the assigned sections with attention to **what** each new paragraph explains and **how** its evidence supports the chapter's question.

- **Division I:** Clarifies NIOC's service company and expansion financing, and the difference between authorization and physical completion of development projects.
- **Division II:** Separates Isfahan steel output from Ahvaz plans; adds the dated Paykan production series; deepens migration routes, the economic roles of different cities, affordable-housing constraints, land speculation, the 1975 share-ownership program, and workplace differences between stable industrial employment and casual construction labor.
- **Division III:** Explains the functional effects and financing of consumer durables, unequal educational opportunity, the 1976 literacy denominator, the measurement of wealth and inequality, and why rising food imports do not by themselves prove nationwide agricultural collapse.
- **Division IV:** Adds a dated inflation comparison, distributional effects of bottlenecks, specific port and credit transmission mechanisms, documentary detail from the 1975 price-control campaign, the Doha two-tier structure, and uneven effects of retrenchment.
- **Division V:** Clarifies patronage through institutional decision points rather than blanket allegations, contextualizes 1971 royal celebrations as **pre-windfall**, distinguishes arms-order forecasts from deliveries, and significantly deepens the historical synthesis of economic capacity, social expectations, and political feedback.

The revised chapter continues to recognize material gains in education, production, infrastructure, public services, and living standards while accounting for different access to those gains. It avoids treating modern consumption as homogeneous Westernization and avoids interpreting the economic changes of 1976–1977 as proof that a revolution had to occur.

## 7. Remaining limitations and final judgement

This completed chapter is an educational historical synthesis, not a critical edition of every archival contract, survey, or official price series. Three reasonable limitations remain:

1. **Statistical comparability.** Petroleum receipts, nominal income, real growth, consumer-price inflation, urban population, and literacy may appear under different fiscal calendars, coverage definitions, or revised statistical series. The chapter specifies the scope of its principal benchmarks and avoids spurious precision, but any future econometric presentation should reconcile the underlying series in a separate data appendix.
2. **Original documentary access.** The 1973 contract and the 1975 urban-land statute were cross-checked using published legal or scholarly records, not a line-by-line retranslation of complete Persian originals. The source windows therefore use appropriately labeled paraphrases and give traceable references.
3. **Live publishing and maps.** The HTML was tested locally; the production URL has not been checked. Geographic instructional questions are present, but a full map illustration is intentionally reserved for the user's later cartographic insertion under the governing MGU workflow.

These limitations do **not** constitute unresolved publication-critical historical or HTML defects. The preliminary audit's required historical corrections, primary-source identities, prose-depth obligations, bibliography problems, and mobile-width table issue have been addressed and rechecked.
