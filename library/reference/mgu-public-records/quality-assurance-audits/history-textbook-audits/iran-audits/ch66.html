Chapter Opening

How could a monarchy that remained wealthy, sophisticated, and institutionally complex become vulnerable to a series of challenges it could not ultimately overcome, and does the word “decline” explain that vulnerability or merely name it?

An analytical reconstruction. The scene that follows is not the report of a single recorded day. It is assembled from the kinds of evidence this chapter examines, chronicles, administrative manuals, European travelers' descriptions, company correspondence, coins, and court painting, to suggest how the Safavid court at Isfahan may be pictured around the year 1700, a few years into the reign of Shah Sultan Husayn. No individual named in it, and no specific conversation, is being reported.

Around 1700 Isfahan was still the political center of one of the largest states in the Islamic world. Petitioners and officials passed through the royal precincts beside the great square laid out under Shah Abbas I, and the machinery of government that served the shah, secretaries who drafted and sealed orders, accountants who reckoned revenue in the accounting unit called the toman, household officers who supervised royal workshops and stores, continued to operate. Revenue and goods reached the capital from provinces administered by the crown and from provinces governed by commanders and tribal notables. Religious scholars sought audiences, foreign envoys and the agents of the English and Dutch East India Companies negotiated at court, and Armenian merchants of the suburb of New Julfa conducted business that reached from Amsterdam and Venice to India. Military commanders held offices and governorships that carried real power. Court ceremony, including the annual New Year presentation of gifts that a Safavid painter dated to 1720–21 would later depict, remained elaborate, and royal building and patronage continued. Court imagery of this kind is evidence of the continuing sophistication of Safavid court culture. It is not, by itself, evidence of political effectiveness, and it is not evidence of decline.

Yet the same court faced difficulties that were visible to those who looked for them. Orders took weeks to reach distant provinces, and obedience to them could not be taken for granted. Provincial governors and tribal leaders pursued interests of their own. Court officials and household servants competed for access to a ruler whose daily contact with the wider world was filtered through them. Fiscal obligations pressed on the treasury, and mounting an army and supplying it for a distant campaign required a coordination of money, appointments, and supplies that was hard to achieve quickly. Nothing in this picture announces the events of 1722. A visitor to Isfahan in 1700 who had predicted the fall of the dynasty within a generation would have been making a remarkable prediction, and the historian who studies these years must resist the temptation to read every difficulty as an early symptom of that outcome.

This is the central methodological problem of the chapter. The fall of Isfahan to Afghan forces in 1722, the subject of Chapter 67, is so dramatic that it has long shaped how earlier decades were remembered. Many older narratives, and many European travelers' accounts on which they relied, treated the century after Shah Abbas I as a steady slide: a line of rulers progressively weakened by the harem, wine, and religious bigotry, presiding over a treasury steadily emptied and an army steadily neglected. More recent scholarship, above all Rudi Matthee's Persia in Crisis: Safavid Decline and the Fall of Isfahan (2012), has questioned that narrative. It points to real achievements in the reign of Abbas II, to the continued capacity of the central government to act, and to the ways in which several unrelated pressures converged only late in the story. The entries on the late shahs in Encyclopaedia Iranica likewise caution against portraying late Safavid government as inactive or incapable. This chapter takes that scholarship seriously without treating it as the last word.

The chapter's argument can be stated in advance. The Safavid collapse cannot be explained satisfactorily by the supposed personal weakness of individual shahs, by the influence of religious scholars, by fiscal pressure, by military change, or by provincial rebellion taken alone. These factors interacted over time, and their effects were uneven across reigns, regions, and social groups. What changed after 1629 was less the existence of institutions than the way they were coordinated, and the state's capacity to mobilize its considerable resources quickly became decisive when several crises arrived together. Historical actors in these decades did not know the outcome, and the possibility of adaptation, reform, or survival remains part of the analysis throughout.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Persian court chronicles, such as the Abbasnama of Muhammad Tahir Vahid Qazvini, record royal activity from within the court. Administrative manuals, notably the Tadhkirat al-Muluk compiled shortly after the dynasty's fall, describe the offices of government as they were remembered. The French jeweler Jean Chardin, the German physician Engelbert Kaempfer, and many other visitors described the court and country vividly but from outside and often with the prejudices of their own societies. Dutch and English company records measure commerce but only at the ports and in the trades that concerned the companies. Coins, decrees, and building inscriptions survive but cover only some places and periods. Each kind of source produces a different portrait of the same decades, a problem Section 66.19 addresses directly.

The chapter observes the boundaries established elsewhere in the textbook. The institutions that made the Safavid state formidable under Abbas I, his military reforms and centralization (Chapter 61), the capital he built (Chapter 62), the commerce that funded it (Chapter 63), the social hierarchy beneath it (Chapter 64), and the religious establishment allied with it (Chapter 65), are treated here only as they bear on what happened to those institutions under his successors. The chapter ends at the threshold of the final catastrophe: it explains the origins and significance of the 1709 Kandahar revolt and the unsuccessful efforts to restore authority in the 1710s, but the Afghan advance on Isfahan, the siege, the surrender, and the foreign interventions that followed belong to Chapter 67. Five divisions of four sections each carry the argument, moving from succession and political continuity to the late court, from the court to finance and the army, from the center to the provinces, and finally to crisis and the historiography of decline.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the centralization achieved under Shah Abbas I strengthened the monarchy while changing the institutions through which succession and political competition occurred.
  2. Trace the political history of the reigns of Safi I, Abbas II, Suleiman, and Sultan Husayn, distinguishing contemporary moral judgments from evidence of institutional consequences.
  3. Assess the reign of Abbas II as evidence of continued Safavid military and administrative capacity after 1629.
  4. Analyze the roles of grand viziers, eunuchs, royal women, palace officials, and commanders in late Safavid government without treating any group as inherently destructive.
  5. Distinguish crown-administered (khassa) lands, provincial (mamalik) territories, and revenue assignments (tuyul), and evaluate the consequences of changing balances among them.
  6. Interpret evidence concerning taxation, silver, coinage, and mints, distinguishing nominal monetary values from purchasing power and a state's resources from its ability to mobilize them.
  7. Evaluate competing arguments about whether prolonged peace after 1639 weakened Safavid military readiness or preserved resources.
  8. Analyze center–province relations using regional examples, including Kandahar, the Caucasus, the Caspian provinces, and Khurasan.
  9. Explain the causes and immediate significance of the 1709 Kandahar revolt without treating Afghan communities as politically uniform.
  10. Evaluate the roles of religious policy, drought, famine, and disease in the late Safavid crisis without treating any single factor as the cause of collapse.
  11. Compare the principal explanatory traditions in the historiography of Safavid decline and the kinds of evidence each uses.
  12. Evaluate whether decline, transformation, overstretch, or crisis is the most useful interpretation of the late Safavid state, avoiding explanations that assume the outcome of 1722.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why might a highly centralized state be harder, rather than easier, for a successor to govern than for its builder?
  2. What does the reign of Abbas II suggest about the claim that Safavid decline began with the death of Abbas I?
  3. How did delegation of government to viziers and palace officials work in practice, and how can historians tell whether it was institutional deterioration or a workable arrangement?
  4. What is the difference between a state possessing substantial resources and a state being able to mobilize them quickly in an emergency?
  5. Why might a long peace both preserve resources and erode military readiness, and how could historians weigh these effects?
  6. How did conditions in Kandahar, the Caucasus, the Caspian provinces, and Khurasan differ, and what do the differences show about the reach of the central government?
  7. In what ways did religious policy affect governance and loyalty, and why is it unsatisfactory to treat it as the single cause of collapse?
  8. How do chronicles, travelers' accounts, company records, and administrative documents produce different portraits of the same period?
  9. What is gained and what is lost by describing the late Safavid state as being in “decline”?
  10. How can historians write about the 1710s without allowing knowledge of 1722 to shape every judgment about what came before?

Historical Context

The Safavid dynasty began as the leadership of a Sufi order centered in northwestern Iran and became a state with Ismaʿil's conquest of Tabriz in 1501 (Chapter 57). For its first century the dynasty ruled with the military support of the Qizilbash, Turkmen tribal forces whose leaders held provincial governorships and large revenue assignments and who sometimes treated the throne as a prize to be competed for. Shah Abbas I, who formally reigned from 1588 to 1629 after an accession struggle beginning in 1587, spent much of his career reducing the dependence of the monarchy on these forces. He built up a standing military establishment of slave soldiers (ghulams) drawn largely from the Caucasus, introduced firearms and artillery on a larger scale, and transferred many provinces to direct crown administration so that their revenues supported the royal household and the new army rather than the Qizilbash commanders. He moved the capital to Isfahan and rebuilt it as a showpiece of royal authority, and he promoted the export of Iranian silk, with Armenians resettled from the Caucasus into New Julfa serving as a commercial network under royal protection. Chapters 61 through 65 examine these developments and the society and religious establishment that surrounded them.

The state Abbas I left was therefore both stronger and different. It was stronger because the crown controlled more revenue and more troops directly than earlier shahs had. It was different because the personal link between a monarch and the instruments of power had grown more important. Abbas I was an experienced soldier and ruthless politician who supervised the new institutions himself. His successors, raised from childhood in the palace rather than in provincial governorships, inherited the apparatus without the experience or the personal ties that had built it. How the apparatus functioned under rulers who had not made it is the subject of this chapter.

The wider environment also changed, though not in a single direction. The Ottoman Empire remained the dynasty's great western rival until the peace of 1639 set a frontier that held for several generations. On the east, the Mughal and Uzbek states bordered the Safavid lands, and the city of Kandahar, a commercial and strategic prize, passed back and forth between the Mughal and Safavid empires. In the north, Russia was emerging as a power along the Caspian and in the Caucasus. In the south, the Persian Gulf trade was reorganized after Abbas I's capture of Hormuz in 1622 and the rise of Bandar Abbas, with the Dutch and English East India Companies as important, if not dominant, participants. Silver, silk, and spices moved across these frontiers in patterns that shifted over the century.

Finally, students should keep in mind the evidence problem. Much of what is known about the seventeenth-century Safavid court comes from European visitors who wrote for audiences accustomed to thinking of “oriental despotism,” from Persian chronicles written under royal patronage, and from the records of European trading companies that were interested in particular commodities and ports. Almost nothing survives in the voices of cultivators, pastoralists, or most townspeople. The chapter therefore proceeds with caution about numbers, which are scarce and often unreliable, and about judgments of character, which were often shaped by the writer's purposes.

Division I · §§66.1–66.4

The Safavid State after Abbas I: Succession, War, and Political Continuity

The transformation of government, 1629–1666. The four sections that follow examine the decades after Shah Abbas I's death to show that they included both costly political struggles and significant demonstrations of institutional resilience.

Section 66.1

The Safavid State in 1629: Abbas I's Legacy and the Problem of Succession

Shah Abbas I died in January 1629 at Ashraf on the Caspian coast of Mazandaran, after a reign of more than four decades. The principal court chronicle of his reign, Iskandar Beg Munshi's Tarikh-i alam-ara-yi Abbasi, was continued after the author's death by a successor, and its record of the final years shows a court that regarded the old shah's death as the end of an era. The government that Abbas left was the most centralized the dynasty had known. At its head stood the shah, supported by a grand vizier who headed the civil administration, a corps of secretaries and accountants, and a household that supervised the royal workshops, stores, and palaces. Alongside this civil apparatus stood a military establishment with several components: Qizilbash cavalry still led by tribal chiefs, the standing corps of ghulams who owed their careers to the shah, musketeers and artillerymen, and provincial forces raised by governors. Revenue from crown-administered provinces fed the royal treasury and supported the new regiments. Reserves accumulated during the reign, which later writers described as large, gave the crown a financial cushion. Provincial governors, some of them ghulam commanders of great wealth and prestige, administered distant regions and commanded troops.

This centralization strengthened the monarchy in obvious ways. The shah could pay and equip soldiers without depending on the tribal chiefs who had once dominated the state. He could draw on revenue from provinces under his direct control, and he could appoint and dismiss governors with less fear of tribal revolt. But centralization also changed how the state worked, and it changed the position of those who would inherit it. In the early Safavid state, princes had often been sent to provinces under the guidance of Qizilbash tutors, where they learned something of governing and gathered followers. The arrangement had its own dangers: tribal factions competed over princes, and succession struggles could become civil wars. Abbas I ended the practice. Suspicious of his sons, he had his eldest son Safi Mirza killed in 1615 and had other sons blinded, a punishment that, by the conventions of the dynasty, disqualified them from the throne because a ruler was expected to be physically whole. Princes were kept in the palace, in the andarun, where they lived under supervision and where contact with the wider world was limited.

The consequences were potentially large, though they should not be exaggerated. The Safavid dynasty had a long record of eliminating rivals: before Abbas, the brother-killing that attended some accessions had left their own scars on the state. Abbas's measures reduced the number of conflicting claimants and, at least for a time, the danger of tribal factions rallying behind a prince. But they also reduced the pool of experienced heirs. When Abbas died, his only suitable male descendant was his grandson Sam Mirza, a young man who had grown up within the palace rather than in provincial office. He succeeded as Shah Safi, at about the age of seventeen. Whatever his intelligence and ability, he had little direct experience of the army, of provincial governors, or of the great nobles whose loyalty he would need.

The section's central point is therefore institutional. A ruler could inherit a highly centralized state without inheriting the personal authority, experience, and political relationships of the monarch who constructed it. Abbas I had made the new institutions work through his own energy and through personal ties with commanders and officials that he cultivated for decades. Those ties did not pass automatically to a successor. Instead, a series of intermediaries stood between the new shah and the state: viziers, household officials, commanders, and the women and eunuchs of the inner palace. How those intermediaries acted, and whether the shah could direct them, would determine much of what happened next. The chapter returns to this problem repeatedly, and Section 66.8 examines it directly.

Nor was the outcome inevitable. A capable and fortunate heir might have mastered the apparatus, as Abbas II later demonstrated in part. The question for historians is not whether weakness was predestined but how the particular arrangements Abbas I left, and the particular choices of his successors, interacted over the following decades.

Section 66.2

Shah Safi I, 1629–1642: Court Purges, Grand Viziers, and the Consolidation of Royal Authority

Safi's reign began with a determined effort to secure the throne. In the early years he ordered the execution or blinding of royal relatives who might serve as alternative candidates, and he removed senior commanders and officials who had risen under Abbas I. The most famous of these cases was that of Imam Quli Khan, governor of Fars, who was executed in 1633 according to the specialist biographical entry (some regional histories give 1632 for the event in 1042 AH). Imam Quli Khan was the son of Allahverdi Khan, one of Abbas I's most trusted ghulam commanders, and he governed Fars and the Gulf coast with an authority that few contemporaries could match. His execution removed a powerful provincial magnate and brought his province more directly under the crown's control.

The logic of such actions deserves analysis rather than simple condemnation. A young shah surrounded by commanders who had served his grandfather, who controlled troops, and who held great provincial power had reason to fear that they might act on their own account, install a more pliable member of the family, or dictate policy. The Safavid past supplied examples. By removing the most powerful figures and replacing them with men who owed their positions to him, Safi sought to turn a formal authority into a real one. In the short term, the strategy succeeded in that he reigned for thirteen years without a successful challenge to his throne. But the costs were real. Experienced commanders and administrators were killed and replaced by men with less experience and more reason for insecurity, since the fate of their predecessors was a warning. Knowledge of provincial conditions, of campaigns, and of the networks through which the state operated was lost. These costs would become more visible when the state faced external pressure, as it did in 1638.

Historians face a problem of evidence in assessing Safi. European visitors, including Adam Olearius, who traveled with a diplomatic mission from Holstein and was in Safavid lands in the later 1630s, described a shah given to heavy drinking and bursts of violence, and later accounts built heavily on these portraits. Persian chronicles of the reign are more restrained and are written within the conventions of court history, which sought to present the shah in a favorable light. Neither type of source tells the whole story, and the lurid detail should not stand in for analysis. It is more useful to ask what the pattern of actions suggests about the political position of the shah, and what institutional consequences followed. On this, the evidence points to a shift in the balance of power within the state: from military magnates with independent bases toward the shah, his household, and the civil administration.

The emergence of the grand vizier Mirza Muhammad Saru Taqi, who held office from about 1634, illustrates the shift. Saru Taqi was an administrator rather than a soldier. His tenure is associated by specialists with close attention to revenue, with the continued expansion of crown administration, and with a growing role for civil officials in the management of the state. In a government where the shah was young and the great commanders had been removed or intimidated, a capable vizier could carry a great deal of authority. That authority could be exercised effectively, and it also made the vizier a target for rivals, as the events of 1645 would show. The shift toward administrators did not mean that the army ceased to matter. It meant that the instruments for controlling the army, assigning governorships, and funding military expenditure were increasingly in the hands of civil officials working in the shah's name.

By the time Safi died in 1642, aged about thirty, the throne was secure and the central administration was functioning. At the same time, the dynasty had lost Baghdad and Kandahar, and its political class had been reshaped by purges. The reign thus offers an example of a general point: actions that strengthen a ruler's immediate position may carry institutional costs that appear later, and a fair account must weigh both.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Grand Vizier, Succession, and Court Faction

Grand vizier (iʿtimad al-dawla; also vazir-i aʿzam). Definition: The shah's chief minister, head of the civil administration and of the apparatus of revenue, correspondence, and appointments. Historical context: Under the Safavids the grand vizier supervised secretaries and accountants, advised on appointments, and handled much of the routine business of government; his influence depended on the shah's confidence and on his own control of information and patronage. Why it matters: The strength or weakness of a grand vizier is a useful indicator of how a reign was governed. Potential misunderstanding: The title is not equivalent to a modern prime minister. A grand vizier served at the shah's pleasure, could be dismissed or killed, and rarely had an independent institutional base.

Succession. Definition: The process by which rule passed from one shah to another. Historical context: The Safavids had no firm law of succession beyond the expectation that the ruler be male, of the dynasty, and physically sound, which meant that disputes were settled by the interplay of court factions, military backing, and the wishes of the reigning shah. Why it matters: Succession was the moment when the balance among competing interests was most visible. Potential misunderstanding: Safavid succession was not simply a matter of primogeniture; practice varied from reign to reign.

Court faction. Definition: A shifting group of courtiers, officials, commanders, household servants, and royal women who combined to advance particular candidates, policies, or interests. Historical context: Factions rarely had a formal structure, and individuals could belong to several overlapping groups. Why it matters: Factional competition shaped appointments and policy and could bring ministers down. Potential misunderstanding: A faction was not a modern political party with a program, and not every disagreement at court was factional.

Section 66.3

Ottoman War, Baghdad, and the Peace of Zuhab, 1639

The reign of Safi coincided with the final phase of the long Ottoman–Safavid struggle, whose broader history belongs to Chapter 59. The sultan Murad IV, an energetic ruler who had consolidated his own authority in the 1630s, campaigned in the east against the Safavids in the middle of the decade, and in 1638 the Ottomans besieged and recovered Baghdad, which Abbas I had taken in 1623. Safavid forces did not relieve the city in time. The loss was a blow to Safavid prestige and to Shi‘i Iran in particular, since Baghdad lay close to the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, central to Shi‘i devotion, and the territory of southern Iraq returned to Ottoman control.

In the same year the Safavid commander at Kandahar, Ali Mardan Khan, defected to the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan and surrendered the city to him. The reasons are connected to the politics of Safi's court, where the fate of other commanders who had fallen foul of the shah gave an official of the frontier reason to doubt his safety, though a full reconstruction of his motives is not possible. The result was that, within a few months, the Safavid state had lost control of two strategic cities at opposite ends of its domains.

The Peace of Zuhab, concluded in May 1639 and associated with the border town also known as Qasr-e Shirin, ended the war. It left Baghdad and southern Iraq in Ottoman hands and left Yerevan and the northwestern provinces under the Safavids, and it established a frontier that, with later adjustments, served as the basis of the western boundary between the two empires for generations. Tribal allegiances along the frontier and the passage of Shi‘i pilgrims to the shrine cities in Ottoman Iraq remained matters of negotiation in later decades. The Ottoman and Safavid empires would not again fight a major war for decades; the next serious conflict came only in the 1720s, after the Safavid state had begun to disintegrate.

The consequences for the Safavid state were complex, and the section's task is to describe them rather than to repeat the earlier history of the conflict. Territorially, the loss of Baghdad and southern Iraq was real, but it was a loss of a frontier province that Abbas I had himself won only recently, and it did not signal a general collapse of state capacity. The peace relieved the crown of the heavy expense of maintaining armies on the western front. It allowed attention and resources to be turned elsewhere, to the eastern frontier, to internal administration, and to the court. It also contributed to changes in military organization and readiness. With the Ottoman front quiet, the pressure to maintain large, experienced forces in a state of constant preparedness declined, and the proportion of revenue directed to the army could be reduced. Whether this was a saving that strengthened the state or a relaxation that weakened it is the subject of Section 66.12.

The peace also shaped diplomacy. With the Ottomans no longer the immediate threat, the Safavids continued to correspond with European powers, which had long hoped for an anti-Ottoman alliance, but the practical value of such alliances diminished. European envoys continued to visit Isfahan, often to seek commercial privileges, and the shift from military alliance to commercial diplomacy is characteristic of the later seventeenth century. The state's external relations came to focus more on trade, Gulf security, and the northern and eastern frontiers.

For the historian, the key point is the distinction between a temporary territorial loss and a general collapse of state capacity. Safavid Iran after 1639 was smaller by one frontier province but enjoyed a longer period of external peace on its western flank than at any earlier time in the dynasty's history. How the state used that peace is a central question for the chapters that follow.

Section 66.4

Shah Abbas II, 1642–1666: Political Recovery, Kandahar, and the Persistence of Safavid Power

Safi died in May 1642, and his son Abbas II, born in 1632, came to the throne as a child. In the early years of the reign, power rested with senior officials, above all the grand vizier Saru Taqi, who continued in office and who directed the administration in the young shah's name. The arrangement was not unusual for a minority, and it worked well enough that the government continued to function. But it also placed an official of non-military background in a position that many of the military elite resented. In 1645 Saru Taqi was assassinated by a group of officers, and the circumstances reveal the tensions between civil and military sides of the government. After his death the office passed to Khalifa Sultan, a man with a religious background who served as grand vizier until the early 1650s and who is remembered as a steady administrator. Court factions continued to compete, and the shah's mother and other figures of the household played roles, but the state held together.

Abbas II began to exercise personal authority as he grew older, and the central achievement of his reign was the recovery of Kandahar. In the winter of 1648–49 he led an army to the city, which the Mughals had held since 1638, and after a siege received its surrender in February 1649. The campaign was a considerable logistical undertaking, for the march lay across the eastern plateau in winter and required food, fodder, and equipment for a large force. The Mughal court did not accept the loss. Armies led by Aurangzeb and later Dara Shikoh besieged Kandahar in 1649, 1652, and 1653, and all failed. The recovery of Kandahar was thus not only a military victory but a demonstration that Safavid forces could be assembled, supplied, and led over long distances to defeat a great power, and that the eastern frontier could be held against it.

The reign included other signs of vigor. Abbas II oversaw the construction of the Chehel Sotun, the pillared palace pavilion at Isfahan that became a setting for royal receptions, and he continued royal patronage of building and the arts. The government maintained a functioning fiscal system and responded to problems as they arose. Foreign observers noted a court that, while given to elaborate entertainment, remained capable of acting. The reign is often cited by specialist historians as a period of genuine political and military achievement, even while its limits are recognized: the shah drank heavily, he died young, and he left no heir with experience of government.

This record matters for the chapter's argument. If the Safavid state were in continuous decline from 1629, the achievements of Abbas II would be hard to explain. The recovery of Kandahar showed that the institutions inherited from Abbas I, the crown's revenue system, the ghulam and musketeer corps, the provincial forces, and the administrative machinery, could still be mobilized by a competent and determined ruler. It supports a more nuanced reading: institutional capacity persisted, and its use depended heavily on the ruler and his ministers. The very dependence on personal leadership was a vulnerability, since each ruler's effectiveness had to be rebuilt anew.

Abbas II died in October 1666, aged about thirty-four, after a reign of twenty-four years. He left no mature heir who had been trained for the task. His death thus returned the state to the problem of succession that had been present since 1629, and the next reign opened with troubles that its astrologers would blame on the stars. Section 66.5

Shah Suleiman, 1666–1694: The Second Coronation and the Politics of Kingship

When Abbas II died in 1666 the court moved quickly to install his son Sam Mirza, who was crowned as Shah Safi II, taking the name of his grandfather Safi I. The new shah was a young man who had been raised in the palace. The first months of his rule were troubled. Chroniclers and European observers record shortages of food in the late 1660s, an earthquake that damaged the city of Shamakhi in 1667, and illness at court. Court astrologers, whose advice was regularly consulted on the timing of important acts, advised that the shah's accession had taken place under an inauspicious configuration and that a second coronation, under a different name, would restore good fortune.

The second coronation took place on 20 March 1668, at the season of the Persian New Year, and the shah ascended the throne again as Suleiman. The ceremony is documented in specialist scholarship and in accounts of the Chehel Sotun palace, where royal ceremonies of this kind were staged. The new name carried associations of its own, since Sulayman, the biblical and Qur’anic Solomon, was a prophet-king known for wisdom and dominion, and its adoption linked the shah to a model of righteous kingship. Rulers elsewhere in the Islamic world also changed names or repeated enthronements for political or ritual reasons, so Suleiman's case, though unusual, was not without parallel.

How should the episode be read? A modern observer may see credulity, but a historian will ask what the ceremony did. It addressed a crisis of confidence in a new reign by publicly renewing the shah's authority; it drew on astrology, which was a respected body of learning at court and in much of the Islamic world; and it involved religious legitimation through the participation of scholars and the invocation of prophetic precedent. It also revealed how the court handled uncertainty, by recourse to ritual and to specialists whose advice was highly regarded. The astrologers had no veto over policy, but their advice shaped the timing of royal acts, a pattern visible throughout the seventeenth century. The second coronation therefore shows kingship as a performed institution that could be repaired by public ceremony.

The reign that followed lasted twenty-eight years, and its character has long been disputed. Many European visitors described Suleiman as a shah who withdrew from the business of government into the pleasures of the palace and who left decisions to ministers and household servants. Jean Chardin, who was in Safavid lands in the 1660s and 1670s, and Engelbert Kaempfer, who accompanied a Swedish embassy and then spent time in Isfahan in the 1680s, offer detailed accounts of the court. Persian chronicles are less critical but also give little evidence of the shah's engagement. Later writers, including those who looked back from the disaster of 1722, were quick to find in Suleiman's reign the origins of decline.

A careful reading distinguishes contemporary moral judgments from demonstrable administrative activity. The reign was a period of relative external peace, of continued royal building, including the Hasht Behesht pavilion at Isfahan, usually dated to 1669, and of sustained diplomatic contact with European states and companies. A raid by Cossack forces led by Stenka Razin on the Caspian coast in 1668–69 caused destruction in the northern provinces and exposed the lack of Safavid naval capacity on the Caspian, but it did not threaten the state's survival. A long-serving grand vizier, Shaykh Ali Khan Zangana, managed the administration for about two decades. These facts do not mean the shah was an active ruler. They show that the government functioned during his reign, and that the question of the shah's personal involvement cannot be settled by anecdote. The changing balance between a monarch's participation and the officials who managed affairs is the subject of the next section.

Section 66.6

Grand Viziers, Eunuchs, Palace Officials, and the Management of Government

When a shah delegated, government continued through a set of offices and relationships that deserve description. At the center stood the grand vizier, who headed the civil administration and who presided over a bureaucracy of secretaries, accountants, and clerks. The chief accountant, the mustawfi al-mamalik, supervised the registers of revenue and expenditure; the secretaries drafted and sealed royal decrees (farmans) and correspondence; and the court chronicler recorded events. Another official, the divan-begi, handled judicial matters outside the sphere of religious law. The royal household, under officers who managed the shah's stores, workshops, and finances, ran in parallel with the state treasury and was itself a major economic institution. These administrative structures are described in the Tadhkirat al-Muluk, a manual of Safavid administration compiled shortly after the dynasty fell, and the picture it gives must be used carefully, since it describes an ideal and was written in retrospect.

Shaykh Ali Khan Zangana, who became grand vizier in 1669, was a Kurdish tribal leader from the Zangana, a tribe of western Iran, who rose through court service and held the office for about two decades. He is generally regarded by specialists as one of the most effective ministers of the later seventeenth century. During his tenure the government dealt with the revenue, with appointments to provincial posts, and with the relations with foreign merchants and envoys. His long term in office provided a measure of continuity, and his career shows that men of tribal origin could attain the highest civil position, which also suggests that the line between military-tribal and civil-administrative elites was not rigid.

The effectiveness of such a minister depended on several relationships. One was access to the shah, which was controlled in part by the household officials and eunuchs who stood at the threshold of the inner palace. Another was control of information: the vizier received reports from governors, tax officials, and agents, and decided what reached the shah and in what form. A third was control of appointments, which affected the loyalty of officials throughout the empire, and a fourth was the flow of revenue and of orders between center and provinces, dependent on messengers, the post, and the seals and documents that gave orders authority. When these relationships held, the government could function with a shah who left much of the work to others. When they broke down, the result could be rivalry and drift.

Eunuchs held particular importance. Palace eunuchs, of varied origins, served as guardians of the inner quarters, as attendants to the shah and his family, as supervisors of the royal household, and, in some cases, as commanders and envoys. Because they were barred from founding families and from the ordinary hereditary claims of the elite, rulers often trusted them; because they controlled access to the shah and to royal women, they were also sought out by others who wished to influence court decisions. Royal women, too, had influence through their roles as mothers, wives, and aunts of rulers, and some exercised considerable political weight, as later sections show.

It is easy to caricature these arrangements. European writers often described Safavid government as controlled by the harem, a notion that reflects their own assumptions about oriental courts as much as the evidence. A more careful account recognizes that eunuchs, women, viziers, and military officers each played political roles, that these roles varied with individuals and circumstances, and that none can be characterized as inherently destructive. A eunuch might be a loyal servant and an able administrator; a vizier might be corrupt or conscientious; a royal woman might protect a son's claim or press for peace. The task for historians is to distinguish the political actions of particular individuals from the structural relationships in which they acted.

Did delegation necessarily represent institutional deterioration? The evidence suggests not. Abbas I himself relied on ministers, as did Abbas II, and the vizierate under Shaykh Ali Khan sustained effective administration for years. The potential weakness lay elsewhere: in the dependence of the system on particular individuals, since each change of minister could bring new alliances and the loss of accumulated experience, and in the possibility that a shah who did not supervise might be unaware of problems until they were serious. Delegation could sustain government, but it required ministers who were competent, secure in office, and trusted by the shah, and in the early eighteenth century, as Section 66.7 shows, such conditions were harder to maintain.

Section 66.7

Shah Sultan Husayn, 1694–1722: Accession, Courtly Patronage, and Political Decision-Making

Suleiman died in 1694, and his son Sultan Husayn succeeded him. Like his predecessors, Husayn had been raised in the palace and had no experience of provincial government or command. Accounts of the succession stress the roles of court officials, eunuchs, and senior religious figures and describe the dying shah as hesitating between two sons, a story that may reflect later reconstruction. Whatever the details, the accession was accomplished without civil war, which says something about the stability of the dynasty's institutions even as it shows how decisions about succession were made within the palace rather than in the open.

The reign lasted until 1722 and fell into two parts that students should keep distinct. For its first fifteen years or so the state continued to function much as before, with ministers managing the administration, the frontiers broadly quiet, and the court engaged in building and patronage. The second part, beginning with the crisis of 1709, was dominated by problems on several fronts. The man who presided over both parts was devout, inclined to the company of scholars and ministers, and later described by many writers as indecisive.

The court that surrounded Husayn included senior ministers, military commanders, the royal household, and religious scholars. Among the latter was Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, the leading scholar of the age, whose influence on religious life and at court is discussed in Chapter 65 and who died in 1699. Ministers came and went. Fath Ali Khan Daghestani, a noble from the northeastern Caucasus, served as grand vizier from approximately 1715 until his removal and blinding in December 1720, an episode examined below. Competing interests at court shaped appointments and policy, as in any government, and the practical difficulty was not that no one governed but that no one could easily coordinate the competing parts.

Husayn's reign was marked by religious patronage and major building. The Madrasa-ye Sultani, also called the Madrasa-ye Madar-e Shah after the shah's mother, was built in Isfahan during the early eighteenth century: sources date important parts of the madrasa to about 1706–1707, while construction of the wider complex extended toward 1714 and a major endowment deed was issued in 1711, and it ranks among the great monuments of Safavid architecture. Its institutional history belongs to Chapter 65; here its significance is twofold. It shows that the dynasty retained the resources and craftsmanship to undertake large projects, and it shows the shah's close relationship with the religious establishment. Neither point is evidence of decline. The patronage of religion and learning was a long-established function of Safavid kingship.

The practical challenges of the reign were administrative coordination, taxation, provincial appointments, military security, and competing court interests. Decisions about appointments could have far-reaching consequences. The decision to appoint Gurgin Khan to the governorship of Kandahar, and to support his methods there, helped provoke the revolt of 1709, as Section 66.14 shows. The response to the revolt, which involved expeditions that were poorly coordinated and costly, was another set of decisions. The dismissal of Fath Ali Khan, which deprived the court of an experienced minister at a moment of growing crisis, was a third. Taken together, such decisions look less like the results of a single weakness of character than like the products of a court in which information, patronage, and competing interests interacted in ways that made firm, coherent action hard.

It is therefore unsatisfactory to explain the dynasty's failure by Husayn's religious devotion or personal character alone. Such explanations were popular among European and later writers, and they derive from the genuine impressions of contemporaries, but they take for granted what needs to be shown: that a different personality would have produced a different outcome, and that the structures in which he acted were adequate to the demands placed upon them. The chapter's method is to examine the practical consequences of decisions, to ask what alternatives were available at the time, and to resist the assumption that those who made them knew what lay ahead. The next section examines the system of court politics that framed those decisions.

Section 66.8

Royal Succession, Court Factionalism, and the Distribution of Political Influence

Court politics in the later seventeenth century revolved around a limited set of arrangements. Princes were raised within the palace, typically in the andarun, under the care of women and eunuchs, with restricted access to the world beyond. Their education included religion, literature, and the arts, but little training in command or government. Access to the princes, and especially to the heir apparent, was controlled by household officials, which made those officials important to anyone who wished to influence a future ruler. Competition among courtiers therefore often centered on proximity to princes, on appointments to the household, and on control of the channels through which petitions and information reached the throne.

The groups that took part in this competition were varied, and treating them as a single faction would be misleading. Royal women, among them the mothers, aunts, and sisters of shahs, had ties of kinship and often wealth of their own and used them to support candidates and ministers. Eunuchs held offices in the household and elsewhere and had networks of their own. Military officers, including ghulam commanders and Qizilbash leaders, sought positions and revenue assignments. Religious figures advised on legitimacy and policy and held posts connected with law and endowments. Administrative families, whose members held offices as secretaries and accountants over generations, formed another network. Alliances among these groups crossed lines, and individuals changed sides as circumstances shifted.

A comparison of the four accessions from 1629 to 1694 shows variety in how this system worked. In 1629 Safi succeeded as the only suitable heir after Abbas I's purge of his sons, and the accession itself was uncontested, though the new shah then moved to eliminate possible rivals. In 1642 Abbas II succeeded as a child, which required senior officials to govern in his name and led, in 1645, to the killing of the grand vizier by military officers. In 1666 Suleiman, an adult, was installed quickly but crowned a second time after calamities, in a ritual repair of legitimacy. In 1694 Husayn's succession involved a more explicit contest of influences within the palace, with eunuchs and religious figures, according to accounts, playing important parts. No accession led to civil war, a point in favor of the system's capacity to manage transfers of power. But in each case the new ruler came to the throne with limited experience, and in each case officials and household members had considerable influence over his early decisions.

The question is whether succession practices protected reigning monarchs at the expense of preparing effective successors. There is a case that they did. By confining princes, the shah reduced the risk that a son would build a base and challenge him, and the dynasty avoided the civil wars that had plagued earlier succession struggles. But the price was heirs who lacked the knowledge and relationships that an effective ruler needed, who relied on intermediaries from the outset, and whose personal authority was hard to establish. The consequence was not that every shah was weak, since Abbas II showed otherwise, but that the system's effectiveness depended on the abilities of ministers and the luck of the moment more than on the preparation of the ruler.

These arrangements also bore on the state's ability to respond to crises. A court in which decisions emerged from the balance of competing groups could be slow, and its decisions could be reversed when the balance shifted. In emergencies, such as the revolts that began in 1709, this slowness mattered. Whether it was a structural feature of palace government or the product of the particular circumstances of the 1710s is the subject of the debate that follows.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Palace Government and Political Effectiveness

The question: Did the concentration of authority within the royal household produce administrative continuity, or did it increasingly undermine the state's capacity for coordinated leadership?

Interpretation A — Continuity: Some historians stress that government by ministers and household officials kept the administration running for decades. The long ministry of Shaykh Ali Khan, the continued collection of revenue, the diplomatic correspondence, and the stability of the throne through four accessions suggest an adaptable system. On this view the palace was not a barrier between ruler and state but a mechanism through which the state operated, and the failures of the early eighteenth century had specific causes rather than reflecting a general weakness.
Interpretation B — Erosion of coordination: Others argue that the concentration of access and information in the palace, together with rulers who lacked experience, made the system dependent on the abilities of a few ministers and vulnerable when they were removed. On this view the system could manage routine business but was ill suited to coordinated responses in an emergency, a weakness that appeared in the 1710s as expeditions were launched without adequate preparation and ministers were dismissed at critical moments.

Evidence: Both sides draw on court chronicles, the administrative manuals, European travelers' accounts, and records of appointments and dismissals. The chronicles tend to confirm administrative activity but are silent on difficulties; European accounts stress seclusion and intrigue but reflect outsiders' assumptions. Documentary evidence on the day-to-day functioning of palace government is thin.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and causation more than chronology: how far the arrangements were a cause of the failures of the 1710s, as opposed to a setting in which they occurred, and how typical the 1710s were of the longer period.

Why it matters: If palace government was a workable arrangement, the late Safavid crisis appears as the product of exceptional pressures that would have overwhelmed any government. If it eroded coordinated leadership, the crisis appears as a failure to which institutions contributed, and earlier decades acquire the character of a gradual weakening.

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives, synthesizing patterns that historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named person and do not describe a single recorded event.

The shah: A frontier appointment is a means of securing a distant province, rewarding loyalty, and avoiding commitments of the crown's own forces, but information about candidates reaches the shah through others, and he must rely on advisers whose interests are not his own.
The grand vizier: The appointment must be affordable, must not offend powerful interests, and must hold up when reports arrive; the vizier controls the paperwork and much of the flow of information, but his position depends on the shah's confidence, and a poor choice can be blamed on him.
A palace eunuch: Access to the shah and to women of the household is a form of influence that can be used for or against a candidate. A servant whose position depends entirely on royal favor may be a reliable ally for a candidate who offers protection, and a dangerous opponent for one who does not.
A military commander: Appointments determine who commands troops and who draws revenue to support them. A commander worries that a civil appointee or a convert from another region may be favored over those who have served for years, and that frontier service may expose his soldiers to hardship without reward.
A provincial official or local notable: A new governor arriving with his own troops, his own debts, and his own expectations may disrupt local arrangements, claim revenue, and favor some communities over others. Local leaders weigh whether to cooperate, to petition the court, or to resist.

Why the perspectives differ: These actors did not have equal power, equal information, or equal freedom of action. The shah and the vizier decided; commanders and provincial leaders adapted. The exercise shows why decisions made in the center could have consequences in the provinces that the decision-makers neither intended nor foresaw.

Section 66.9

Crown Lands, Revenue Assignments, and the Changing Fiscal Foundations of Monarchy

The Safavid fiscal system rested on a distinction between two kinds of territory. Khassa lands were administered directly by the crown, through officials appointed by the shah, and their revenue flowed to the royal treasury and household. Mamalik (also called divan) provinces were governed by appointed governors, often Qizilbash chiefs or other commanders, who collected the revenue, kept a share for themselves and their troops, and passed on part to the center. Within the mamalik, revenue assignments called tuyul were granted to officers and officials in lieu of salary, entitling the holder to collect specified revenue from a district in return for service, usually military. Other arrangements, including grants of exemption from tax (soyurghal) and the revenues of endowments (vaqf), completed a complex mosaic. The system was not static, and the relative weight of the different arrangements changed over time.

Under Abbas I, the crown's holdings grew dramatically. He transferred a number of provinces, among them Mazandaran, Gilan, Yazd, Kashan, and Qum, from the mamalik to crown administration. The incentives were political and economic. Politically, the transfers deprived Qizilbash chiefs of the revenue bases on which their independence rested. Economically, they gave the crown access to the proceeds of rich provinces, particularly the silk-producing provinces of the Caspian region, and so to cash, which was needed to pay and equip the new ghulam regiments and to fund the shah's building and diplomacy. The expansion of crown administration was thus a central part of the strategy of centralization described in Chapter 61.

The relationships among revenue collection, governorships, military obligations, and central expenditure were intricate. In a mamalik province, a governor collected revenue, raised and paid his contingent, and was expected to support the central government with troops when called on. In a crown province, officials sent by the center collected revenue for the treasury, from which the shah paid the standing army and household. In the first case, the center had less direct control over resources but fewer administrative burdens; in the second, it had more control but also greater responsibility, since it had to supervise the officials who did the collecting and to pay the soldiers who had once been supported locally.

Administrative centralization could therefore increase the monarchy's direct financial resources while reducing the resources available to particular military commanders. There were consequences for provincial supervision, elite loyalty, and local fiscal pressures. Crown administration required more officials and closer oversight, which strained the capacity of the center, and some scholars note that the use of arrangements such as tax-farming, in which the right to collect revenue was granted for a fee, could raise cash quickly but at a cost to cultivators and to long-term supervision. Commanders who lost revenue assignments had less incentive to maintain troops, and the loyalty of provincial elites depended more on the shah's favor than on their own resources. Local communities experienced these changes through the identity and conduct of the officials who collected taxes, which could vary greatly.

It would be a mistake, however, to claim that revenue centralization necessarily weakened the state. The enlarged crown revenues funded the army that recovered Kandahar in 1649, the buildings of Isfahan, and the administrative apparatus that continued to function through the seventeenth century. The question is not whether centralization was good or bad but how its benefits and costs were distributed and whether the center could manage what it had taken on. The evidence suggests that crown administration remained significant throughout the later seventeenth century, that the boundary between crown and provincial revenue shifted with circumstances, and that a state with a large share of its revenue under direct control could in principle mobilize resources more quickly than one dependent on its governors, though in practice the ability to do so depended on the competence of officials and the efficiency of communications.

Section 66.10

Taxation, Silver, Coinage, Mints, and the Problem of Fiscal Capacity

The Safavid state drew revenue from several sources. The land tax on cultivated land, collected in cash and in kind, was the largest. Customs duties and road tolls produced revenue from commerce, and the Gulf port of Bandar Abbas was a major source of customs revenue. Taxes on crafts, markets, and urban trades added to the total, while extraordinary levies were imposed for particular needs. The crown's own estates, workshops, and in some periods its involvement in the silk trade provided additional income. The system worked through officials at various levels, and its effectiveness depended on their honesty, the quality of records, and the willingness of communities to pay.

The monetary system rested on silver. The principal coin was the abbasi, a silver piece introduced under Abbas I and named for him, valued at 200 dinars in the standard scheme of accounts; large sums were reckoned in tomans, an accounting unit of 10,000 dinars, so that a toman equaled fifty abbasis. Lesser silver coins, copper coins for small transactions, and, more rarely, gold pieces completed the system. Coins carried the name of the shah, a religious formula, the name of the mint, and the date, and were struck in mints in cities across the empire. Because Iran had limited domestic silver sources, the metal came mainly through trade: exports of silk and other goods brought in European and other coin and bullion, which the mints then converted into Safavid coin. The supply of silver therefore depended on the balance of trade and on the movement of precious metals across the wider world.

Maintaining adequate coin circulation was a persistent problem. Coins wore, were clipped, were hoarded, or were exported, and the state periodically reminted them, a process that could bring revenue to the crown but disturb the market. The number and location of working mints, and the weights and fineness of coins, changed over the period. Such changes can reflect policy, the availability of metal, the logistics of reminting, or the politics of local administration, and they have to be interpreted with care. In particular, the closure of a mint is not automatically evidence of economic collapse: it may reflect centralization of production, a reorganization of supply, or a pause in reminting. Nor does a decline in coins struck at one place show a decline in economic activity at large. Historians who use coins as evidence must ask what the change reflects before drawing conclusions.

Expenditures were heavy. The court and royal household, the payment of soldiers and officials, the patronage of religious institutions, and the governance of provinces all drew on revenue, and the crown sometimes faced pressure to find cash for particular purposes. Students should avoid the old assumption that the late Safavid treasury was simply empty. The evidence is mixed and difficult to quantify, and it suggests periods of strain alongside periods of adequacy. A more useful question is whether the state could mobilize its resources quickly when a crisis arose.

The distinction between possessing resources and mobilizing them is important. A state might have substantial wealth in the form of revenue-producing provinces, a treasury in Isfahan, and a large agricultural base, yet be unable to deliver money, supplies, and troops to a distant frontier within the weeks or months in which an emergency required. Revenue collected in provinces had to be forwarded, accounted for, and allocated; payments to troops had to be made on time; and supplies had to be gathered along routes that could be long and sparsely provisioned. These problems of mobilization, as much as the absolute level of resources, are central to understanding how the state responded to the crises of the 1710s. Research on Safavid coinage and administration, including the work of Rudi Matthee and Willem Floor, provides the documentary foundations for this discussion.

Section 66.11

Silk, Commerce, New Julfa, and the Changing International Economic Environment

This section returns selectively to the commercial networks examined in Chapter 63, focusing on their political and fiscal consequences under the later shahs. Raw silk, produced mainly in the Caspian provinces of Gilan, Mazandaran, and Shirvan, was Iran's principal export to the west, and its trade had been a pillar of the crown's finances since the time of Abbas I. Armenian merchants of New Julfa, the suburb of Isfahan to which Abbas had resettled Armenians from the Caucasus, handled much of the trade and extended their networks to Europe, India, and beyond. European East India Companies, chiefly the Dutch and the English, sought to buy silk and other goods at Safavid ports and to ship them to Europe and elsewhere, and they negotiated with the crown for privileges.

Records of the Dutch and English companies provide among the best quantitative evidence for the seventeenth-century Safavid economy, but they must be used with caution. They record purchases and sales by the companies, not the whole of Iran's trade. They indicate that European purchases of Iranian silk grew in the earlier seventeenth century and then fluctuated and, in some periods, declined, as competing sources of silk such as Bengal and China became available and as European demand shifted. Contracts between the crown, Armenian merchants, and the companies changed over time, and the balance of advantage among these parties shifted with them. The companies also brought in silver and other commodities, so that changes in trade affected the supply of metal on which the coinage depended.

The New Julfan Armenians remained important throughout the period. Their networks of commerce, kinship, and credit linked Isfahan to Amsterdam, Venice, and Indian and Southeast Asian ports, and their activities generated revenue for the crown through customs and, according to some accounts, through loans and services. They also faced political and religious pressures that varied with time, as Section 66.16 shows. The Persian Gulf ports, especially Bandar Abbas, were gateways to the Indian Ocean trade, in which Safavid merchants and officials took part alongside Indian, Arab, and European traders. By the early eighteenth century, Omani naval power was growing in the Gulf, a development that, as the next chapters show, had political consequences.

What did these changes mean? Historians distinguish three possibilities: a loss of prosperity, a redirection of trade, or both. Declining silk exports to Europe could mean that Iran was less prosperous, but silk was also sold to markets in India, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia, and domestic silk and textile manufacture served local demand. A decline in the volume of European purchases could reflect the redirection of trade rather than its disappearance. Revenue to the crown from silk and customs might fall without the economy as a whole contracting. At the same time, the crown's finances were connected with the silk trade, and a decline in cash income from that source would have been felt at court, a point scholars emphasize.

The central difficulty is measurement. National economic performance cannot be reliably derived from the surviving records of European companies, which are concentrated on a few ports and commodities, or from the few major trading cities for which evidence exists. Information on rural production, internal trade, and the many small-scale transactions of ordinary life is scarce. Students should therefore treat claims of general economic collapse, or of general prosperity, with skepticism. The evidence supports a more modest conclusion: that the international economic environment changed in ways that put pressure on particular sources of royal revenue, that merchants and officials adapted, and that the consequences for the state's fiscal capacity were real but not easy to measure. This section does not repeat the full trade history of Chapter 63, which should be consulted for the commercial institutions themselves.

Section 66.12

From Repeated Warfare to Prolonged Peace: The Army and Military Readiness

The Safavid army of the later seventeenth century was a composite force. Qizilbash cavalry, organized under tribal chiefs, remained an important element, though their position had diminished since the days when they were the backbone of the state. The standing corps of ghulams, recruited largely from Georgians, Armenians, and Circassians, formed regiments of cavalry and foot soldiers who were paid by the crown and who owed their careers to it. Musketeers (tufangchis) and artillerymen (tupchis) provided firepower, and provincial governors and tribal leaders raised additional levies for particular campaigns. The army was commanded by officers such as the qurchi-bashi, the commander of the royal guard, and the qullar-aghasi, the head of the ghulam corps, and it depended on supply systems that included grain stores, fodder, and transport animals.

Recruitment, equipment, and supply were the work of different institutions. The crown paid the ghulam regiments and musketeers from its revenues, while provincial levies were supported by their commanders' revenue assignments. Equipment, including firearms and artillery, was produced in royal workshops and acquired through trade. Supplying an army on the march was a major undertaking in a country where long stretches of road were sparsely settled, and the Kandahar campaign of 1648–49 showed what could be achieved when the arrangements worked.

After the Peace of Zuhab in 1639, the western front, long the main theater of war, fell quiet. Abbas II's eastern campaign was an exception, and for the next several decades the Safavid state fought few large wars. The effects of prolonged peace on military expenditure, training, command experience, and readiness are matters of debate. On the one hand, less spending on the army meant savings for the crown. On the other, the officers who commanded in the 1710s had less experience of large-scale campaigning than their predecessors, and units that were rarely mobilized might deteriorate. Some observers remarked on gaps between the paper strength of units and the number of men who could actually be put in the field, though such claims are difficult to verify and the textbook gives no figures.

A distinction is necessary between the existence of military forces and their capacity to assemble, supply, and coordinate an effective campaign. The Safavid state in 1700 still had troops, commanders, arsenals, and revenue. What was uncertain was whether those elements could be brought together quickly, paid on time, and led by commanders trusted by the court. That capacity depended on fiscal administration, on appointments, on the quality of communication, and on the political priorities of the court, as the following study shows.

Competing arguments weigh the effects of peace. One holds that prolonged peace weakened the army by reducing experience and discipline and by encouraging economies that left units understrength. Another holds that peace preserved resources and could have supported reform, had the court chosen to use it for that purpose, and that the real problem lay in how the peace dividend was used. A third notes that other armies of the period, including those of the Ottomans and the Mughals, faced their own difficulties, and that military performance cannot be explained by peace alone. These arguments are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence does not allow a decisive conclusion. What can be said is that peace was a condition that interacted with fiscal, political, and organizational factors, and that its consequences depended on what the state did with it.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Military Responsiveness in the Late Safavid State

Long-term causes

The shift of resources from tribal commanders to the crown, the reliance on a standing corps whose pay depended on central revenue, the long peace on the western front after 1639, and a monarchy whose rulers were not trained in command.

Short-term causes

Fiscal strain at particular moments, changes of ministers and commanders, uncertainty about the loyalty and abilities of appointees, and rivalries among court factions over who should lead an expedition.

Immediate trigger or context

The revolt at Kandahar in 1709 and the frontier crises that followed, which required forces to be gathered, paid, and supplied for distant campaigns.

Event or transformation

Safavid expeditions in the 1710s were slow to assemble and poorly coordinated, and several failed, though forces and commanders existed and the state continued to function.

Immediate effects

The loss of control over Kandahar, damage to the prestige of the court, and encouragement to rebels and raiders elsewhere.

Long-term consequences

A state facing multiple frontier crises with limited ability to concentrate its strength, a condition that Chapter 67 shows becoming decisive.

Historical complexity: Military responsiveness was not a single variable. Fiscal policy determined whether troops could be paid; appointments determined who led them; organization determined how quickly they could be assembled; and court priorities determined whether a frontier crisis received attention. No one of these explains the outcome alone, and each could have been different.

Section 66.13

The Center and the Provinces: Governors, Local Elites, and the Problem of Administrative Control

The Safavid government administered territories far from Isfahan through a layered system of officials and intermediaries. At the provincial level stood a governor, a beglerbegi or hakim, who commanded local forces and was responsible for order, and beside him a revenue official, often a vizier, who supervised taxation and accounts. Below them were district officials, tribal leaders, local military forces, and religious notables such as judges and heads of endowments. In towns and villages, headmen and elders served as intermediaries between communities and the state, collecting taxes, mediating disputes, and representing local interests. Merchants and craft guilds had their own forms of organization and their own relations with officials. The effectiveness of royal government depended on how these layers worked together.

Communication was a basic problem. Orders traveled by courier and royal decree, and a message from Isfahan could take days or weeks to reach distant provinces. Tax remittances moved in the opposite direction, accompanied by accounts that had to be checked. The center sometimes dispatched special commissioners to inspect provincial administration or investigate complaints, but such missions were occasional and could themselves become sources of exactions. Appointment disputes, in which rival candidates for a governorship sought the favor of officials at court, were common, and accusations of corruption against governors and tax officials were frequent. The Tadhkirat al-Muluk and the reports of European observers both record the complaints, though neither can be taken as a neutral account, since the former was written to explain how administration should work and the latter often relied on partial knowledge.

The experience of royal government varied across the empire. In provinces under direct crown administration, such as Gilan and Mazandaran on the Caspian coast and, after the early 1630s, Fars, officials appointed by the center collected revenue and the crown's presence was relatively strong. In provinces where powerful local families or military commanders retained substantial influence, the position was different. The Georgian valis of Kartli and Kakheti governed their territories as vassals of the shah, with local dynasties and local armies. Kurdish rulers, among them the Ardalan family in Kurdistan, held hereditary positions under the crown, and Arab tribal leaders in Khuzestan exercised considerable autonomy. In such regions, the shah's authority rested on negotiated relationships, on the appointment of members of local families to office, and on the threat of force, rather than on direct administration.

The documented regional examples show that the effectiveness of royal government varied with region, with period, and with the competence of officials. A province that paid taxes regularly and kept order in one decade might become troublesome in the next if a governor was replaced or a local leader gained strength. The central question is how far the center could correct such problems. In the seventeenth century, the court showed that it could: Abbas II's government supervised frontier provinces effectively, and the Safavids retained the Caucasus and the western plateau. In the early eighteenth century, the ability to respond to local crises came under severe strain, as the cases of Kandahar, Shirvan, and Khurasan show. The differences should not be exaggerated into a picture of uniform weakness. At the core of the empire, in Isfahan, Fars, Yazd, Kashan, and nearby provinces, the evidence indicates that administration, revenue collection, and local order continued to function until the very end. The problem lay chiefly at the frontiers, where the combination of distance, local power, and external pressure was most acute.

Section 66.14

Kandahar and the Eastern Frontier: Afghan Communities, Governors, and Political Alienation

Kandahar was a contested frontier city. Situated on the routes linking the Iranian plateau with Kabul and northern India, and with Herat and Khurasan, it commanded trade in horses, textiles, and other goods, and it lay on the border between the Safavid and Mughal worlds and not far from the Uzbek states to the north. The Safavids had taken it under Abbas I in 1622, lost it to the Mughals in 1638, and recovered it in 1649. Its possession was a matter of strategic and commercial importance, and its political and social arrangements reflected the long history of competing authority.

The population of Kandahar and its hinterland was mixed. Pashtun tribal groupings, chiefly the Ghilzai and the Abdali, formed a large part of it, and so did Persian-speaking townspeople, Qizilbash settlers and officials, Baluch, and others. Students should avoid treating the Afghan communities as politically uniform. The Ghilzai and the Abdali were distinct groupings with rivalries of their own, and each was divided into clans and lineages whose interests could conflict. Some leaders cooperated closely with the Safavid government; others resisted. The Abdali, for instance, supplied cavalry to the Safavids and were centered more on the Herat and western Kandahar regions, while the Hotak, a Ghilzai clan, were based in the Kandahar area. Most Pashtuns were Sunni Muslims, while the Safavid state was officially Shi‘i, a difference of religious identity that could become politically charged but that was not in itself a cause of conflict. Local political structures included tribal chiefs, town notables such as the kalantar, and the Safavid governor with his officers and garrison. Relations among them depended on revenue, military service, and the personal conduct of those in authority.

The conflict that led to the revolt of 1709 centered on Gurgin Khan, the Georgian ruler Giorgi XI of Kartli, who had entered Safavid service, converted to Islam, and been given high responsibilities. Around 1704 the court appointed him governor of Kandahar and commander of its forces, charging him with restoring order in a turbulent frontier region. He arrived with a contingent of Georgian troops, and his methods, as they are described in the sources, were harsh. Accounts allege exactions and abuses by his officers and soldiers, and they describe a deterioration of relations with the local population. Safavid officials who had experience of the frontier might have handled the situation differently, but the court's choice reflected its practice of entrusting provinces to commanders whose loyalty was considered reliable and whose forces could be brought from elsewhere in the empire.

Mir Wais Hotak, the kalantar of Kandahar and an important leader of the Ghilzai, emerged as the principal local figure in conflict with Gurgin. According to the accounts, Gurgin suspected him of disloyalty and had him sent to Isfahan, where he spent time at court, impressed some officials, and was eventually allowed to return. Later tradition adds that he performed the pilgrimage to Mecca and obtained a legal opinion sanctioning resistance to the governor, a story that historians treat with caution because the evidence is late and may reflect later efforts to give the revolt religious legitimacy. On his return, in April 1709, Mir Wais and his supporters killed Gurgin and many of his Georgian troops and took control of Kandahar.

The immediate significance of the revolt was considerable. The Safavid government lost a major frontier city and a base for dealing with the eastern borderlands. The local rulers who replaced the governor, though initially more concerned with local control than with attacking Isfahan, presented a lasting challenge that the court could not remove. The section's purpose is to show how a local political and military conflict, arising from the interactions of a frontier society, an unpopular governor, and a court's decisions, developed into a major challenge to Safavid authority. Whether religion, finance, ethnic differences, or personal conflict mattered most remains debated; the more probable view is that they interacted. The detailed Afghan campaigns, and the subsequent conquest of Isfahan, belong to Chapter 67.

Section 66.15

The Caucasus, Caspian Provinces, Tribal Frontiers, and Regional Security

The geographical limits of late Safavid administration are best understood through a series of regional cases rather than as a single frontier. Each region had its own history, its own elites, and its own relations with the center and with neighboring powers.

In Georgia, the kingdoms of Kartli and Kakheti were ruled by Georgian dynasts who served as valis under the shah. Georgians also formed an important element of the ghulam corps, and members of Georgian noble families served in the Safavid government, as the career of Gurgin Khan illustrates. The relationship between the valis and the court involved appointments, conversion to Islam, and tests of loyalty, and it was vulnerable to the interference of neighboring powers. Shirvan, on the western shore of the Caspian, had a largely Sunni population, and it bordered Daghestan, whose mountain communities had their own leaders and a tradition of raiding. The Safavid administration at Shamakhi dealt with local khans and religious leaders, and the relationship between the governor and the mountaineers was a matter of continuing negotiation. Nobles from Daghestan served at the court, as the vizier Fath Ali Khan shows, so that ties between the center and this region were not only hostile.

The Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, administered directly by the crown, were the heart of the silk economy and a major source of revenue. They were also exposed from the sea, as the raid of Stenka Razin in 1668–69 had shown, because the Safavid state had no meaningful naval presence on the Caspian. Khurasan, in the northeast, was a frontier against the Uzbeks and the Turkmen and contained the shrine city of Mashhad, a major religious and economic center. Herat, to the east, was held by the Abdali Afghans, whose revolt around 1716–17 further strained the state. Along the southern coast and in the Gulf, the Safavids faced challenges from Baluch raiders and from Omani naval forces, who seized Bahrain in 1717. The tribal borderlands of the west and south, home to Kurdish, Lur, Arab, and other groups, were subject to the usual movements of raids, migrations, and shifting alliances, which sometimes drew in the crown and sometimes passed without its notice.

The relationships among local elites, royal representatives, military garrisons, religious communities, and external powers differed from place to place, and generalization is hazardous. Raids, migration, tribal realignment, and rival claims to authority were frequent in the borderlands, and the central government's capacity to respond depended on its ability to coordinate forces, to pay for them, and to find reliable commanders. The Russian state, under Peter the Great, was beginning to take an interest in the Caspian and the Caucasus, and the mission of the envoy Artemii Volynskii to Isfahan in the 1710s shows its growing involvement. The Ottomans, for their part, would be drawn in later.

A comparison shows variety. In regions where royal administration remained functional, such as the central provinces, Fars, and the Caspian crown provinces, the state collected revenue and maintained order. In areas of heightened instability, notably Kandahar, Herat, Shirvan, and parts of the Gulf coast, the state's authority came under serious challenge. The distinction between provincial insecurity and the descent of every region into disorder matters: insecurity at the frontiers did not mean that the whole empire was disintegrating at once. The danger arose from the combination of pressures in several places at the same moment, which stretched the capacity of a court whose decision-making was slow, as Section 66.18 shows.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Beglerbegi, Vali, and Kalantar

Beglerbegi. Definition: A governor-general of a major province, with military and administrative authority. Historical context: Safavid beglerbegis were appointed by the shah and could be dismissed; some held office for long periods. Why it matters: The conduct of governors shaped local experience of royal government. Potential misunderstanding: A beglerbegi was not a hereditary ruler, although families might hold office for generations.

Vali. Definition: A regional ruler who governed a territory in the shah's name, most notably the Georgian valis. Historical context: Valis combined hereditary local legitimacy with the shah's appointment and were expected to provide troops and tribute. Why it matters: The vali system shows how the Safavids ruled frontier regions through negotiated relationships. Potential misunderstanding: The word does not imply independence; the vali's position depended on the shah's confirmation.

Kalantar. Definition: A local headman or notable who represented a town, district, or community and was responsible for collecting taxes and maintaining order. Historical context: Kalantars connected communities to the state and often had hereditary standing. Mir Wais Hotak is described in the sources as holding this role at Kandahar. Why it matters: Kalantars show how authority operated at the level between the central government and ordinary people. Potential misunderstanding: A kalantar served both his community and the state, and the two roles could conflict.

Section 66.16

Religious Policy, Minorities, Social Tensions, and Political Loyalty

The political consequences of religious policy under the later Safavids are among the most debated questions of the period. The dynasty had made Twelver Shi‘ism the state religion in the sixteenth century, and by the later seventeenth century the Shi‘i religious establishment, discussed in Chapter 65, held considerable standing at court and in society. The question for this chapter is not the history of that establishment but how religious policy affected the monarchy's ability to govern and retain support.

Under Sultan Husayn, the court's relationship with religious scholars was close, and a number of measures are associated with the reign. Reports describe orders prohibiting wine and other practices of which the clergy disapproved, as well as pressure on Sufi groups regarded with suspicion by the leading scholars. Sunnis, who formed the majority in some frontier regions, and non-Muslim communities, including Armenians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, were subject to restrictions and, in some periods, to pressure to convert. Policies varied from time to time and from place to place, and administrative enforcement was imperfect: an order issued in Isfahan might be applied energetically in one province and ignored in another, depending on the governor, local conditions, and the willingness of officials to take trouble. Specific episodes, including the dates and extent of orders concerning particular communities, are debated by specialists and are not narrated here; the detailed treatment of religious minorities belongs to Chapter 64 and the institutional history of the ulama to Chapter 65.

Religious policy could affect governance in several ways. It bore on taxation, since non-Muslims paid a poll tax and conversion reduced the number of payers, and since forced or pressured conversion could disrupt the commercial networks that generated customs revenue. It bore on political loyalty, since communities that felt threatened might look elsewhere for protection or withdraw cooperation. It affected communal security, since official hostility could embolden local opponents. And it bore on relations with local populations, since a shah who identified closely with one religious tendency might appear to others as the partisan of a faction rather than the ruler of all his subjects. Sunni communities in Shirvan, Daghestan, and the Kandahar region lived under a Shi‘i state, and how they were treated by governors and tax officials could shape their willingness to support or oppose it.

It does not follow that religious tension was the single cause of the dynasty's failure. The revolt at Kandahar, as noted, had economic, political, and personal dimensions as well as a religious one, and the Lezgi and Daghestani raiders who sacked Shamakhi in 1721 were motivated by plunder and local politics as well as by religious solidarity. Many Sunnis served the Safavid state loyally, and many Shi‘is, including some clerics, were critical of particular policies. The question is how religious and social tensions interacted with other factors: how a court policy, applied by officials of varying competence in regions with their own histories, could alienate potential allies at a moment when they were needed.

For the later Safavids the problem was therefore practical as well as doctrinal. A state that relied on the cooperation of provincial elites, tribal leaders, and local communities could not afford to alienate them without cost, and religious policy was one of several things that bore on that cooperation. Its effect should be assessed alongside fiscal pressure, military organization, and provincial politics, not in isolation.

Section 66.17

Drought, Famine, Disease, and the Vulnerability of the Safavid Economy

Environmental and demographic pressures were part of the setting in which the late Safavid state operated. Agriculture on the Iranian plateau depended on scarce and uneven rainfall and on irrigation drawn from rivers, springs, and underground channels, so that a poor season of rain or snowmelt could reduce harvests across wide areas. Locusts, floods, and outbreaks of disease added to the risks. The sources document shortages and famine episodes in the 1660s, including crises associated with the opening years of Shah Suleiman's reign, as well as locust outbreaks and harvest failures at other times in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. European travelers, Persian chroniclers, and company agents recorded high prices and hardship, though their reports are scattered and are rarely precise about the extent or duration of a crisis. Population figures for the period are highly uncertain, and the textbook does not give them.

A shortage unfolded through a chain of connected effects. A failed harvest raised the price of grain and bread, the staple of most diets. Transport was a limitation: grain moved by camel, mule, and cart over long distances at high cost, so that surplus in one region could not easily relieve scarcity in another, and the movement of grain over any distance in time of need required organization and money. Merchants and officials might hold stocks for sale at higher prices. Disease could follow weakened populations. Governments could respond by remitting taxes, distributing grain, regulating bread prices, or punishing hoarders, and European observers record some such efforts, though the results were uneven and depended on the efficiency and honesty of those who carried them out.

Shortages were experienced differently by different groups. Urban wage earners and the poor of the towns, whose income was fixed or irregular and who bought their bread in the market, felt rising prices most directly. Cultivators lost their harvest and, with it, the means to pay taxes, repay debts, and buy seed for the next season, so that a single bad year could drive families into dependence on creditors or into flight. Pastoral communities, with herds that depended on pasture and water, faced their own hardships in drought. Merchants and landholders with stocks could profit, though a prolonged crisis disrupted trade. State officials and soldiers paid in cash or kind might find their income worth less, or delayed, as revenue fell.

The relationship between agricultural hardship and state revenue was real but not simple. Because the land tax was the main source of income, a poor harvest reduced the revenue that could be collected, and the crown might be forced to grant remissions or accept arrears. At the same time, officials could press harder on a reduced population, and the burden of taxation could rise even as capacity to pay declined. The political response varied. In some places communities bore hardship without revolt; in others, hardship contributed to local discontent and flight. It cannot be assumed that famine everywhere produced an identical political response, and historians need to look at the particular circumstances of each case.

It is also necessary to distinguish the immediate causes of particular shortages from the broader question of institutional decline. A drought is an event of nature, and its effects on a society depend on how that society is organized, but a famine in the 1660s does not by itself explain the crises of the 1710s. What environmental history contributes is a reminder that the state operated in a setting of recurring vulnerability, that its revenue was tied to harvests, and that the experience of ordinary people was shaped by forces over which they and the government had limited control.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life: Shortage and Fiscal Pressure in Town and Village

Note: the following is an analytical reconstruction of patterns documented in many sources, not an account of a particular year or person.

In a Safavid town in a bad year, the daily meal turned on the price of bread. A household of day laborers might spend most of its income on grain, and a rise in price forced choices among food, fuel, and rent. Bakers, millers, and grain dealers were visible figures in this economy, and market officials were responsible for supervising weights and prices, though their enforcement depended on their own interests and on the strength of the authorities behind them. Townspeople watched the supply of grain and the behavior of those who held it, and complaints about hoarding were common in times of shortage.

In the village, the cultivator faced the tax collector as well as the weather. Revenue was assessed on expected production and collected partly in cash and partly in kind, and a poor harvest left a family with less to eat and less to sell while the claims of the state and of creditors remained. Village headmen mediated between households and officials, appealing for remissions or distributing the burden. Some families borrowed, some left for towns, and some joined pastoral kin. Because most written sources were produced by officials, chroniclers, merchants, and foreign visitors, the experience of such families must be inferred from indirect evidence, including tax records, prices, and travelers' remarks about deserted villages and the movement of people.

Section 66.18

The Crisis of the Early Eighteenth Century, 1700–1721

The final two decades before the fall of Isfahan saw an accumulation of political and military difficulties. In the early years of the century the state continued to function much as before, though signs of strain appeared on the periphery, including raids by Baluch groups in the southeast. The decisive turn came with the revolt at Kandahar in 1709, the origins of which Section 66.14 described. The loss of the city was a blow to Safavid prestige and to the security of the eastern frontier, and the court's inability to restore control became one of the central facts of the 1710s.

Safavid responses were not absent. Between 1710 and about 1713, a series of expeditions was sent against Kandahar, the first under Gurgin Khan's nephew, who was killed in the fighting. The expeditions failed. Reasons advanced by historians include difficulties of assembling and supplying forces over long distances, uncertainty of command, rivalries among officers, and the capacity of the rebels to exploit local terrain and local support. Whatever the balance among them, the failures damaged the court's credibility and encouraged others. Mir Wais Hotak held Kandahar until his death in 1715, when authority passed to a relative, and the Safavid state was unable to reverse the loss.

Other pressures accumulated. Around 1716–17 the Abdali Afghans of Herat rose in revolt, adding a second eastern challenge and showing that the Afghan communities, far from acting as a single bloc, pursued their own interests and rivalries. In 1717 an Omani force seized Bahrain, a reminder of weakness in the Gulf. Baluch raids troubled the southeast. In the northwest, tensions in Shirvan and Daghestan grew, and the court's relationship with Georgia was strained. At court, the grand vizier Fath Ali Khan Daghestani, who held office from approximately 1715, was removed and blinded in December 1720 after intrigues in which his enemies at court prevailed, depriving the government of an experienced minister at a time of crisis. In August 1721 Lezgi and other Daghestani forces sacked the city of Shamakhi in Shirvan, an event that exposed the weakness of the northwestern frontier and had consequences for Russian and Ottoman interest in the region.

Regional challenges interacted with the court's capacity to coordinate responses. Each crisis demanded attention, money, and commanders, and the court had difficulty prioritizing among them. Fiscal demands, administrative divisions, and the weakening of royal authority in particular regions combined with problems of mobilization to make a coordinated response hard to achieve. The crises were not part of a single plan or movement; they were separate events with separate causes, such as local grievances at Kandahar, the ambitions of Abdali leaders, the raiding traditions of the Baluch and mountain peoples, and the opportunism of external powers. It was the fact that they arrived within a short span, and that the central administration was slow in responding to each, that gave them their cumulative effect.

By 1721 the state faced a dangerous combination of pressures. It still had its capital, its treasury, a large part of its revenue, and many commanders and soldiers, and the possibility of adaptation, reform, or political survival had not disappeared. Whether the court could have rallied is a question that historians continue to ask; what can be said is that the means of a coordinated response were under strain and the likelihood of one was diminishing. The narrative of how the pressures became decisive, including the Afghan advance on Isfahan, the siege, and the surrender, belongs to Chapter 67, and this chapter does not narrate it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Safavid Institutions from 1629 to 1721

What changed?

  • The balance of power moved from military magnates with independent bases toward the shah, his household, and civil officials.
  • Rulers came to the throne with less direct experience of command and administration than Abbas I had.
  • The long peace with the Ottomans after 1639 reduced the army's experience of large-scale war.
  • The position of crown administration within the fiscal system shifted, and the international trade on which parts of royal revenue relied changed.
  • Pressure on the frontiers intensified sharply after 1709.

What remained continuous?

  • The dynasty held the throne through four peaceful accessions, and its legitimacy rested on kingship, religion, and ceremony.
  • Isfahan remained the capital and administrative center, with its offices, secretariat, and treasury.
  • Twelver Shi‘ism remained the state religion and the religious establishment retained its influence.
  • The core provinces continued to pay taxes and maintain order.
  • Royal patronage of building and the arts continued.

When did change occur, and what was its pace? Change was gradual and uneven, not abrupt. The shift in the balance of power took place mainly in the 1630s and 1640s; effects of the long peace accumulated over decades; the most sudden change came after 1709.

Who experienced change most directly? Court officials and commanders experienced the changes in the balance of power; frontier communities, the people of Kandahar and Shirvan, felt the crises of the 1710s most directly; cultivators felt the effects of fiscal pressure and harvest failure.

Who experienced greater continuity? Residents of the core provinces and of Isfahan saw much that seemed unchanged for most of the period.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against reading a smooth line of decline into the record and supports a view in which long-term vulnerabilities interacted with short-term crises.

Section 66.19

Was Safavid Iran in Decline? Historical Interpretations and Competing Explanations

No section of the Safavid volume carries more analytical weight than this one, because the idea of decline has shaped almost all later writing about the period. The question is not only why the dynasty fell but whether the long interval before the fall is best understood as a process of decay. Historians have offered several explanatory traditions, each with its own evidence and its own limits.

Dynastic and moral explanations attribute the dynasty's fall to weak rulers, court luxury, intoxication, palace seclusion, and personal character. These explanations have deep roots. Many European visitors, and some Persian writers, depicted the later shahs as indolent, devoted to pleasure, and dominated by women, eunuchs, and clerics, and the view was repeated by many later historians. They have the merit of drawing on real observations: several of the shahs did drink heavily, and the court was a place of luxury. But they have weaknesses. They rely heavily on outsiders' accounts and on writers who knew the outcome, they assume that character explains political outcomes, and they cannot account for the achievements of Abbas II, who also drank, or for the long stability of the dynasty.

Institutional explanations emphasize succession practices, administrative centralization, the distribution of offices, and problems of military coordination. They draw on the evidence of appointments, the administrative manuals, and the course of the expeditions of the 1710s, and they have the merit of focusing on structures rather than personalities. Their limitation is that the institutional record is thin and that it is difficult to show that the arrangements which failed in the 1710s were the same that had worked in earlier decades, or to say what alternative arrangements would have been feasible.

Economic explanations emphasize fiscal pressure, changes in silk commerce, coinage and silver supply, and the limits of revenue mobilization. They use coins, company records, and fiscal documents, and they have the merit of linking political capacity to material resources. But, as Sections 66.10 and 66.11 showed, the evidence is partial: changes in mint activity or silk exports do not establish empire-wide collapse, and the surviving records focus on a few ports and commodities.

Social and provincial explanations stress local discontent, religious tensions, frontier political relationships, and regional resistance. They draw on accounts of the revolts, on provincial histories, and on records of local conflict, and they have the merit of looking beyond the court. Their limitation is that sources for provincial and rural society are scarce and often come from outsiders, and that revolts had varied causes which cannot be reduced to a single grievance.

Explanations stressing contingency and external pressures emphasize the interaction of specific decisions, political shocks, frontier challenges, and military events. They treat the fall as the outcome of a particular conjunction and ask what might have happened had a decision gone differently. Their strength is that they take seriously the uncertainty facing historical actors. Their weakness is that they can seem to explain too little, reducing the story to a sequence of accidents, and that it is hard to say which contingencies mattered most.

The different kinds of sources produce different portraits of the same period. Travelers' accounts, such as those of Chardin and Kaempfer, offer vivid description of the court and the capital but reflect the preconceptions of European observers and their limited access to the provinces. Persian chronicles written under royal patronage emphasize ceremony, appointments, and campaigns and seldom criticize the ruler. Company records measure trade, but only of the commodities and ports in which the companies were interested. Administrative documents, such as the manuals and decrees, show how government was supposed to work, though the best-known manual was compiled after the fall. Modern scholarship reads these sources against each other, but it too is shaped by the questions each generation asks.

Older narratives, which present the period as a story of inevitable decay, and more recent scholarship, which emphasizes institutional endurance, adaptation, and the consequences of interacting crises, differ more in emphasis than in the evidence they use. The older view found its coherence in knowledge of the outcome. The newer view, associated notably with Rudi Matthee's Persia in Crisis, questions the assumption that every earlier development was a step on the road to 1722. Students should recognize that the outcome of 1722 does not establish that decline was inevitable, and that a historian who knows the ending is always tempted to read the earlier story backward. The best interpretations attend to evidence of weakness without assuming that the weakness was decisive from the start.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Inevitable Decline or Interacting Crises?

The question: Was the fall of the Safavid state the outcome of a long and essentially continuous decline that began with the death of Abbas I, or the result of a convergence of crises acting on a state that had retained considerable capacity?

Interpretation A — A long decline: Proponents of this view point to the sequence of rulers raised in the palace, the narrowing of the political class, the reliance on ministers and household officials, the loss of military experience in a long peace, and the strains on royal finances. On this view the events of 1709–1722 exposed weaknesses that had developed over a century.
Interpretation B — Interacting crises: Proponents of this view point to Abbas II's achievements, to the long period of peace and administrative continuity, to the late timing of the frontier crises, and to the multiple, largely independent causes of the revolts. On this view the state retained resources and institutions, and its vulnerability emerged chiefly when several challenges struck together and when decisions at court failed to produce a coordinated response.

Evidence: Both sides use the same evidence: chronicles, travelers' accounts, administrative documents, coins, and company records. They differ in which they emphasize and how they weigh the achievements of Abbas II against the failures of the 1710s.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is chiefly one of interpretation and causation: whether the vulnerabilities of the 1710s were the cumulative result of long-term processes or were produced by a conjunction of recent events. It also involves terminology, since “decline” may be used for any weakening or only for continuous deterioration.

Why it matters: The answer affects how earlier reigns are judged. If decline was continuous, the reigns of Safi, Suleiman, and Husayn are stages in a process. If crises interacted, those reigns need to be assessed on their own terms, and the possibility that different decisions might have led to different outcomes remains open.

Section 66.20

Historical Synthesis: The Strengths, Vulnerabilities, and Uncertain Future of the Safavid State

The preceding sections can now be drawn together. The late Safavid state had persistent strengths. It had established institutions: a secretariat, a treasury, a standing military corps, and a network of provincial offices. Its monarchy retained a legitimacy based on dynastic descent, religious association, and ceremony, which was strong enough to carry the throne through four accessions without civil war. It had agricultural and commercial resources, including the revenues of the core provinces, the silk of the Caspian, and the trade of Isfahan and the Gulf. It maintained diplomatic relationships with European, Ottoman, Mughal, and other powers. Its provincial administration worked in many regions, and its patronage of architecture, painting, and learning produced a rich culture. The reign of Abbas II showed that these strengths could be used to effect.

It also had vulnerabilities. The succession system protected sitting rulers but produced heirs with little experience, so the effectiveness of government depended on ministers and household officials whose positions were insecure. The military establishment was large but, after a long peace, unpractised, and its mobilization depended on fiscal arrangements and appointments that could be slow or uncertain. Fiscal coordination was demanding: crown revenue was substantial but had to be collected, moved, and spent across long distances, and it was exposed to shocks from harvests and trade. Provincial relationships rested on negotiation with local elites, and religious and social policies could strain them. Above all, the state's capacity to respond to several crises at once was limited by the way decisions were made at court.

Distinguishing long-term institutional developments from short-term political failures is essential. The shift of power toward the household and civil administration, the pattern of confining princes, and the long peace were long-term developments with both advantages and costs. The appointment of Gurgin Khan, the handling of Mir Wais, the failed expeditions, and the dismissal of Fath Ali Khan were short-term decisions that might have been otherwise. A satisfactory explanation requires both: the long-term developments created conditions in which short-term failures were more likely and more damaging, but they did not dictate the particular failures that occurred.

Which interpretive term is most useful? “Decline” captures a perception, shared by many contemporaries and later writers, of weakening, but it implies a continuous downward path that the evidence does not support, and it tends to import knowledge of the outcome. “Transformation” points to real change in how the state worked, from a tribal-military monarchy toward a household-centered, bureaucratic one, but it is neutral about whether the change was beneficial. “Overstretch” draws attention to the number of frontiers and the strain on resources, though the empire's frontiers were not unusually extended by the standards of its neighbors. “Crisis” names the convergence of pressures in the 1710s without prejudging its causes. No single word suffices. A careful account uses them in combination: a state that had been transformed in ways that left it vulnerable to coordination failures, and that faced a crisis of several simultaneous challenges.

The Safavid monarchy had not simply ceased to exist as a functioning state. Rather, it confronted a convergence of pressures that exposed critical weaknesses in its ability to coordinate an effective defense of imperial authority. The collapse was neither an inevitable consequence of Abbas I's death nor the product of a single ruler's failures; it emerged from the interaction of long-term vulnerabilities and contingent political crises. Chapter 67 examines how those vulnerabilities became decisive during the Afghan invasion and the collapse of Safavid sovereignty, and it begins from the point at which this chapter stops: a state still in possession of its capital and much of its power, but facing dangers it was struggling to meet.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Safavid state between the death of Shah Abbas I in 1629 and the crisis that preceded the fall of Isfahan in 1722, and it has asked whether the concept of decline adequately explains the dynasty's end. Its central argument is that the collapse cannot be explained by the weakness of individual shahs, the influence of religious scholars, fiscal pressure, military change, or provincial rebellion taken alone. These factors interacted over time, their effects were uneven, and the most serious pressures were concentrated in the final dozen years.

Division I showed how the centralization achieved under Abbas I strengthened the monarchy while changing the institutions through which succession and political competition occurred. His successors inherited a highly centralized state without inheriting his personal authority and relationships. Shah Safi's reign saw the elimination of rivals such as Imam Quli Khan and the rise of administrators such as the grand vizier Saru Taqi, with real costs in experience. The losses of Baghdad and Kandahar in 1638 were followed by the Peace of Zuhab in 1639, which gave the western frontier a durable settlement.

The reign of Abbas II (1642–1666) provided the chapter's most important counterevidence to the idea of continuous decline. After a minority marked by the assassination of Saru Taqi, the shah recovered Kandahar in 1649 and held it against Mughal sieges, demonstrating that the inherited institutions could still mobilize and supply an army at a distance. The Abbasnama of Muhammad Tahir Vahid Qazvini, a court chronicle, records the campaign from the court's perspective.

Division II treated the later court as a system rather than a sequence of strong and weak rulers. Suleiman's second coronation in 1668 revealed how kingship was repaired through ritual, astrology, and religious legitimation in a time of calamity. Grand viziers such as Shaykh Ali Khan Zangana, eunuchs, royal women, and palace officials managed government, and delegation could sustain effective administration even as it made the system dependent on particular individuals. Sultan Husayn's reign combined patronage and building with decisions whose practical consequences, rather than his devotion or character as such, shaped events. Succession practices protected sitting rulers but prepared heirs poorly, a point debated in the Historical Debate on palace government.

Division III examined the material foundations of power. The expansion of crown lands under Abbas I increased the monarchy's direct resources while reducing those of military commanders, with mixed consequences that cannot be reduced to a judgment of strength or weakness. Taxation, silver, and coinage supported a monetary system whose changes must be read carefully and cannot be taken as automatic evidence of economic collapse, and the Quantitative Study emphasized the distinction between nominal value, silver content, and purchasing power. Changes in the silk trade and the international economy put pressure on particular sources of revenue, though the evidence does not permit confident claims about national prosperity. The long peace after 1639 had competing effects on the army, and the Cause and Effect study showed that military responsiveness depended on fiscal policy, appointments, organization, and court priorities.

Division IV turned to the provinces. The effectiveness of royal government varied across the empire, with strong direct administration in crown provinces and negotiated relationships in regions ruled through local dynasties and tribal leaders. The revolt at Kandahar in 1709 arose from the interaction of a frontier society composed of distinct Afghan groupings, an unpopular governor in Gurgin Khan, and the court's decisions, and it became a lasting challenge to Safavid authority. The Caucasus, the Caspian provinces, Khurasan, and the tribal borderlands offered different regional cases, and religious policy was shown to be one factor among several affecting loyalty and governance, not a single cause of collapse.

Division V examined environmental stress, the crisis of 1700–1721, and the historiography of decline. Drought, famine, and disease affected different social groups differently and tied the state's revenue to the harvest. In the 1710s a series of failed expeditions, revolts at Kandahar and Herat, the seizure of Bahrain, the fall of an experienced vizier, and the sack of Shamakhi combined to create a dangerous convergence of pressures, with responses slowed by problems of coordination.

The historiographical analysis compared dynastic and moral, institutional, economic, social and provincial, and contingency-based explanations, and showed how travelers' accounts, Persian chronicles, company records, administrative documents, and modern scholarship produce different portraits of the same decades. It argued that the outcome of 1722 does not establish that every earlier development was a step toward inevitable collapse.

The synthesis concluded that the late Safavid state retained substantial political, economic, and institutional resources, but that its capacity to coordinate them came under increasing pressure from court politics, fiscal and military arrangements, provincial tensions, social divisions, and changing external circumstances. Decline, transformation, overstretch, and crisis each capture part of the story, and none suffices alone. The monarchy had not ceased to function; it confronted a convergence of pressures that exposed critical weaknesses in its ability to coordinate a defense. Chapter 67 examines how those vulnerabilities became decisive.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it models a way of thinking about the end of a state that avoids reading history backward. The fall of Isfahan is so dramatic that it invites a story of inevitable decay, yet the evidence shows institutions that worked, a ruler who achieved real victories, and a crisis whose timing and combination were far from preordained. The habit of asking what historical actors knew and what choices they had is a skill that applies well beyond Iran.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the early modern Islamic world and beyond: the difficulty of maintaining coordination in large centralized states, the dependence of royal authority on intermediaries, the relationship between fiscal capacity and military power, and the importance of the frontier as the place where central authority is tested. Comparable problems faced the Ottoman and Mughal states in the same period, and the Safavid case is best understood in that wider setting.

The developments examined here shaped what followed. The collapse of Safavid sovereignty, examined in Chapter 67, opened a period of fragmentation and of new political forces, and later Iranian states inherited both the institutions and the memory of the Safavid experience. The debates about decline, causation, and evidence that this chapter introduces recur whenever historians try to explain how long-established states weaken.

Within the larger textbook, the chapter closes the account of Safavid institutions begun in Chapters 61 through 65 by showing how they fared when the conditions that created them changed. It leaves the reader at the threshold of catastrophe, prepared to read the narrative of Chapter 67 with attention to the many ways in which what happened might have been otherwise.
