## 3. Historical corrections incorporated

### 3.1 Territorial centralization and pastoral society (§§82.1–82.4)

The original province-level explanation, tribal military campaigns, and account of seasonal pastoral economies were retained. The Khuzestan paragraph's mistaken cross-reference to **Chapter 81** for Sheikh Khazal's removal was corrected to **Chapter 80**. The judgment that criticizing settlement itself would be “anachronistic” was replaced by an evidence-based distinction among voluntary settlement, gradual economic change, forced sedentarization, and deportation. No unverified casualty total was added. The differentiated experiences of Qashqai, Bakhtiari, Lur, Kurdish, Turkmen, and other communities remain central to the narrative.

### 3.2 Religious authority and Civil Code (§§82.5–82.8)

The Civil Code passage now identifies its major enactment stages: **8 May 1928**, **26 January 1935**, and **30 October 1935**. The revision distinguishes religious jurisprudence as a source of substantive rules from clerical control of courts and adjudication. The source window identifies Articles **1105** (husband's headship), **1118** (wife's separate property), **1119** (contractual stipulations and possible delegated divorce authority), and historically applicable **1133** (husband's wider divorce power). It expressly warns against reading later consolidated/amended texts as unchanged 1930s enactments. See [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Civil Code”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/civil-code/).

The Marriage Law of **1931** is no longer characterized as only a registration measure. The revised text identifies registration requirements, penalties concerning marriage of girls below thirteen, and the limited contractual protections recognized through Article 4 and subsequently reflected in Article 1119. These protections are not presented as equal rights in marriage or divorce. The revised account clarifies the distinction between religious validity and state recognition of marriage/divorce, and considers disparities in access to courts. See [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Family Law iii: In Modern Persia”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/family-law-iii-in-modern-persia/).

### 3.3 Nationalism, language, education (§§82.9–82.12)

The addition on the government's foreign-name convention distinguishes the **25 December 1934 diplomatic circular** from the requested use of “Iran” and “Iranian” by foreign governments from **21 March 1935**; it does **not** claim that Iranians began calling their country Iran in 1935. Verified through the [British parliamentary explanation of 20 February 1935](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1935/feb/20/persia-official-name). The discussion of Ferdowsi commemorations is linked to government-sponsored historical memory without asserting that Iranian cultural nationalism originated under Reza Shah. The University of Tehran's formal inauguration on **4 February 1935** is identified from specialist scholarship ([*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Education xvii: Higher Education”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/education-xvii-higher-education/)). Civil registration is developed as both an administrative benefit and a means of tracking individuals.

### 3.4 Men's dress laws and the Goharshad crisis (§§82.13–82.14)

The revised text distinguishes the cabinet measure of **26 September 1928**, the parliamentary law of **27 December 1928**, and the cabinet directive requiring brimmed hats on **8 July 1935**. It adds the constitutional dispute over penalties, officially recognized religious exemptions, and material consequences for occupations and communities. This chronology is anchored to [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Clothing xi”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/clothing-xi/) and Houchang Chehabi's 1993 study of dress policy.

For the **12–13 July 1935 Goharshad violence**, the chapter now identifies Ayatollah Sayyed Hossein Tabatabai Qomi's travel and confinement in Tehran, Sheikh Mohammad-Taqi Gonabadi (Bohlul), Governor Fathollah Pakravan, and shrine official Mohammad Vali Khan Asadi. It distinguishes the suppression of the protest from later administrative punishment. The original erroneous implication that Bohlul was arrested by Iranian authorities following the mosque assault has been removed; his escape to Afghanistan and subsequent imprisonment there are separately described. Casualty numbers remain qualified because of discrepant sources. Documentary basis: [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Khorasan xi”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/khorasan-xi-history-in-the-qajar-and-pahlavi-periods/).

### 3.5 Women, unveiling, and differentiated social consequences (§§82.15–82.20)

The **Second Congress of Eastern Women (27 November–2 December 1932)** and its demands for suffrage, equal educational and employment opportunity, wages, and family reform are now explicitly discussed. The **Kanun-e Banovan**, founded in 1935, is treated as both a provider of resources and a constraint on independent organization. Source: [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Feminist Movements iii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/feminist-movements-iii/).

The January **1936** unveiling source window is grounded in three identifiable sources, each distinct in function: Reza Shah's public statement (listed in *Modern Iran: A History in Documents*), the **17 Dey 1314** issue of *Ettela'at*, and the **British Legation Annual Report for 1936, paragraph 242**. The latter documents local enforcement including pressured official receptions, restrictions on transport and services, and police intervention. These are labeled as paraphrases, not invented quotations. The chapter uses the commonly given **8 January 1936** ceremony date while acknowledging 7/8 January descriptions in the scholarly record. Source: [Qatar Digital Library, British Annual Report for 1936](https://www.qdl.qa/en/archive/81055/vdc_100056661167.0x0000b3).

The minority-community analysis now includes the targeted closure of the Baha'i **Tarbiyat school in Tehran in May 1934** and distinguishes it from generally applicable national-curriculum rules; see [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Bahaism x: Baha'i Schools”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bahaism-index/bahaism-x/). The concluding synthesis uses post-1941 relaxations as an example of different degrees of institutional durability without narrating the Allied invasion reserved for Chapter 84.
