## 3. Substantive historical corrections incorporated

### 3.1. The Fifteenth Majles, 1949

**Earlier draft issue:** It erroneously included Mossadegh among the Fifteenth Majles deputies who blocked approval of the Gass–Golsha'iyan Supplemental Agreement.  
**Correction:** The chapter now identifies Hossein Makki and other nationalist opponents as the parliamentary obstructionists, while separately treating Mossadegh's leadership in the **Sixteenth Majles** and its Oil Commission. The agreement's 17 July 1949 date is supported by the detailed *Encyclopaedia Iranica* entry and surviving Persian text.  
**Verification:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Oil Agreements in Iran”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/oil-agreements-in-iran/); [“Golšāʾiān, ʿAbbāsqoli”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/golsaian/).

### 3.2. Legislative stages in March–May 1951

**Earlier draft issue:** It conflated the Senate's 20 March approval of the principle with the date of imperial assent.  
**Correction:** The completed account distinguishes **15 March (Majles), 20 March (Senate), the late-April implementation law, and 1 May (imperial assent)**. It also explains the legal significance of 20 March as a financial/accounting reference date while retaining the distinct assent chronology.  
**Verification:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Oil Agreements in Iran”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/oil-agreements-in-iran/); [UK parliamentary publication of the 1951 Act](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1951/jun/11/persian-oil-nationalisa-tion-act).

### 3.3. Twenty-five percent compensation-security provision

**Earlier draft issue:** It presented twenty-five percent of oil proceeds as though automatically allocated for compensation.  
**Correction:** The final text specifies the **conditional, mutually agreed deposit of up to 25% of current oil revenue after operating costs** as security for possible claims, without treating that amount as a definitive award or automatic transfer to AIOC.  
**Verification:** [Hansard, 11 June 1951, English text of the nationalization legislation](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1951/jun/11/persian-oil-nationalisa-tion-act).

### 3.4. National Iranian Oil Company

**Earlier draft issue:** The draft suggested that NIOC had existed as an embryonic institution before nationalization.  
**Correction:** The revision identifies **1951 as the company's establishment** in connection with the nationalization program and distinguishes it from earlier Iranian state oversight of foreign concessions.  
**Verification:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Oil Agreements in Iran”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/oil-agreements-in-iran/).

### 3.5. The July 1952 uprising and the Majles

**Earlier draft issues:** The draft dated Qavam's departure to 22 July and treated the incompletely elected Seventeenth Majles as an uncomplicated Mossadegh majority.  
**Corrections:** The manuscript now distinguishes **21 July (Qavam's resignation amid the 30 Tir uprising)** from **22 July (Mossadegh's parliamentary renomination and the ICJ judgment)**. The Seventeenth Majles is described as incompletely elected, with shifting political allegiances, rather than a stable and comprehensive mandate.  
**Verification:** [FRUS, July 21, 1952, telegram](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d94); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, elections under Qajar and Pahlavi monarchies](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/elections/elections-i-under-the-qajar-and-pahlavi-monarchies-1906-79/).

### 3.6. July 1952 ICJ decision and oil negotiations

The chapter retains the legally essential distinction between the **International Court of Justice's determination that jurisdiction was lacking** and any hypothetical ruling about the substantive lawfulness of Iran's nationalization or the amount of compensation. The chronology of the Jackson and Stokes missions, World Bank proposals, and Truman–Churchill approaches is developed further. The 1954 post-coup consortium is not introduced as though it were an accomplished 1953 settlement.  
**Verification:** [ICJ case record](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/16); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, oil agreements](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/oil-agreements-in-iran/); [FRUS documentary series](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran).

### 3.7. Oil production and the scale of economic disruption

The revised §86.13 supplies a concrete **production** comparison: approximately **32.5 million metric tons in 1950**, about half that volume in 1951, and approximately **1.03 million metric tons in 1952**. It explicitly distinguishes these amounts from petroleum **exports, government receipts, and overall economic output**. It does not present a speculative precise national unemployment or inflation figure as established fact.  
**Verification:** [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Economy ix. In the Pahlavi Period”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/economy-ix/).

### 3.8. The referendum and constitutional authority, August 1953

The newly written §86.19 addresses the government-supporter resignations, separate Tehran and provincial voting dates (**3 and 10 August**), problems of ballot secrecy, the constitutional controversy surrounding the 1949 amendment to Article 48, and the difference between a reported popular majority and an established legal right to dissolve parliament. Primary Source Window 3 identifies a **July 1953 U.S. intelligence report** as a contemporary source about American observations and expectations, not as a neutral adjudication of Iranian law.  
**Verification:** [FRUS, document 257, July 1953 intelligence report](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d257); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, elections](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/elections/elections-i-under-the-qajar-and-pahlavi-monarchies-1906-79/).

## 4. Scholarly interpretation and instructional balance

**Iranian agency:** Mossadegh is presented as an important constitutional nationalist and an active political decision maker, not as either a flawless democratic emblem or the sole cause of later crisis. Rival deputies, the Shah, ministers, political parties, trade unions, bazaar constituencies, and religious activists have distinct motives and institutional capacities.

**International power:** British interests are differentiated into commercial, financial, diplomatic, and military considerations. The United States is treated as an initially more active mediator whose policies and strategic assessments evolved rather than as one timeless actor. The text distinguishes a buyers' boycott and financial restrictions from a continuous formal naval blockade.

**Regional and social history:** The expanded sections on Khuzestan and Abadan connect technical industry, class, employment categories, migration, households, housing, and local commerce to the national economic conflict. This avoids presenting oil history solely as cabinet diplomacy.

**Constitutional interpretation:** The 30 Tir uprising, emergency decree powers, Seventeenth Majles disputes, and August referendum are examined through both representative-government principles and the frailty of the monarchy's constitutional institutions. The text avoids using knowledge of the later coup as proof that the pre-coup constitutional controversy had only one possible interpretation.

**Source method:** The three Primary Source Windows distinguish documentary paraphrase from literal quotation, identify author/date/source type where applicable, and ask students to consider purpose and evidentiary limits. The chapter does not include fabricated verbatim quotations.
