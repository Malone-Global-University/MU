## 3. Historical fact-check and correction register

1. **Nayiniyappa's dates and charges.** The revision states the documented **6 June 1716** conviction for alleged tyranny and sedition; public whipping, confiscation, imprisonment and family banishment; death in custody in **1717**; conviction overturned in **1719**; and formal rehabilitation in **1720**. Charges and interested accusations are not equated with proven wrongdoing. The difference between hostile judicial records and later petitionary testimony is explicit. Principal source: Danna Agmon, *A Colonial Affair* (Cornell UP, 2017), introduction and ch. 4 (pp. 93–120), https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctt1vjqrc3.
2. **Charter of 1600 and subsequent bullion permissions.** Clarified the **31 December 1600** incorporation and initial fifteen-year exclusive trading privilege, its home-jurisdiction limitations, and the difference between this charter and **later separate bullion-export licenses**. British Library **IOR/A/1/2** identifies the surviving contemporary transcript; the original was lost. British Library's administrative history traces later bullion permissions to separate charter-series items: https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/036-000178601.
3. **William Hawkins.** Corrected the conflation between his arrival at **Surat in August 1608** and his arrival at Jahangir's court at **Agra in April 1609**. See José Vicente Melo, “The English Khan and the Mughal Dominion,” in *Jesuit and English Experiences at the Mughal Court* (2022), https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-96588-4_5.
4. **Fort St George, 1639.** Clarified the role of Francis Day and Andrew Cogan and the Damarla Nayaka authorities, notably Ayyappa and Venkatadri Nayaka; distinguished a locally negotiated trading-site grant from a sovereign territorial transfer. Historical discussion: *History of the City of Madras* (1939), and the Chennai District government's official history, https://chennai.nic.in/history/.
5. **Fort St David acquisition.** Checked **1690**, and preserved the role of Fort St David as an English base during the First Carnatic War. Tamil Nadu Cuddalore District official history: https://cuddalore.nic.in/tourist-place/fort-st-david/.
6. **Portuguese loss of northern Konkan holdings.** Distinguished the Maratha acquisition of the Salsette stronghold of Thane in **1737** and the conquest of Bassein in **1739**, rather than collapsing them into one 1739 operation.
7. **VOC corporate and commercial history.** Maintained the 1602 foundation, Batavia as headquarters from 1619, Indian textile sourcing, and the distinctive intra-Asian trading network; repaired a source window that attributed broad competitive claims to an unread 1741 Van Imhoff memoir. Contemporary 1730 evidence now has a verifiable British Library catalogue reference.
8. **Indian merchant attributions.** Checked the independently operating Surat merchants **Virji Vora** and **Mulla Abdul Ghafur** against Ron Harris, *Going the Distance* (2020), ch. 6, https://academic.oup.com/princeton-scholarship-online/book/34203/chapter-abstract/289624954; and Madras merchant **Kasi Viranna**, active as a chief merchant in the 1660s–1680, against Yogesh Sharm, “A Life of Many Parts,” *The Medieval History Journal*, 1, no. 2 (1998), pp. 261–290, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/097194589800100204. Revised presentation avoids treating Indian firms as European subsidiaries.
9. **Farrukhsiyar's 1717 privileges.** Distinguished the annual **3,000-rupee** arrangement for official Bengal trade from related ministerial instructions on **dastaks** and the conditions governing village leases. The official grant did not transfer political sovereignty. Reference: Banglapedia, “Farrukh Siyar's Farman,” https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Farrukh_Siyar%E2%80%99s_Farman.
10. **Madras urban layout.** Corrected the claim that the European “White Town” lay in a separate outside zone adjacent to the fort. The core Fort St George precinct housed the company and much European residential activity; the extensive Indian town developed around and beyond it. Recognized limits of racialized settlement categories and everyday interactions across the walls.
11. **Treaty of Paris (1763), Article XI.** Compared against the published treaty text. The explicit prohibition on French fortifications and troops applied to the **subah of Bengal**, not identically to every restored French factory. Article XI recognized **Muhammad Ali Khan** for the Carnatic and **Salabat Jang** for the Deccan; the latter formula required qualification because Salabat Jang had already been displaced in practice by Nizam Ali Khan. Treaty text: Yale Law School Avalon Project, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/paris763.asp.
12. **Period boundaries.** Repaired the opening's implication that British activity in Bengal only became political *after* 1763; it was already changing after 1757. Detailed Plassey–Buxar–Diwani developments are properly reserved for **Chapter 62**. The full late-century Company state is **Chapter 63**, and wider Anglo-Mysore/Maratha conflicts are **Chapter 64**.
13. **The Carnatic Wars.** Maintained the 1746–48, 1749–54, and 1756–63 divisions; ensured Anwaruddin Khan, Chanda Sahib, Muhammad Ali, the Nizams' rival claimants, Dupleix, Bussy, Lally, and Coote remain participants in a **multisided** political history, rather than a predetermined two-company military contest.
14. **Social and economic evidence.** Expanded the risks taken by chief merchants, dependence on weaving communities, women's largely undocumented manufacturing contributions, limits of invoices as evidence of welfare, and company dependence on locally recruited sepoys and military-credit relationships. These passages are interpretive historical synthesis, not newly discovered archival testimony.

## 5. Historiography and comparison

The chapter's interpretive center aligns with the outline: European companies entered already complex South Asian markets and increasingly became armed actors through regional politics, capital mobilization and military employment. The revised sections avoid a simple replacement chronology in which Portuguese, Dutch, French, and English institutions each replace their predecessors wholesale. They distinguish the VOC's corporate/intra-Asian network from the English company's growth, and distinguish French political intervention from the durability of Tamil merchants and intermediaries.

The two historical debates remain analytical rather than artificial oppositions. The first balances European organizational advantages against dependence on Indian commercial networks; the second examines contingency in the movement from trade to territorial politics. The chapter recognizes the changing social position of producers and brokers without asserting that all trade was voluntary or that all procurement was coerced. Scholarly foundations include Om Prakash, *European Commercial Enterprise in Pre-Colonial India* (Cambridge UP; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/european-commercial-enterprise-in-precolonial-india/7714E1D87BC2BBB8F4B6CF23BDBFFE17), Tapan Raychaudhuri on Coromandel trade, S. Arasaratnam on merchant–company procurement, Danna Agmon on intermediary power, and scholarship on Indian merchant family firms. These are cited as analytical frameworks rather than invented page-specific quotations.
