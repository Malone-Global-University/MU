## 1. Scope and governing plan

The manuscript was revised from the uploaded Chapter 80 draft (`Pasted text(20261122-054155).txt`), not rebuilt from another chapter. The governing plan is the approved five-division, twenty-section production plan and the authoritative outline's assignment: wartime occupation and famine-related crises, competing imperial forces, oil politics, regional insurgencies, the 1921 coup, Reza Khan's rise and the parliamentary termination of Qajar rule. The original title, sequence, and CSS and script content remain intact. The narrative begins with the institutional inheritance of 1911–1912 and ends at the December 1925 dynastic transition. Coronation and subsequent Pahlavi institutions remain Chapter 81's responsibility; later centralization, developmental policies, and oil nationalization remain assigned to their subsequent chapters.

The consolidated revision addresses the previous scholarly audit, with particular weight on Divisions IV and V and on the broader social and economic history. No new numbered historical sections were introduced. The seven-part retrospective synthesis of the Qajar period is retained and supplemented by developed narrative explaining interactions between diplomatic pressure, finance, reform, parliamentary politics, wartime crisis, and military consolidation. Qajar accomplishments and enduring constitutional ideals are distinguished from dynastic survival.

## 4. Documentary source windows

All three source windows remain in their assigned locations. Their source-based paraphrases are clearly marked, and context, perspective, limitations, and student questions are retained. No unverified quotations have been introduced.

1. **§80.10:** The 9 August 1919 agreements and accompanying letters as transmitted in John W. Davis's dispatch of 2 September 1919, FRUS 1919, II, document 681. The English documentary text was inspected. The window identifies the six clauses, separate loan, and associated letters.
2. **§80.14:** Norman to Curzon, 21 and 25 February 1921, *Documents on British Foreign Policy, 1919–1939*, first series, XII, pp. 729 and 731, identified through Firuz Kazemzadeh's published specialist account. Martial-law measures are documented through Niloofar Shambayati's specialist reconstruction. The archival originals and proclamation text were **not directly inspected**. The student-facing window states the indirect provenance and does not pretend to quote an original. It distinguishes evidence of contemporary British accommodation from a conclusion about all planning responsibility.
3. **§80.20:** Amory's 31 October 1925 telegram, FRUS 1925, II, doc. 541, the December notification reproduced in doc. 549, and Helen Miller Davis's English constitutional compilation (1953; 1970 reprint), p. 122. The latter's rendered page was visually checked against the amended Articles 36–38 and the unchanged wording presented as Article 40. The resolution and the amendment are distinct acts; a diplomatic translation is not a full parliamentary transcript.

## 8. Final editorial disposition

**Approve for first-edition textual publication and editorial lock.** The revision satisfies the governing chapter structure and substantive narrative responsibilities. Its constitutional discussion now correctly presents the October resolution and the December amendments as distinct acts and does not assume an amendment procedure existed in the 1906–1907 texts. The humanitarian history recognizes uncertainty without minimizing documented suffering; the political account preserves Iranian agency while acknowledging documented foreign assistance and coercion.

No further general rewriting or expansion is recommended. Future intervention should be limited to demonstrated factual errors, stronger documentary evidence, or publication-critical issues. The chapter closes Volume VI and establishes the historical basis for Volume VII without taking over the assigned narrative of the Pahlavi state.
