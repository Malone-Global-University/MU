## 3. Historical accuracy and corrections

**Caste and precolonial history (§§73.1–73.2):** The chapter avoids both absolute colonial invention and timeless/uniform continuity. Varna is distinguished from jati, and social status is considered through local evidence, caste-based exclusion, occupation, kinship, political authority, ritual and change over time.

**Census (§§73.3–73.4):** The earlier counts of approximately 1867–1872 are distinguished from the broadly synchronous 1881 census. The 1901 census date is separated from the 1903 publication of its principal all-India report. Both H. H. Risley and E. A. Gait appear in source attribution; social-precedence ranking is explicitly not assumed uniform across provinces. The census study is anchored to *Census of India, 1901*, Vol. I Part I and Vol. I-A tables XIII and XVI, not a generic unnamed volume.

**Ethnography (§§73.5–73.6):** William Crooke is not presented as entirely rejecting anthropometric methods; his use of measurement and interpretation is distinguished from Risley's racial conclusions, informed by C. J. Fuller's 2017 work. Colonial racial science is consistently described as historical ideology rather than valid biology.

**Tribe, coercion and recruitment (§§73.7–73.9):** The chapter differentiates diverse communities and regional histories instead of treating 'tribe' as a uniform social fact. The Criminal Tribes Act is identified as **Act XXVII of 1871**, initially with substantive provisions applying to **Punjab, the North-Western Provinces and Oudh**. Statutory notification/registration, administrative enforcement, and the ideology of inherited criminality are not conflated. Retrospective 'denotified community' terminology is clearly located in post-independence history. The martial-races ideology is treated as a developing, non-biological justification for recruitment policy.

**Race and law (§§73.12–73.14):** Racial privilege is shown across institutions while preserving distinctions among formal regulations and social practices. The Ilbert Bill study identifies the **1884 jury compromise allowing European British defendants to claim juries with at least half European members** in relevant cases. Gendered rhetoric is discussed as historical argument, not reproduced as fact.

**Education (§§73.15–73.17):** Dates checked for 1835 Macaulay Minute, 1854 Wood Despatch, 1857 three presidency universities, Punjab University 1882, Allahabad 1887, Hunter Commission appointment 1882 and report 1883. Universities' initial affiliating/examining role is distinguished from later teaching functions. Unequal access is presented as a question of institutions, finances, caste, gender and geography.

**Print (§73.18):** The 1878 law is identified as **Act IX of 1878** and linked to the stable WIPO Lex record; its differences in linguistic and territorial application are qualified. Its repeal in 1882 is distinguished from an end to all press controls.

**Historiography (§73.19):** Interpretative approaches associated with Cohn, Dirks, Bayly and Fuller are introduced with bibliographic references; they are identified as broad scholarly emphases, not invented quotations or fictitious page claims.
