## 4. Material historical findings and resolutions

**Famine of 1769–1770.** The original 80-word paragraphs were not wrong merely because short, but could not explain the interaction of rainfall failure, household vulnerability, grain markets, distribution, revenue demands and inadequate administrative coordination. The final distinguishes climatic trigger from social mechanisms, identifies differences between western and eastern Bengal, refuses to present a contemporary claim of one-third population mortality as census fact, and avoids presenting either deterministic environmental explanations or a single-cause Company-conspiracy theory as settled. John R. McLane, Baijayanti Chatterjee, Tirthankar Roy and John Lidwell-Durnin are used with attention to their varying sources and interpretive emphasis. No province-wide numerical death toll is asserted as established.

**Richard Becher correspondence.** A dated 20 November 1770 account is identifiable from Lidwell-Durnin's 2026 study, including 11,500 rupees for rice charity, 500 for removing bodies, and 3,000 for feeding elephants. These are expenditures reported for Becher's area and administrative record, not a provincial famine-relief total; the surviving account has not been collated against a manuscript image. Source Window 1 is now next to §63.3 on famine responsibility rather than displaced to the end of §63.7.

**1771 versus 1772.** The Court of Directors' August 1771 instruction to 'stand forth as diwan' is distinguished from Hastings's actual implementation in 1772. The reforms expanded direct supervision but did not eliminate dependence on Indian officials. The fiscal narrative differentiates nominal assessment, actual collection, revenue-farming obligations and cultivating households' rents.

**Justice Plan and differentiated courts.** The Committee of Circuit's 1772 justice plan is anchored to an identified British Library item **IOR/H/420, pp. 31–55**, described in the catalogue as a printed plan enclosed in a 3 November 1772 Bengal letter. Published scholarship also associates the plan with 15 August 1772 proceedings. The final distinguishes the Company's 1772 civil/criminal adalats, the **royal Supreme Court established in 1774**, the **1781** jurisdictional adjustment, and the judicial reform associated with **Regulation II of 1793**. Source Window 2 is correctly integrated in §63.7. Nandakumar's trial and execution are recorded without presenting the contested allegation of political conspiracy as proven.

**British parliamentary supervision.** The Regulating Act of 1773, executive arrangements effective 1774, Pitt's India Act of 1784 and Board of Control, and strengthened executive powers in 1786 are distinguished. The Company was not instantaneously made a directly governed Crown colony. British Parliament's own public historical explanation and specialist research support the main institutional sequence.

**Burke and Hastings.** The published first-day opening speech of **15 February 1788** and Hastings's **1795 acquittal** are checked. Burke's intervention is presented as prosecution advocacy and constitutional argument, not a judicial finding. The reference to P. J. Marshall in Debate 2 is deliberately restrained; his scholarship cannot responsibly be condensed to a simple endorsement of British administrative improvement.

**Land revenue and Cornwallis.** The 1789–1790 decennial arrangements conditionally contemplated perpetuity, but an unconditional, formal fixing of the public land assessment was proclaimed on **22 March 1793** with the directors' approval. Regulation I's original 1793 historical clauses are not confused with later-amended online reprints showing inconsistent dataset metadata. The final clearly distinguishes fixed public revenue from landholders' power to charge rents. It does not claim that all cultivators' rights disappeared instantaneously or that the settlement covered all India.

**Estate sales and the 'sunset' narrative.** The final distinguishes the general potential for estate sales due to arrears from especially stringent later enforcement provisions associated with **Regulation VII of 1799**. No unqualified claim assigns the mature 'sunset law' in its later form directly to the March 1793 proclamation.

**Courts, knowledge, and army.** Regulation II's explicit argument for depriving revenue officers of judicial authority is reflected. The 1793 legal settlement is treated as institutional aspiration rather than proven equal access to justice. Indian scribes, qanungos, legal specialists, bankers, creditors and sepoys are given agency; military-financial expansion is analyzed without duplicating the wars of Chapter 64.
