## 3. Substantive corrections and expansions

1. **1925 constitutional transition (§81.2):** The manuscript explains the 31 October Majles action and December constituent-assembly amendments as distinct steps. Articles 36–38 are now analyzed separately: the dynasty and hereditary crown, the criteria and exclusions for the heir, and regency arrangements. The date difference between scholarly accounts identifying 11 December and an English constitutional text identifying 12 December remains explicitly qualified. The absence of an ordinary amendment procedure in the original constitutional settlement is explained.
2. **1926 coronation (§81.3):** The ceremony of 25 April 1926 at Golestan Palace is treated as a modern act of dynastic legitimation using adapted Iranian royal traditions. Self-coronation is not represented as a wholly unprecedented invention, and Sasanian imagery is distinguished from direct institutional continuity.
3. **Army and conscription (§§81.5–81.8):** Expanded the amalgamation of Cossack and Gendarmerie traditions, command structures, logistics, provincial garrisons, coercive functions, and policing. The 6 June 1925 conscription law is identified with age twenty-one, two years of active service, reserve liability and the historically changing exemption regime. The treatment of households, religious scholars and minority communities is differentiated. The later SAVAK is not projected backward into this period.
4. **Bureaucracy and state-builders (§§81.9–81.10):** Expanded ministry practice, record keeping, appointment politics, center–province communication, and the distinct contributions of Teymurtash, Davar, Foroughi, Firuz, Hedayat and Taqizadeh. Institutional achievement is not attributed exclusively to the Shah.
5. **Bank Melli and fiscal authority (§81.11):** The May 1927 enabling legislation and September 1928 commencement of operations are distinguished, with Kurt Lindenblatt and Otto Schniewind placed in their documented roles. The later acquisition of banknote functions is not conflated with the bank's founding, and the institution is not anachronistically presented as the later central bank.
6. **Judicial sovereignty (§81.12):** The 1927 diplomatic notice and abolition of capitulatory arrangements effective 10 May 1928 are distinguished. Davar's institutional reforms are developed without prejudging the substantive religious and civil-law material assigned to Chapter 82.
7. **Majles and cabinet (§§81.13–81.14):** The tightening control of elections beginning particularly with the Seventh Majles in 1928 is distinguished from the later near-complete subordination of parliament. Court-ministry gatekeeping, cabinet expertise, and the growing incentives against candid advice are developed.
8. **1931 anti-collectivist law (§81.15):** The source window identifies a published English archival rendering of the law and distinguishes Article 1's imprisonment terms for specified association offenses (three to ten years) from Article 5's propaganda penalties (one to three years). The archival rendering's varying Gregorian date references are not falsely reconciled as a single certain day.
9. **Political repression (§81.16):** Modarres's killing in 1937, Teymurtash's death in custody in 1933, Davar's suicide in 1937, and Firuz's murder in custody (1938 in an established specialist chronology) are distinguished as cases with different evidentiary histories. Teymurtash's death is no longer framed as an equally balanced dispute between natural causes and deliberate killing. Unverified details of the manner of death are not asserted as fact.
10. **Dynastic succession (§81.19):** Added the 1938–1939 adjustments involving Princess Fawzia's Iranian legal status. Corrected the misleading implication that the government possessed no working succession mechanism in 1941; Foroughi helped accomplish a rapid constitutional accession of Mohammad Reza Shah, even though the political system depended heavily on the previous monarch.
11. **Final synthesis (§81.20):** Retains the distinction between increasing state capacity and diminishing representative accountability, and evaluates the role of a collaborative founding elite, administrative continuity, coercion and political legitimacy.

## 4. Primary-source verification record

### Primary Source Window 1 — December 1925 succession amendments

**Document:** Amendments to Articles 36–38 of the Supplementary Fundamental Law.  
**Text consulted for verification:** Iran Data Portal, `https://irandataportal.syr.edu/wp-content/uploads/iran1925.pdf`; also *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Constitutional Revolution iii. The Constitution,” `https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/constitutional-revolution-iii/`.

The new window contains a documentary paraphrase of each article, the institutional context, questions about law versus consent, and a clearly described limit concerning the assembly's political freedom. The chapter does not reproduce unverified Persian quotations. Some secondary sources date the constituent assembly action to 11 December; the translated legal text identifies 12 December. Both conventions are acknowledged.

### Primary Source Window 2 — 1925 compulsory military service law

**Document:** Majles compulsory conscription enactment of 6 June 1925.  
**Specialist verification:** *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, “Army vi. Pahlavi Period,” `https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/army-vi/`; “Iran ii. Iranian History (2), Islamic Period,” `https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/iran-ii2-islamic-period-page-5/`; Stephanie Cronin, “Conscription and Popular Resistance in Iran, 1925–1941,” *International Review of Social History* 43 (1998): 451–471.

The revised window is labeled as a **paraphrase of documented provisions**, not a reproduced passage from a fully collated Persian statute. It correctly specifies the age and active-duty duration and notes later amendments, avoiding the original draft's unresolved instructions to check these facts.

### Primary Source Window 3 — June 1931 state-security/anti-collectivist law

**Document:** The 1931 legislation criminalizing certain associations and political propaganda.  
**Archival English rendering:** Library of Congress, compilation of translated state-security and penal provisions (Iran section, Articles 1–7), `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/llglrdppub/2019668938/2019668938.pdf`.

The relevant English rendering includes association and propaganda offenses with separate penalty ranges. The compilation dates the underlying law and commencement differently in Gregorian notation; the chapter uses **June 1931** rather than claiming a day that all source editions support. The document is a **later English compilation**, not a critically collated original Persian edition. The window is therefore explicitly a paraphrase, with provenance and limitations visible to students.

**Source-policy finding:** No invented attributed quotations were inserted. The first source window is grounded in an identified constitutional text; the second relies on documented statutory provisions as presented in specialist historical scholarship; the third relies on an identified archival English rendering. A definitive Persian-text collation was **not** performed and is not claimed.
