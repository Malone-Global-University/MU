## 3. Historical and methodological verification

### 3.1 Indus discovery and historiography

- **Confirmed:** The public announcement of the Indus Civilization appeared in *The Illustrated London News* on **September 20, 1924**. The chapter now gives the exact article title, **“First Light on a Long Forgotten Civilization,”** with a direct link to the transcription and related illustrated issue.
- **Confirmed with necessary restraint:** Sahni's and Banerji's early fieldwork, the colonial Archaeological Survey's institutional role, the post-1947 division of sites and collections, and the continued undeciphered status of the script. The account does not assign the civilization to a modern national, religious, or linguistic identity on insufficient evidence.
- **Methodological standard maintained:** The distinction between archaeological artifacts, modern political interpretations, and unresolved linguistic claims is preserved.

**Principal record:** [John Marshall, 1924, original text transcribed by Harappa.com](https://www.harappa.com/content/first-light-long-forgotten-civilization); [archive context and illustrations](https://www.harappa.com/content/tabloid-illustrated-london-news).

### 3.2 Indo-Aryan origins and scientific dating

The chapter's distinction among comparative linguistics, archaeological context, population genetics, and historical language is defensible. It correctly avoids treating ancient genetic ancestry as a direct certificate of language, ethnicity, or modern political identity. The Indus script's language is left unresolved.

**Mehrgarh correction:** The 2025 radiocarbon research concerns *tooth enamel sampled from 23 Neolithic graves* and proposes a much later start for the excavated aceramic Neolithic **cemetery**, rather than establishing the date of every deposit or activity at the entire site. The revised chapter now makes that distinction when referring to changes in the site's chronology.

**Research:** [Mutin et al., “New radiocarbon dates of human tooth enamel reveal a late appearance of farming life in the Indus Valley,” *Scientific Reports* (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92621-5).

### 3.3 Independence speeches and Pakistan's founding principles

- **Confirmed:** Nehru's midnight Constituent Assembly address of August 14–15, 1947 contains his familiar short phrase **“tryst with destiny.”**
- **Confirmed:** Jinnah's August 11, 1947 address speaks of freedom of worship and religion's relationship to the business of the state. The short excerpt used in the manuscript has been retained with links to a text of the speech.
- **Confirmed:** Pakistan's 1949 Objectives Resolution affirms divine sovereignty while providing for representative exercise of authority and specifying protections for minority religious practice.
- **Interpretive caution maintained:** A founding address is evidence of declared ideals, not automatic proof of later governmental policy or of a wholly settled vision of nationhood.

**Documents:** [Nehru's address](https://nehruarchive.in/documents/a-tryst-with-destiny-aug-14-15-1947-j9le4z); [Jinnah's address](https://www.pakistani.org/pakistan/legislation/constituent_address_11aug1947.html); [Objectives Resolution](https://www.pakistani.org/pakistan/constitution/annex.html).

### 3.4 Bangladesh's 1971 death toll

**Correction incorporated:** The manuscript previously placed Bangladesh's official figure of three million alongside lower estimates without clearly distinguishing counting methods. The revised paragraph distinguishes direct violent deaths from broader war-related excess mortality, including illness, deprivation, displacement, and refugee-camp deaths. These categories do not share a single denominator, and a comprehensive figure remains disputed.

For context, a 2025 peer-reviewed study attempted to estimate excess mortality among 1971 refugees in India. Its results should be understood as a category-specific scholarly estimate rather than as a definitive replacement for every estimate of wartime deaths.

**Research:** [“Death toll among the Bangladeshi refugees of the 1971 war,” *PLOS ONE* (2025)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0320760).

### 3.5 Sri Lanka's final-war civilian casualties

**Correction incorporated:** A 2011 UN panel stated that the available record did **not rule out** civilian deaths on the order of up to 40,000 during the final phase of the war. It did not establish 40,000 as a verified number. The final manuscript now states the panel's finding and its insistence on a proper investigation, as well as the Sri Lankan government's dispute of the larger estimates.

**Document:** [UN Secretary-General's Panel of Experts on Accountability in Sri Lanka, report section III (2011)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Report_of_the_Secretary-General%27s_Panel_of_Experts_on_Accountability_in_Sri_Lanka/III_Nature_and_Scope_of_Alleged_Violations).

### 3.6 Nepal's armed conflict and other contested memories

**Clarification incorporated:** Approximately 17,000 deaths in Nepal's 1996–2006 armed conflict are described as a **commonly cited estimate**, not as an exact audited total. The account recognizes different memories among monarchists, democrats, Maoists, and marginalized communities. Bhutan's Lhotshampa refugee history and Sri Lankan and Bangladeshi commemorative disagreements remain differentiated rather than collapsed into one interpretation.

### 3.7 NCERT textbook revisions

**Correction incorporated:** The text now distinguishes the curriculum-rationalisation process announced in **2022** from the appearance of revised textbooks in **2023–24**. It identifies the removal or shortening of *particular passages* on Mughal history, 2002 Gujarat violence, Hindu extremists' hostility toward Gandhi, and the RSS ban, rather than implying that every account of Gandhi's assassination disappeared from every textbook. It retains the institution's stated post-pandemic rationale and the contemporary scholarly and political criticism.

**Source:** [*The Indian Express*, NCERT revisions and publication sequence (2023)](https://indianexpress.com/article/education/in-ncert-textbook-prune-mughal-era-delhi-sultanate-emergency-impact-and-2002-riots-8538769/).

### 3.8 Ayodhya and archaeological evidence

**Correction incorporated:** The revised section separates B. B. Lal's excavations from subsequent interpretations of pillar-base features, and distinguishes the 2003 ASI report from what the Supreme Court's **November 9, 2019** judgment actually found. The Court accepted evidence of an earlier structure of non-Islamic, Hindu religious origin but did **not** establish that a temple had been demolished specifically to build the mosque. It condemned the 1992 demolition and assessed the title dispute under legal principles, not archaeology alone.

**Document:** [Supreme Court of India, *M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das*, 2019 judgment](https://www.scobserver.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Ayodhya_Final_Judgment3.pdf) (linked for readers; this audit's interpretation also relied on independently accessible judgment summaries).

### 3.9 Keeladi and the interpretation of Tamil antiquity

**Correction incorporated:** A new substantive paragraph in §111.15 distinguishes:

1. Dating of the excavated occupation and sampled deposits;
2. Dating and archaeological context of individual inscribed sherds;
3. Inferences about the development of Tamil-Brahmi writing; and
4. Modern political and institutional interpretations of those findings.

The May 2025 ASI request for revisions to the excavation report and the excavator's defense of his chronology are recorded as a methodological and institutional dispute, **not** as automatic proof that the discoveries or proposed dates are invalid.

**Sources:** [Tamil Nadu Department of Archaeology, Keeladi](https://www.tnarch.gov.in/keeladi); [*The Indian Express*, ASI request for the excavation report's revision (2025)](https://indianexpress.com/article/cities/chennai/asi-archaeologist-keeladi-report-citing-need-authentic-data-10024141/).
