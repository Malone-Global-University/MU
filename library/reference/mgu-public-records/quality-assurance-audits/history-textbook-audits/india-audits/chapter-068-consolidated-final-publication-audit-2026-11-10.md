## 4. Principal historical verification and amendments

**Vellore (1806).** The rising occurred on **10 July 1806**. The narrative distinguishes changes to dress and religious marks, fear of conversion, and the disputed role of Tipu Sultan’s family. British Library **IOR/H/507** and **IOR/H/510** are authentic catalogue records; their existence does not authenticate every conclusion reached by investigators. No unverified casualty figure was inserted.

**Barrackpore (1824).** The narrative identifies the **47th Bengal Native Infantry**, service and transport grievances connected to the Burmese expedition, and armed suppression. A verified British Library collection **IOR/L/MIL/5/388, Coll. 115**, is paired with the **22 March 1827 Hansard** debate. Opposing parliamentary arguments are distinguished rather than merged into a neutral factual summary.

**Southern Polygar Wars and Marudhu (1801).** The separate 1799 campaign associated with Kattabomman and the wider **1800–1801** resistance of the Marudhu leaders are differentiated. The 1801 proclamation is discussed through a **later source-transmission study**, not reproduced as an authenticated original-language quotation. The British Library’s **IOR/L/MIL/17/3/337** establishes the existence of contemporaneous Madras Army general orders for March–August 1801; individual pages remain uncollated. Modern historical writing identifies the proclamation tradition, but cannot alone certify a precise original text.

**Paika (1817).** The uprising is placed after the **1803** conquest of Odisha, with attention to Khurda, Bakshi Jagabandhu, Kandha participants, revenue and military service, and prolonged negotiations. His surrender is situated in **1825**. The chapter rejects the unsupported universal assertion that 1817 was India’s first resistance to British rule.

**Kittur (1824–1831).** Rani Chennamma’s dispute concerns Company interference in a succession involving an adopted heir. The narrative avoids falsely applying Dalhousie’s later formally associated **Doctrine of Lapse** to 1824. Chennamma’s imprisonment and death in **1829** and Sangolli Rayanna’s execution in **1831** are distinguished.

**Bhil, Bhumij, and the Jungle Mahals.** The chapter avoids constructing a single uprising from separate confrontations. The Company’s pejorative **“Chuar”** is explicitly analyzed as a colonial label rather than used uncritically as an ethnic identification.

**Kol (1831–1832).** The movement is located in **Chotanagpur** and related to land alienation, outsider intermediaries, police, revenue, and distinctive communities. The Bihar State Archives confirms the existence of a document titled **“Insurrection in Chota Nagpur, 1832.”** The chapter does not pretend its unexamined leaves contain a specific quotation or statement. The event is not confused with the later Munda Ulgulan of 1899–1900.

**Khasi (1829–1833).** The road dispute linking **Sylhet and Assam**, U Tirot Sing of Nongkhlaw, the 1829 outbreak, and subsequent confinement are distinguished from later Jaintia and Garo conflicts. Company rule in the Khasi Hills is not portrayed as one uniform, immediate annexation of all Khasi polities.

**Santhal Hul (1855–1856).** The chapter identifies Damin-i-Koh, the Bhognadih assembly of **30 June 1855**, Sidhu and Kanhu and the roles of Chand and Bhairav, and agrarian, administrative, and religious explanations. It does not invent casualty totals or dismiss Thakur as merely a deceptive rhetorical instrument. The recorded **Kanhu examinations from December 1855** are treated as interrogation/translation-mediated sources: published headings and the dates of the underlying inquiries require witness-by-witness comparison. The **21 July 1856** parliamentary discussion corroborates that British critics themselves acknowledged moneylender, police, and revenue abuses.

**Faraizi and Mappila.** Haji Shariatullah’s return in **1818**, his death in **1840**, and Dudu Miyan’s agrarian emphasis are supported by scholarly accounts. Mappila outbreaks beginning in **1836** are not conflated with the later uprising of **1921**, and the interaction between religion and agrarian conflict is retained.

**Historical boundaries.** The chapter stops before the actual events of 1857 and avoids describing local or dynastic opposition as a coordinated modern national independence movement. It also rejects the opposite claim that opposition to the Company could have no anticolonial content before a modern nationalist organization existed.

## 5. Primary-source windows: what is and is not verified

The five required **source-study windows** exist and are anchored to identifiable documents, collections, documentary editions, or an openly marked original-transmission problem. **Their interpretative excerpts are editorial paraphrases, not directly collated verbatim primary-source translations.** One window (Marudhu) includes a verified contemporary orders collection as a comparator but the original proclamation has not been authenticated or transcribed. Consequently, the edition is a verified *textual/source-method teaching edition*, **not** an original-document facsimile edition and **not** a promise of five page-collated primary-source extracts.

1. **Marudhu and the proclamation, June 1801** — later government-published discussion, plus British Library **IOR/L/MIL/17/3/337**, contemporaneous Madras general orders; original proclamation witness not collated.
2. **Vellore, 1806** — British Library **IOR/H/507**, **IOR/H/510**, original record collections catalogued, no manuscript-page quotation.
3. **Barrackpore, 1824** — British Library **IOR/L/MIL/5/388, Coll. 115**, plus verified published **Hansard, 22 March 1827**.
4. **Chotanagpur, 1832** — Bihar State Archives’ identified insurrection record; catalogue-level authentication only.
5. **Santhal Hul, 1855–1856** — Kanhu interrogation tradition documented in Tarapada Ray’s edition and examined critically by Peter B. Andersen; distinguish publication metadata from direct manuscript collation.

All five source windows include contextualization, a clear paraphrase/transmission disclaimer, analysis, limitations, and four student questions. The documentary distinction is material and should be retained if reproducing or excerpting these features on the live website.
