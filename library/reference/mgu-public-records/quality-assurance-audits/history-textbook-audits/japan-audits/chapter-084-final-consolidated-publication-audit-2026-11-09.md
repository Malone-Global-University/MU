## 3. Historical and source-critical corrections applied

1. **Constitutional drafting:** distinguishes the Matsumoto proposal, private constitutional drafts, February MacArthur Notes, Government Section drafting, Japanese legal revision, and Diet amendments. Source basis: National Diet Library document records.
2. **Article 9:** distinguishes both paragraphs; identifies the Ashida Amendment as an addition at the start of paragraph two, not the invention of the entire clause. Later rearmament debates are only foreshadowed.
3. **Women’s suffrage:** dates enactment to December 17, 1945; records 39 female representatives out of 466 in the April 10, 1946 election (8.4%); makes clear that suffrage predated constitutional effectiveness.
4. **Land reform:** distinguishes the first 1945 reform from October 21, 1946 Law No. 43; explains purchase, compensation, resale, local farmland committees, and regional exceptions. Approximate tenant-cultivated land shares of 46% before reform and 9% by 1955 are explicitly about arable area, not the number of farm households.
5. **Education:** verifies the March 31, 1947 enactment and April implementation of the basic education laws; distinguishes junior-high implementation in 1947, upper-secondary in 1948, and university reorganization in 1949.
6. **Family law and labor:** distinguishes the 1947 promulgation and 1948 operation of revised Civil Code family rules; explains labor rights and MacArthur’s January 31, 1947 ban on the planned February 1 strike.
7. **Political inclusion:** distinguishes the December 1945 suspension of voting rights for former colonial subjects, May 1947 alien-registration treatment, and the 1952 nationality consequences of Japanese administrative interpretation. Okinawa’s separate occupation administration and the continuing inequality faced by Ainu and Burakumin communities are addressed in depth.
8. **Source windows:** all three are explicitly labeled *editorial paraphrases*, not purported original quotations or facsimile-collated translations. A documented Home Ministry voting poster replaces a hypothetical everyday-life example.
