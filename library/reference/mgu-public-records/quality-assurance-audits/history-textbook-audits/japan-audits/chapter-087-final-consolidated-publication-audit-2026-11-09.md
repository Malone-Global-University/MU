## I. Editorial decision and scope

The chapter fulfills the approved five-division, twenty-section production plan. The chapter's principal purpose is to explain the interaction of organized crime, the *bakuto* and *tekiya* legacies, postwar informal markets, contested market space, demobilization, Japanese and Allied enforcement, former colonial subjects, and the emerging postwar underworld in the years 1945–1952. It does **not** infer that all informal vendors were gang members, assign collective criminality to Korean, Taiwanese, or Chinese communities, or project the later structure of the yakuza uncritically backward into 1945.

Chapter 85 owns the household-survival and informal-exchange history; Chapter 86 owns intimate relations and sexual exploitation under occupation; Chapter 87 focuses on coercive territorial and commercial authority, policing, violence, and criminal organizations. It foreshadows the Cold War and economic recovery only where those developments explain the transition into the early 1950s.

The first edition is a fully written historical-text HTML chapter with explicit source and documentation limits—not an assertion of exhaustive multilingual archival-facsimile, original manuscript, or original photographic-negative collation.

## IV. Key historical and documentary verification

### A. The difference between informal commerce and organized crime

The text differentiates informal or unlawful survival trading, market stall allocation, fee collection, protection provision, extortion, and organized coercive violence. Its analysis of *bakuto* and *tekiya* uses historical categories and rejects the idea that a modern, uniform, honor-bound yakuza institution can be read backward into the prewar and occupation periods. Contextual authorities include Eiko Maruko Siniawer, *Ruffians, Yakuza, Nationalists* (2008); Peter B. E. Hill, *The Japanese Mafia* (2003); and Christopher Aldous, *The Police in Occupation Japan* (1997). These works should be read for their own evidentiary scope rather than treated as interchangeable sources.

### B. Kyoto military and Japanese police cooperation, May 28, 1946

The dedicated source study links the Shōwakan archival video record of a Kyoto black-market enforcement activity on **May 28, 1946**, showing American military police and Japanese police interacting with service personnel and civilians. It describes visible evidence carefully; the film does not by itself establish culpability, convictions, overall crime rates, or the unseen organization of market trading.

- Shōwakan archival video: https://search.showakan.go.jp/search/video/detail.php?id=90061904

### C. Shibuya: July 19 conflict and August 5 raid are distinct events

The manuscript **does not conflate** the July 19, 1946 Shibuya Incident with the subsequent **August 5, 1946** black-market police operation. The July conflict is described as a confrontation entangled with former colonial subjects, competing territorial claims, Japanese police jurisdiction, and the collapse of the old empire, not as an uncomplicated “gang war.” Where specific fatality counts are given, the account attributes **six Taiwanese residents and one Japanese police officer** to Adam Cathcart's published reconstruction instead of pretending to have independently collated all police and diplomatic files. The source is historical scholarship with clearly delineated limits.

- Cathcart, “Bullets of a Defeated Nation: The 1946 Shibuya Incident”: https://academic.oup.com/hong-kong-scholarship-online/book/37673/chapter-abstract/331995662
- Shōwakan August 5 raid photograph and its catalogue caption: https://search.showakan.go.jp/search/photo/detail.php?id=90016051
- Complementary photograph: https://search.showakan.go.jp/search/photo/detail.php?id=90016053

The photo-catalogue account of approximately **280 police officers** is correctly treated as a statement about that particular **August raid**, not as the number of July combatants or a general crime estimate.

### D. Former colonial subjects and market authority

Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese residents appear as historically distinct populations, not as uniform ethnic blocs or shorthand for criminality. The discussion distinguishes discrimination and police classification from actual evidence of criminal organization. Hatsuda Kōsei's research on Tokyo black markets supplies detailed spatial history, intergroup negotiation, and documentary market cases. It also cautions against reading specific association membership figures as national gang statistics.

- Hatsuda, “Tokyo’s Black Markets as an Alternative Urban Space”: https://journals.sagepub.com/eprint/ZAPNWI3GSPTDABR6FXFP/full
- Tessa Morris-Suzuki's work on legal and imperial transitions is included in the contextual foundation: https://apjjf.org/Tessa-Morris-Suzuki/2210/article

### E. Police reform and legal chronology

The Police Law (**Law No. 196, December 17, 1947**) and its **March 7, 1948 effective date** are distinguished; the 1954 reorganization is treated as a later change, not a feature of the immediate 1947 settlement. The narrative examines municipal police, national rural police, public safety oversight, staffing and coordination limits, and the differences between new legal powers and actual enforcement capacity.

- English rendering of 1947 Police Law: https://jahis.law.nagoya-u.ac.jp/lawdb/l/322a0196en
- Official gazette/implementation record: https://jalii.law.nagoya-u.ac.jp/official_gazette/nag_pdf/19480306g_eb.00000.010.000_0010.0010.0_a.099400.00883800.pdf
- National Police Agency institutional retrospective: https://www.npa.go.jp/hakusyo/h16/hakusho/h16/html/F2101020.html

### F. Markets, photographs, and policy

The source register contains separately identified Shōwakan photographs of Shinbashi and other urban markets, dated police or price inspection scenes, a July 1946 policy documentary reference, and local municipal historical resources. The text distinguishes appearance in a photograph from evidence of a person's motives or organizational membership; it also distinguishes later redevelopment from the initial post-surrender emergency.

- Shinbashi market catalogue: https://search.showakan.go.jp/search/photo/detail.php?id=90015672
- Hosei University Ōhara Institute, July 1946 market regulation: https://oisr-org.ws.hosei.ac.jp/research/dglb/explanation/exp_detail/?exp_id=4-012-9001322

### G. Organizational and political claims

The emerging Yamaguchi-gumi, Taoka Kazuo and other named actors are presented in their contemporary context. The text avoids describing post-1950 criminal consolidation as already completed in August 1945, and it treats political brokerage and alleged strikebreaking links as specific historical propositions needing attributed evidence, not proof that all crime was directed by the state or one political faction.

## V. Source Register and evidentiary qualification

The manuscript contains **35 individually linked register entries**. Categories include books and peer-reviewed research, legal enactments, Shōwakan film and photograph catalogue records, local and national archives, police institutional histories, and specialized documentary studies. All register links are explicit URLs in the final HTML, with no fabricated local paths or fictitious document titles used as a substitute for sourcing. Citation is editorial rather than formal page-by-page legal-brief citation throughout the narrative.

**Limitations stated expressly for publication:**

1. Not all Japanese-language police, court, colonial-subject, or military records were independently collated against original physical files or image facsimiles. Archive catalogue metadata and published scholarly transcriptions were relied upon where stated.
2. Archival photograph or film descriptions in teaching windows are editorial analyses of identified materials rather than claims of comprehensive frame-by-frame, negative-level inspection.
3. A mapped spatial study is included as a text-guided external-map exercise. No original historical cartographic plate is embedded in the HTML.
4. Some named criminal organizations' long-term histories depend on secondary scholarship. The chapter avoids false certainty about individual membership, alleged political patronage, or criminal offense totals where record categories are inadequate.
5. Historical counts associated with a particular market association, raid, or judicial investigation are **not** generalized to the entire Japanese population, to all black markets, or to members of former colonial communities.

These limits are compatible with a responsibly qualified **first-edition scholarly textual textbook**, not with claiming an exhaustive original-archive critical edition.
