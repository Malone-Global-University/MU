## III. Historical corrections and scholarly safeguards

**Colonial legal status (§§79.1–79.4).** Distinguished Japanese nationality and colonial political inequality. Explained that the restricted Diet representation legislation for Korea and Taiwan passed in April 1945, but no election was held before surrender. Separated Korean volunteer recruiting (1938), Korean military conscription (1944), Taiwanese army volunteering (1942), and Taiwanese military conscription (1945); noted specific Indigenous Taiwanese experience. Differentiated colonial territories, Manchukuo, and military occupation.

**Labor coercion (§§79.5–79.9).** Developed company recruitment (from September 1939 to 1942), government-coordinated mediation (1942 to September 1944), and direct compulsory labor requisition (late 1944–1945) with evidence that coercion could occur at earlier stages too. Separated legal contracts from actual ability to refuse, leave, collect wages, or secure redress. Distinguished Asian civilians from Allied POWs in railway, mine, camp, and transport histories. Included the Sandakan marches with explicit care about the different denominators used for camp prisoners and march survivors.

**Comfort stations and sexual violence (§§79.10–79.13).** Explained military involvement and the varying roles of private intermediaries, police, and military commands. Distinguished recruitment by force, deception, debt, and constrained economic choice from conditions inside the stations. Did not invent a definitive empire-wide victim total. Differentiated military sexual slavery under organized conditions from individual and widespread rape during operations. Developed Kim Hak-sun as a documented witness and distinguished her August 14, 1991 public press conference from her later 1997 interview.

**Atrocity and responsibility (§§79.14–79.19).** Kept the Nanjing massacre established while acknowledging casualty-definition debates. Separated Sook Ching victims in Singapore from possible wider Malayan counts; differentiated deliberate killing at Manila from combat-related civilian deaths; covered Unit 731 and the separate postwar U.S./Soviet investigations. Explained command responsibility through effective control, actual or constructive knowledge, and failure to take reasonable preventive or punitive measures, with care not to claim identical legal formulations across historical courts. Corrected the Tokyo tribunal disposition: **28 defendants indicted, 25 convicted, two died during proceedings, one declared unfit**. Distinguished established findings from disputed legal remedies and politicized memory.

**Evidence discipline.** No invented verbatim primary-source quotations, no fabricated conversations of survivors, no false precision for deaths or conscription figures, and no collective attribution of criminal responsibility to a nationality. Narrative descriptions are synthesized from historical scholarship and identified official-source records; granular assertions have not all been checked against original Japanese manuscript images.

## IV. Three documentary Primary Source Windows

1. **Mooniady Ramasamy.** The Australian DVA reproduces an extract mediated through Nakahara Michiko, “Malayan Labor on the Thailand–Burma Railway,” in Paul H. Kratoska, ed., *Asian Labor in the Wartime Japanese Empire* (2005), p. 257. The paraphrase now describes documented work and shelter conditions; the accessible excerpt, not an authenticated full first-generation interview, is identified explicitly. https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/prisoners
2. **Japanese Army Ministry memorandum, March 4, 1938, JACAR C04120263400.** Original catalog identifies the file and three Japanese-language images. UCLA provides historical annotation. Window includes military recruiting oversight, reputational concerns, and references to methods resembling kidnapping, without treating editorial paraphrase as a collated translation. https://www.jacar.archives.go.jp/das/meta-en/C04120263400 ; https://international.ucla.edu/cks/care/japanesedocs/250735
3. **Kōno Statement, August 4, 1993.** The official Japanese government statement acknowledged military involvement, coercion in many recruitment cases, and harm to women; the textbook distinguishes this 1993 government assessment from a contemporaneous wartime record. https://www.mofa.go.jp/a_o/rp/page25e_000343.html

All three feature windows identify creator or witness, date/provenance, editorial paraphrase, limits, linked record, and four historical questions. They were checked against accessible institutional descriptions and public texts; they should **not** be represented as fully collated original-language transcriptions.

## VII. Publication boundaries and known limitations

**Approved scope:** a source-identified, historically substantial **textual HTML first edition** for the MGU Japanese History Series. The Geography Study is a text exercise, not an embedded cartographic plate. The Ramasamy testimony is accessible through a later scholarly excerpt rather than an original interview manuscript. The Army Ministry memorandum was checked against JACAR's archival catalog and UCLA's annotation, but its original three images were not exhaustively collated into a critical Japanese-to-English translation. Large-scale casualty estimates remain qualified and tied to source-specific counting categories. The chapter does not claim to be an exhaustive critical edition of all wartime records.

These disclosed limits do not obstruct the publication of the historical-text edition. A separately commissioned archival-facsimile or illustrated-atlas edition could require additional work, but routine micro-passes to this finished textual edition are not warranted.
