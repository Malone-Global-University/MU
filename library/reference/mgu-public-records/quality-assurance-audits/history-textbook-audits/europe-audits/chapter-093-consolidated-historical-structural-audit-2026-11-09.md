## Necessary and prudent corrections implemented

1. **§93.4, population (factual error):** Original England 1801 estimate `about 8.7 million` changed to `about 8.4 million`. Julian Hoppit, *Journal of British Studies* 50 (2011), Table 1 reports England 8.4 million in 1801; contemporaneous enumerations put it near 8.35 million. This must not be confused with England and Wales (~8.9 million in 1801).
2. **§93.5, coal output (factual error):** Original `about 15 million [tons] by 1800` changed to `about 10 million by 1800`. Rolf Peter Sieferle, *The Subterranean Forest* (2001), Table 13 puts annual average British production at about 10 million tons in 1791–1800 and 13.9 million in 1801–1810. Approximately 15 million belongs closer to the later decade, not 1800.
3. **§93.20, worker emigration regulation (date correction):** `1825` changed to `1824` for repeal of the British artisan-emigration prohibition. Fabrice Bensimon, *Artisans Abroad* (2023), and the UK parliamentary record of August 10, 1843, identify repeal in 1824; the machinery-export prohibition ended in 1843 (which was already correct).
4. **§93.12, Henry Cort patent order (precision correction):** Distinguished 1783 grooved-rolling patent from 1784 puddling patent and clarified the coal-fired reverberatory furnace. Sources: European Route of Industrial Heritage biography and historical-metallurgy research.
5. **Chronology preview, §§93.7 and 93.9, back-matter Key Terms (historical legal precision):** Corrected an oversimple account of the Calico Acts. The 1700 restrictions targeted imported printed/dyed Asian cottons for domestic consumption. The 1721 ban also encompassed domestically printed calicoes. The 1736 Manchester Act permitted printing of certain British-made linen-warp/cotton-weft fustians. These were changing rules, not a uniform blanket protection for every British cotton textile.
6. **§§93.9–93.10, ambiguous personal name (reader-critical):** Distinguished John Kay of Bury, associated with the flying shuttle, from John Kay of Warrington, the clockmaker who collaborated with Richard Arkwright. They were different historical individuals.
7. **§93.2, Europe's city ranking (uncertain categorical claim):** Replaced the unqualified claim that London was Europe's largest city in the 1720s with the defensible `one of the largest cities in Europe` in view of historical estimates for Istanbul and boundary/measurement differences.
8. **§93.12, Iron Bridge (chronological clarity):** Rephrased the construction/opening date as `constructed in 1779 and opened in 1781`.

These are narrow textual corrections, not structural redesign, new sections, or broad rewriting. The corrected HTML preserves the draft's historical argument and pedagogical scope.

## Checks that passed without modification

- Raw-cotton imports: around 4.8 million pounds averaged over 1771–75 and roughly 56 million by 1800, broadly consistent with documented customs-based series. The text's cautious statement about the rapid 1830s growth is supportable.
- Slave-trade magnitude: 'roughly three million' transported aboard British ships is a defensible rounded figure; UK National Archives estimates around 3.1 million.
- Iron output: about 250,000 tons in 1805–06; roughly two million by 1850 is a defensible rounded magnitude (some series give ~2.25 million).
- Railway mileage: 'roughly six thousand miles' in Britain near 1850 is defensible for Great Britain, although United Kingdom route mileage including Ireland is recorded at 6,621 at end-1850; do not change this without specifying whether Ireland is included.
- Watt primary-source box: the separate-condenser and hot-cylinder paraphrase accurately reflects the 1769 patent, and it is expressly labelled editorial paraphrase, not a verbatim text. A museum holds and describes the original: Powerhouse Collection, object 92/1. The 1781 rotary and 1782 double-acting patent chronology is supported by original patent publications.
- Regionally differentiated Britain; coal/water/steam sequencing; varied adoption of spinning and weaving technologies; slavery/empire connections; and debates over causes and living standards are generally nuanced and proportionate.
