## 3. Historical corrections and external evidence
### A. Sweden and the 1749 population series — corrected
Original draft mixed the Swedish realm, which included Finland, with a Sweden-only population statement. The corrected source window explicitly identifies **1,764,724** as the total in the *Sweden-only* reconstructed official historical series on 31 December 1749, with **828,545 men and 936,179 women**, and states that the realm-wide figure including Finland was larger. Verified in the source facsimile: *Historisk statistik för Sverige*, Part I, 2nd ed. (1969), table 2, printed page 44, https://gupea.ub.gu.se/server/api/core/bitstreams/9627f7eb-1e28-4d74-b5f5-8f7fb9ec4601/content .
### B. England versus England and Wales — corrected
Original draft incorrectly called an approximately 8.9-million 1801 census figure England-only. The revised passage identifies the **England and Wales** geographical scope, notes approximation and earlier reconstruction, and distinguishes the 1801 enumeration from earlier estimates. Source: Office for National Statistics, *Story of the Census*, https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/storyofthecensus/ ; *Local Population Studies* primary-enumeration analysis, https://www.localpopulationstudies.org.uk/PDF/LPS81/LPS81.pdf .
### C. European population aggregates — qualified
Opening, main prose, and summary now explicitly present the 1700/1800 estimates as a **broad range dependent on series and territory** rather than two precisely comparable enumerated statistics. Approximate ranges are not represented as a verified uniform census of continental Europe. The distinction matters because Russia, Ottoman lands, and changing Polish frontiers complicate comparisons.
### D. Atlantic slave trade — verified and specified
The narrative now attributes the approximately **6.5 million people embarked in 1701–1800** to SlaveVoyages. The estimates table gives **2,560,629** embarked in 1701–1750 and **3,933,989** in 1751–1800, total **6,494,618**. The chapter correctly distinguishes embarkation from arrival and does not confuse eighteenth-century totals with the whole 1501–1866 trade. Source: https://www.slavevoyages.org/assessment/estimates .
### E. Industrial and consumer history — corroborated chronology
The retained chronology differentiates Darby at Coalbrookdale (1709), Newcomen atmospheric steam engine (1712), the Bridgewater Canal (1761), Arkwright 1769 patent and Cromford 1771, Watt 1769 condenser patent and Boulton partnership 1775, Crompton mule later in 1770s, and selected continental industrial activity toward the end of the century. The chapter avoids an instantaneous European factory revolution and differentiates invention from adoption. Wedgwood Frog Service dated **1773–1774** is corroborated by Salisbury Museum and Birmingham Museum of Art. Sources: https://salisburymuseum.org.uk/collections/wedgwood-frog-plate/ and https://www.artsbma.org/collection/frog-service-plate/ .

## 4. Documentary and interpretive integrity
**All ten source windows are editorial paraphrases, catalog-grounded source descriptions, or identified source-method studies, not collated primary-language transcripts.** Their labels and limitations remain visible. No invented shelfmark, register entry, merchant transaction, or forged direct quotation was introduced.
- **Window 1 — Swedish tables:** specific government series and table 2, numerical geography corrected; Sweden-only vs Swedish realm distinguished.
- **Window 2 — Colyton, Devon:** real parish-register corpus identified; now explicitly connects to E. A. Wrigley, “Family Limitation in Pre-Industrial England,” *Economic History Review* 19 (1966): 82–109, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0289.1966.tb00962.x; no manuscript folio is reproduced.
- **Windows 3–7 and 9:** named authors and works with dates already identified in Claude source (Jethro Tull, Arthur Young, Daniel Defoe, Adam Smith, Olaudah Equiano, Richard Arkwright); interpretation expanded in corresponding narrative sections.
- **Window 8 — Cloth industry:** replaced an unsourced generic merchant-account exercise with **a traceable South Yorkshire cloth merchant manuscript notebook (1772–1773), YAS/MS2022**, recorded in UK National Archives 2021 Leeds accessions, alongside the **Leeds White Cloth Hall** archival series (SC MS Cloth / MS 283), University of Leeds. Catalog-level evidence is clearly distinguished from any unexamined individual transaction. Sources: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/accessions/2021/21returns/21ac206.htm and https://explore.library.leeds.ac.uk/special-collections-explore/167063 .
- **Window 10 — Wedgwood:** changed from an unspecified correspondence collection to identifiable surviving **Frog Service plates**, Salisbury Museum and Birmingham Museum of Art, accession 1986.638; notes the discrepancy between 944 and 952 counts in catalog descriptions. A plate is evidence of product and design, not a transcript of the firm’s correspondence. Sources: https://salisburymuseum.org.uk/collections/wedgwood-frog-plate/ and https://www.artsbma.org/collection/frog-service-plate/ .
- **Quantitative urban-share task:** retained as an explicit external dataset exercise; *no fictional de Vries/Bairoch percentages* were introduced. The Swedish numerical evidence is documented in its own window, but the optional urbanization box is not represented as a complete built-in numerical dataset.
