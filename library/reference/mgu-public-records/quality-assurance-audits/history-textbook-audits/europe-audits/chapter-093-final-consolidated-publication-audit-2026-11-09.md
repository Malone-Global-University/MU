## I. Source and scope controls

The assigned chapter purpose is to examine coal, steam, textiles, factories, capital, labor, agriculture, transportation, and why industrialization began first in Britain. The supplied authoritative outline places Chapter 93 in Volume IV, Part XV, followed by Chapter 94 on industrialization's spread across Europe, Chapter 95 on cities and workers, and Chapter 96 on capitalism, socialism and the social question. The review used three supplied controls: Authoritative Chapter Outline(2), European History Instructions for Claude-Ai(2), and ADDITIONAL SERIES-WIDE PRODUCTION RULES(2).

Chapter 93 develops a five-part argument: (I) preindustrial conditions; (II) comparative explanation of British precedence; (III) mechanization and energy; (IV) institutions and spatial expansion of production; and (V) labor, results, limits and transition to continental Europe. It does not materially usurp Chapters 94–96, 99, 101 or the preceding empire chapters.

## III. Historical corrections preserved from the preliminary direct audit

1. **§93.4, 1801 population:** England's 1801 figure was corrected from 8.7 million to approximately **8.4 million**, distinguishing England from England and Wales combined. Source: Julian Hoppit, *Journal of British Studies* 50 (2011), Table 1.
2. **§93.5, coal production:** An earlier 1800 estimate of ~15 million tons was corrected to **approximately 10 million tons**. Sieferle's Table 13 places the higher magnitude in the subsequent decade.
3. **§93.20, industrial-emigration restrictions:** Repeal of restrictions on artisan emigration was corrected to **1824**, while machinery-export restrictions ended in **1843**. Source: Fabrice Bensimon, *Artisans Abroad* (Oxford, 2023).
4. **§93.12, Henry Cort:** The text distinguishes **1783 grooved rolling** from **1784 puddling** patents, rather than treating them as an undifferentiated joint event.
5. **Chronology and §§93.7/93.9, calico legislation:** The 1700, 1721 and 1736 laws are distinguished, rather than being described as a blanket ban on every British cotton textile.
6. **§§93.9–93.10, John Kay:** The flying-shuttle inventor **John Kay of Bury** is distinguished from the later **John Kay of Warrington**, who collaborated with Arkwright.
7. **§93.2, London:** An unqualified claim of Europe's largest city in the 1720s was softened to **one of the largest** because historical comparisons depend on city boundaries and uncertain Istanbul estimates.
8. **§93.12, Iron Bridge:** Construction is dated **1779**; opening **1781**.

## IV. Final editorial revisions applied after that preliminary audit

9. **§93.8, comparative theory:** Robert C. Allen's high-wage/cheap-energy model is presented as one *influential* explanation, not a universally settled comparative fact. The comparisons with China and India expressly acknowledge disputed cross-regional wage evidence and causal weighting. This avoids equating a historian's model with an uncontested measurement. Review: Jane Humphries, *Economic History Review* (2013), and Allen's reply (2015).
10. **§93.7, calico legislation:** The chronology now includes **1774 relaxation/repeal of the principal domestic-use restriction on printed all-cotton calicoes**, important to the growth of the British all-cotton fabric trade. Review: Metropolitan Museum of Art chronology and recent scholarship on cotton handloom manufacture.
11. **§93.19, occupational composition:** The wording about 1851 work is recast as agriculture being a **major** employer of men and domestic service an **especially important** employer of women; this avoids an unverified strict ranking across heterogeneous census categories.
12. **§93.11, Watt source identification:** The source caption now places James Watt **in Glasgow at the time of his 1769 patent**, avoiding possible confusion with his later Birmingham partnership. The document's source text remains explicitly an **editorial paraphrase**, not a purported verbatim excerpt.
13. **Review-section hierarchy:** Seven review sections' headings use correct semantic hierarchy. The surrounding CSS retains their visual treatment.
14. **Mobile chronology table:** A width-limited, horizontally scrollable chronology table now prevents unintended horizontal scrolling of the whole page on narrow screens.
15. **Small-screen chapter numeral:** The large decorative numeral is suppressed at widths up to 600px to prevent overlap with the series heading. The volume/part identification and chapter footer remain in the document.
16. **Accessibility:** The sidebar table-of-contents `<nav>` now has an explicit accessible label.

These revisions preserve the 20 numbered historical sections, their IDs, the chapter argument and existing teaching apparatus. No artificial length expansion or additional section was introduced.

## V. Primary-source and numerical evidence check

- The source window paraphrasing **James Watt's 1769 patent** is identified correctly as a paraphrase of the principle of keeping the steam cylinder hot and condensing steam in a separate vessel. It is supported by the Powerhouse collection's description of its original patent specimen (Object 92/1). It is **not** advertised as a facsimile-collated verbatim quotation.
- Cotton-import growth magnitudes are appropriately rounded and consistent with historical tabulations near the relevant dates.
- A rounded total of roughly three million captive Africans transported on British vessels is supportable as a historical order of magnitude; the chapter does not confuse British-ship voyages with the entire Atlantic trade.
- Pig-iron growth, approximate railway mileage near 1850, and the gradual shift from water to steam are described as estimates and geographically qualified where needed. A historical series for Great Britain records over 6,000 miles of open railway by 1849, consistent with the chapter's rounded near-1850 figure.
- The chapter correctly avoids claiming immediate wage convergence with productivity growth, drawing attention to contested reconstructions of living standards and real wages.
- The slavery/empire section distinguishes material connections with cotton from genuinely disputed quantitative claims about how important slave-trade profits were in financing overall British industrial investment.

**Verification scope:** This was a targeted historical source audit of high-risk dates, claims and statistical magnitudes, not exhaustive archival verification of every factual clause. Map and geography exercises are text-guided and have no embedded historical atlas plate.
