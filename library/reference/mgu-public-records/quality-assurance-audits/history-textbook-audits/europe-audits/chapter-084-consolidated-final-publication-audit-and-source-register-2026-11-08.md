## 3. Historical corrections and verification

1. **Frederick II:** The 1752 Political Testament is a confidential succession document, not a public constitutional proclamation; it preserves noble landholding while endorsing royal diligence and certain protections against excessive peasant obligations. Source: German History in Documents and Images (GHDI).
2. **Prussian code chronology:** Frederick died in 1786; the General Law Code was first issued in 1791, modified, and became effective June 1, 1794 under Frederick William II. The chapter and its source window explicitly distinguish stages. Source: GHDI, *The General Law Code for the Prussian States*.
3. **Prussian schooling:** The 1763 General School Regulation is distinguished from evidence of actual teaching or attendance. Source: GHDI, *Frederick the Great, Compulsory Education Decree (1763)*.
4. **Maria Theresa:** General School Ordinance, December 6, 1774, associated with Felbiger. Her 1768 penal legislation regulated continued torture; an abolition edict followed in 1776 in Austrian hereditary lands. Sources: Austrian State Archives; Austrian Ministry of Justice historical material.
5. **Joseph II:** The 1781 Christian toleration arrangements are not conflated with separate Jewish edicts, including Lower Austria January 1782. The 1781 patent on serfdom altered personal conditions while preserving labor obligations and dues attached to land. Sources: GHDI; YIVO Encyclopedia.
6. **Habsburg resistance:** The Brabant Revolution (1789–1790) was a distinct uprising in the Austrian Netherlands. Hungary’s intense constitutional opposition is not described as an identical military revolt. The reign’s reversals and policy survival are differentiated.
7. **Catherine II:** The Nakaz (1767) was instruction to a Legislative Commission, not an enacted representative constitution. Provincial reorganization (1775) and the two corporate charters (1785) are distinct events. Noble privilege and continuing serfdom are explicit. Sources: Yale Law Library; bilingual charter edition and catalog.
8. **Portugal:** Real Mesa Censória was created April 5, 1768; Coimbra’s statutes were issued in 1772; the educational and censorship programs are treated together. Sources: Brazilian National Archives and National Library.
9. **Spain:** Esquilache unrest dates to 1766; the crown’s Jesuit expulsion orders to 1767. The scope extends across overseas possessions. Source: Library of Congress, royal order and decree records.
10. **Tuscany and Sweden:** The Tuscan Leopoldina of November 30, 1786, abolished the death penalty and torture, with later reversal acknowledged. Sweden’s 1772 political settlement and the 1789 Act of Union and Security are distinguished. Sources: State Archives of Florence; Swedish Riksdag.

### Editorial cautions maintained

- These document studies paraphrase accurately identified source arguments and never represent the paraphrases as collated verbatim quotations.
- The bibliography includes institutional catalogs and translated editions; the project does **not** claim exhaustive original-language facsimile collation.
- No historical cartographic plates are embedded; the map studies tell students how to investigate geography with independently dated atlas material.
- Institutional reforms are examined through intended design and limitations rather than assumed universal successful implementation.
- Modern equal-rights terminology is not projected uncritically onto eighteenth-century estate privileges and limited toleration.
