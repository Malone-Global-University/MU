## 4. Historical and bibliographic consolidated verification

**4.1 Fa Ngum and independent attestation.** The historical Fa Ngum is distinguished from the detailed founding story. The publication identifies the **Sumanakutaparvatta stele**, published in George Cœdès, *Recueil des inscriptions du Siam*, vol. 1 (1924), as the specific Sukhothai witness discussed by modern scholarship. No unverified inscription number, complete translation, or assertion of direct facsimile inspection is introduced. The conventional **1353** foundation date remains characterized as traditional.

**4.2 Lorrillard's early inscriptional chronology.** The epigraphy discussion now identifies a **1510–1511 Sanakham-area stele** as the earliest securely dated member of the relevant Lan Xang Lao-stele series according to Lorrillard's 2020 study, with later Luang Prabang texts also distinguished. The date measures the surviving evidence, not Buddhism's first arrival. Earlier, adjacent, and undated epigraphic materials are explicitly differentiated. Complete 2020 and 2021 article/chapter bibliographic metadata are supplied from the École française d'Extrême-Orient bibliography.

**4.3 Plain of Jars.** The expanded account separates manufacture/emplacement of early megalithic jars from later funerary use. Dating work reports possible early placement and eighth–thirteenth-century CE burials at some sites; this does **not** establish a direct dynastic line to medieval Phuan or Lan Xang rulers. This correction accords with Shewan et al., *PLOS ONE* 16 (2021), e0247167, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0247167, and follow-up archaeological research.

**4.4 Ming Laowo references.** The chapter now places the reported **Laowo recognition and tributary exchanges around 1402–1404**, with particular attention to the tradition of recognition of Sam Saen Thai in **1404**. The date is corroborated in Pierre-Bernard Lafont's documented 1962 review of Maha Sila Viravong's chronicle synthesis and is represented cautiously as later scholarship on diplomatic notices, not as a directly collated Chinese memorial or a map of borders.

**4.5 1479 invasion.** The Đại Việt campaign under Lê Thánh Tông is contextualized through the annalistic record and John K. Whitmore's comparative analysis of the Hồng Đức campaigns. Popular white-elephant explanations remain labeled later traditions; no invented troop counts or precise casualty totals are added.

**4.6 Documentary editions:** all three feature windows now name identifiable textual/modern scholarly witnesses:
- Souneth Phothisane, *The Nidan Khun Borom: Annotated Translation and Analysis* (University of Queensland doctoral dissertation, 1996).
- Maha Sila Viravong, *Phongsavadan Lao* (Vientiane: Ministry of Education, 1957), a modern compilation rather than a medieval autograph.
- *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*, Vietnam Social Sciences Institute, four-volume Hà Nội, Nhà xuất bản Khoa học xã hội edition (1993), alongside Whitmore (2004).

The source register contains **16 identified entries** including these editions, and the original unresolved 'to be specified' placeholders were replaced. Source window paragraphs still clearly say they are **editorial paraphrases or scholar-mediated summaries**, not verified translations. The 2022 study on Khmer influence is fully attributed to *Decoding Southeast Asian Art: Studies in Honor of Piriya Krairiksh* (River Books), pp. 154–167. The NIAS historiography collection is identified as the 2003 cataloged edition.

## 6. Explicit first-edition limits

1. No exhaustive original-language collation against all Lao manuscript witnesses, original Sukhothai stele facsimiles, Chinese Ming court records, or the Vietnamese annals. Edition-specific references do not claim otherwise.
2. All three windows are explicitly **editorial paraphrases or published-scholarship-mediated summaries**, not direct archival quotations.
3. Map exercises are textual instructions requiring a separate historical atlas; no embedded map plates are supplied.
4. The highly uneven survival of early Lao documents constrains precise reconstruction of Fa Ngum's campaigns, royal regulations, dates, and religious life. The narrative preserves those uncertainties rather than asserting them away.

These limits are disclosed, appropriate to the first-edition scholarly textual format, and **do not block publication** when accurately labeled.
