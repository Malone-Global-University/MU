## 3. Historical and historiographical corrections implemented

### 3.1 Origins and leadership of Dobama Asiayone

The original draft collapsed the founding circle of **30 May 1930** into later factional leadership, presenting Ba Thaung, Ba Sein, and Tun Oke together as founders. The final narrative and Table 5 distinguish the original initiative around **Thakin Ba Thaung** from the later prominence of **Thakin Ba Sein and Thakin Tun Oke**. This follows the distinction made in Khin Yi, *The Dobama Movement in Burma (1930–1938)* (1988), whose publishing descriptions identify Ba Thaung as the organizer of the founding initiative.

The revision preserves Dobama's internal diversity and does not interpret its use of *thakin* as evidence that all activists endorsed identical cultural, racial, or socialist programs.

### 3.2 Oilfield strike chronology

The draft originally placed the start of the Chauk strike in **late 1938**. The final chapter correctly places the prolonged Chauk stoppage in **January 1938**, with subsequent extension, political mobilization, and the widely remembered march later that year. The chronology distinguishes initial workplace action from the later national political crisis. This distinction is independently supported by the surviving labor account *The Strike War* and by historical research on Thakin Po Hla Gyi.

The revised material also documents the specificity of labor demands: eight-hour working day, pay arrangements, workers' housing, medical treatment, company schools, and dismissed workers' rights. Women's and families' participation is explicitly addressed rather than treating the strike only as a march by male nationalist leaders.

### 3.3 Government of Burma Act legal citation

The original bibliography incorrectly combined the regnal years of George V and Edward VIII. The final bibliography identifies the statute as **Government of Burma Act 1935, 26 Geo. 5, c. 3**, with royal assent on **2 August 1935**. Section 129.17 distinguishes the 1935 enactment from the commencement of a separate Burmese constitutional system in **April 1937**. No passage treats separation from British India as national independence.

The statutory discussion explains the Crown's continuing authority, governor's powers, bicameral legislature, and separate or functional representation, including Karen, Indian, labor, and commercial categories.

### 3.4 The 1931 rebellion report and the Saya San problem

The report is now identified by its full title: *East India (Burma Rebellion): Report on the Rebellion in Burma up to 3rd May, 1931, and Communiqué Issued by the Government of Burma, 19th May, 1931*. Library cataloguing supports **Cmd. 3900**. The revised source study uses the documented description of initial outbreaks in Tharrawaddy on **22 December 1930**, the report's references to **village athins**, and its discussion of **capitation tax** protests. The chapter does not attribute every later incident to a demonstrable command issued by Saya San.

Maitrii Aung-Thwin's *The Return of the Galon King* is used to frame the distinction between archival evidence, investigative allegations, the legal construction of conspiracy, and later nationalist commemoration. Casualties, arrests, convictions, and executions are kept in distinct categories; no synthetic totals were manufactured.

### 3.5 Student unrest and the Revolution of 1300

The description of the 1936 *Oway* controversy retains the verified English title *Hell Hound at Large*, distinguishing Aung San's editorial responsibility from U Nu's related but separate conflict with university authorities. The narrative identifies the February 1936 strike as part of a larger network of student collective action rather than simply the beginning of Aung San's personal rise.

The final manuscript places Aung Kyaw's fatal injury during the Rangoon police confrontation of **December 1938** without assigning an unsupported exact day of death. The Mandalay police shootings are placed in **February 1939**; contemporary and retrospective accounts differ in the dates assigned to meetings, arrests, the march, and the shooting. The revised narrative retains the established significance of **seventeen deaths in Mandalay**, while explicitly preserving uncertainty about the detailed sequence. There is no invented reconciliation.

### 3.6 Constitutional and social inclusion

The 1936 election is treated as a contest that produced **no overall party majority**; Ba Maw's government is identified as a coalition, not the inevitable product of a landslide. The role of **Daw Mya Sein** in the Burma Round Table Conference is expanded from a biographical notice into a discussion of political representation. Women are treated as organizers, fundraisers, strikers, and participants in debates about education and legal rights.

The section on minority and frontier politics distinguishes **Bamar-majority nationalist organizations** from Karen, Shan, Kachin, Chin, and other communities' different constitutional and territorial concerns. Indian workers, merchants, clerks, financiers, and settlers are not presented as a homogeneous group; the manuscript separates colonial economic structures from the choice to perpetrate communal violence.

## 5. Scholarly completeness and interpretive balance

The revised narrative now gives a substantive account of:

- The loss of monarchy, reconfiguration of the sangha, Buddhist lay activism, and the transition from YMBA to GCBA and the wunthanu networks.
- The 1920 university boycott, national schools, the institutional mechanics of dyarchy, and tensions between constitutional participation and noncooperation.
- Rural credit and land loss, the social and geographical variations of Saya San's rebellion, colonial intelligence and courtroom interpretation, and the subsequent legal construction of a unifying rebellion narrative.
- The Dobama movement, political identity of the thakins, later factional leadership, and the relationship between student radicalism and organized labor.
- Oilfield employment conditions, the workers' specific demands, the march and 1938–1939 crisis, police action in Rangoon and Mandalay, and the politics of the Revolution of 1300.
- The 1935–1937 constitutional settlement, Ba Maw's coalition, women's political participation, non-Bamar concerns, the emergence of socialist and communist organizations, and the political crisis of 1939–1940.

The interpretive narrative avoids presenting British defeat or Aung San's eventual leadership as inevitable. It does not conflate rural revolt with Dobama's organizational politics, nor ethnic diversity with a single settled conception of the nation. Chapter 136 retains the full Japanese occupation and wartime Burma Independence Army narrative.

## 7. Remaining scholarly and production limitations

These are documented limitations, not unresolved blockers requiring a new general narrative expansion:

1. A complete diplomatic transcription of every colonial administrative source was not performed; the documentary passages used in the source windows have targeted support.
2. Competing reports of the specific date of the February 1939 Mandalay shooting remain unreconciled. The narrative correctly uses a month-level chronology rather than inventing precision.
3. Colonial casualty and arrest categories for the 1930–1932 rebellion remain heterogeneous. The manuscript declines to invent an aggregate statistic.
4. Some books and original Burmese-language publications in the bibliography would benefit from subsequent title-page and edition-level reconciliation; the bibliography is substantively adequate but not a complete critical bibliography.
5. The user will insert all map illustrations. Geography-study pedagogy is intact, and the absence of illustrations does not reopen this audit.
6. Final production-server deployment, site-wide styles, external links, and any later inserted visual assets were not assessed.
