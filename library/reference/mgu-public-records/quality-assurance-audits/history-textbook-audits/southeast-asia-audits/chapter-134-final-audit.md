Chapter 133 is assigned to the regional Great Depression; Chapter 135 covers the Malayan campaign and fall of Singapore. These chapter boundaries were preserved.

This is the independent audit of the revised manuscript, not an authorial audit supplied by the original drafting model. It documents changes made during revision and checks actually performed. ## 3. Substantive editorial revisions actually completed

1. **Section 134.4 — ideology and historical agency.** Expanded the discussion of the distinct uses of Pan-Asian rhetoric by Japanese officials and Southeast Asian audiences; sharpened the difference between a declaration and evidence of subsequent practice. In the 1938 primary-source paraphrase, replaced an overly categorical assertion about Chiang Kai-shek's government with the conditional language of the November 1938 statement.
2. **Sections 134.5–134.6 — resource security.** Expanded petroleum refining, transport, dollar payments, foreign company interests, and the distinction between securing commodities commercially and exercising coercive control over colonial production.
3. **Section 134.7 — British Malaya and Singapore.** Added substantive treatment of rubber and tin production systems, labor, export infrastructure, Malaya's differentiated administrative jurisdictions, and varied local responses. The analysis now explains why seizure of a producing area would not guarantee continued deliveries.
4. **Section 134.8 — Indochina, Thailand, and China supply corridors.** Expanded railway, road, port, truck, transshipment, and monsoon constraints; differentiated routes supporting Chongqing from bases supporting Japan's projected southern offensive.
5. **Section 134.9 — Kantokuen.** Identified the 1941 Kwantung Army Special Maneuvers as mobilization and contingency preparations for war against the Soviet Union, rather than ordinary training. Clarified that the contemplated invasion did not occur and that the northern option remained under review even as the south took priority.
6. **Sections 134.10–134.11 — maritime and colonial vulnerability.** Added constraints on operational control of sea lanes and clarified the continuing authority of Dutch and Vichy French administrations after the European defeats in 1940.
7. **Sections 134.13–134.14 — imperial defense systems.** Expanded Singapore's dependence on mobile forces rather than fixed installations, Operation Matador's diplomatic implications for Thai sovereignty, and the political/operational constraints of defending the Philippine Commonwealth.
8. **Section 134.16 — economic restrictions.** Corrected the stepwise July 1940 export-control chronology, explained the distinction between legal authority, proclamations, licensing, and the later effective oil embargo, and separated the March 11, 1941 Lend-Lease statute from China's May 6 eligibility date. Replaced misleading wording in the sanctions table.
9. **Section 134.17 and related apparatus — southern Indochina.** Corrected the sequencing: U.S. announcement July 25 and Executive Order 8832 July 26 preceded Japanese troop entry into southern Indochina on July 28. Adjusted the source discussion, chapter summary, and research project so they no longer imply the freeze followed an already completed occupation.
10. **Sections 134.19–134.20 — diplomacy and the outbreak of war.** Expanded source analysis distinguishing the Hull Note's tentative wording from its reception as an ultimatum. Added Kota Bharu's priority relative to Pearl Harbor without specifying an unsupported single interval: official institutional accounts time different stages of landings and attacks.
11. **Source access and bibliography.** Added direct archival/documentary links to each of the five primary-source windows and six specially described primary-record references to the bibliography. Retained paraphrase labels; no quotation was fabricated.
12. **Responsive HTML.** Updated mobile styling of chronology and data tables to keep wide tables internally scrollable instead of overflowing the viewport; otherwise retained the provided chapter's design shell and script.

## 5. Instructional evaluation

The revised chapter does more than enumerate events. Each division connects circumstances, actors, documentary evidence, alternatives, and consequences. The commodity and logistics discussion distinguishes production from delivery; the colonial-defense discussion distinguishes military resources from political authorization; and the diplomatic account distinguishes an original text from the interpretation imposed by its recipients.

The five primary-source features identify the creator, document type, historical context, and interpretive limits, and carry labeled paraphrases rather than invented quotations. The expanded source access makes document-focused study easier to reproduce. The assigned debate, geographic, chronology, comparison, and cause-and-effect exercises remain embedded in their intended sections. Comparative geography exercises are usable without final map images.

## 7. Remaining qualifications and limitations

The historical verification focused on the contested and consequential claims surfaced by the initial independent audit, and on newly revised passages. The bibliography includes credible standard scholarly references and clearly identified archives, but **every edition, imprint, and page-number citation was not independently catalog-verified**. The source windows are responsibly labeled paraphrases, not certified translations of all original Japanese-language documents. Where a window combines an original policy decision and its diplomatic reception, its linked English-language records support source criticism but do not substitute for a full diplomatic edition of the Japanese original.

A small number of sections contain fewer than 650 narrative words; the agreed section average was a planning guide, not an absolute condition, and the complete chapter now exceeds the 13,000-word narrative minimum without padding. The subject ends at the December 1941 onset of war and appropriately defers campaign details to later chapters.
