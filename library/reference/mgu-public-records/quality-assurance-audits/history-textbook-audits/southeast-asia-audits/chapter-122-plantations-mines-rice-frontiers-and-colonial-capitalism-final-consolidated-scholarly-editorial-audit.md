## 3. Historical accuracy corrections and substantive improvements

1. **Lower Burma (§122.13):** Distinguishes British military conquest of Lower Burma in 1852 from the annexation proclamation of January 1853; expands the delta's rice economy, finance and social consequences.
2. **Dutch Cultivation System (§§122.2, 122.6):** Distinguishes introduction in 1830 from the phased post-1870 reform of the sugar and agrarian regimes; explains continued government coffee obligations, legal treatment of customary rights and state-domain claims, rather than presenting instant abolition in 1870.
3. **Tin industry (§§122.9–122.10):** Treats commercial dredging as beginning at the end of the pre-1914 chronology (first successful Kinta Valley dredge in 1913); does not project postwar European dredging dominance backward over nineteenth-century Chinese mining enterprises.
4. **East Sumatra contract labor (§122.12):** Distinguishes the 1880 Coolie Ordinance (Indisch Staatsblad 1880 No. 133), the 1889 revision and actual enforcement practices, and separates legal provisions from Van den Brand's contemporary criticism.
5. **Burma property law (§122.15):** Analyzes Section 8 of the Lower Burma Land and Revenue Act (1876), acknowledging permanent/heritable/transferable occupancy rights subject to reservations and revenue obligations; distinguishes original legislation from subsequent amendments in online compiled texts.
6. **Petroleum (§122.11):** Differentiates Burmah Oil, Royal Dutch, Shell and the 1907 Royal Dutch–Shell corporate grouping without presenting the 1907 transaction as the founding of a modern unified corporation.
7. **Economic interpretation throughout:** Explains estate capital costs, commodity marketing, the relationship between export value and welfare, Asian entrepreneurship, peasant debt exposure, the spatial selectivity of infrastructure, environmental effects and the contrast with sovereign Siam.

Chronological horizon is primarily 1750–1914. Later works appear only as scholarship interpreting earlier conditions and the pre-1914 narrative does not claim future events happened before their dates.

## 5. Quantitative evidence and geography

Three contextualized economic data tables now provide distinct historical measures:

- **Federated Malay States railways:** miles open at the end of the years 1896 (150), 1900 (234), 1905 (396), and 1907 (428), following the 1908 statistical manual. Railway mileage is a proxy for network expansion, not a measure of equitable development.
- **British Malaya rubber area:** planted acreage from a historical ownership-disaggregated series, with 1910 European 377,000 and Asian 164,000 acres, 1913 646,000 and 428,000 acres, and 1914 672,000 and 490,000 acres. The figures count planted acreage, not harvested output, and the ownership categories and coverage are those of the cited compilation.
- **Burmese rice exports:** selected quinquennial average export volumes (thousands of tons), including 1875: 679, 1900: 1,492, 1910: 2,052. These series indicate trade expansion but do not establish real incomes or uniform agrarian prosperity.

Two original, embedded instructional maps show (1) major colonial commodity-producing regions and export gateways and (2) the Tin–Rubber–Railway–Port relationships in British Malaya. These are interpretative geographical diagrams using modern outline reference geometry; the captions warn against reading point symbols or schematic routes as surveyed historical production boundaries. The images are embedded in the HTML for standalone viewing; no external asset is required.
