## 3. Statistical and source verification

- [AWM, F Force study](https://wm.awm.gov.au/read/burma-thailand-railway): reports approximately 62,000 Allied prisoners and 11,979 deaths, approximately 19%. Also gives a possible civilian workforce of 180,000. The chapter no longer falsely compares 20% as greater than 25%.
- [DVA, Prisoners and labourers](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/prisoners): estimates approximately 200,000 Asian laborers and up to 90,000 civilian deaths; [AWM, Stolen Years](https://www.awm.gov.au/visit/exhibitions/stolenyears/ww2/japan/burmathai) gives approximately 75,000 civilian deaths. Estimates are attributed separately, not combined into a precise rate. These sources use differing tallies for POW losses; the chapter selects the internally paired AWM F Force numerator and denominator for the demographic example, not an authoritative universal total.
- [Takuma Melber, 2016](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/labour-recruitment-of-local-inhabitants-as-romusha-in-japaneseoccupied-south-east-asia/4F5497C20D928D214D809915EDC9E015): estimates 200,000–300,000 deployments outside Java, including 31,000 to Malaya and more than 40% to Sumatra. These estimates do not establish deaths or unique repatriations.
- [DVA, Burmese and Thais](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/burmese-thais): supports Thai financing estimate of 491 million baht and Boon Pong's assistance.
- [DVA, Hellfire Pass](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/hellfire-pass): supports approximately 415 km, 75 m by 25 m cutting and 16 October 1943 completion. The text retains the original mid-October/16–17 wording with DVA's date identified; independent resolution of the alternate daily date was not undertaken.
- [DVA, F Force](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/f-force) and [AWM, Kappe account catalogue](https://shop.awm.gov.au/products/the-death-railway-the-personal-account-of-lieutenant-colonel-kappe-on-the-thai-burma-railroad): support the approximate seven-thousand strength and more-than-three-thousand deaths. No complete nominal roll was reconciled; retain approximate wording.
- [DVA, Australian POWs in Thailand](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/asia/thailand) and [camps near Hellfire Pass](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/camps): support medical personnel in civilian camps, including Kinsaiyok.
- [DVA, operations](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/operation) and [postwar dismantling](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/destruction): support repair, evacuation, Allied administration and recovery of railway materials.
- [AWM, Rakuyō Maru](https://www.awm.gov.au/articles/blog/70th-anniversary-sinking-rakuy-maru): confirms railway survivors among those transported in September 1944.
- [DVA, Australian POWs 1941–1945](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/resources/australian-prisoners-war-1941-1945): supports Hellfire Pass memorial opening on 24 April 1998.

The civilian totals remain estimates. No precise civilian mortality rate, exact overall romusha death total, or claim equating disappearance with death has been introduced.

## 4. Primary-source correction

The original window offered two anonymous composite paraphrases. These were removed. Source A is [Shirlene Noordin's 2022 BiblioAsia account](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-18-issue-3-oct-dec-2022-grandfather-romusha-thai-burma-railway/) about Kosman Hassan, grounded in family recollection and research; it is explicitly distinguished from a contemporary source or direct interview. Source B is Stanley Foch Arneil's diary entry dated 21 December 1943, as reproduced by the [AWM Changi exhibition](https://www.awm.gov.au/visit/exhibitions/stolenyears/ww2/japan/changi), with the [archive collection C90733](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C90733) identified. The revised exercise uses attributable paraphrases, not invented or extended quotations. Both source questions and limitations have been revised. The full original diary was not transcribed. The family account is not presented as a verbatim firsthand interview.

[W. L. Cheah's 2025 article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0738248025101077) provides an additional source-based treatment of civilian women and the Kudo Butai proceedings: ten of thirteen prosecution witnesses were Asian civilians. The article's abstract and body contain inconsistent workforce/mortality language; its abstract figure was not used as a death estimate. Its discussion of testimony and legal proceedings supports the chapter's specific additions. The underlying complete trial transcript was not independently retrieved.

## 5. Legal and diplomatic checks

- [ICRC, 1929 POW Convention](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/gc-pow-1929) and [FRUS 1942, document 680](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1942v01/d680) establish the treaty text and Japan's qualified assurance concerning American POWs. [Document 705](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1942v01/d705) confirms nonratification. The chapter separates the Hague rules, the 1929 convention, Japan's assurances, customary law and the subsequent 1949 framework.
- [Japanese MOFA historical overview](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/hakusyo/04_hakusho/ODA2004/html/honpen/hp102010000.htm) supports the 1954 Burma, 1956 Philippines, 1958 Indonesia and 1959 Republic of Vietnam reparations chronology. [MOFA 1985](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/other/bluebook/1985/1985-3-4.htm) distinguishes quasi-reparations. The official Japanese account is evidence of its classification, not a neutral judgment about adequacy.
- No universal test of command responsibility or uniform outcome for individual civil litigation is asserted. The revised chapter distinguishes criminal accountability, interstate settlements, compensation and humanitarian recognition.
