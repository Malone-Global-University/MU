## 3. Historical verification and direct corrections

### 3.1 Shi Bi after Java, §46.25 — CORRECTED

The submitted text recorded one-third-family-property confiscation attributed generally to a later Chinese account and omitted corporal punishment. **`Yuan shi`, juan 162**, Shi Bi biography, explicitly describes **seventeen strokes** as well as confiscation of **one-third** of his family property, and notes the later return of seized property. The separate **`Yuan shi`, juan 131**, Ike Mese biography, describes property confiscation but does not give an identical corporal-punishment report. The final passage differentiates those records and adds a short discussion of their evidentiary use.

Primary text: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=129554&if=en&remap=gb ; https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7162  
Ike Mese: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7131

### 3.2 Kyawswa and Myinsaing, §§46.11–46.12 — CHRONOLOGY QUALIFIED

The submitted draft asserted **December 1297** as the definitive deposition date. Historian **Than Tun (1959)** discusses an inscription dated December 17, 1297 using language about a “dethroned king” but also records G. H. Luce's reading of **Chinese evidence placing deposition in June–July 1298**. Michael Aung-Thwin subsequently argued that an inscription can record an earlier ceremony but have been carved or framed in a later context. The audited text presents the contestation and explains the interpretive issue instead of selecting one asserted certainty.

Than Tun, “History of Burma: A.D. 1300–1400,” *Journal of the Burma Research Society*, XLII, ii (1959), pp. 119–120: https://www.uclmyanmar.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/History-of-Burma-AD-1300-1400.pdf

Michael A. Aung-Thwin, *Myanmar in the Fifteenth Century: A Tale of Two Kingdoms* (University of Hawai‘i Press, 2017), first chapter, discussion of Kyawswa and the 1297 inscription. This edition's exact original-book pagination was not inspected independently for this audit; the distinction is also reported in the contemporaneous argument discussed by Than Tun.

The 1300–1301 Myinsaing campaign, ca. 12,000 troops, withdrawal in 1301, and ending Yuan garrison presence in 1303 are broadly compatible with Than Tun's account, but exact casualty and bribery figures remain qualified.

### 3.3 Early Yuan–Tai diplomacy, §46.28 — IDENTIFICATION QUALIFIED

The submitted narrative associated *Xian* and a supposed separate 1299 “Sukhothai” entry with a particular interpretation. The edited text avoids that specific unverified inference and presents the scholarly disagreement over **Xian's location**, including the link between a **1293 mission and Phetchaburi**, without assuming every Xian mission came from Ramkhamhaeng's Sukhothai.

Relevant scholarly discussion: University of Michigan dissertation *State, Community, and Ethnicity in Early Modern Thailand, 1351–1767*, discussion of Yuan documents and changing identifications of `xian`: https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/151629/jsfsmith_1.pdf

Remaining evidence limit: Specific identification of all Malay Peninsula, Sumatran, and Tai polities in Yuan transcriptions was not exhaustively checked against each entry in the Chinese records. Uncertain identifications remain presented with qualifications.

### 3.4 Lê Tắc, §46.1 and source table — SOURCE DATE PRECISED

The original “first publication in 1335” phrasing was replaced with the dated **1333 authorial preface**, recorded in Vietnam's Institute of Hán-Nôm Studies bibliographic catalogue. The author and the work's relevance as a Yuan-era perspective are supported.

Source: Institute of Hán-Nôm Studies, `AN NAM CHÍ LƯỢC`: https://hannom.vass.gov.vn/?cid=61496&pageid=46955&sachid=24

### 3.5 Serutu Island inscription, §46.22 — VERIFIED CORE

Hung, Hartatik, Ma'rifat, and Simanjuntak's 2022 peer-reviewed open-access article records an inscribed stone from Serutu and connects it with the Yuan voyage of **1293** and a **500-ship** fleet. The draft appropriately distinguishes this material evidence from other ship numbers recorded in the chronicles. It was discovered in 2017, and the published study is dated 2022.

Source: Hung et al., “Mongol fleet on the way to Java: First archaeological remains from the Karimata Strait in Indonesia,” *Archaeological Research in Asia* 29 (2022), 100327, DOI 10.1016/j.ara.2021.100327: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352226721000738

### 3.6 Java and Kudadu, §46.21–46.25 — VERIFIED CORE

The existence, date (1294), purpose, and association with Raden Wijaya of the **Kudadu inscription** are supported by the University of Hamburg's compiled source register. The chapter appropriately treats the text as a royal charter with its own legitimation interests rather than a neutral narrative.

Source: *Materials for the Medieval History of Indonesia*, University of Hamburg: https://www.spaetmittelalter.uni-hamburg.de/java-history/JavaInscriptions/Java1294.00.00.html

### 3.7 Zhou Daguan, §46.27 — VERIFIED CORE AND DEEPENED

The embassy arrived at Angkor in **August 1296** and returned in **1297**. The completed section now discusses the limitation that his descriptions represent a visitor's urban/courtly viewpoint and passed through later textual compilation; the evidence cannot independently establish conditions for every Khmer subject.

Source: Peter Harris, trans., *A Record of Cambodia: The Land and Its People* (2007), and overview at https://angkordatabase.asia/books/zhou-daguans-customs-of-cambodia-new-translation

### 3.8 Other historical safeguards retained

- Avoids presenting the destruction of Pagan in 1287 as an uncontested fact.
- Distinguishes the first Mongol attack on Đại Việt (1257–1258) from Yuan-dynasty campaigns in the 1280s.
- Treats Raden Wijaya's actions and Javanese sources as separate perspectives from Yuan military reports.
- Distinguishes formal tribute and investiture from sustained direct control.
- Avoids independently asserting that the Trần consciously re-enacted the 938 Bạch Đằng tactics in 1288.
- Does not replace uncertain troop and casualty figures with fabricated modern precision.

## 5. Remaining provenance limitations and editorial acceptance

This is a scholarly synthesized textbook chapter, **not** a critical edition of every *Yuan shi*, *An Nam chí lược*, Burmese inscription, local chronicle, and Javanese inscription. It has been checked directly for the publication-critical disputed points above, and the key material evidence is documented. Some narrative background on Yuan–Tai and Malay polities, the precise locations of source-transcribed ports, and administrative practices in Yunnan relies on secondary scholarship rather than an exhaustive entry-by-entry collation. The text generally marks those matters as interpretive or provisional.

The educational features and source windows are paraphrases or analyses, not fabricated verbatim primary-source quotations. Full source-window verification at the level of an edited critical anthology would be a separate scholarly project and is not claimed here. The chapter's broad comparative argument is historically supportable, and all identified publication-critical discrepancies were corrected.
