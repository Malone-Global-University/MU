## 4. Major corrections and source checks

### Ming dates and dynastic sequence

- Changed **1403** to identify the **28 October imperial order to dispatch Yin Qing**, rather than imply that his arrival was already completed in 1403; noted his arrival may have occurred in **1404**.
- Distinguished **1405 investiture**, 1409 diplomatic contacts, **1411** Parameswara's personal court visit, **1414** report of the earlier ruler's death by a successor, and **1424** succession evidence, without claiming a single harmonized early royal genealogy.
- Retained conventional *approximate* reign-date frameworks and the distinction among Parameswara, Iskandar Shah, and the early royal names. No invented exact conversion date.
- Principal studies: Wang Gungwu (2005), *The Opening of Relations between China and Malacca, 1403–05* (pp. 1–25); Christopher H. Wake (1964), *Malacca's Early Kings and the Reception of Islam*.

### Maritime taxation and commercial institutions

- Corrected the misleading western-*tax* / eastern-*presents-only* contrast in §§64.9 and the Pires source study §64.11. Eastern merchants could also face **required discounted sale of part of their cargo**.
- Borschberg's comparative account describes about **25%** of eastern cargo sold under discounted terms, corresponding to about **5% effective burden**, plus gifts and charges, while western merchants commonly paid about **6%**. These are contextual estimates, not universal constants for every trader or throughout the sultanate's history.
- The final narrative explicitly distinguishes documented early-sixteenth-century practice from inferred earlier arrangements and separates merchants, brokers, laborers and the court as participants with unequal bargaining power.
- Primary references: Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental*, trans./ed. Armando Cortesão, Hakluyt Society, 1944; Peter Borschberg (2020), *The Melaka Empire, c. 1400–1528*, pp. 263–293 (especially p. 279).

### Malay literary and legal manuscript traditions

- Strengthened §64.15's treatment of **Raffles MS 18**, the **Shellabear** family of versions, contested **1612** redaction convention, and earlier textual layers. The ruler–subject covenant window explicitly names Brown/Roolvink (1970) as an English editorial foundation, not a personally collated manuscript passage.
- Corrected discussion of the Terengganu inscription to the conventional **702 AH / 1303 CE** reading versus **Ahmat Adam's 708 AH / 1308 CE** argument. Neither date is direct evidence that the surviving Melaka law compilations were enforced unchanged c. 1400.
- Identified the British Library's **Add MS 12397**, copied in Singapore between 17 February and 11 July **1821**, as a later witness to the land and maritime law traditions; Liaw Yock Fang's critical *Undang-undang Melaka* edition (1976) is correctly identified.
- Framed each legal code as a layered text whose copies, literary ideals and enforcement histories must be distinguished.

### Political interpretation

- Deepened analysis of varying degrees of control (direct administrative presence, marriage diplomacy, tributary relations, maritime influence, retrospective claims), avoiding modern bounded-territory models.
- Deepened the reasons Melaka remained important after Zheng He's last voyage in 1433, while refusing to project Pires's mature 1510s institutions unchanged onto the polity's founding.
- Qualified any claim that Ming diplomatic influence was either a standing protectorate or militarily irrelevant.
