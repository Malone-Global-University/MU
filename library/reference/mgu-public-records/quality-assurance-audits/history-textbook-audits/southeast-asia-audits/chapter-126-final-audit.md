## 1. Governing assignment and production method

The authoritative outline, *SouthEast Asia Chapter Outline(9).txt*, identifies Chapter 126 as **Indonesian Nationalism** and assigns Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, the Indonesian Communist Party, Sukarno, the PNI, youth movements, the 1928 Youth Pledge, Dutch repression and competing visions of Indonesia. The Universal Master Instructions and approved Chapter 126 five-division, twenty-section plan govern historical method, instructional features, scope and review apparatus. The supplied scaffold, `Pasted text(20261122-072418).txt`, was inspected in full. The completed Chapter 125 HTML supplied the stylesheet, chapter shell and navigation model.

This revision treats the scaffold as an organizational foundation, not a nearly complete narrative. Every direct historical section was developed into sustained textbook prose. The approved twenty section titles and feature positions are retained. Tables and reference apparatus are newly completed. The draft's short text is not merely supplemented by redundant feature boxes to create an apparent narrative count: direct narrative is measured separately.

Chronological concentration is approximately 1900–1942. Earlier colonial conquest, economic conditions, education and religious reform are contextualized selectively, in keeping with Chapters 103–104 and 121–125. The collapse of Dutch military authority in March 1942 is the endpoint. The occupation, revolutionary moment and independence declaration remain assigned principally to Chapters 138 and 146–148. The succeeding Chapter 127 on Vietnamese nationalism is foreshadowed only as a comparative transition. Chapter numbering remains continuous across volumes.

## 3. Scholarly audit and factual remediation

### 3.1 Nationhood, education and early organizations (126.1–126.4)

- Replaced the categorical assertion that Indonesia did not exist as a political community in 1900 with an explanation of the difference between established cultural, trade and religious connections, colonial territorial consolidation, and an explicitly Indonesian claim to sovereignty. Colonial population categories are identified as historical administrative and legal classifications, not equivalent to modern ethnic or citizenship definitions. The 1930 census does not measure nationalist affiliation.
- Explained unequal educational investment under the Ethical Policy using Ewout Frankema's 2013 study. Enrollment estimates remain shares of total population, not percentages of school-age children or literacy rates. The description of STOVIA and the new professional classes does not entail a deterministic connection between schooling and political allegiance. Independent Muslim and nationalist schools receive separate treatment.
- Replaced “first modern native association” with a qualified landmark interpretation of Budi Utomo. Discusses the 20 May 1908 founding, Wahidin's scholarship initiative, early educational and cultural objectives, the political role of senior officials, and the October Yogyakarta congress. Akira Nagazumi's specialist work anchors the distinction between original objectives and later national commemoration. Subsequent political activity and the 1935 merger are not projected back onto 1908.
- Reconstructed Sarekat Islam's disputed origin chronology, distinguishing Rekso Roemekso in Surakarta, Tirto Adhi Soerjo's assistance and the 1911–1912 development into SI; 1905 is not asserted as a uniquely established institutional foundation. Shiraishi's chapter specifically documents the Laweyan mutual-protection network, communal confrontations and legal-status problem. The central association's 18 March 1916 recognition is supported by its institutional history. Initial anti-Chinese commercial tensions are acknowledged without generalizing about either ethnic or religious group. The reported membership estimate range and SI's own claim are distinctly labeled.

**Verification basis:** Shiraishi, chapter 2, pp. 41–90, https://doi.org/10.7591/9781501737848-006; von der Mehden, Oxford Bibliographies, https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195390155-0071; SI institutional chronology, https://sii.or.id/artikel/sejarah-singkat-syarikat-islam-indonesia; Nagazumi, catalogue/preview https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Dawn_of_Indonesian_Nationalism.html?id=-UkKAQAAIAAJ; Frankema, https://doi.org/10.14203/jmi.v39i2.595.

### 3.2 Socialism, communism and colonial repression (126.5–126.8)

- Differentiated the 1912 Indische Partij from the Comite Boemi Poetra, which issued Suwardi's July 1913 pamphlet in Dutch and Malay. The administrative measures against the three leaders are not described as a normal criminal sentence for a single press offense. The debate over cross-racial Indies patriotism establishes a recurring problem of future political membership.
- Distinguished the VSTP's earlier existence, foundation of the ISDV in 1914, May 1920 reorganization into the communist association, and adoption of the Indonesian party name in 1924. Removed the implausible chronological claim concerning the Soviet Union and Mongolia; the party is described as among Asia's earliest communist parties. Crawford's article details the Malay and Dutch names adopted in 1920 and the significance of translation.
- Expanded the 1921 dual-membership restriction, 1923 formation of Sarekat Rakyat, ideological differences and local organizational overlap. Tan Malaka's educational activity, international connections and argument against premature revolt are distinct from the decisions of other leaders. The text does not equate all Marxist activity with formal PKI membership or Islamic leadership with rejection of all social reform.
- Expanded the defeat of the 1923 railway strike, leadership disagreements, Prambanan decision, regional outbreaks in Java and West Sumatra in November 1926 and January 1927, and subsequent repression. Xie's dissertation documents the arrests, banishment estimate, limits of Comintern direction and persistence of transnational communist networks. Numerical categories are not added together or treated as a roster of guilty persons. Boven-Digoel is identified as a location of administrative internment; the chapter does not infer that the later nationalist detainees there were involved in the communist uprising. The 1927 establishment of PARI is distinguished from restoration of the original open PKI.

**Verification basis:** Tsuchiya, *Southeast Asian Studies* 15(2), 131–152, https://doi.org/10.20495/tak.15.2_131; Ons Land publication record, https://www.onsland.nl/gebeurtenissen/publicatie%20als%20ik%20eens%20nederlander%20was; Crawford, *Modern Asian Studies* 55(3), 697–733, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0026749X20000104; Xie, *Estranged Comrades* (2018), introduction and repository text, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/79314435. McVey (1965), Benda and McVey (1960), Ingleson (1986) and Kahin's regional study (1999) are specialist foundations for further reading, not claims of a new page-by-page review of those works.

### 3.3 Nationalist thought, PI and PNI (126.9–126.12)

- Clarified the overseas association's development from Indische Vereeniging through the Indonesian names of the 1920s. Historical accounts differ on the precise year for the transition to the Perhimpunan Indonesia name and journal name (1924 or 1925); the narrative identifies the 1924–1925 transition and the 1925 independence manifesto. Hindia Poetra is placed during, not before, the First World War. Established Hatta's role in Brussels in 1927 and the arrest and acquittal of the four students in 1927–1928. Stutje's account establishes that the PI had a small active membership despite its international visibility.
- Substantially expanded the political meaning of Sukarno's 1926 essay, distinguishing the three traditions, shared opposition to imperialism, unresolved questions of social and religious authority and the later Nasakom formulation. Marhaenism is treated as an evolving philosophy and the famous encounter with a smallholder as a biographical tradition rather than an independently verified conversation.
- Established the original nationalist association's foundation on 4 July 1927, the 1928 party name and the December 1927 federation. Noncooperation, self-reliance, political education, public campaigning and coalition building are discussed as institutional strategies. The party's membership limits are not confused with a definitive constitutional conception of future citizenship.
- Clarified arrests on 29 December 1929, the Bandung Landraad proceedings of 1930, defendants and defense, Sukarno's four-year sentence, PNI dissolution in April 1931 and release in December 1931. The speech's invocation of Articles 153-bis and 169 is identified as the defendant's own account rather than used as an independent reconstruction of the indictment. The printed defense is not presented as a verbatim transcript of trial testimony. The original PNI is distinguished from the later Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia.

**Verification basis:** Poeze and Schulte Nordholt, *Merdeka*, prewar chapter, https://doi.org/10.1515/9789048560844-002; Klaas Stutje, “Herald of a Failed Revolt,” pp. 309–324 (2020), https://pure.knaw.nl/portal/en/publications/herald-of-a-failed-revolt-mohammad-hatta-in-brussels-1927/; Sukarno's 1963 edition, https://id.wikisource.org/wiki/Nasionalisme%2C_Islamisme%2C_dan_Marxisme; Cornell translation catalogue, https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp104218; *Indonesia Menggugat*, https://id.wikisource.org/wiki/Indonesia_Menggugat; national archives chronology, https://pusdipres.anri.go.id/page-timeline?jenis=submenu_sub&menu_id=30; Museum Dewantara Kirti Griya chronology of the defendants, charges and sentences as transcribed at https://id.wikisource.org/wiki/Halaman%3A20_Mei_Pelopor_17_Agustus_-_Museum_Dewantara_Kirti_Griya.pdf/34.

### 3.4 Youth, women and cultural politics (126.13–126.16)

- Corrected the Jong Java chronology: Tri Koro Darmo was established on 7 March 1915 and adopted the name Jong Java at the congress in Solo on 12 June 1918. The changing constituency is interpreted without assuming all members abandoned regional identity. Other regional and religious associations are included; they are not represented as a single homogeneous youth movement.
- Distinguished the First Youth Congress in 1926 from the Second Youth Congress of 27–28 October 1928. The full resolution's third provision is reproduced as a concise excerpt with editorial translation: it upholds Indonesian as the language of unity, not as the only language spoken in Indonesia. The chapter includes the wider resolution's references to sources of solidarity and dissemination, the PPPI's organizational role and the violin performance of Indonesia Raya. The collective authorship of the congress's decision is preserved.
- Expanded the First Women's Congress (22–25 December 1928) with the principal organizers R.A. Sukonto, Nyi Hajar Dewantara and Suyatin Kartowiyono; addresses on marriage and divorce, child marriage, education and women's social standing; subsequent federation; and the careers of women in religious and nationalist institutions. The variant rendering Perikatan/Perserikatan Perkumpulan Perempuan Indonesia is noted and the initials PPPI distinguished from the students' organization. Susan Blackburn's edited proceedings are identified as a specific documentary resource. Speaker titles and agendas are mediated by the Ministry of Culture's description, not fabricated quotations from unavailable original speeches.
- Developed the distinct political significance of Bahasa Indonesia, songs, flags, newspapers and cultural journals, including the role of indirect readership. A described scenario in the everyday-life feature is explicitly analytical reconstruction, not an invented diary. The chapter does not infer support from circulation or attendance alone.

**Verification basis:** Museum Sumpah Pemuda, https://muspada.kemenbud.go.id/jong-java/ and https://muspada.kemenbud.go.id/sejarah-sumpah-pemuda/; Ministry of Culture, https://esi.kemenbud.go.id/wiki/Kongres_Wanita_Indonesia_I; retrospective movement record, https://id.wikisource.org/wiki/Buku_Peringatan_30_Tahun_Kesatuan_Pergerakan_Wanita_Indonesia/Bab_1; Blackburn's edited proceedings, https://www.ipgbook.com/the-first-indonesian-women-s-congress-of-1928-products-9781876924485.php; Lin Hongxuan, https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n10284/html/ch01.xhtml.

### 3.5 The 1930s, coercion and the endpoint (126.17–126.20)

- Expanded Partindo, PNI Baru and Permi as distinct political projects with overlapping intellectual influences and different organizational priorities. The archive chronology establishes Sukarno's entry into Partindo on 1 August 1932 and Hatta's return in August 1932, rather than immediate membership after release in December 1931. The four August 1933 reported membership estimates are sourced to Lin Hongxuan's ANU Press chapter and geographic scopes are indicated. The pedagogical model of the Pendidikan is not described as necessarily immune from repression or as permanently limited to urban elites.
- Distinguished criminal prosecution, administrative exile, police surveillance, restrictions on meetings and administrative press bans. The 1931 Persbreidelordonnantie is identified with its date and Staatsblad number, carrying forward the verified legal chronology in Chapter 124. Exiles are separated chronologically: Sukarno to Ende in 1934 and Bengkulu in 1938; Hatta and Sjahrir to Boven-Digoel in 1934 and Banda Neira in 1936. The second arrest of Sukarno is dated late July 1933, avoiding the tendency to treat later administrative measures as the date of his arrest. Text discusses the effects on ordinary participants and families as well as famous detainees.
- Established the petition of 15 July 1936, vote of 26–20, transmission on 1 October, rejection in November 1938, foundation of GAPI on 21 May 1939, and parliamentary campaign. The proposal is for equal-basis constitutional negotiations and gradual autonomy, not immediate transfer of full sovereignty. Distinguishes Parindra and Gerindo, identifies cross-community signatories and GAPI leadership, and sets the Visman Commission within the wider crisis of fascism and war. The council vote is not a referendum. GAPI is not described as having secured the desired parliament.
- The final section explains the relationship between surviving political capacities and persistent ideological divisions, including Islamic cooperation, communist continuities, noncooperating organizations, constitutional activism, youth, women and culture. It ends with Dutch military collapse in March 1942, not with Indonesian sovereignty. The outcome is explained through international military developments as well as the earlier political context, not through an inevitable victory of one nationalist strategy.

**Verification basis:** Indonesian National Archives chronology (above); Lin Hongxuan, in Galway and Opper (eds.), *Experiments with Marxism-Leninism in Cold War Southeast Asia* (ANU Press, 2022), ch. 1, https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n10284/html/ch01.xhtml; Abeyasekere, “The Soetardjo Petition,” *Indonesia* 15 (April 1973), 81–107, https://hdl.handle.net/1813/53559; Indonesian Ministry of Culture, https://esi.kemenbud.go.id/wiki/Petisi_Soetardjo and https://esi.kemenbud.go.id/wiki/Gabungan_Politik_Indonesia. For the 1933 arrest, compare the retrospective institutional chronology and M.F. Mukhti's account of party successors at https://www.historia.id/article/sanak-saudara-partai-nasionalis-vqzak.

## 6. Bibliographic verification and residual limitations

Bibliography increased from seven short references to 40 identified items, including contemporary documents, later historical transcriptions, scholarly studies, institutional records and official statistical reproduction. Bibliographical records for Crawford, Frankema, Tsuchiya, Stutje, Abeyasekere, the 1970 Cornell translation, Poeze and Schulte Nordholt, Xie and the 2008 Blackburn edition were checked against accessible publisher, repository or library records. Directly accessed text, article extracts, institutional summaries and historical transcriptions are identified at point of use. General scholarly monographs are retained as established foundations and recommended further reading; this audit does not claim that every page, edition, citation or bibliographic detail of all 40 works has been independently checked. The original Dutch colonial police files, full first-edition newspapers and official court file were not exhaustively reviewed. No unpublished documents are presented as inspected. No historical quotations were manufactured to conceal access limitations.

Where accessible government and institutional pages return a search-indexed extract but direct opening is restricted (including some Indonesian encyclopaedia and repository pages), the verification is based on the accessible extracted text and corroborating scholarly records. Those routes may require ordinary library access or later rechecking. The Soetardjo and women's congress sources should be supplemented with original proceedings if the publisher is able to obtain them for a future documentary edition. These are limitations of evidence access, not assertions that the documents do not exist.

The chapter does not rely on modern population percentages to represent historical colonial conditions, extrapolate nationalist membership from a census, combine overlapping legal categories, or treat names in later political history as unchanged institutional designations. Important interpretive uncertainty is retained explicitly, including the SI origins and PI naming transition. The chapter does not claim full independence was the endpoint of the events of 1942.
