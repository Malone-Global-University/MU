## 4. Historical and source-focused consolidated corrections

1. **The 1379 eastern interlocutor and the partition hypothesis.** §52.3 uses Toru Aoyama's 1992 reassessment: the eastern figure in the 1379 Ming notice may be associated with Wengker, and the Ming's east/west categories do not demonstrate two formally partitioned territorial states. The alternative of rival court compounds is explained without being made dogma. Source: Aoyama, *Southeast Asia: History and Culture* 21 (1992), 65–87; https://doi.org/10.5512/sea.1992.65 .
2. **Paregreg and Chinese reports.** §§52.4–52.5 retain a conventionally dated civil-war climax around 1406 but distinguish later diplomatic/indemnity entries in 1407–1408. The reported 170 Ming personnel are explicitly a source-reported figure, not a Javanese death toll or independently verified body count. Aoyama emphasizes the 1406 political outcome; Geoff Wade labels the violence involving Ming personnel under 1407. The chapter avoids silently merging those documentary datings. Sources: Aoyama 1992; Geoff Wade, *Ming China and Southeast Asia in the 15th Century: A Reappraisal*, NUS ARI Working Paper 28 (2004), https://ari.nus.edu.sg/publications/wps-28-ming-china-and-southeast-asia-in-the-15th-century-a-reappraisal/ .
3. **Waringin Pitu.** §52.6 and its source window specify the 22 November 1447 copper-plate grant issued under Dyah Kertawijaya for the sacred foundation Rajasa Kusumapura; royal kin and ceremony are treated as evidence of institutional persistence, not proof of uniformly effective sovereignty. Indonesian Ministry of Education, *Prasasti dan Raja-Raja Nusantara*, Waringin Pitu entry, https://repositori.kemendikdasmen.go.id/24363/1/Prasasti%20dan%20raja-raja%20nusantara.pdf .
4. **Late succession.** §52.7 identifies successive rulers as scholarly reconstructions with gaps and ambiguous titles; the 1473 Pamintihan charter is included as a dated point of evidence. J. Noorduyn, “Majapahit in the Fifteenth Century,” *Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde* **134, nos. 2–3** (1978), pp. 207–274, https://brill.com/downloadpdf/journals/bki/134/2/article-p207_2.pdf .
5. **Ma Huan.** §52.11 distinguishes first-person observation, later compilation, a 1416 foreword, 1444 prefatory material, and the 1451 afterword/publication tradition. The earlier draft's claim “published 1433” is corrected and the chronology preview rewritten accordingly. Ma Huan, *Ying-yai Sheng-lan*, edited/translated by J. V. G. Mills (1970); bibliographical history also summarized at https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=en&remap=gb&res=792434 . The first edition is not extant in identical form, and the chapter says so.
6. **Coastal ports, Melaka and Islamization.** §§52.9–52.16 distinguish commercial growth from political sovereignty and religious conversion from independent Muslim rule. They avoid portraying Demak as definitively sovereign by an unsupported early date or reducing the region to Hindu-Buddhist extinction followed by Islam.
7. **1478 and the 1486 evidence.** §§52.17–52.20 distinguish a retrospective chronogram from contemporary legal sources. The Petak and Trailokyapuri I–III charters of 1486 attest a royal claimant and record conflict involving Majapahit; therefore they support neither an absolute end in 1478 nor a claim that no disruption occurred. Noorduyn's interbranch conflict/reconquest interpretation is explicitly labeled a reconstruction rather than a secure account of every event.
8. **Early sixteenth-century boundary.** Tomé Pires is used only as a bounded retrospective epilogue. The conventionally cited c. 1527 endpoint is not asserted as a secure contemporaneous terminal date. Full accounts of succeeding Islamic polities remain outside the assigned Chapter 52 responsibility.
