## 4. Historical and documentary verification

The following records and research resources were consulted in this revision, using the text, displayed excerpts, catalogue records, or publisher descriptions as indicated. A linked bibliography is not a representation that every source was read in full. Claims were narrowed when available material did not justify a more specific interpretation.

### 4.1 Surrender and command responsibility

- **Issued General Order No. 1, September 2, 1945:** [Nagoya University SCAPIN-1](https://jahis.law.nagoya-u.ac.jp/scapindb/docs/scapin-1). The displayed full directive and attachment were inspected, including paragraphs I(a), I(c)(1), I(c)(2), I(e), I(f), II, VI, and IX. The assigned recipients are the Republic of China's commander for northern Indochina; SEAC for southern Indochina, Burma, Thailand, Malaya, Java, Sumatra, Celebes, the Lesser Sundas including Timor, Halmahera, Dutch New Guinea and other listed islands; Australian Military Forces for Borneo and designated southwest Pacific territories; and US Army Forces, Pacific for the Philippines. The order permits surrender to the designated commanders or their representatives; it does not confer political sovereignty.
- **Surrender diplomacy:** [FRUS, 1945, VI, document 412](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v06/d412) reproduces the Allied reply of August 11. [US National Archives, Instrument of Surrender](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/surrender-of-japan) confirms the final subordination of Japanese governmental authority to the Supreme Commander. The revision does not treat the Japanese initial reservation as a mutually accepted guarantee of imperial prerogatives.
- **Borneo and Timor:** [Australian War Memorial, F07316](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/F07316) supports the Baba–Wootten surrender date; [Australian DVA, Japanese Surrender](https://clik.dva.gov.au/book/export/html/14809) identifies Dyke's Dutch Timor role. [Australian DFAT document 139](https://www.dfat.gov.au/about-us/publications/historical-documents/Pages/volume-08/139-commonwealth-government-to-cranborne) supports the BBCAU's Australian military organizational status and British participation. The chapter distinguishes these temporary military responsibilities from political sovereignty and postwar colonial status.
- **British return:** [National Library Board, Surviving the Japanese Occupation](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-12-issue-4-jan-mar-2017-surviving-jpnese-occupation/) supports the September 5 return to Singapore. [Raffles Library and Museum (1942–1945)](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=25493e94-6ed6-4add-b765-a7469c907e74) supports the library example.

### 4.2 Humanitarian recovery and constitutional restoration

- [Australian DVA, Prisoners and labourers on the Burma–Thailand Railway](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/prisoners) identifies separate prisoner and worker histories and Henk Hovinga's repatriation research. [Shirlene Noordin, My Grandfather Was a Rōmusha, BiblioAsia (2022)](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-18-issue-3-oct-dec-2022-grandfather-romusha-thai-burma-railway/) supplies published family history and a recollection of volunteer assistance to surviving Asian laborers. The text deliberately does not equate relief coverage, release, transport, and social reintegration. It avoids projecting Allied POW statistics onto Asian workers. No comprehensive romusha recovery total is claimed.
- [National Library Board, The Stories They Could Tell](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-13-issue-4-jan-mar-2018-stories-they-tell/) identifies the partial and institutionally situated character of surviving BMA records. It supports the methodological comparison of official records and civilian evidence, not a claim that every household experienced currency replacement in the same way.
- [Josef Silverstein, The Political Legacy of Aung San, document 6, pp. 35–38](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctv1nhmh9.10): accessible indexed text of the agreed conclusions of September 7 establishes the 5,200 ceiling and limited additional eligible intake. The revised passage does not claim the whole PBF entered the regular army.
- [Commonwealth Executive Order No. 30, March 8, 1945](https://lawphil.net/executive/execord/eo1945/eo_30_1945.html) supports the substantive financial-administrative example in §144.11. It reactivated operating appropriations and relevant existing law. The exercise distinguishes documented legal action from actual territorial implementation.

### 4.3 Contested restoration in Indonesia and Indochina

- [Nationaal Archief, WO 203 catalogue](https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/onderzoeken/archief/2.22.21/invnr/117/file/NL-HaNA_2.22.21_117_0003) identifies military headquarters documentation on Java, Sumatra, AFNEI and RAPWI. This catalogue is a research route, not a complete reading of the unit records. The 5th, 23rd and 26th Indian formations are identified without implying identical arrival or command dates; the precise formal Christison appointment chronology has not been fully reverified and no newly asserted exact appointment date was inserted.
- [NIOD, Diplomatie en geweld](https://www.niod.nl/en/publications/diplomatie-en-geweld/) and [Beyond the Pale](https://www.niod.nl/en/publications/beyond-the-pale/) identify the international-context study and the research programme on Independence, Decolonization, Violence and War in Indonesia, 1945–1950. The summary differentiates the violence of the immediate transition from the ensuing independence war and does not convert either into a claim of collective guilt.
- [Peter Neville, Britain in Vietnam: Prelude to Disaster, 1945–46](https://www.routledge.com/Britain-in-Vietnam/Neville/p/book/9780415487474), publisher description, explicitly identifies the controversy over Gracey and French colonialism and constraints on resources. [Journal article, “Kicking out the Vietminh”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/30036312), indexed excerpt, demonstrates that the interpretation of Gracey as simply disobeying orders is contested. The chapter neither exonerates nor assigns undocumented private intentions to Gracey. It distinguishes the outcome of British decisions from the question of policy authorization.
- Shawn McHale, [Ethnicity, Violence, and Khmer-Vietnamese Relations](https://www2.gwu.edu/~sigur/assets/docs/publications/journals/McHale_2013_Ethnic%20Violence.pdf), indexed passage, identifies French General Leclerc as responsible for the arrest of Son Ngoc Thanh; searchable excerpt from *Cambodge: The Cultivation of a Nation, 1860–1945* identifies October 15. The revision corrects British arrest attribution and distinguishes the surrender mandate from French political restoration.
- Bruce M. Lockhart, [Narrating 1945 in Lao Historiography, pp. 129–163](https://www.academia.edu/77605088/Historiography_power_and_identity_History_and_political_legitimization_in_Laos), displayed substantial excerpt, supports the October 12 proclamation, October 20 deposition vote, Khammao Vilai's cabinet, and the subsequent November relinquishment of royal power under pressure. These events did not produce a uniform or permanent political settlement. The revised conclusion no longer treats Laos as restored to French authority by December 1945.

### 4.4 Thailand and the peace settlement

- [Pridi Banomyong Institute, full Thai-language Peace Proclamation](https://pridi.or.th/th/content/2021/08/797), including original publication citation: Royal Thai Government Gazette, vol. 62, no. 44, August 16, 1945, pp. 503–506. The complete reproduced Thai text was read. The proclamation identifies Pridi as Regent and Tawee Bunyaketu as countersignatory. Its specified territorial undertaking concerns territory entrusted to Thailand by Japan. The French Indochinese territorial question is therefore not represented as conclusively resolved by the proclamation.
- [FRUS, 1945, VI, document 945](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v06/d945) confirms the difference between the British state-of-war position and the American liberation policy. [FRUS document 968](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v06/d968) confirms the contested rice negotiations; [document 953](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v06/d953) concerns proposed agreements, not final terms. The final [UK Treaty Series No. 10 (1951), January 1, 1946 agreement and exchanges](https://treaties.fcdo.gov.uk/data/Library2/pdf/1951-TS0010.pdf) is linked as the definitive treaty reference. Only indexed treaty material was inspected in this revision; not every clause or later implementing agreement was independently verified. The narrative was narrowed accordingly, omitting the unsupported exact rice-contribution comparison and claim of a British UN-admission undertaking.
- [FRUS, 1946, VIII, Siam chapter](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v08/ch11) explicitly reports the Thai Supreme Court's March 23, 1946 ruling and release of the defendants. The chapter now differentiates constitutional invalidation of retrospective penal provisions from an ordinary acquittal or repeal.
- Tarling's [Rice and Reconciliation](https://thesiamsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/1978/03/JSS_066_2c_Tarling_RiceAndReconciliationAngloThaiPeaceNegotiations1945.pdf) provides a specialist research path into the negotiations. Bibliographical corrections include the 59–111 page range and Ooi Keat Gin's full *Post-war Borneo, 1945–50* subtitle. These metadata corrections follow the preceding bibliographic audit; the complete books and all edition records were not independently reinspected here.

### 5.2 §144.17 — Thai Peace Proclamation

Replaced the initial secondhand paraphrase and the instruction for the library to obtain the missing text. The new exercise is based on the complete Thai-language reproduction. It identifies the original Gazette publication, maker, date, countersignatory, source type and perspective. The English rendition is explicitly an editorial paraphrase, not an official translation or direct quotation. It identifies the constitutional argument, the specific territorial undertaking, review of hostile laws, redress, and maintenance of order. It does not present the Regent's claims concerning public opinion or constitutional validity as independent proof. Five source-analysis questions address authorship, purpose, meaning, political context and evidentiary limitations. The original Gazette scan and an independently authenticated English translation were not inspected; the accessible complete Thai transcription supplies the documentary basis.

### 5.3 §144.20 — Historical Debate

The debate has been reframed to distinguish scholarly fields of emphasis, evidence, causation, and the genuine controversy about British intervention in southern Indochina. Rather than ascribe specific unsupported political verdicts to Bayly, Harper, Reid, Kahin, Marr, Callahan or Donnison, it identifies their works as research paths. Neville's publisher description and the indexed opposing-to-the-simple-overreach interpretation provide identifiable support for the narrower debate about Gracey's role. It identifies what further evidence would distinguish explanations and asks students to compare territories. No two-camp caricature of scholarly opinion is imposed.

## 8. Remaining limitations and editorial decisions

1. Full texts of the primary-source reproductions used in both formal windows were inspected. The Thai text was paraphrased in English rather than represented as an official translation. The original physical archival documents and the Thai Gazette facsimile were not examined.
2. The military formations and formal appointment chronology, dates of regional landings, individual local negotiations, full treaty clauses, and bibliographic metadata are not exhaustively independently checked. The chapter uses appropriately qualified claims and avoids adding a precise unverified formal AFNEI appointment date. The precise scope and implementation of Thai rice obligations are left to the treaty and specialist sources.
3. Several monographs and archival collections are supplied for further study. Publisher metadata, excerpts, and catalogue entries were used where full texts were unavailable. The final chapter's bibliography makes that distinction explicit; it does not certify that all 43 entries were read in full.
4. The use of section-level inline research links and a linked bibliography was chosen to improve traceability while retaining the existing model's styling. Counts include repeated link occurrences. The bibliography includes existing entries inherited from the draft, not all of which were individually checked for link resolution or edition details.
5. The manuscript retains the original educational design, including the original navigation structure and responsive CSS, pending the user's ordinary local browser check. The final page is somewhat longer than the proposed maximum, but the direct historical narrative target and all instructional requirements are satisfied.
6. The regional distribution remains comparative rather than encyclopedic: detailed histories of Surabaya, the Indonesian revolution, Vietnam's August Revolution and subsequent war, and Cambodian and Lao independence are reserved for later assigned chapters. This is a purposeful boundary decision, not an omission requiring another general expansion pass.
