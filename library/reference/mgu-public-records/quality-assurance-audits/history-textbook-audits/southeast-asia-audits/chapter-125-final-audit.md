## 3. Historical remediation and source verification

### 3.1 Religious authority, agency and jurisdiction

The original statement that royal religious authority ended or became purely nominal in conquered or protected territories was overbroad. The manuscript now differentiates the complete removal of the Konbaung throne from the survival of protected monarchies in Cambodia and the Malay states and the Nguyễn court in Annam. Siam is explicitly independent. British Malaya is not treated as a single legal jurisdiction. Property, religious court jurisdiction, local patronage and control of schools are treated as mechanisms of institutional change rather than generic outcomes of colonialism.

Attention is given to non-exclusive religious practices, different religious constituencies within the same jurisdiction, migrant associations and the historical inadequacy of census categories as comprehensive accounts of religious life. The historical census category “animist” is explicitly identified as an administrative grouping rather than an adequate description of diverse indigenous traditions. The original scaffold's use of broad categories has thus been retained only with necessary explanation.

### 3.2 Burmese Buddhism

The revision distinguishes abolition of kingship from disappearance of monastic government. It identifies the persistence of the thathanabaing office, dispute over succession after 1895 and eventual recognition in the early twentieth century. Rather than claim the state uniformly withdrew from religious life, the account explains its reluctance to exercise the former ruler's role and the continued importance of local patrons. The discussion incorporates Alicia Turner's distinction between preservation of the sāsana and retrospective nationalist interpretation.

Ledi Sayadaw's career is placed in the context of Abhidhamma, print and the expansion of lay instruction without crediting him with the invention of meditation. His Manual of Insight is now identified in the U Ñāṇa translation, 1965 collection edition. The publisher's description of the print history and the accessible English text were consulted: https://www.aimwell.org/vipassanadipani.html and https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/manualofinsight/index.html. The later English publication date is not mistaken for the original composition date. Braun's specialist work anchors the interpretation; detailed claims about individual teaching lineages are cautiously phrased.

### 3.3 Siam and French Indochina

The manuscript develops the distinction between the Dhammayut and Mahanikai orders, Mongkut's earlier monastic activity, the 1902 Sangha Act, Wachirayan Warorot's educational role and the persistence of regional sources of authority. Khruba Siwichai is presented as a historical example of conflict over monastic legitimacy, not as evidence of universal provincial resistance.

The institutional sequence for Cambodia and Laos was checked against Søren Ivarsson, *Creating Laos*, pp. 123–124, accessible extract: https://www.academia.edu/69606354/Creating_Laos_the_making_of_a_Lao_space_between_Indochina_and_Siam_1860_1945. It establishes the 1914 Phnom Penh Pali school, 1925 Royal Library, 1928 Lao clergy reorganization, 1930 Buddhist Institute, 1931 Vientiane and 1933 Luang Prabang sections. The revision distinguishes colonial cultural policy from the purposes of indigenous scholars and avoids portraying Lao and Cambodian Buddhism as extensions of Siamese Buddhism.

The dates of Vietnamese Buddhist associations and their journals were checked against Ninh Thị Sinh, “The Rise of Vietnamese Nuns: Views from the Buddhist Revival Movement (1931–1945),” *Religions* 13 (2022), 1189, https://doi.org/10.3390/rel13121189. The 1931 Cochinchina, 1932 Annam and 1934 Tonkin association dates are distinguished from the subsequent first issues of Từ Bi Âm, Viên Âm and Đuốc Tuệ. The article's survey of 56 pieces by Buddhist nuns is identified as a publication count, not a population count or enrollment statistic. The study period extends to 1945, and the chapter explicitly notes this limit when citing its evidence.

### 3.4 Islamic reform

The revised account distinguishes Meccan study and pilgrimage, Egyptian publishing, Jawi networks, the diversity of reformist and established scholarship, and the relationship to local education. Ijtihād and taqlīd are explained as complex interpretive practices rather than a simplistic reason-versus-imitation binary. The chronology of Al-Imam and Al-Munir remains intact, with named editors and Minangkabau institutions introduced. The specific article “Pelajaran,” Al-Imam 1(1), 23 July 1906, pp. 21–26, is established through Mohammad Redzuan Othman and Abu Hanifah Haris, *British Journal of Middle Eastern Studies* 42 (2015), 465–480, note 40, https://doi.org/10.1080/13530194.2015.1005965. That note identifies the two underlying Al-Manār articles of April 1899. The source window does not manufacture an excerpt or claim an independent translation of the original Malay article.

The account of Muhammadiyah identifies Ahmad Dahlan, education, administration, welfare and local financing. Aisyiyah's formal leadership is distinguished from mentorship. The institutional biography of Siti Bariyah, https://aisyiyah.or.id/siti-bariyah/, directly substantiates her role as first chair from 1917, participation in religious interpretation and the 1926 periodical. Siti Walidah's subsequent leadership is acknowledged. Rahmah el Yunusiyah and Diniyah Puteri provide an additional example of women's independent educational agency. The NU narrative now explains Hasyim Asy'ari, Wahab Hasbullah, the pesantren networks, the Hijaz Committee and political changes in Arabia; it avoids explaining traditionalist organization as refusal of all change. MIAI is introduced as evidence of selective organizational cooperation, not as a unitary political movement.

The broader narrative draws on established specialist literature, notably Roff, Noer, Laffan and van Bruinessen. Individual school enrollment and membership totals are not introduced without separate documentary support. Organizational founding years are used as chronological markers, not as measures of social reach.

### 3.5 Christianity and religious innovation

Christianity is situated in its distinct colonial and precolonial settings, with cases from Burma, Sumatra, the Philippines, Vietnam and Portuguese Timor. The discussion avoids equating local Christian communities with colonial administrations. It explains local teachers and evangelists, mission schooling, the limits of conversion indicators and indigenous clergy's claims to religious authority.

The Philippine source deficiency is remediated through the 1906 Philippine Supreme Court decision *Barlin v. Ramirez*, G.R. No. L-2832, November 24, 1906: https://angkaalaman.com/library/decision/gr-l2832-1906-11-24/. The reproduced November 9, 1902 Lagonoy resolution and November 14 letter of Vicente Ramirez were inspected in the accessible transcription. The resolution's conditional separation and stated continuity of faith are distinguished from the subsequent judicial disposition of a property dispute. The document is explicitly not identified as the national founding proclamation. The text distinguishes August 1902 founding, Aglipay's subsequent leadership, the religious changes that occurred after the founding, and the legal distinction between religious liberty and property ownership.

Cao Đài's October 7 declaration and October 15 manifesto are treated as distinct documents, followed by the November inauguration. The annotated English translation by Jérémy Jammes and Trương Tấn Nghiệp is identified by title, volume pages and DOI: https://doi.org/10.30965/25217038-01501011. Its 2023 volume designation and 2024 online publication are distinguished. The manuscript uses the translated title and a clearly qualified analysis of the scholarly edition's description, not an invented doctrinal quotation. The Hòa Hảo account identifies its 1939 emergence, Huỳnh Phú Sổ and links to local Buddhist-inspired renewal while reserving later military history for subsequent chapters. Both religious movements are described according to their own religious and institutional purposes, not reduced to later political roles.

### 3.6 Political causation and intercommunal relations

The final division has been expanded from 158 to 2,940 direct narrative words. The revised mechanism of mobilization is: religious obligations and recognized authority support institutions; institutions accumulate personnel, funds, records and trusted networks; specific political controversies can activate those resources; participants choose whether and how to mobilize; state response and coalition formation influence outcomes. This does not presume every religious organization pursued independence.

The pagoda footwear controversy, disputes over independent schooling in the Netherlands Indies, Philippine parish litigation, and the interpretation of Saya San's rebellion serve as cases. The discussion distinguishes official categorization from participants' objectives, including the limits of colonial trial evidence. The treatment of violence in Burma in the 1930s distinguishes economic, administrative, ethnic, religious and political factors and rejects collective blame. The chapter remains a history of institutional and moral organization rather than a premature account of the national movements in later chapters.

## 5. Comparative and numerical evidence

Seven dedicated tables are present in chapter order, separate from the chronology preview and the instructional timeline.

1. Religious authority and jurisdictions, c. 1930: qualitative legal and institutional comparison, not an exhaustive statement of statutory authority.
2. Religious classification in Burma, 1931: original historical statistical evidence. J. J. Bennison, *Census of India, 1931, Vol. XI: Burma, Part I—Report* (1933), marginal table 7, p. 49. The original report and page image (PDF page index 69) were inspected: https://www.burmalibrary.org/sites/burmalibrary.org/files/obl/docs22/1931_Census_of_India-Vol-XI-Burma1-tpo.pdf. Government catalogue: https://censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/31795. The table reproduces percentages of the province, urban population and rural population, not the share of each religious group living in towns. Each column has a different denominator. The rural column totals 100.1 because of rounding. No modern religious percentages are substituted. The approved plan contemplated comparative historical census figures across jurisdictions; this edition employs a documented within-Burma comparison instead of an unsupported cross-colonial dataset. The restricted coverage is identified directly in the table note. A further cross-jurisdictional comparison would require definitions and original census reports, not estimates invented for coverage.
3. Buddhist reform institutions: dated organizational evidence cross-checked with Ivarsson and Ninh Thị Sinh for Indochina; institution dates distinguished from periodical publication.
4. Islamic organizations and periodicals: dates, locations and institutional characteristics, not membership estimates.
5. Christian and new religious institutions: formative periods and types, no conflation of mission networks with single founding dates.
6. Educational, charitable and publishing infrastructure: documentary milestones and the Ninh Thị Sinh article count, with scope and unit specified.
7. Religious reform and political mobilization: comparative synthesis that distinguishes institutional concerns, political uses and limits to inference.

No unsupported numerical membership, attendance, donation or conversion series has been fabricated. The quantitative evidence is narrower than ideal for a region-wide statistical study; this limitation is not hidden. Qualitative comparisons are used where historical categories and counts cannot be made comparable in the available evidence.

## 7. Bibliography and evidentiary limitations

Thirty-seven entries comprise contemporary documents, later editions, statistical sources, scholarship and research guides. Directly consulted or checked resources include the census report and its catalogue; the accessible Ledi translation and publisher's notes; the Othman and Haris article's accessible abstract and detailed bibliographical notes; the Lagonoy documents in the accessible 1906 judgment; the Cao Đài annotated translation's bibliographical record and abstract; the Ivarsson excerpt; the Ninh Thị Sinh full article; and the Aisyiyah institutional biography. Their direct references appear where relevant in the text. Other specialist books are included as identifiable scholarly foundations and further reading, with thematic pointers at the end of each historical section. Full independent page-level verification and library catalogue confirmation of every retained book was not performed. The bibliography does not claim such comprehensive verification.

This audit does not certify claims for which the original source has not been inspected, and distinguishes the verified identity of a text from verified content of every sentence. The two mediated source windows and restricted comparative census coverage are the principal remaining documentary limitations. They have been disclosed in the manuscript itself. The scholarly approach is to supply an accurate source-specific reading or cautious historical synthesis in preference to inventing an extract or numerical series. Additional archival verification of fine-grained founding histories, local institutional arrangements and the surviving editions would enrich future scholarly work. The broad comparative claims should continue to be interpreted in light of the specified reading and evidence notes rather than as discrete archival findings in each paragraph.

## 10. Final editorial disposition
