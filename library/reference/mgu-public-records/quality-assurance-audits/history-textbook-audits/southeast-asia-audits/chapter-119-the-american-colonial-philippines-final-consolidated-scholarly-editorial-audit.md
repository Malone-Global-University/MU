## 3. Main historical corrections and verification

1. **Sedition Act/Organic Act sequence corrected.** The Sedition Act (Act No. 292) was enacted **November 4, 1901**, *before* the Philippine Organic Act of **July 1, 1902**. The revised text also explains that the specified prohibition on advocacy of independence was associated with the legally recognized condition of war or insurrection. Direct source: [Philippine Senate, Act No. 292](https://issuances-library.senate.gov.ph/legislative-issuance/act-no-292).
2. **1907 electoral franchise corrected.** The literacy eligibility category under Act No. 1582 required **English or Spanish**, not merely a Philippine language. Alternative qualifying categories included specified pre-August 1898 officeholding or minimum property/tax requirements, with age/residence and gender restrictions. The text distinguishes approximately **104,966 registered** from roughly **98,000 votes cast**, without confusing either with the whole adult population. Direct source: [Philippine Supreme Court E-Library, Act No. 1582](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/28/20564).
3. **Friar-land purchase figures corrected.** The December 22, 1903 contracts specified approximately **164,127 hectares**, or approximately **405,570 acres**, for **US$7,239,784.66**. Sellers comprised named corporate estate holders and the Recollect order, rather than a direct conveyance by the Vatican. The final text distinguishes contracted acreage from completed redistribution and discusses surveying, financing, and tenant access. Direct source: [Act No. 1120](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/28/20477).
4. **Thomasite chronology refined.** Approximately **48 teachers** arrived on the *Sheridan* in July 1901; **540** reached Manila aboard the *Thomas* on **August 21, 1901**. Local Filipino teachers remain central to the explanatory narrative. Verification: [NHCP Thomasites historical marker](https://philhistoricsites.nhcp.gov.ph/registry_database/in-memoriam-thomasites/).
5. **Cholera estimates disaggregated.** Contemporary registers counted **166,252 reported cases and 109,461 deaths** during the 1902–1904 epidemic. Later retrospective population estimates are distinguished as different methodologies, not asserted as identical direct counts. The text warns against automatically adding epidemic figures to wartime casualty totals. Verification: [U.S. Army medical history](https://achh.army.mil/history/book-spanam-gillet3-ch11/).
6. **Bud Dajo casualties qualified.** The chapter describes roughly six hundred or more local deaths as a broad contemporary/secondary estimate, distinguishes American fatalities from killed-and-wounded totals, and emphasizes that civilians were among the dead. It does not imply universal agreement on exact numbers. The 1906 operation remains analytically distinct from the 1913 Bud Bagsak campaign.
7. **The 1908 *El Renacimiento* case clarified.** The October 30, 1908 editorial *Aves de Rapiña* is attributed to **Fidel A. Reyes**, while the publication and subsequent libel proceedings involved other editorial and publishing figures. The 1910 Philippine Supreme Court proceedings and 1914 U.S. Supreme Court proceedings are distinguished. Sources: [*United States v. Ocampo*](https://r2.jur.ph/decisions_html/u-s-v-ocampo-47523.html); [*Ocampo v. United States*, 234 U.S. 91](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/234/91).
8. **Terminology refined.** Military occupation is not defined as exempt from law; appropriate laws of war and administrative regulations are distinguished. The book also differentiates Filipino political participation, limited electoral representation, and ultimate American sovereignty.

## 4. Narrative expansion and scholarly framing

The chapter was expanded holistically rather than through disconnected micro-passes. Section 119.4 now better explains the different functions of courts, prosecutors, police, and locally recruited constables, and why labeling a political opponent a bandit could matter. Section 119.9 addresses differences between Luzon railways, inter-island coastal shipping, the Benguet road, ports, and communication cables. Section 119.11 examines tenants' practical access to the purchased estates, title registration, instalment payments, and cadastral costs. Section 119.16 explains the distinct administrative structures of the Mountain and Moro Provinces, including competing local authorities and the historical circumstances of Bud Dajo and Bud Bagsak. Sections 119.18–119.20 add close analysis of the nationalist press, labor and women's associations, resident commissioners, and the non-inevitability of Filipinization and independence.

Across the entire chapter, additional narrative separates official policy from lived experience, particularly regarding the language of schooling, access to public health, barriers to legal redress, export-market advantages, and the selective geographic reach of government. It preserves contingency and regional social differences rather than presuming a single path toward modern nationhood.
