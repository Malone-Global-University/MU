### 3. Documentary and historical corrections — consolidated

1. **Proto-Southwestern Tai.** The eighth–tenth-century dispersal is accurately framed as Pittayawat Pittayaporn's historical-linguistic reconstruction, not a dated migration event. Publication data for the 2014 *MANUSYA* study are supplied, pp. 47–68, DOI `10.1163/26659077-01703004`.
2. **Nanzhao and Dali.** The manuscript retains the crucial distinction between Nanzhao's end in 902 and the Mongol capture of its successor Dali in 1253. The obsolete “lost Thai kingdom” narrative is analyzed rather than repeated; Christian Daniels's peer-reviewed citation is completed: *Journal of Southeast Asian Studies* 52(2) (2021): 188–213, DOI `10.1017/S0022463421000424`.
3. **Irrigation, ethnic succession and local political organization.** O'Connor's 1995 agricultural specialization argument remains an interpretive framework, explicitly compared with older Mon/Khmer agrarian evidence. Masashi Okada, *Japanese Journal of Southeast Asian Studies* 50(1) (2012): 3–38, DOI `10.20495/tak.50.1_3`, supports the caution against using later mueang arrangements as unchanged medieval institutions.
4. **Indigenous agency.** Corrected the article title to Nathan Badenoch and Shinsuke Tomita, “Mountain People in the Muang: Creation and **Governance** of a Tai Polity in Northern Laos,” *Southeast Asian Studies* 2(1) (2013): 29–67, DOI `10.20495/seas.2.1_29`. Its relatively later evidence is identified as a methodological comparison, not proof of an eighth-century settlement.
5. **Nùng Trí Cao.** The Song primary-source window now identifies *Song shi*, **juan 495** (Guangyuan frontier) and **juan 290** (Di Qing biography) alongside Li Tao's *Xu Zizhi tongjian changbian*. Nùng Trí Cao's fate after defeat remains uncertain; there is no assumption that imperial ethnonyms directly establish the chief's language.
6. **Ahom traditions.** The Ahom window identifies Golap Chandra Barua's 1930 *Ahom-buranji* edition and the Sukapha/Shukapha section beginning at **p. 34**. The conventional 1228 arrival and 1253 Charaideo date remain marked as later chronicle traditions, not eyewitness records. No invented manuscript folio reference is supplied.
7. **Wat Si Chum.** The Sukhothai Inscription No. 2 window now identifies the inscription's **early Thai language**, its fourteenth-century context and Christian Bauer's study, *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 56(3) (1993): 525–565. The paraphrase distinguishes dynastic and Buddhist political memory from direct evidence of initial Tai settlement and does not assert an unattested face-and-line citation.
8. **Dvaravati archaeology.** Replaced the unidentifiable bibliographic placeholder with Stephen A. Murphy, *Journal of Southeast Asian Studies* 47(3) (2016): 366–392, DOI `10.1017/S0022463416000242`.
9. **Contested identities.** *Syam*, Zhou Daguan's reported Siamese, later Lawa ritual roles, and modern Tai/Thai national categories are consistently treated with period- and source-specific caution.
