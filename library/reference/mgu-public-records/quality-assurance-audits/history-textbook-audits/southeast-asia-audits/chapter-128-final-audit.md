## IV. Historical accuracy, corrections, and conceptual safeguards

**1. Chronological naming.** Nguyễn Tất Thành is used for early life, Nguyễn Ái Quốc for much of the 1919–1941 political period, and Hồ Chí Minh as the subject's later public name. The manuscript avoids projecting the later name or the mature political program back onto the traveler of 1911.

**2. The 1919 petition.** The chapter correctly characterizes the eight-point *Revendications du peuple annamite* as a reform petition seeking rights and representation, not a communist revolutionary manifesto or a clear demand for full independence. It treats the petition as the product of a Paris expatriate milieu, with Phan Văn Trường's drafting contribution distinguished from Nguyễn Ái Quốc's public role. It avoids falsely labeling Nguyễn Thế Truyền a proven coauthor of the 1919 document. The source-window provenance identifies the 18 June 1919 submission to Robert Lansing preserved in U.S. archival records.

**3. Tours and the Comintern.** The December 1920 French Socialist Party congress, 1921 intercolonial association, *Le Paria* from 1922, and 1923–1924 Moscow period are separated. The narrative treats Leninist influence as political and organizational, rather than accepting retrospective accounts of a single instantaneous ideological conversion as sufficient evidence.

**4. Guangzhou and revolutionary organization.** The 1925 Youth League and *Thanh Niên*, the 1927 publication of *Đường Kách Mệnh*, and the role of overseas and domestic cadres are developed. Cadre education is distinguished from mass membership, and the effects of political changes in China are not omitted.

**5. The three organizations of 1929.** The first *Đông Dương Cộng sản Đảng* (June 1929), *An Nam Cộng sản Đảng*, and *Đông Dương Cộng sản Liên đoàn* are differentiated. The first body is not confused with the party taking a similar English name in October 1930.

**6. February and October 1930.** The early-1930 Hong Kong consolidation produced the Vietnamese Communist Party; the Communist League's subsequent admission on 24 February is distinguished from the initial merger. The exact dates of the founding conference are stated as contested rather than resolved from commemorative practice. In October the party adopted the Indochinese Communist Party name and political theses associated with Trần Phú. Comparison of class alliances, geography, and national liberation appears in both narrative and Table 4.

**7. Nghệ–Tĩnh.** The movement is presented as an interaction of workers' action, rural economic grievances, local political traditions, cadres, and varied peasant agency. The term “soviets” is qualified: the committees did not comprise one uniformly administered Russian-style state. The Hưng Nguyên protests and colonial suppression are covered without manufacturing a single casualty or arrest figure. William Duiker's documented account supports restoration of colonial control by approximately mid-1931.

**8. Political imprisonment and the Hong Kong case.** The chapter separates French prison and administrative practices from British colonial court procedure. It identifies Francis Loseby's legal role and avoids the original inaccurate shorthand that Nguyễn Ái Quốc was simply released once in early 1933. The passage states a more complicated sequence of detention, attempted release, renewed difficulty, and eventual departure. No unverified precise itinerary was invented.

**9. Mid-1930s leadership.** Lê Hồng Phong, Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai, Hà Huy Tập, Trần Phú, and Trường Chinh are treated as institutional actors, not secondary characters in an unbroken one-person command narrative. The Macau Congress dates of 27–31 March 1935 are supported by specialist work.

**10. Popular Front and Marxist rivalry.** The 1936–1939 legal/semi-legal opening is not conflated with general democratic self-government. The final manuscript corrects the prior factual error: the Trotskyist election victory of April 1939 was for the **Cochinchina Colonial Council**, **not** the Saigon Municipal Council. It distinguishes cooperation around *La Lutte* from later disagreement on colonial defense taxation and other issues.

**11. Wartime boundary and the Việt Minh.** The text differentiates Japanese intervention from an immediate total end to French administrative power in 1940. The Bắc Sơn and Cochinchina uprisings are distinguished. The Eighth Central Committee Plenum at Pác Bó on 10–19 May 1941, the Việt Minh's founding associated with 19 May, and public program issuance on 25 October are not collapsed into one event. The Việt Minh is distinguished from direct identity with the Communist Party. The narrative ends before wartime expansion and 1945.

**12. Historiography.** The chapter rejects predetermined leadership, monocausal ideology, and claims that communism was a mere foreign import. It also declines the converse simplification that international party institutions had no effect on Vietnamese decisions. It documents conflicting political strategies and distinguishes participants' grievances from centrally published party goals.

The bibliography includes some entries identified by **source collection, organization, or document title**, not an individually collated edition with stable pagination; that limitation remains explicit. No unsupported numerical series was introduced merely to satisfy the seven-table requirement. The reference apparatus is suitable for an instructional survey but not presented as a critical scholarly edition of the original communist party archive.
