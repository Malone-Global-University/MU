### 4. Historical and bibliographic corrections applied

**Myazedi quadrilingual inscription, §53.9.** Standardized the prose and chronology to **1112/1113 CE**, noting that UNESCO's register documentation uses **1113**. The four languages are Pyu, Mon, Burmese, and Pali; the text demonstrates a royal-family Buddhist dedication and multilingual practice. It is **not** proof of a later imported Sri Lankan ordination lineage. UNESCO object record: https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/myazedi-quadrilingual-stone-inscription.

**Dhammaceti / Kalyani, §53.17.** Added details attributed to the historical published record: **22 senior monks (theras) and 22 disciples** on the mission, and the monument's **ten inscribed slabs** (traditionally first three Pali, remaining seven Mon). The direct digitized 1892 translation, printed page 75, records the missionary party's numbers; Taw Sein Ko's introduction, page v, records the slabs and that his edition was collated from **two palm-leaf manuscripts without direct access to the stones**. The chapter explicitly distinguishes these published details from an independently re-collated facsimile and separates reform events in the late 1470s from the precise carving dates. Sources: https://www.burmalibrary.org/docs09/Kalyani_Inscriptions.pdf (introduction p. v; English translation p. 75) and https://www.burmalibrary.org/en/the-kalyani-inscriptions-erected-by-king-dhammaceti-at-pegu-in-1476-a-d-text-and-translation.

**Anne M. Blackburn bibliography.** Split the original combined reference into two individually identified verified items:

1. “Buddhist Connections in the Indian Ocean: Changes in Monastic Mobility, 1000–1500,” *Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient* **58**, no. 3 (2015): **237–266**, DOI 10.1163/15685209-12341374; https://doi.org/10.1163/15685209-12341374.
2. “Sīhaḷa Saṅgha and Laṅkā in Later Premodern Southeast Asia,” in D. Christian Lammerts, ed., *Buddhist Dynamics in Premodern and Early Modern Southeast Asia* (Singapore: ISEAS, **2015**), **307–332**, DOI 10.1355/9789814519076-012; https://doi.org/10.1355/9789814519076-012.

**Other bibliographic improvements.** Identified Skilling's 1997 journal pages **93–107** and DOI 10.2143/JIABS.20.1.3285933; clarified the Jayawickrama edition of *Jinakālamālī* and source-register limitations. The source register now has **15 independently list-formatted entries** in three source-type groups. The chapter's named regional chronologies (Chapata, Sumana/Wat Suan Dok, Lithai, Fa Ngum, and the 1477 Chiang Mai council) remain explicitly qualified as approximate, traditional, or disputed where warranted; their uncertainty is not falsely resolved.

**Historical framing retained.** The chapter correctly avoids identifying every early Pali inscription with a demonstrable single Theravada monastic lineage, avoids treating Sri Lankan influence as exclusively one-way, and avoids asserting that Theravada caused Pagan or Angkor to collapse. Material and textual evidence from Pyu, Dvaravati, Pagan, Tai-speaking polities, Cambodia, and Lower Burma is used comparatively rather than to impose a single origin story. See Peter Skilling (1997), https://doi.org/10.2143/JIABS.20.1.3285933; Anne Blackburn (2015), references above; and Zhou Daguan, *A Record of Cambodia*, Peter Harris trans. (2007).
