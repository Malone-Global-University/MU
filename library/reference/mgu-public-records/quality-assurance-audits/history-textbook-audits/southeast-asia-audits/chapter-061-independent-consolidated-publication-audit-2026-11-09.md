## 3. Historical evidence — verified and correction-worthy
### 3.1 Strong, substantially accurate interpretation
- The argument for gradual, spatially uneven political and urban reorganization is consistent with Penny, Hall, Evans and Polkinghorne (2019), whose proxies imply weakening land-use intensity in Angkor Thom by the early fourteenth century, and Carter et al. (2019), whose excavation and chronological model establish later/renewed occupation at Angkor Wat with a temporal gap in their dated samples.
- Treating Buckley et al. (2010) drought/monsoon evidence and Day et al. (2012) West Baray data as contributions rather than proof of a single universal system failure is methodologically sound.
- Zhou Daguan’s visit (1296–1297), description of active markets, court ceremony, and distinct groups of religious practitioners are appropriate; his labels are Chinese descriptions requiring cautious identification rather than incontrovertible classifications of modern denominations. His report of a royal son-in-law succeeding is grounded in his text, but its details should remain identified as testimony/hearsay and supplemented by inscriptions.
- **1431–1432:** appropriately characterized as conventional and based on later chronicle accounts; independent evidence for fifteenth-century early-Ayutthayan-style Buddhist sculptures makes an intervention/period of presence plausible without dating every artifact to that year. Polkinghorne, Pottier and Fischer (2018) indeed report **more than fifty analogous images**. Numbers and find-spots should not be presented as equivalent to a securely dated period of occupation.
- Henri Mouhot’s posthumously published French travel writing appeared in **1863** (in *Le Tour du monde*), and the “rediscovery” narrative is correctly rejected. Bernard-Philippe Groslier’s classic hydraulic-city argument was formally published in **1979**, with earlier development/circulation of the idea in the 1970s; the manuscript’s broad attributions are supportable.
### 3.2 Required correction: Indravarman III and K.754 (1308)
- Introductory person-list and review material suggest that Indravarman III’s association with Pali Buddhism is merely “later tradition.” This is materially incomplete: **K.754**, dated **1308** (some secondary discussions write 1308/1309), is a **contemporary bilingual Pali–Khmer inscription** associated with Śrīndravarman/Indravarman III, Buddhist patronage and the abdication episode. It is a firm epigraphic datum, although the significance and extent of courtwide Theravada change remain debated. Add and interpret K.754 in §§61.9 and 61.11, and revise the major-people entry and chronology as appropriate. Do not interpret one inscription as proof of an empirewide instantaneous conversion.
- A documented scholarly discussion: Cœdès, “La plus ancienne inscription en pāli du Cambodge,” *Bulletin de l’École française d’Extrême-Orient* 36 (1936), pp. 14–21; Andrew Harris et al. (2022) discuss K.754; modern scholarship also notes earlier Pali documents elsewhere, so avoid calling it the first Pali ever written in all Cambodia.
### 3.3 Accession and inscription chronology
- The familiar approximate reign **Jayavarman VIII c. 1243–1295** appears in reference chronologies, but inscription-focused work places the start of his securely documented reign closer to **1270/1271**. The date should not be presented as a single unambiguous certainty; the manuscript currently marks it as approximate, which is acceptable but could better distinguish the two reckoning traditions.
- **Indravarman III / Śrīndravarman** is conventionally dated **1295–1307/1308**. The manuscript’s `1295–c.1308` is defensible; add the 1308 inscription and acknowledge the end date nuance. His relationship by marriage to Jayavarman VIII’s daughter and the son-in-law succession story are separately discussed in epigraphic and Zhou sources; avoid making every detail independently corroborated by Zhou.
- **K.488 (1295)**: correctly recognized as the last *securely dated* central Angkorian Sanskrit inscription commonly cited; **K.470 (1327)**: a dated Khmer-language Bayon inscription. However, undated Sanskrit **K.300** has been associated with a royal reign after 1327. Hence **“last dated Sanskrit” ≠ “last Sanskrit text of any kind”**. Add one explicit qualifying sentence in §61.11 to prevent students from inferring a total cessation of Sanskrit in 1295.
- Editions should be pinpointed: K.470 in Cœdès, *Inscriptions du Cambodge* II (1942), p. 187; the K.488/Maṅgalārtha inscription is discussed in Finot’s 1925 publication (around p. 393) and cited by modern Angkor scholarship. Use precise references only after checking the relevant edition; do not fabricate a translated excerpt.
### 3.4 Source identities and intellectual attribution
- **Evans et al. (2013):** complete as *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* **110(31):12595–12600**, DOI **10.1073/pnas.1306539110** (verified PubMed). The current entry omits volume and pages.
- **“The Landscape of Angkor Wat Redefined” (2015):** authors are **Damian Evans and Roland Fletcher**, *Antiquity* **89(348):1402–1419**, DOI **10.15184/aqy.2015.157**. Correct the authorless entry.
- **Andrew Harris et al. (2022):** full paper title is “Towards a temporal assessment of Angkor Thom’s **formative** Theravada ‘Buddhist Terrace’ archaeology,” *Asian Archaeology*, DOI **10.1007/s41826-022-00056-y**. The manuscript omits “formative,” full authors and identifying DOI. Coauthors Tin Tina, Soeng Sreytouch, He Horth, Chheng Vouchnea and Chhouk Somala.
- **Khmer chronicles:** Vickery’s **1977 Yale dissertation** is an important critical *analysis* of the chronicles, but **not a substitute for a named edition/translation of the relevant Khmer chronicle text**. A directly relevant available edition is **Khin Sok (ed.), *Chroniques royales du Cambodge*, Tome II: *De Bañā Yāt à la prise de Laṅvaek (de 1417 à 1595)*, EFEO, 1988**, with French translation and comparison of variants. It should be cited as a possible edition and passages verified before treating it as a text personally collated. Keep Vickery as historiographical critique.
- **Royal Chronicles of Ayutthaya** (Cushman translated, Wyatt edited, Siam Society, 2000), Buckley, Day, Penny, Carter, Hall, Polkinghorne and the 2023 Evans synthesis are substantively identified correctly. The 2018 Polkinghorne/Pottier/Fischer chapter is pp. **99–132** per Flinders University repository.
- Source register currently includes a literal **pre-publication editorial reminder** that items 5, 6 and 10 need checking. Replace this with completed bibliographic details and concise limits of primary-source collation; publication-facing content must not display outstanding editor instructions.
### 3.5 Causal confidence and omissions
- The draft correctly refrains from exact population-loss estimates and from asserting uniform hydraulic failure; retain those safeguards.
- The draft’s assertion that Zhou’s descriptions imply “Pali-using” monks is plausible in the broader cultural setting but Zhou’s Chinese terms do not independently identify the language of the texts. Treat as an inference and integrate K.754 as corroborating epigraphic evidence.
- Stronger treatment of patronage, local communities, changing maritime connections, differential land use and the geographic limits of the dataset will help the 1,700-word expansion become substantive rather than repetitive.
