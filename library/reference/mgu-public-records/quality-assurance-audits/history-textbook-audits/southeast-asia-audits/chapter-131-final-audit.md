## 4. Specific historical corrections to the condensed source draft

### 4.1 Civil administration before the Organic Act

The draft compressed the creation of civilian government into the **1902 Organic Act**. The completed chapter distinguishes the Philippine Commission's earlier legislative activity, the Spooner Amendment of 1901, and **Taft's appointment as civil governor on July 4, 1901**, from the subsequent Organic Act of July 1, 1902. This is supported by the U.S. House of Representatives' official historical essay: <https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/APA/Historical-essays/Exclusion-and-Empire/The-Philippines/>.

### 4.2 The 1916 Jones Act

The chapter retains the correct distinction between a conditional congressional statement of intended independence and a fixed date or automatic transfer of sovereignty. It describes the elected Senate and House and the continuing powers of the governor-general and Washington. Verification: <https://lawphil.net/consti/cons1916.html>; Philippine Senate history: <https://legacy.senate.gov.ph/about/history.asp>.

### 4.3 Independence negotiations, 1933–1934

The completed treatment distinguishes the rejection of **Hare–Hawes–Cutting in 1933** from the accepted **Tydings–McDuffie Act of March 24, 1934**. It describes the revised strategic-reservations arrangements, the continuing commodity and tariff questions, and the political rivalry among Quezon, Osmeña, and Roxas without reducing the dispute to personalities. Primary act: <https://teachinglegalhistory.unl.edu/s/oer/item/1542>. U.S. legislative context: <https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/APA/Historical-essays/Exclusion-and-Empire/The-Philippines/>.

### 4.4 Peasant movements and Sakdalism

The expanded text differentiates the **Colorum uprisings of the 1920s**, the **1931 Tayug rebellion associated with Pedro Calosa**, and the **Sakdal uprising of May 2–3, 1935**. Religious expectation, agrarian grievances, and anticolonial politics are treated as intersecting possibilities, not a single deterministic ideology. The narrative explicitly distinguishes Sakdal party membership, sympathizers, uprising participants, and Benigno Ramos's later wartime political choices. Relevant scholarship: Milagros C. Guerrero, “The Colorum Uprisings: 1924–1931,” *Asian Studies* 5:1 (1967), <https://www.asj.upd.edu.ph/index.php/archive/139-vol-05-no-1-1967>; David R. Sturtevant, *Popular Uprisings in the Philippines, 1840–1940* (1976); Motoe Terami-Wada, *Sakdalistas’ Struggle for Philippine Independence, 1930–1945* (2014).

### 4.5 Original 1935 Constitution versus 1940 amendments

The completed chapter identifies the original 1935 charter's unicameral National Assembly and six-year presidential term without immediate reelection. It separates the **1940 changes**—bicameral legislature, four-year presidency with one reelection, and the Commission on Elections—from the original document. Ratification on May 14, 1935, and Commonwealth inauguration on November 15, 1935, are correctly sequenced. Sources: <https://officialgazette.gov.ph/constitutions/constitution-day/> and <https://lawphil.net/consti/cons1935.html>.

### 4.6 Women’s suffrage

The chapter correctly identifies Commonwealth Act No. 34, approved **September 30, 1936**, the April 30, 1937 plebiscite, the threshold of **300,000 affirmative votes**, and the reported outcome of **447,725 in favor and 44,307 against**. It adds the campaign and agency of women's associations rather than portraying enfranchisement as an automatic grant by Quezon. Sources: <https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/29/23944> and <https://ncca.gov.ph/about-culture-and-arts/in-focus/a-celebration-of-herstory-filipino-women-in-legislation-and-politics/>.

### 4.7 National-language executive order and source discrepancy

The draft asserted **December 13, 1937** without qualification for Executive Order No. 134. The completed chapter gives **December 1937** and explains an unresolved discrepancy in the reproduced public record: the Supreme Court E-Library index and LawPhil executive-order index label it **December 13**, while the operative document reproduced by the Supreme Court states in its closing attestation that it was done on **December 30, 1937**. The Senate legislative index also records December 30. This is a genuine source-metadata issue; the precise day is intentionally not stated unqualified in instructional narrative. Sources: <https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/5/77655> and <https://issuances-library.senate.gov.ph/executive-issuance/executive-order-no-134-s-1937>.

### 4.8 1941 presidential opposition

During final verification, an erroneous identification of **José P. Laurel** as Quezon's main opponent in the 1941 presidential election was found in the expanded draft and **corrected to Juan Sumulong** before the final file was assembled. A contemporary American report and election scholarship support the correction. Source: <https://time.com/archive/6603621/territories-bedroom-campaign/>; contemporaneous U.S. High Commissioner reports describe Quezon’s decisive win over Sumulong. No incorrect Laurel identification remains in the delivered manuscript.

### 4.9 Land settlement and national inclusion

The chapter expands the voices of Muslim and indigenous communities rather than describing their incorporation as inevitable. It discusses the **1935 Dansalan petition** and Commonwealth Act No. 441 of June 3, 1939, establishing the National Land Settlement Administration. Its treatment differentiates administrative claims of development from local land and customary-rights claims. Sources: <https://lawphil.net/statutes/comacts/ca1939/ca_441_1939.html>; Thomas M. McKenna, *Muslim Rulers and Rebels*, online University of California Press edition <https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/muslim-rulers-and-rebels-everyday-politics-and-armed-separatism-in-the-southern-philippines.html>.

### 4.10 Defense legislation and prewar endpoint

Commonwealth Act No. 1, the National Defense Act, is correctly dated **December 21, 1935**. The chapter distinguishes a legal military framework from procurement, organization, training, and strategic capacity and ends at the December 1941 outbreak of war, without reproducing Chapter 137's Japanese occupation narrative. Source: <https://lawphil.net/statutes/comacts/ca1935/ca_1_1935.html>.

## 6. Interpretation, balance, and instructional completeness

The final chapter maintains a central historical argument: American-supervised institutions enlarged Filipino self-government while retaining foreign sovereignty and allowing elite political power, export dependence, and rural inequality to persist. It does not treat either an independence date or elections as proof that social conflicts had been resolved.

The narrative distinguishes institutional milestones from social practice; explains why elites could be committed nationalists and beneficiaries of hierarchy simultaneously; develops the agrarian, labor, Socialist, Communist, and Sakdal perspectives; treats women's organizations as independent political actors; and includes the contested political claims of Muslim and indigenous communities in Mindanao, Sulu, and the Cordillera. Rural movements are not reduced to undifferentiated communism. World War II's outcome is not treated as inevitable from the standpoint of prewar decision-makers.

Cross-chapter boundaries are preserved: Chapters 116–119 contain earlier reform, revolution, war, and the initial American occupation; Chapter 133 addresses regional Depression history; Chapter 137 covers conquest and occupation. The discussion of immediate defense threats belongs here only as the end-point of the Commonwealth narrative.

**Maps:** The two geography studies contain complete explanatory instructions and analytic questions. Actual map illustrations remain intentionally deferred for the user to insert. Their absence is **not** an audit discrepancy or publication blocker.
