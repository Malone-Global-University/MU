## IV. Major substantive revisions performed

### A. World trade and commodity-price transmission (Division I)

The revision explains how price changes passed through international markets, brokers, banks, port accounting, tax demands and rural credit arrangements. It distinguishes financial quotations from producer prices, physical output and family purchasing power.

**Replaced Table 2** with identifiable historical observations drawn from Robert E. Elson's economic-history discussion in *The Cambridge History of Southeast Asia*, Volume II, rather than conceptual 1928-index values or guessed annual series:

| Commodity and market | 1929 value | Later observed value | Later value / 1929 value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rubber, London | 10.25 pence/lb | 2.30 pence/lb (1932) | 22.4% |
| Java sugar, average wholesale | 13.66 guilders/quintal | 5.61 guilders/quintal (1934) | 41.1% |
| Cochinchina rice | 7.15 piastres/quintal | 1.88 piastres/quintal (1934) | 26.3% |

All ratios were checked by direct division and rounding to one decimal place. The table explicitly cautions that the periods, locations, currencies and marketing levels differ. The percentages are **not** a common regional price index or real-price series.

**Bibliographic source:** Robert E. Elson, “International Commerce, the State and Society: Economic and Social Change,” in Nicholas Tarling, ed., *The Cambridge History of Southeast Asia*, vol. 2 (Cambridge University Press, 1992). [Publisher chapter record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-southeast-asia/international-commerce-the-state-and-society-economic-and-social-change/652F37A90EF2C315B4840E113A8E2BD4).

### B. Currency chronology and Siam (Divisions I and III)

The manuscript now consistently distinguishes the **May 1932** renewal of Siam's sterling link and withdrawal of gold-conversion arrangements, undertaken under the monarchy, from the **24 June 1932** constitutional revolution. The gold-bloc comparisons correctly identify the Dutch and French monetary differences and the Philippines' relationship to the U.S. dollar. The language explaining the relatively strong Netherlands Indies guilder was tightened to avoid claiming that the currency automatically increased local export receipts.

**Verification:** “The Currency, Banking, and Exchange System of Thailand,” *IMF Staff Papers* (1950), [historical article](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/024/1950/002/article-A006-en.xml); cf. *Four Colonies and a Kingdom*, comparative study of fiscal, trade and exchange policy.

### C. Estate production, tin and Java sugar (Division II)

Expanded explanations connect the biological lag between rubber planting and output, industrial tin demand and capital-intensive mining, and Java sugar-mill economics with labor and land-rental consequences. The number of operating Java sugar mills is identified as approximately **179 in 1930** and **about 39 by 1935**; the revision warns readers against confusing an idle mill with permanent demolition.

The chapter preserves the historically defensible start dates of the **International Tin Control Scheme (1 March 1931)** and **International Rubber Regulation Agreement (1 June 1934)**. For the former, the House of Commons exchange of 20 February 1931 specifically concerns the expected arrangements; for the latter, contemporary diplomatic documentation identifies agreement details.

**Primary documentary checks:** [House of Commons, Hansard, 20 February 1931](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1931/feb/20/nigeria-and-malay-states-tin-restriction); [U.S. Office of the Historian, 1934 rubber negotiations](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v01/d532).

### D. Rural debt, family income and national/regional differentiation (Division III)

The Burma delta section substantially develops the relationship among cash rents, mortgages, foreclosure, Chettiar creditors, changing land titles and tenancy. It distinguishes recorded property transfers from an exhaustive measure of food consumption or welfare, following Ian Brown's caution. The Siam section differentiates the surveys' measurement periods, samples and assumptions, and the Philippine section develops comparisons among sugar, coconut, abaca and metropolitan districts. The household chapter treats gender, mobility, subsistence, kin-based protection and their limits without treating any one community as representative of the region.

**Verification:** Ian Brown, “Rural Distress in Southeast Asia during the World Depression of the Early 1930s,” *Journal of Asian Studies* 45, no. 5 (1986): 995–1025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2056606); James M. Andrews, *Siam: Second Rural Economic Survey, 1934–1935* (1935), [catalog record](https://books.google.com/books?id=8QgeAAAAIAAJ); Daniel F. Doeppers and W. G. Wolters, relevant chapters in *Weathering the Storm* (2000).

### E. Fiscal policy, migration and organized labor (Division IV)

The revision replaces an overbroad assertion that India banned all unskilled emigration to Malaya in 1938 with the narrower documented decision: a prohibition of **assisted emigration to Malaya effective 15 June 1938**, not a total ban on every category of Indian migration. It distinguishes the Straits Settlements **Aliens Ordinance of 1932** from restrictions and administrative practice extending into 1933, correcting the glossary, chronology and student questions as well as the narrative.

The fiscal section now uses the Straits Settlements' 1932 annual report as a source-critical example. It documents reported revenue of about **$26.60 million in 1931** and **$44.56 million in 1932**, including “Miscellaneous Receipts” rising from about **$0.86 million** to **$14.47 million**. This accounting composition explains why a revenue increase is not evidence of broadly recovered trade and employment. The report also records 1932 expenditures of about **$34.20 million**. The chapter correctly distinguishes the Straits Settlements jurisdiction from all British Malaya.

**Verification:** [1932 Straits Settlements annual report, published 1933](https://seadelt.net/Asset/Source/Document_ID-426_No-01.pdf); [Economic History Malaysia, “South Indian Labour in Malayan Rubber Estates: Profits over People, 1884–1941”](https://www.ehm.my/publications/articles/south-indian-labour-in-malayan-rubber-estates-profits-over-people-1884%E2%80%931941); contemporary International Labour Office reports, colonial immigration reports, and secondary migration history.

### F. Politics, recovery and legacy (Division V)

Additional material distinguishes evidence for economic grievance from proof of a direct causal origin of rebellion or constitutional change. Saya San's rebellion, the Nghệ-Tĩnh movement, Philippine Sakdal protest, Dutch Indies repression and strikes during recovery are analyzed as different political outcomes. The revised conclusion differentiates short-run commodity recovery from long-run consequences for land title, wages, migrants, social trust and colonial legitimacy. Japan's 1941–1942 invasion remains reserved for subsequent chapters.

## IX. Final limitations and editorial judgment

The final manuscript still relies in places on historical syntheses rather than direct local archive series. This is appropriate for an advanced general textbook, provided that the distinction between source observations, model assumptions and interpretive inference remains visible. The chapter now makes that distinction, especially in Tables 2 and 5 and the primary-source windows.

**No known uncorrected factual error or omitted required historical division was identified in the revised manuscript after this audit.** That statement is limited to the substantive claims and source issues reviewed; it is not a guarantee against any remaining typo, catalog discrepancy, or historical point a specialist might later refine. Further line-editing or additional maps are optional publication enhancements, not conditions on the present manuscript's historical completeness.
