## 3. Plan compliance and intellectual structure

**Division I — 138.1–138.4: Conquest and colonial collapse.** Colonial institutions, the multi-island campaign and oil strategy, the Java surrender, and the Army/Navy jurisdictional division are fully developed. The 8 March Kalijati negotiations are distinguished from the 9 March surrender broadcast. Military geography and the differences between Java, Sumatra, and eastern naval territories remain explicit.

**Division II — 138.5–138.8: Administration and nationalist mobilization.** The expanded sections explain the working authority of Japanese military government, indigenous officials and neighborhood associations; Sukarno's and Hatta's strategic cooperation and responsibility for mobilization; Three-A, PUTERA, Jawa Hokokai and advisory councils; and differing Muslim political and religious responses. Public recognition of Indonesian leadership is not confused with sovereignty.

**Division III — 138.9–138.12: Extraction, famine and civilian conditions.** Expanded causal explanations distinguish control of oilfields from actual oil output and shipment, and food production from access and distribution. The Java famine and romusha sections now differentiate population loss, excess deaths, local labor, transported workers, and estimates based on incomplete records. Women's wartime work, education and sexual violence receive measured, non-sensational treatment.

**Division IV — 138.13–138.16: Militarization, opposition, regional divergence.** PETA, Heiho and Sumatran Giyūgun are distinguished; the scope and limits of the approximately 37,000 figure for Java's PETA are explained. Youth organizations are analyzed both as wartime mobilization and as sources of experience later redirected toward independence. Blitar, Aceh, Singaparna, Kalimantan, and eastern naval territories receive additional contextualization; memorial casualty totals for Pontianak/Mandor are not presented as confirmed counts.

**Division V — 138.17–138.20: Defeat and threshold of independence.** The September 1944 Koiso announcement is contextualized by Japanese strategic setbacks; BPUPKI debates distinguish the 1 June Pancasila address from the 22 June Jakarta Charter and later constitutional adoption. PPKI's August 1945 creation and the precise Dalat sequence are clarified. The narrative stops at the threshold of the 17 August proclamation, reserving its detailed negotiations and the subsequent revolution to Chapters 146–148.

The original approved governing historical argument remains intact: Japanese imperial extraction and oppression could coexist with institutional changes that Indonesian nationalists subsequently used in an independent struggle. This is presented as a historically contingent outcome, not a vindication of military occupation.

## 4. Factual corrections and qualified claims

### 4.1 PPKI and Dalat, August 1945 — corrected

The original date range **9–11 August** incorrectly identified the Terauchi meeting. The revised section and timeline separate **PPKI's formation on 7 August**, the delegation's **departure on 9 August**, the actual **Dalat audience on 12 August**, Japan's **surrender announcement on 15 August**, and the independence proclamation of **17 August**. The main narrative no longer makes the departure and meeting synonymous.

Reference: Indonesian Education Ministry historical publication identifying the Dalat meeting on 12 August: https://repositori.kemendikdasmen.go.id/27854/1/Kajian%20Gedung%20Kawasan%20Menteng%20Jilid%20II%20Munasprok.pdf . This publication was verified through its indexed description; no claim is made that all pages were independently audited.

### 4.2 Kalijati surrender, March 1942 — clarified

The manuscript now distinguishes the **8 March negotiations** from the **9 March broadcast of surrender orders** and avoids implying the collapse of the central Java command ended all resistance in the archipelago. This is a precision correction rather than an alteration of the accepted campaign chronology.

### 4.3 Java famine — sharpened demographic method

Pierre van der Eng, “Mortality from the 1944–1945 Famine in Java, Indonesia,” *Australian Economic History Review* 64, no. 2 (2024): 192–212, estimates roughly **1.8 million excess deaths** during occupation and a larger **3.3 million net population loss** that incorporates missing births. The 1944 and 1945 allocations of excess mortality are approximately 0.7 million and 1.1 million. The revised main narrative and famine feature explain why these are modeled estimates rather than a count of individually verified starvation deaths. This avoids the draft's undifferentiated “several hundred thousand to over two million” claim.

Reference: https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/mortality-from-the-1944-1945-famine-in-java-indonesia-2/ .

### 4.4 Romusha — differentiated quantities and testimony

Takuma Melber's article in *International Review of Social History* (2016) provides a scholarly foundation for estimates of roughly **200,000–300,000 workers transferred from Java to other occupied territories**. This is **not** a count of all laborers compelled to work within Java, nor a mortality total. The text distinguishes recruitment, deployment, survivors, and deaths, and expressly notes the limits of contemporary documentation.

Reference: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/labour-recruitment-of-local-inhabitants-as-romusha-in-japaneseoccupied-south-east-asia/4F5497C20D928D214D809915EDC9E015 .

### 4.5 PETA strength and regional Giyūgun — qualified

The frequently cited figure of about **37,000 PETA personnel on Java** remains an approximate, temporally dependent count; Sumatran **Giyūgun** is treated separately under the Twenty-Fifth Army. This correction avoids adding regional formations into a Java-based figure without evidence. It also avoids claiming that PETA itself survived intact as the later Indonesian national army.

### 4.6 Pontianak and Mandor killings — qualified

The high memorial figure associated with the 1943–1944 West Kalimantan killings is explicitly identified as a memorial claim, not a verified demographic census. The text distinguishes uncertainty in totals from the established nature of mass repression and destruction of local political leadership.

### 4.7 Tadashi Maeda — precise institutional attribution

Maeda is identified in the relevant earlier passage as a naval liaison officer; the final-crisis section describes the role of the rear admiral in Jakarta. His relationships are situated within inter-service differences rather than treated as evidence that the Japanese occupation command unanimously supported immediate Indonesian independence.
