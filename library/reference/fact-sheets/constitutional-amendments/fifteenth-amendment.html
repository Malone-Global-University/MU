What Is the Fifteenth Amendment?

The Fifteenth Amendment is a two-section constitutional amendment adopted during Reconstruction after the Civil War. It prohibits the United States and every state from denying or abridging a United States citizen's right to vote because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

The amendment was designed principally to protect Black male suffrage after emancipation and to prevent federal, state, and local governments from restoring racial political exclusion. Its second section grants Congress authority to enforce the prohibition through appropriate legislation.

The amendment does not establish a complete universal right to vote. It prohibits specified grounds of discrimination while leaving room for otherwise lawful voter qualifications and election regulations. That distinction allowed governments and political organizations to develop facially race-neutral devices that were designed or administered to disfranchise African Americans.

Constitutional Text

Section 1

The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude—

Section 2

The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

Historical Context

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, and the Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship, due process, and equal protection. Neither amendment directly prohibited racial discrimination in voting.

Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment contemplated a reduction in representation when a state denied or abridged voting rights of adult male citizens, but it did not directly command states to permit Black men to vote. Congress did not consistently apply the proposed representation penalty.

During congressional Reconstruction, federal law required reconstructed Southern governments to establish broader male suffrage. African American men registered, voted, participated in constitutional conventions, organized politically, and won local, state, and federal office.

Black political participation generated organized white resistance. During the 1868 presidential election and the surrounding period, African American voters and white Republicans faced intimidation, economic retaliation, assault, and murder. Congressional Republicans concluded that statutory requirements and state constitutions were insufficient without a permanent constitutional prohibition against racial disfranchisement.

Drafting and Congressional Debate

Congress debated numerous proposals before agreeing on the final language. Legislators were divided among those who opposed a federal suffrage amendment, those who favored universal male suffrage, and those willing to prohibit only racial qualifications while allowing states to maintain other voting requirements.

Broader proposals would have prohibited educational, property, nativity, or other qualifications. The final amendment instead prohibited denial or abridgment only when based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

Senator William M. Stewart of Nevada played a prominent role in guiding the proposal through the Senate. The House approved the final resolution on February 25, 1869, and the Senate approved it the next day by a vote of 39 to 13.

The final text omitted sex as a protected category. Women citizens therefore received no protection from sex-based disfranchisement under the Fifteenth Amendment. The exclusion intensified divisions within the woman-suffrage and abolitionist movements.

Ratification

Congress submitted the amendment to the states on February 26, 1869. Nevada became the first state to ratify it on March 1, 1869.

Ratification remained connected to congressional Reconstruction. Virginia, Mississippi, Texas, and Georgia were required to ratify the amendment as a condition of regaining representation in Congress.

Iowa's ratification on February 3, 1870, supplied the required approval of three-fourths of the states then counted in the Union. Nebraska and Texas subsequently ratified before Secretary of State Hamilton Fish issued the formal proclamation on March 30, 1870.

Some states rejected the amendment, delayed ratification, or attempted to withdraw approval. These disputes did not prevent its recognition as part of the Constitution.

Scope of the Amendment

Protected Status

Section 1 protects citizens against voting discrimination based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude. The phrase previous condition of servitude prevented governments from denying voting rights because a citizen had formerly been enslaved.

Government Conduct

The amendment applies to both the federal government and the states. It also reaches local governments and election officials whose authority derives from a state.

Denial and Abridgment

The amendment prohibits both outright denial and abridgment. A voting system can violate the amendment by restricting, burdening, weakening, or manipulating voting rights because of race even when it does not impose a complete formal ban.

Citizenship Requirement

The text protects the voting rights of United States citizens. It does not require that noncitizens be permitted to vote.

All Elections Governed by Public Authority

The protection is not limited to presidential or congressional elections. It applies to federal, state, and local voting governed by public authority.

What the Amendment Did Not Establish

No General Universal-Suffrage Clause

The amendment did not create an unconditional affirmative right for every citizen to vote in every election. Early Supreme Court doctrine described it as protecting citizens from racial discrimination in the exercise of voting rights rather than independently defining every qualification for suffrage.

No Protection Against Sex Discrimination

The amendment did not prohibit denial of voting rights on account of sex. The Nineteenth Amendment later supplied that constitutional prohibition.

No Age-Based Protection

The amendment did not establish a national voting age. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment later prohibited denial or abridgment of voting rights of citizens age eighteen or older on account of age.

No General Prohibition of Poll Taxes

Poll taxes were not expressly prohibited by the Fifteenth Amendment when imposed without an explicit racial classification. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment later prohibited poll taxes in federal elections, and the Supreme Court invalidated poll taxes in state elections under the Fourteenth Amendment.

No Automatic Protection From Every Facially Neutral Rule

Residency rules, registration procedures, criminal-disqualification provisions, literacy requirements, and other qualifications were not automatically unconstitutional merely because they burdened voting. They could violate the amendment when adopted or administered for a racially discriminatory purpose.

Immediate Political Effect

The amendment invalidated express racial qualifications in state voting law. States could no longer lawfully reserve voting to white citizens.

African American men voted in large numbers during Reconstruction and helped elect Black officials at the local, state, and federal levels. Hiram Revels of Mississippi entered the United States Senate in February 1870, shortly after ratification, and Joseph Rainey of South Carolina entered the House later that year.

Black voters supported public education, civil-rights measures, new state constitutions, infrastructure development, and multiracial Republican governments. Political participation varied by state and locality and remained dependent on physical security and federal enforcement.

The amendment did not create Black political participation from nothing. African Americans had already organized conventions, petitions, Union leagues, political associations, and campaigns for suffrage. The amendment secured a constitutional rule that strengthened those efforts.

Congressional Enforcement

Section 2 authorizes Congress to enforce the amendment through appropriate legislation. Congress used this authority to enact Reconstruction-era Enforcement Acts addressing racial obstruction of voting and political violence.

Enforcement Act of 1870

The first Enforcement Act prohibited racial discrimination in voter registration, penalized interference with voting rights, gave federal courts enforcement authority, and authorized federal officers to act against violations.

Additional Enforcement Legislation

Congress enacted additional measures in 1871 addressing election fraud, intimidation, conspiracies, and organized violence. The Ku Klux Klan Act strengthened federal authority to respond when conspiracies and state inaction deprived citizens of constitutional rights.

Federal Institutions

Enforcement depended on federal courts, United States attorneys, marshals, military forces in some circumstances, congressional oversight, and executive commitment. The Department of Justice, created in 1870, became an important federal enforcement institution.

Federal enforcement weakened as Reconstruction ended, national political priorities changed, judicial decisions narrowed federal authority, and white supremacist governments regained control in the South.

Evasion and Disfranchisement

Because the amendment prohibited specified racial grounds rather than every voting qualification, governments developed devices written in facially neutral language but designed or administered to remove African Americans from the electorate.

Violence and Intimidation

White supremacist organizations, employers, landlords, political officials, and armed groups used murder, assault, threats, eviction, job loss, and economic retaliation to suppress Black registration, voting, candidacy, and political organization.

Poll Taxes

Payment requirements disproportionately excluded poor voters and could be administered selectively. Cumulative poll taxes required payment for multiple prior years before a person could vote.

Literacy and Understanding Tests

Officials required prospective voters to read, interpret, or explain constitutional language. Registrars exercised broad discretion and commonly applied easier standards to white applicants than to Black applicants.

Grandfather Clauses

Grandfather clauses exempted people from difficult voting requirements when they or their ancestors had been eligible to vote before emancipation. Because enslaved people and most free Black people had been excluded from voting, the exemption favored white voters.

White Primaries

In one-party political systems, exclusion from a party primary could determine the result of the general election. Political parties and states attempted to characterize primary exclusion as private conduct outside constitutional control.

Registration Manipulation

Officials limited registration hours, concealed requirements, rejected applications for minor errors, demanded vouchers, applied character tests, purged voter rolls, or refused to process Black applicants.

Racial Gerrymandering and Vote Dilution

Political boundaries and election structures could be manipulated to exclude Black voters from a jurisdiction, fragment communities, concentrate voters, or prevent an electoral majority from exercising effective political power.

Felony Disfranchisement

States could exclude people convicted of crimes, but discriminatory selection of offenses, unequal prosecution, and racially motivated administration could turn criminal-disqualification rules into instruments of racial political exclusion.

Early Judicial Interpretation

United States v. Reese

In 1876, the Supreme Court held portions of the Enforcement Act of 1870 invalid because they were not limited clearly enough to discrimination based on race, color, or previous servitude. The Court stated that the amendment did not itself confer suffrage but created a constitutional right to exemption from discrimination on the prohibited grounds.

United States v. Cruikshank

Also in 1876, the Court overturned federal convictions arising from the Colfax Massacre and narrowly construed federal power over private violence. The decision weakened federal protection against organized attacks on Black political participation.

Ex parte Yarbrough

In 1884, the Court upheld federal power to punish private conspirators who assaulted a Black citizen to prevent him from voting in a congressional election. The decision recognized broad federal authority to protect federal elections and the Fifteenth Amendment's guarantee.

These cases demonstrate that early doctrine was not uniform. The Court sometimes sustained federal protection but also imposed technical and federalism-based limits that reduced Reconstruction enforcement.

Judicial Treatment of Discriminatory Devices

Guinn v. United States

In 1915, the Supreme Court invalidated grandfather clauses in Oklahoma and Maryland because they were designed to preserve white voting while evading the Fifteenth Amendment.

Smith v. Allwright

In 1944, the Court held that Texas could not permit the Democratic Party to exclude Black voters from its primary. The primary was an integral part of the state-controlled election process rather than constitutionally protected private discrimination.

Gomillion v. Lightfoot

In 1960, the Court held that Alabama could not redraw Tuskegee's municipal boundaries to remove nearly all Black voters while retaining white voters. Racial manipulation of political boundaries could violate the Fifteenth Amendment.

City of Mobile v. Bolden

In 1980, a Supreme Court plurality concluded that a constitutional vote-dilution claim under the Fifteenth Amendment required proof of racially discriminatory purpose rather than discriminatory effect alone.

Congress responded to the effects-versus-intent controversy by amending Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act in 1982 to establish a statutory results test based on the totality of circumstances.

Voting Rights Act of 1965

Nearly a century after ratification, state and local governments continued using tests, intimidation, violence, administrative obstruction, and structural devices to suppress African American voting. Earlier case-by-case litigation was too slow to dismantle entrenched systems.

Congress enacted the Voting Rights Act of 1965 expressly to enforce the Fifteenth Amendment. The law prohibited racial discrimination in voting nationwide and created stronger federal remedies.

Section 2

Section 2 establishes a nationwide prohibition against voting qualifications, standards, practices, or procedures that deny or abridge voting rights on account of race or color. Congress amended it in 1982 to provide a results-based statutory standard under the totality of circumstances.

Tests and Devices

The act suspended literacy tests and related devices in covered jurisdictions. Later federal law extended the prohibition nationwide.

Federal Examiners and Observers

Federal personnel could register eligible voters and observe elections in jurisdictions where local administration could not be trusted to protect voting rights.

Preclearance

Section 5 required certain covered jurisdictions to obtain federal approval before implementing changes in voting practices. The jurisdictional coverage formula was contained in Section 4(b).

Immediate Effect

The act produced rapid increases in Black voter registration and participation by replacing slow individual litigation with direct federal oversight and preventive enforcement.

Modern Voting-Rights Doctrine

South Carolina v. Katzenbach

In 1966, the Supreme Court upheld major provisions of the Voting Rights Act as appropriate enforcement of the Fifteenth Amendment. The Court recognized Congress's authority to use strong remedies against persistent and evasive racial discrimination.

Allen v. State Board of Elections

In 1969, the Court interpreted the preclearance requirement broadly to cover changes capable of diminishing minority voting power, not merely rules governing registration or the physical act of casting a ballot.

Shelby County v. Holder

In 2013, the Supreme Court invalidated the Section 4(b) coverage formula because it relied on outdated data and imposed unequal federal burdens on states without sufficient current justification. The Court did not invalidate Section 5 itself, but preclearance under that provision became inoperative unless Congress adopts a valid new coverage formula or a court imposes targeted preclearance through another statutory provision.

Brnovich v. Democratic National Committee

In 2021, the Court identified guideposts for evaluating Section 2 challenges to voting rules governing the time, place, or manner of casting ballots. The decision made some vote-denial claims more difficult but did not eliminate Section 2.

Allen v. Milligan

In 2023, the Court affirmed a Section 2 challenge to Alabama's congressional districting plan and declined to rewrite the established framework for evaluating minority vote-dilution claims.

Louisiana v. Callais

In 2026, the Court substantially revised the Section 2 vote-dilution framework. It required circumstances supporting a strong inference of intentional discrimination and held that Section 2 did not justify Louisiana's race-predominant creation of an additional majority-Black congressional district on the record before the Court.

The Court stated that it was not overruling Allen v. Milligan, but later proceedings recognized that Callais significantly changed the governing analysis. The decision also emphasized that statutory remedies must remain within Congress's enforcement authority under Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment and consistent with the Fourteenth Amendment.

Constitutional and Statutory Voting Claims

A direct Fifteenth Amendment claim and a claim under the Voting Rights Act are related but not identical.

A constitutional claim ordinarily requires proof that government denied or abridged voting rights because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Modern doctrine generally requires intentional discrimination for a direct constitutional violation.

A statutory claim depends on the language Congress enacted. Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act has contained a results-focused totality-of-circumstances rule since 1982, although Supreme Court decisions define and limit how that rule operates in particular categories of cases.

The distinction matters because Congress may enact preventive and remedial legislation that reaches some practices beyond conduct a court would independently find unconstitutional, but enforcement legislation must remain appropriately connected to the amendment's guarantees.

Relationship to Other Constitutional Amendments

Thirteenth Amendment

Abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, subject to its punishment exception. The Fifteenth Amendment addressed racial political exclusion after emancipation.

Fourteenth Amendment

Established citizenship, due process, and equal protection and included a representation penalty for certain voting restrictions. Voting discrimination may implicate both the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

Nineteenth Amendment

Prohibits denial or abridgment of voting rights on account of sex. It addressed a category omitted from the Fifteenth Amendment.

Twenty-Fourth Amendment

Prohibits poll taxes in federal elections. The Supreme Court later invalidated state-election poll taxes under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Twenty-Sixth Amendment

Prohibits denial or abridgment of voting rights of citizens age eighteen or older on account of age.

Key Distinctions

Right to vote
The legal ability to participate in an election, subject to constitutional and lawful regulatory conditions.
Denial
A complete refusal to permit a qualified citizen to register, vote, or have a ballot counted.
Abridgment
A restriction, burden, manipulation, or weakening of voting rights short of complete denial.
Discriminatory purpose
An intent to deny or abridge voting rights because of race or another prohibited status.
Discriminatory effect
A practice's unequal practical impact, whether or not direct evidence establishes discriminatory intent.
Vote denial
A barrier affecting a person's opportunity to register, cast a ballot, or have the ballot counted.
Vote dilution
An election structure or districting system that weakens a protected group's effective electoral power.
Disfranchisement
The removal, denial, or practical destruction of voting rights.
Preclearance
Advance federal review of proposed election changes before they may take effect in a covered jurisdiction.
Formal equality
The use of rules written without an express racial classification.
Substantive access
The practical ability to register, vote, have a ballot counted, organize politically, and exercise meaningful electoral power without discrimination or coercion.

Why the Fifteenth Amendment Matters

The Fifteenth Amendment established that racial political exclusion was not merely a matter of state policy. It was a violation of the national Constitution subject to federal enforcement.

The amendment enabled African American men to claim political membership through voting, officeholding, party organization, legislation, and public administration during Reconstruction. It helped create the first sustained period of interracial democracy in the former Confederate states.

Its history also demonstrates the difference between constitutional recognition and effective enforcement. White supremacist violence, facially neutral restrictions, administrative discretion, political-party exclusion, and structural manipulation substantially defeated the amendment for decades.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 showed that constitutional rights may require detailed federal institutions, preventive review, litigation authority, federal registration, and continuing oversight to become practically effective.

Modern disputes over election administration, districting, vote dilution, discriminatory intent, federalism, and congressional enforcement remain connected to the amendment's central command that race cannot lawfully determine whose political voice counts.

Summary

The Fifteenth Amendment was adopted during Reconstruction to prohibit federal and state governments from denying or abridging a citizen's voting rights because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. It also authorized Congress to enforce that prohibition through appropriate legislation.

The amendment invalidated express white-only voting qualifications and supported an extraordinary expansion of Black political participation. It did not establish universal suffrage, protect women from sex-based disfranchisement, or prohibit every facially neutral voting qualification.

Violence, administrative discrimination, poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries, racial gerrymandering, and other devices substantially defeated its practical operation after Reconstruction. Federal legislation and court decisions gradually challenged these systems, culminating in the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

The amendment remains the constitutional foundation for federal action against racial discrimination in voting, while modern litigation continues to define the boundary among constitutional intent requirements, statutory results tests, state election authority, equal protection, and congressional enforcement power.
