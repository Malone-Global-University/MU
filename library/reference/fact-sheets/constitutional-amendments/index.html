Constitutional Amendments

Constitutional Amendments fact sheets explain formal changes to the United States Constitution. Each entry identifies the amendment’s text, ratification history, principal provisions, legal significance, enforcement authority, historical context, and continuing effect on American government and civil rights.

Collection Overview

This collection serves as the canonical location for fact sheets centered on individual amendments to the United States Constitution. Related laws, court cases, political events, and historical periods remain in their respective categories and are connected through cross-references. Amendment entries are arranged numerically so readers can follow the development of constitutional rights, governmental powers, and institutional rules over time.

Bill of Rights

  • First Amendment

    Protects religious liberty, freedom of speech and the press, peaceful assembly, and the right to petition the government.

  • Second Amendment

    Protects the right of the people to keep and bear arms and refers to the importance of a well-regulated militia.

  • Third Amendment

    Restricts the government from quartering soldiers in private homes without the owner’s consent.

  • Fourth Amendment

    Protects against unreasonable searches and seizures and establishes requirements for the issuance of warrants.

  • Fifth Amendment

    Establishes protections involving grand juries, double jeopardy, self-incrimination, due process, and government takings of private property.

  • Sixth Amendment

    Guarantees important rights in criminal prosecutions, including a speedy and public trial, an impartial jury, notice of accusations, confrontation of witnesses, and assistance of counsel.

  • Seventh Amendment

    Preserves the right to a jury trial in certain civil cases in federal courts and limits judicial reexamination of jury findings.

  • Eighth Amendment

    Prohibits excessive bail, excessive fines, and cruel and unusual punishments.

  • Ninth Amendment

    Clarifies that listing particular rights in the Constitution does not deny or disparage other rights retained by the people.

  • Tenth Amendment

    Reserves powers not delegated to the United States, and not prohibited to the states, to the states or the people.

Early Constitutional Corrections

  • Eleventh Amendment

    Limits federal judicial authority over certain lawsuits brought against states by citizens of another state or a foreign country.

  • Twelfth Amendment

    Revised the Electoral College process by requiring electors to cast separate votes for president and vice president.

    • Reconstruction Amendments

    • Thirteenth Amendment

      Abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States, except as punishment for a crime after conviction, and authorized Congress to enforce the amendment through appropriate legislation.

    • Fourteenth Amendment

      Established constitutional rules governing national and state citizenship and prohibited states from abridging privileges or immunities, depriving persons of due process, or denying equal protection of the laws.

    • Fifteenth Amendment

      Prohibited the United States and the states from denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude, and authorized congressional enforcement.

    Progressive Era Amendments

    • Sixteenth Amendment

      Authorized Congress to collect income taxes without apportioning them among the states according to population.

    • Seventeenth Amendment

      Established the direct election of United States senators by voters rather than selection by state legislatures.

    • Eighteenth Amendment

      Established national Prohibition by banning the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors for beverage purposes.

    • Nineteenth Amendment

      Prohibited the United States and the states from denying or abridging voting rights on account of sex.

    Modern Government and Electoral Amendments

    • Twentieth Amendment

      Changed the beginning and ending dates of presidential and congressional terms and addressed certain presidential succession contingencies.

    • Twenty-First Amendment

      Repealed the Eighteenth Amendment and restored substantial state authority over the transportation and distribution of alcoholic beverages.

    • Twenty-Second Amendment

      Limits the number of times a person may be elected president and addresses service exceeding two years of another president’s term.

    • Twenty-Third Amendment

      Granted the District of Columbia participation in presidential elections through appointed electors.

    • Twenty-Fourth Amendment

      Prohibited poll taxes and other taxes as conditions for voting in federal elections.

    • Twenty-Fifth Amendment

      Establishes procedures governing presidential succession, vice-presidential vacancies, and presidential inability.

    • Twenty-Sixth Amendment

      Prohibited the United States and the states from denying voting rights to citizens eighteen years of age or older on account of age.

    • Twenty-Seventh Amendment

      Prevents changes to congressional compensation from taking effect until after an intervening election of representatives.
