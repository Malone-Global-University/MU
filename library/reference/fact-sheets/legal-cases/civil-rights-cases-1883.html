Civil Rights Cases (1883)

The consolidated Supreme Court decision that invalidated the federal public-accommodations provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 and limited Congress's power to prohibit private racial discrimination under the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

What Were the Civil Rights Cases?

The Civil Rights Cases were five cases decided together by the United States Supreme Court in 1883. Each arose under the public-accommodations provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875, which required equal access to inns, public conveyances, theaters, and other places of public amusement without discrimination based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

The cases asked whether Congress possessed constitutional authority to regulate discrimination committed by private businesses and individuals. The Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment restricted states and state officials rather than private conduct, while the Thirteenth Amendment did not, in the majority's view, authorize Congress to classify exclusion from public accommodations as a badge or incident of slavery.

The decision did not declare racial discrimination lawful in every context. It determined that the particular federal statute lacked a sufficient constitutional basis as applied to private conduct within the states. The distinction between a wrongful act and federal constitutional authority to regulate that act was central to the Court's reasoning.

Why the Title Is Plural

The title Civil Rights Cases refers to a group of separate proceedings rather than one lawsuit between two parties. Four were federal criminal prosecutions or enforcement proceedings against individuals accused of violating the 1875 Act. The fifth was a civil action brought by a married couple against a railroad company.

The Supreme Court considered the cases together because they presented a common constitutional issue: whether Sections 1 and 2 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 could regulate racial discrimination by private providers of lodging, entertainment, and transportation.

The collective case name should not be confused with the Civil Rights Act itself. The Act was legislation adopted by Congress in 1875; the Civil Rights Cases were the judicial proceedings in which the Supreme Court reviewed the constitutionality of its principal public-accommodations provisions.

Reconstruction and the National Protection of Civil Rights

The Civil War and emancipation transformed the constitutional status of African Americans. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, the Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and prohibited states from denying due process or equal protection, and the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting.

Congress enacted enforcement legislation to give practical effect to these constitutional changes. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 protected specified rights involving contracts, property, litigation, and legal security. The Enforcement Acts addressed election interference and organized racial violence. Federal officials also prosecuted state officers and private actors under laws intended to protect the rights of newly emancipated citizens.

By the 1870s, however, federal commitment to Reconstruction was weakening. White supremacist violence, political opposition, disputed elections, adverse court decisions, and the restoration of conservative state governments reduced federal protection. The Civil Rights Act of 1875 became the last major civil-rights statute enacted during Reconstruction.

The Civil Rights Act of 1875

Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts had pressed for legislation protecting equal access to public institutions and facilities. After extended debate and substantial revision, Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1875 on March 1, 1875.

The final law was narrower than Sumner's earlier proposals. Public-school integration was removed from the enacted bill. The law nevertheless attempted to establish a national rule against racial discrimination in major public accommodations and included a separate prohibition against racial exclusion from jury service.

Section 1: Equal enjoyment

Section 1 declared that all persons within United States jurisdiction were entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the accommodations, advantages, facilities, and privileges of inns, public conveyances on land or water, theaters, and other places of public amusement. Conditions and limitations could be imposed only when they applied alike to citizens of every race and color, regardless of previous enslavement.

Section 2: Enforcement and remedies

Section 2 imposed liability on persons who violated Section 1. It authorized a monetary forfeiture to the injured person and permitted criminal prosecution, thereby placing enforcement in federal court rather than leaving victims solely to state law.

Jury-service protection

A separate section prohibited disqualification from jury service because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. The Supreme Court had upheld federal authority to punish racial discrimination by a state judge selecting jurors in Ex parte Virginia in 1880. The Civil Rights Cases addressed the public-accommodations sections, not the jury provision.

Public Accommodations and Private Power

Inns, common carriers, theaters, and similar establishments were privately owned but served the public. Their owners controlled access to transportation, lodging, and public entertainment—services essential to travel, commerce, and ordinary civic participation.

The distinction between purely private choice and public obligation was therefore contested. Common-law traditions imposed duties on innkeepers and common carriers to serve eligible customers. States also regulated rates, safety, licenses, routes, and service obligations. Supporters of the 1875 Act argued that businesses inviting the public to use essential services could not use race as a lawful condition of access.

The Supreme Court majority nevertheless treated the discrimination charged in the consolidated cases as private conduct unless it was supported by a state law, state official, or other governmental action. Justice Harlan disputed that characterization because he viewed the regulated businesses as exercising public or quasi-public functions.

The Five Consolidated Cases

United States v. Stanley

The federal government prosecuted Stanley for allegedly denying a person of color the accommodations and privileges of an inn or hotel. The case reached the Supreme Court through a certificate stating that the judges below had divided over the constitutionality of the Act.

United States v. Ryan

Ryan was accused of refusing a person of color a seat in the dress circle of Maguire's Theatre in San Francisco, California. The federal circuit court sustained a challenge to the information, and the United States sought Supreme Court review.

United States v. Nichols

Nichols was prosecuted for allegedly denying a person of color the accommodations and privileges of an inn or hotel. As in Stanley, divided views in the lower federal court brought the constitutional question before the Supreme Court.

United States v. Singleton

Singleton was indicted for denying an individual full enjoyment of the Grand Opera House in New York. The Supreme Court's statement noted that the indictment did not identify the alleged victim's color, but it charged that the denial was not based on a reason legally applicable to citizens of every race and color.

Robinson and Wife v. Memphis and Charleston Railroad Company

Richard A. Robinson and his wife brought a civil action seeking the statutory penalty after a railroad conductor refused to permit Mrs. Robinson, a woman of African descent, to ride in the ladies' car. A jury found for the railroad after the trial judge allowed consideration of the conductor's claimed nonracial reason for the exclusion. The Robinsons sought Supreme Court review.

How the Cases Reached the Supreme Court

The five proceedings did not arrive through one procedural route. Stanley, Nichols, and Singleton came through certificates of division, a procedure used when judges in a federal circuit court disagreed on a controlling legal question. Ryan reached the Court by writ of error after the lower court sustained a challenge to the prosecution. Robinson came by writ of error following a civil verdict for the railroad.

The four federal enforcement cases were submitted together in November 1882 without appearances or briefs for the defendants. The Robinson case was submitted separately on written briefs in March 1883. The Court then issued one opinion resolving the common constitutional issue.

The procedural differences mattered to the final disposition, but the constitutional analysis applied across all five cases. Once the Court concluded that Sections 1 and 2 lacked constitutional authority as applied within the states, the federal prosecutions could not proceed under those provisions.

Questions Before the Supreme Court

  • Did Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment authorize Congress to prohibit racial discrimination by private owners and operators of public accommodations?
  • Could Congress reach the same conduct under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment as legislation against slavery or its badges and incidents?
  • Were Sections 1 and 2 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 valid as applied to conduct occurring within the states?
  • Did the cases require the Court to decide the Act's validity in federal territories, the District of Columbia, or interstate commerce?

The Supreme Court's Decision

Justice Joseph P. Bradley delivered the opinion for eight members of the Court. The majority held Sections 1 and 2 unconstitutional and void as applied within the states. It found no sufficient authority in either the Thirteenth or Fourteenth Amendment for Congress to regulate the private refusals alleged in the consolidated cases.

The ruling rested on two distinct constitutional conclusions. First, the Fourteenth Amendment addressed action by states and their officials rather than ordinary private conduct. Second, although the Thirteenth Amendment reached private slavery without requiring state action, the Court narrowly defined the badges and incidents against which Congress could legislate and excluded discrimination in public accommodations from that category.

The Court affirmed the lower-court judgments in Ryan and Robinson. In the other prosecutions, it directed that judgment be entered according to its conclusion that the challenged provisions were unconstitutional.

The Fourteenth Amendment and the State-Action Doctrine

The first section of the Fourteenth Amendment declares that no state shall make or enforce specified laws, deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process, or deny any person equal protection. The majority emphasized the amendment's references to state responsibility.

From this language, the Court concluded that the amendment did not itself prohibit every private act of racial discrimination. A private hotel owner, theater operator, or railroad employee did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment merely by acting discriminatorily unless the conduct could be attributed to the state.

This interpretation became known as the state-action doctrine. It distinguishes governmental conduct, which the Fourteenth Amendment regulates, from private conduct, which generally requires another constitutional or statutory basis for federal regulation.

The doctrine does not restrict state governments from prohibiting private discrimination under state law. It limits the use of the Fourteenth Amendment as the direct constitutional source of federal authority over private actors.

Corrective Rather Than General Legislation

Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment gives Congress power to enforce the amendment through appropriate legislation. The majority interpreted that power as corrective or remedial. Congress could counteract unconstitutional state laws, official acts, or state failures attributable to government, but it could not use Section 5 to replace state law with a general national code governing relations among private persons.

The Court reasoned that the 1875 Act did not depend on proof that a state had enacted a discriminatory law or that a state official had denied equal protection. It applied even in states whose laws formally protected equal access and whose courts were prepared to provide a remedy.

Because the Act directly punished private discrimination without identifying a prior constitutional wrong by a state, the majority held that it exceeded Congress's Fourteenth Amendment enforcement authority.

Later doctrine continued to recognize a difference between constitutional rights violated by government and statutory rules that regulate private conduct. The precise scope of Congress's Section 5 authority has changed across later decisions, but the state-action premise of the Civil Rights Cases remains influential.

The Thirteenth Amendment Analysis

The Thirteenth Amendment differs from the Fourteenth Amendment because its prohibition against slavery and involuntary servitude is not limited to state action. It operates against private as well as governmental conduct. Congress therefore argued that the 1875 Act could be sustained as legislation enforcing freedom and eliminating the badges and incidents of slavery.

The majority accepted that Congress could legislate directly against slavery and its incidents. It identified historical incidents of slavery such as compulsory service, restraints on movement, inability to own property or enter contracts, and legal incapacity to testify or seek judicial protection.

The Court nevertheless concluded that a private refusal of lodging, transportation accommodations, or theater admission did not impose slavery or a legally recognized badge of slavery. It characterized such discrimination as an ordinary civil injury to be addressed under state law unless state action created a Fourteenth Amendment violation.

This narrow understanding of congressional power under the Thirteenth Amendment remained influential for decades. In 1968, Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. adopted a broader approach, holding that Congress could determine that private racial discrimination in property transactions constituted a badge or incident of slavery and could prohibit it through the Civil Rights Act of 1866.

Questions the Court Did Not Decide

The opinion contained several express reservations. These limitations are important because later summaries sometimes describe the decision more broadly than its formal holding.

  • The Court decided the Act's validity as applied within the states, not whether Congress could enforce it in federal territories or the District of Columbia.
  • The Court did not decide whether Congress could prohibit discrimination on lines of public conveyance operating between states under the Commerce Clause.
  • The Court did not definitively decide whether equal access to the listed accommodations was a right constitutionally demandable through some other legal route.
  • The Court did not invalidate the Act's separate jury-discrimination provision.
  • The Court did not hold that states lacked authority to prohibit discrimination by private businesses under state constitutions, civil-rights statutes, common law, or regulatory power.

Justice Harlan's Dissent

Justice John Marshall Harlan was the sole dissenter. He argued that the majority interpreted the Reconstruction Amendments too narrowly and sacrificed their purpose through an artificial distinction between public and private conduct.

Harlan viewed innkeepers, common carriers, and places of public amusement as businesses affected with a public interest. Their owners exercised privileges, franchises, or public functions and had long been subject to legal duties toward the public. In his view, racial exclusion by such actors was not equivalent to an entirely private social choice within a home or private association.

He also interpreted national citizenship and the Fourteenth Amendment as supporting direct congressional protection against racial discrimination in fundamental civil rights. Congress, he argued, could protect rights created or secured by the national Constitution rather than waiting for a state legislature to enact an openly discriminatory statute.

Under the Thirteenth Amendment, Harlan regarded exclusion from public accommodations because of race as connected to the subordinate civil status imposed by slavery. He warned that the majority's interpretation would leave the rights of freedom without the national protection previously supplied to slaveholders' constitutional interests.

Harlan's dissent did not control the 1883 judgment, but later civil-rights jurisprudence and scholarship frequently treated it as a more expansive account of national responsibility and the public character of regulated accommodations.

How the Decision Worked in Practice

The ruling removed the federal cause of action and criminal sanction created by Sections 1 and 2 of the 1875 Act for discrimination in public accommodations within the states. Federal prosecutors could no longer rely on those provisions to charge hotelkeepers, theater operators, or carriers solely for private racial exclusion.

Individuals denied service were generally left to remedies available under state law. Those remedies varied widely. Some states had civil-rights statutes or common-law duties applicable to inns and carriers. Other states tolerated private discrimination or later required racial segregation by law.

The practical effect was particularly severe because the decision arrived while Reconstruction governments were collapsing and white-controlled state governments were restricting Black political power. A doctrinal rule requiring state action offered limited protection when state institutions refused to regulate discrimination, failed to enforce formally neutral laws, or themselves developed segregation systems.

Relationship to Strauder and Ex parte Virginia

Three years earlier, the Supreme Court had enforced the Fourteenth Amendment against racial discrimination in jury selection. In Strauder v. West Virginia, it invalidated a state statute limiting juries to white male citizens. In Ex parte Virginia, it upheld federal prosecution of a state judge who excluded Black citizens while performing an official jury-selection function.

The Civil Rights Cases distinguished those decisions because they involved state law or a state official exercising governmental authority. The majority treated private innkeepers, theater operators, and carriers differently when no state action was alleged.

The comparison illustrates the operational boundary created by the doctrine. Congress could address racial discrimination attributable to a state, but the Court held that the same amendment did not authorize direct federal regulation of ordinary private discrimination.

The Collapse of Reconstruction

The decision came after the withdrawal of federal troops from remaining Reconstruction political disputes in 1877 and during the restoration of white Democratic control across the South. African American voting, officeholding, jury participation, education, labor rights, and personal security were already under sustained attack.

The 1875 Act had represented an attempt to preserve a national floor of equal access after emancipation. By invalidating its public-accommodations provisions, the Court narrowed the federal government's role precisely when many state governments were becoming less willing to protect Black citizens.

The decision did not itself create every later segregation law. It removed an important federal barrier and announced a constitutional framework under which private discrimination was generally beyond the Fourteenth Amendment unless connected to government action.

Relationship to Jim Crow

Jim Crow was the broader system of racial segregation, political disfranchisement, economic control, unequal public institutions, and racial violence that expanded after Reconstruction. The Civil Rights Cases became one of the legal developments that facilitated this system.

Private businesses could exclude Black customers without federal liability under the invalidated provisions. States and municipalities also began enacting laws requiring separation in transportation, education, and public facilities. Where segregation was required by state law, the state-action requirement was technically satisfied, but later decisions—most notably Plessy v. Ferguson—interpreted equal protection to permit state-mandated “separate but equal” facilities.

The combined effect of the Civil Rights Cases and Plessy was to restrict federal constitutional protection against both private exclusion and government-mandated segregation for much of the Jim Crow era.

Later Development of the State-Action Doctrine

The state-action doctrine remained part of Fourteenth Amendment law, but courts developed circumstances in which formally private conduct could be attributed to government.

In Shelley v. Kraemer in 1948, private racially restrictive housing covenants were not themselves treated as Fourteenth Amendment violations, but judicial enforcement of the covenants constituted state action. In Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority in 1961, the Court found state action where a discriminatory restaurant operated within and financially benefited a publicly owned facility.

Other cases examined public functions, governmental coercion, significant encouragement, joint participation, and institutional interdependence. These doctrines did not abolish the public-private distinction, but they demonstrated that government responsibility can exist even when the immediate actor is nominally private.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Commerce Clause

Congress returned to public-accommodations legislation during the modern Civil Rights Movement. Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination in specified hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other establishments connected to interstate commerce.

Rather than relying principally on the Fourteenth Amendment to regulate purely private conduct, Congress used its Article I power to regulate interstate commerce. It documented how racial discrimination obstructed travel, burdened commerce, and restricted the movement of goods and people.

In Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States and Katzenbach v. McClung, both decided in 1964, the Supreme Court unanimously upheld Title II as a valid exercise of the Commerce Clause. The decisions supplied the effective federal protection that the 1883 ruling had withdrawn from public accommodations.

The use of a different constitutional power was significant. The Court did not need to overrule the state-action holding of the Civil Rights Cases to sustain federal regulation of private businesses affecting interstate commerce.

Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. and the Thirteenth Amendment

In Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. in 1968, the Supreme Court held that the Civil Rights Act of 1866 prohibited private racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property. The Court concluded that Congress could rationally determine what constituted the badges and incidents of slavery and legislate against them under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment.

Jones did not formally eliminate every proposition in the Civil Rights Cases, but it substantially broadened the understanding of congressional Thirteenth Amendment power. Private racial discrimination could be reached without proving state action when Congress validly acted to remove a badge or incident of slavery.

Later federal statutes protecting contracts and property rights have likewise operated against private discrimination. The continuing question is not whether the Thirteenth Amendment reaches only government, but whether the regulated practice falls within Congress's legitimate enforcement authority concerning slavery and its enduring incidents.

Historical and Constitutional Significance

The Civil Rights Cases established a durable constitutional distinction between government discrimination and private discrimination. That distinction shaped litigation, legislation, and federal enforcement for generations.

The decision also narrowed the national government's capacity to defend African American civil rights at a decisive historical moment. It directed victims toward state law even as numerous states were disfranchising Black voters, tolerating racial violence, and constructing segregation.

Constitutionally, the case became a foundation of the state-action doctrine and an early limitation on Congress's enforcement power under the Reconstruction Amendments. Later decisions broadened federal authority through the Commerce Clause and the Thirteenth Amendment, but the Fourteenth Amendment generally continues to require governmental responsibility.

The case therefore represents both a specific ruling on the 1875 Act and a larger conflict over federalism: whether civil rights created or secured after slavery would be primarily protected by the national government or left to states and private-law remedies.

Key Distinctions

Civil Rights Act of 1875
The federal statute requiring equal access to specified public accommodations and prohibiting racial exclusion from jury service.
Civil Rights Cases
The five consolidated Supreme Court proceedings that reviewed the Act's public-accommodations provisions.
State action
Conduct legally attributable to a state, including legislation, official acts, judicial enforcement, and some forms of governmental participation in nominally private conduct.
Private discrimination
Discriminatory conduct by a nongovernmental person or institution not legally attributable to the state. It may still violate federal or state statutes even when it does not directly violate the Fourteenth Amendment.
Corrective legislation
Legislation designed to prevent or remedy constitutional violations by states. The majority described Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment in these terms.
Direct legislation
A law regulating conduct without requiring a prior state violation. The Court held that the 1875 public-accommodations provisions operated directly on private actors.
Badge or incident of slavery
A condition, disability, or practice sufficiently connected to slavery that Congress may regulate it under the Thirteenth Amendment. The scope of this concept expanded in later doctrine.
Public accommodation
A business or facility offering lodging, food, transportation, entertainment, or other services to the public and subject to applicable legal duties.
Constitutional prohibition
A rule imposed directly by the Constitution, such as the Fourteenth Amendment's restriction on states.
Statutory prohibition
A rule enacted by a legislature under an identified constitutional power, such as Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception: The Court invalidated the entire Civil Rights Act of 1875

The formal holding invalidated Sections 1 and 2 as applied within the states. The separate jury-service provision was not the subject of the decision and had been upheld in the context of official jury discrimination.

Misconception: The Court held that private racial discrimination was morally acceptable

The majority's legal conclusion concerned federal constitutional authority. It treated the conduct as potentially wrongful under state law while holding that the Reconstruction Amendments did not authorize the challenged federal provisions.

Misconception: The Fourteenth Amendment can never apply when a private party is involved

Nominally private conduct may constitute state action when government compels, enforces, significantly supports, jointly participates in, or becomes sufficiently connected to the conduct under later doctrine.

Misconception: The decision settled Congress's Commerce Clause power

The Court expressly reserved the question of federal authority over interstate public conveyances. Congress later relied on the Commerce Clause for the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Misconception: The case and Plessy v. Ferguson decided the same issue

The Civil Rights Cases concerned federal power over private discrimination. Plessy concerned whether a state segregation law violated equal protection.

Misconception: Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. erased the state-action doctrine

Jones broadened Congress's Thirteenth Amendment power over private discrimination. It did not convert all private discrimination into state action under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Why the Civil Rights Cases Matter

The decision matters because it defined the constitutional boundary between public and private discrimination at the end of Reconstruction. By requiring state action for Fourteenth Amendment enforcement, the Court prevented Congress from using that amendment as a general authority to regulate private racial exclusion.

It also matters because legal remedies depend on institutional power. The Court directed victims toward state protection at a time when many states were refusing to protect African Americans or were actively constructing systems of segregation and disfranchisement.

Finally, the case explains why later civil-rights legislation relied on multiple constitutional foundations. Congress used the Commerce Clause to regulate public accommodations in 1964 and the Thirteenth Amendment to reach certain private discrimination involving property and contracts. The 1883 decision therefore remains essential to understanding federalism, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and the legal architecture of modern civil-rights enforcement.

Summary

The Civil Rights Cases were five consolidated Supreme Court proceedings challenging the constitutionality of the Civil Rights Act of 1875's public-accommodations provisions. The cases arose from alleged discrimination in hotels, theaters, and railroad transportation and presented the question whether Congress could directly prohibit private racial discrimination under the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

The Court held 8-1 that Sections 1 and 2 were unconstitutional as applied within the states. The majority interpreted the Fourteenth Amendment as applying to state action and limiting Congress to corrective legislation directed at governmental violations. It also held that private exclusion from public accommodations was not a badge or incident of slavery within its narrow interpretation of the Thirteenth Amendment. Justice Harlan dissented, arguing that the regulated businesses performed public functions and that the Reconstruction Amendments authorized national protection of equal civil rights.

The ruling removed federal protection against public-accommodations discrimination during the collapse of Reconstruction and contributed to the legal environment in which Jim Crow expanded. Later Congresses used the Commerce Clause to enact the Civil Rights Act of 1964, while Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. broadened federal power under the Thirteenth Amendment. The state-action doctrine established by the 1883 decision, however, remains a central part of Fourteenth Amendment law.
