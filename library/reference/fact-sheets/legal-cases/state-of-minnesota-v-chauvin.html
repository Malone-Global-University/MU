State of Minnesota v. Chauvin

The Minnesota criminal prosecution in which a Hennepin County jury found former Minneapolis police officer Derek Michael Chauvin guilty of second-degree unintentional felony murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter for causing George Floyd's death, followed by entry of judgment on the felony-murder count and a 270-month state prison sentence.

Case Identity

State of Minnesota v. Chauvin was a state criminal homicide prosecution.

It arose from Chauvin's use of force while acting as a Minneapolis police officer.

The case proceeded from complaint through jury trial, sentencing, direct appeal, discretionary-review petitions, and post-conviction litigation.

Formal Caption

The district-court caption identified the State of Minnesota as plaintiff and Derek Michael Chauvin as defendant.

The reported appellate caption is commonly shortened to State v. Chauvin.

The formal case number distinguishes this prosecution from federal and tax proceedings involving Chauvin.

State Jurisdiction

Minnesota courts possessed jurisdiction because the alleged homicide occurred in Minneapolis, Hennepin County.

The charges arose under Minnesota criminal statutes.

Federal constitutional rules governed subjects such as due process, confrontation, impartial jury, and police reasonableness, but the offenses were state crimes.

Hennepin County District Court

The Minnesota Fourth Judicial District Court tried the case.

The district court ruled on bail, discovery, joinder, severance, venue, cameras, evidence, instructions, posttrial motions, conviction entry, and sentencing.

Its legal rulings were later reviewed under the applicable appellate standards.

Judge Peter A. Cahill

Judge Peter Cahill presided over the trial and sentencing.

The judge was responsible for law, procedure, admissibility, instructions, courtroom administration, and sentence.

The jury—not the judge—decided guilt.

State Prosecution Team

Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison assumed leadership of the prosecution.

The Attorney General's Office worked with the Hennepin County Attorney's Office and appointed outside attorneys.

The prosecution represented the State rather than Floyd's family or the City of Minneapolis.

Defense

Eric Nelson served as Chauvin's trial counsel.

The defense challenged the cause of death, the reasonableness of force, venue, jury impartiality, evidentiary rulings, instructions, prosecutorial conduct, and sentence.

Defense advocacy did not require Chauvin to prove innocence.

Presumption of Innocence

Chauvin was presumed innocent before the verdict.

The State bore the burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

The burden never shifted to the defense.

George Floyd

George Floyd was a 46-year-old Black man living in the Minneapolis area.

A store employee reported suspected use of a counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

The resulting police encounter ended in Floyd's death and became a central event in modern civil-rights history.

Initial Police Response

Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng arrived first.

They encountered Floyd in a vehicle, removed and handcuffed him, and attempted to place him in a police vehicle.

Derek Chauvin and Tou Thao later arrived.

Beginning of the Street Restraint

Officers placed Floyd prone on the street.

Chauvin positioned pressure on Floyd's neck and upper body while other officers restrained his torso and legs.

The Court of Appeals placed the start of the prone restraint at approximately 8:19 p.m.

Nine Minutes and Twenty-Nine Seconds

The appellate opinion described the restraint as approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds.

Floyd became progressively less responsive during that period.

Earlier complaint calculations and some sentencing language used slightly different estimates based on the evidence then available; the appellate chronology is the controlling published summary.

Floyd's Statements

Floyd repeatedly said he could not breathe.

He expressed pain, distress, fear, and concern that he would die.

His speech eventually ceased.

Bystanders

Bystanders observed the restraint and urged officers to check Floyd's condition.

Some asked officers to stop the force or provide medical care.

Their recordings and testimony became important evidence.

Bystander Video

Darnella Frazier recorded a sustained view of the restraint.

The video allowed jurors to observe timing, officer positions, Floyd's condition, and bystander warnings.

The recording became globally influential but remained one item within a larger trial record.

Police Body-Camera Evidence

Body-worn cameras captured earlier stages, officer speech, positioning, and portions of the restraint.

The recordings supplied multiple visual and audio perspectives.

Each camera had limitations in angle, obstruction, motion, and field of view.

Loss of Responsiveness

Floyd stopped pleading and became nonresponsive before officers ended the restraint.

Kueng checked for a pulse and stated that he could not find one.

Chauvin continued restraint until emergency personnel arrived and officers moved Floyd.

Emergency Medical Response

Emergency medical personnel transported Floyd and attempted resuscitation.

Floyd was pronounced dead.

The timing and adequacy of medical response formed part of the causation evidence but did not displace analysis of the preceding restraint.

Medical Examiner's Findings

Hennepin County Chief Medical Examiner Dr. Andrew Baker classified the death as a homicide.

The cause was cardiopulmonary arrest complicating law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression.

Heart disease and fentanyl use were listed as contributing conditions rather than the sole cause.

Meaning of Homicide in Death Investigation

A medical-examiner homicide classification means death resulted from another person's actions.

It is not itself a legal conclusion that murder occurred.

The jury separately applied Minnesota criminal statutes and the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard.

Initial Complaint

Hennepin County prosecutors filed the case on May 29, 2020.

The initial charges included third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

A criminal complaint alleges probable cause and does not establish guilt.

June 3, 2020 Amended Complaint

The amended complaint added second-degree unintentional felony murder.

It retained third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

The amendment also reflected the Minnesota Attorney General's assumption of prosecutorial leadership.

Count One—Second-Degree Unintentional Felony Murder

Count One alleged that Chauvin caused Floyd's death without intent to kill while committing or attempting to commit third-degree assault.

The theory is commonly called felony murder.

It required proof of death, causation, the predicate assault, and the offense location and date.

Felony-Murder Doctrine

Felony murder permits murder liability when death occurs during commission or attempted commission of a qualifying felony.

The State did not need to prove a purpose to kill.

It did need to prove the intentional force constituting assault and the resulting death.

Predicate Third-Degree Assault

The predicate felony was third-degree assault.

The prosecution had to establish intentional application of force causing substantial bodily harm.

The jury did not need to find that Chauvin intended the precise level of injury or intended death.

Assault-Harm Theory

Minnesota assault law includes intentional infliction or attempted infliction of bodily harm.

The relevant intent concerns the physical act of applying force.

The Court of Appeals rejected the argument that this converted the offense into strict liability.

Substantial Bodily Harm

Third-degree assault requires substantial bodily harm.

Minnesota law includes temporary but substantial disfigurement, substantial impairment or loss of bodily function, or fracture.

The State relied upon Floyd's loss of consciousness, physiological injury, and death-producing restraint.

No Intent to Kill Required

Second-degree unintentional felony murder expressly applies without intent to cause death.

The absence of a murder purpose does not defeat the count.

The predicate assault supplies the felony component.

Count Two—Third-Degree Murder

Count Two alleged an eminently dangerous act evincing a depraved mind without regard for human life.

The maximum statutory penalty was twenty-five years.

The jury returned a guilty verdict, but the district court did not enter a separate conviction or sentence on that count.

Depraved-Mind Murder

Third-degree murder addresses death caused by eminently dangerous conduct demonstrating a depraved mind.

Minnesota appellate doctrine concerning whether conduct must be directed at more than one person changed during the Chauvin litigation.

Because no judgment was entered on Count Two, the direct appeal declined to resolve Chauvin's Noor-based challenge.

Count Three—Second-Degree Manslaughter

Count Three alleged culpable negligence creating an unreasonable risk and consciously taking a chance of causing death or great bodily harm.

The maximum statutory penalty was ten years.

The jury returned a guilty verdict, but no separate conviction or sentence was entered.

Culpable Negligence

Culpable negligence exceeds ordinary carelessness.

The jury instruction required an unreasonable risk and conscious taking of a chance of death or great bodily harm.

The count supplied a distinct homicide theory from felony murder.

Alternative Homicide Theories

Prosecutors may submit legally supported alternative counts arising from one death.

The jury was instructed to consider each count separately.

Minnesota conviction-entry rules later prevented multiple formal homicide convictions for the same criminal act.

Authorized Police Force

Minnesota law permits reasonable force when an officer lawfully makes an arrest or performs specified duties.

Authorization ends when force becomes unreasonable.

A lawful arrest therefore does not immunize an officer from assault or homicide liability for excessive force.

Objective Reasonableness

The jury was instructed to evaluate force from the perspective of a reasonable officer at the precise moment.

The totality of circumstances included threat, resistance, suspected offense, duration, restraint, and information known to officers.

Subjective good or bad motives were not the constitutional test.

State's Burden on Authorized Force

Once authorized-force law was submitted, the State bore the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the force was not authorized.

The defense did not have to prove reasonableness.

The instruction preserved the criminal burden of proof.

Causation

The jury had to find that Chauvin caused Floyd's death.

His conduct needed to be a substantial causal factor rather than the only cause.

The law permits multiple contributing causes.

Superseding Cause

An intervening event relieves criminal responsibility only when it becomes a superseding sole cause under the instruction.

Heart disease, drugs, stress, restraint, and other conditions could all be evaluated.

The State did not need to prove Floyd had no preexisting vulnerability.

Victim's Preexisting Conditions

A defendant generally takes the victim as found.

A condition making death more likely does not eliminate causation when the defendant's conduct remains a substantial factor.

The medical dispute therefore concerned contribution and mechanism, not whether Floyd was perfectly healthy.

Initial Dismissal of Third-Degree Murder

Before trial, the district court dismissed the third-degree-murder count.

The ruling rested on then-contested Minnesota doctrine concerning conduct directed at a particular person.

The State pursued an interlocutory appeal.

State's Interlocutory Appeal

The Minnesota Court of Appeals ruled that then-binding precedent required reconsideration of third-degree murder.

The case returned to the district court.

Judge Cahill reinstated Count Two before trial.

State v. Noor

The Minnesota Supreme Court later clarified third-degree-murder doctrine in State v. Noor.

Chauvin invoked Noor on direct appeal.

The Court of Appeals declined to decide the issue because Count Two was unadjudicated.

Joinder With Other Officers

The prosecution initially contemplated a joint trial involving Chauvin, Thao, Kueng, and Lane.

Joinder can improve efficiency but creates evidence, scheduling, prejudice, and defense-conflict concerns.

The district court ultimately tried Chauvin separately.

Separate Trial

Chauvin's trial was severed from the other officers' state proceedings.

COVID-19 courtroom limits and the complexity of a four-defendant trial affected scheduling.

The separate trial confined the jury's task to Chauvin's individual criminal responsibility.

Change-of-Venue Motions

The defense sought to move the trial from Hennepin County because of publicity and community pressure.

The court denied transfer after extensive juror screening and other safeguards.

The Court of Appeals held that the district court acted within its broad discretion.

Continuance Motions

The defense requested delay based on publicity, discovery, security events, and the City's civil settlement.

The district court concluded that delay was not required.

The appellate court found no abuse of discretion.

Jury Sequestration

The defense requested broader sequestration before deliberations.

The district court used instructions, controlled juror exposure, and later sequestration.

The Court of Appeals held that the court took sufficient steps to protect impartiality.

Live Audiovisual Coverage

The trial received exceptional live audiovisual coverage because of pandemic conditions and intense public interest.

The court imposed rules intended to protect jurors, witnesses, and proceedings.

Broadcast access made the evidentiary presentation unusually transparent for a Minnesota criminal trial.

Pretrial Publicity

The case received worldwide news coverage.

The legal question was not whether jurors had heard of the event but whether they could decide from admitted evidence and instructions.

Voir dire examined exposure, opinions, protest participation, safety concerns, and ability to remain impartial.

Minneapolis Civil Settlement

During jury selection, Minneapolis announced a major civil settlement with Floyd's family.

The defense argued the timing increased prejudice.

The trial court reexamined jurors and excused jurors whose answers showed the announcement affected impartiality.

Daunte Wright Shooting During Trial

A police officer killed Daunte Wright in nearby Brooklyn Center during the Chauvin trial.

The event produced protests and renewed security concerns.

The district court questioned whether external events required additional measures and continued the trial.

Public Comments by Elected Officials

Statements by public officials, including Representative Maxine Waters, became part of defense arguments concerning pressure and mistrial.

The trial court criticized outside commentary but did not find a basis to end the trial.

The appellate court considered the wider publicity record and found no reversible prejudice.

Juror Questionnaires

Prospective jurors completed detailed questionnaires.

Questions addressed knowledge of the incident, opinions of Floyd and Chauvin, police experiences, protest views, media exposure, and impartiality.

Written responses guided individual voir dire.

Voir Dire

Counsel and the court questioned prospective jurors individually.

Jurors were excused for cause when they could not set aside fixed views.

Both sides also exercised peremptory challenges within constitutional limits.

Impartial Jury Standard

The Constitution requires impartiality, not ignorance of a highly publicized case.

The trial court evaluated credibility and capacity to decide on evidence.

The Court of Appeals found no actual or presumed prejudice requiring reversal.

Juror Safety Concerns

Some jurors understood that the verdict could produce strong public reactions.

General awareness of social consequences does not automatically prove bias.

The appellate court deferred to the trial judge's questioning and findings.

Jury Selection Completed

The court selected twelve jurors and alternates after extended questioning.

Jurors were instructed to avoid news and outside discussion.

Their identities were initially protected under court orders.

Opening Statements

Opening statements began March 29, 2021.

The State previewed a case based on video, police standards, medical causation, and the prolonged nature of force.

The defense previewed lawful-force and alternative-causation theories.

State's Trial Theory

The State argued that Floyd died because Chauvin used unreasonable force long after any resistance ended.

It emphasized the duration, prone position, loss of responsiveness, absence of pulse, and failure to reposition.

It maintained that drugs and disease were contributing conditions rather than superseding causes.

Defense Trial Theory

The defense argued that officers faced a dynamic arrest, crowd distraction, possible excited delirium, drug effects, and serious heart disease.

It contended that Chauvin's force was reasonable from an officer's perspective.

It disputed the State's medical mechanism and criminal intent theories.

Civilian Witnesses

Bystanders described Floyd's distress and officers' continued restraint.

Their testimony supplied direct perceptions and emotional context.

The court instructed the jury to decide from admissible evidence rather than sympathy or public reaction.

Off-Duty Firefighter Testimony

Genevieve Hansen identified herself as a firefighter and asked to provide medical help.

Officers did not allow her to approach.

Her testimony supported notice of a medical emergency and availability of assistance.

Use-of-Force Bystander Testimony

Donald Williams described the restraint using his experience in combat sports and observed Floyd becoming unresponsive.

He called emergency dispatch after the incident.

His testimony was evaluated alongside police-practices and medical experts.

Cup Foods Evidence

Store employees and surveillance video described events leading to the police call.

The suspected offense involved a counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

The relatively minor suspected crime was relevant to proportionality of force.

Dispatcher and Surveillance Evidence

Dispatch and surveillance evidence documented timing and officer response.

A dispatcher observing a city camera became concerned by the prolonged restraint.

The evidence supported chronology independent of bystander footage.

Chief Medaria Arradondo

Minneapolis Police Chief Medaria Arradondo testified that Chauvin's actions violated department policy and training.

He distinguished initial restraint from continued force after Floyd ceased resisting.

Department policy was evidence of training and reasonableness but did not itself define the criminal statutes.

Police Supervisors

MPD supervisors described reporting, force review, medical response, and policies.

Lt. Richard Zimmerman testified concerning department practices and the need to stop force when a person is controlled.

The defense challenged whether some witnesses had complete tactical context.

Use-of-Force Expert Testimony

The State presented multiple witnesses on police force and training.

They addressed proportionality, threat, resistance, prone restraint, neck pressure, de-escalation, recovery position, and medical aid.

The appellate court rejected the claim that this testimony was impermissibly cumulative.

Sergeant Jody Stiger

Los Angeles Police Sergeant Jody Stiger served as an outside use-of-force expert.

He concluded that force became unreasonable after Floyd was controlled and no longer resisting.

The jury remained responsible for the legal verdict.

State Medical Experts

The State presented specialists in pulmonology, pathology, emergency medicine, and forensic medicine.

They addressed breathing mechanics, prone restraint, oxygen deprivation, cardiac arrest, fentanyl, heart disease, and video indicators.

Their combined theory was that restraint and compression caused fatal oxygen deprivation.

Dr. Martin Tobin

Pulmonologist Martin Tobin explained how Floyd's position and external pressure impaired breathing.

He used video, body position, respiratory physiology, and visible changes to support oxygen-deprivation causation.

The defense cross-examined his assumptions and conclusions.

Dr. Andrew Baker

Medical examiner Andrew Baker testified concerning the autopsy and death certificate.

He identified law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression as central causes.

He acknowledged serious heart disease and fentanyl as contributing conditions.

Defense Police-Practices Evidence

Former officer Barry Brodd testified for the defense.

He characterized portions of the restraint as consistent with police practice and distinguished restraint from force.

The State challenged that analysis using video and policy.

Defense Medical Evidence

Forensic pathologist David Fowler offered alternative medical explanations involving heart disease, drugs, and possible environmental exposure.

He disputed the State's oxygen-deprivation analysis.

Jurors weighed his testimony against the medical examiner and State experts.

Carbon-Monoxide Theory

The defense raised possible carbon-monoxide exposure from the police vehicle.

The State presented rebuttal information concerning blood testing and lack of evidentiary support.

The appellate court later rejected claims that the prosecution's handling of the subject required a new trial.

Fentanyl Evidence

Toxicology showed fentanyl and other substances.

The defense argued intoxication could explain behavior and death.

The State's experts explained that toxicology levels cannot be interpreted apart from tolerance, observed respiration, restraint, and the complete medical record.

Heart-Disease Evidence

Floyd had significant coronary disease and cardiac enlargement.

The defense argued the condition increased the likelihood of sudden cardiac death.

The State accepted vulnerability while maintaining Chauvin's force was a substantial causal factor.

Excited-Delirium Evidence

The defense discussed excited delirium and officers' concern about sudden medical crisis.

The State argued Floyd's observed conduct did not justify continuing pressure after he became unresponsive.

The concept did not replace the statutory requirements for reasonable force and medical response.

Chauvin Did Not Testify

Chauvin exercised his constitutional right not to testify.

The jury was instructed not to draw any adverse inference.

The State retained the entire burden of proof.

Close of Evidence

The defense rested on April 15, 2021.

The State presented limited rebuttal evidence.

The court then finalized instructions and closing procedures.

Closing Arguments

The parties argued competing interpretations of force, causation, medical evidence, and police duties.

Closing argument was not evidence.

Jurors were instructed to rely on testimony, exhibits, and law.

Final Jury Instructions

Judge Cahill instructed the jury on all three offenses, causation, police force, reasonable doubt, witness credibility, expert testimony, and unanimity.

Instructions defined the law the jury was required to apply.

The Court of Appeals later reviewed challenged portions as a whole.

Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Reasonable doubt is a doubt based on reason and common sense after careful consideration of the evidence.

The State was not required to eliminate imaginary doubt.

A conviction required proof of every element to unanimous juror satisfaction.

Separate Consideration of Counts

The jury could find Chauvin guilty or not guilty on any count.

One verdict did not mechanically determine another.

Each verdict form required a separate unanimous decision.

Causation Instruction

The court instructed that Chauvin's conduct was a cause if it was a substantial causal factor.

Other contributing causes did not excuse the offense.

A superseding cause had to become the sole cause to break legal causation.

Third-Degree-Assault Instruction

The jury was told that Chauvin had to intentionally inflict bodily harm and that substantial bodily harm resulted.

The State did not need to prove intent to inflict the substantial degree of harm.

The Court of Appeals upheld that formulation.

Police-Force Instruction

The court instructed that officers may use reasonable force when lawfully making an arrest.

Reasonableness depended on the totality of circumstances viewed from an objective police perspective.

The State had to disprove authorized force beyond a reasonable doubt.

Liability for Crimes of Another Instruction

The instructions included general language concerning liability for crimes committed by another person.

Chauvin challenged aspects of the instruction on appeal.

The appellate court found no reversible error because the evidence and arguments treated Chauvin as the principal actor.

Implicit-Bias Instruction

Jurors were instructed to guard against conscious and unconscious bias.

They were required to avoid decisions based on race, public opinion, sympathy, or stereotypes.

The instruction reinforced impartial fact-finding.

Jury Deliberations

The jury began deliberating after closing arguments and instructions.

Jurors were sequestered during deliberations.

They returned verdicts the following day.

Guilty Verdicts

On April 20, 2021, the jury returned guilty verdicts on all three counts.

The court polled the jurors and confirmed unanimity.

Chauvin's bail was revoked, and he was remanded into custody.

Verdict Versus Conviction Entry

A guilty verdict is the jury's decision.

A judgment of conviction is formally entered by the court.

The distinction became important because only Count One was adjudicated as a conviction.

Minnesota Included-Offense Rule

Minn. Stat. § 609.04 limits multiple convictions for one criminal act when offenses are included within another.

The district court entered conviction on the most serious adjudicated count.

Counts Two and Three remained as unadjudicated guilty verdicts.

Entered Count-One Conviction

The formal judgment was second-degree unintentional felony murder.

That conviction supplied the sentence and direct appellate judgment.

Descriptions stating three entered homicide convictions are legally imprecise.

Unadjudicated Counts Two and Three

The third-degree-murder and manslaughter verdicts remained part of the trial record.

They did not produce separate judgments or sentences.

The Court of Appeals therefore declined to decide the Noor challenge to Count Two.

Posttrial Motions

The defense sought a new trial and other relief.

Arguments included alleged prosecutorial misconduct, juror issues, evidentiary rulings, discovery, publicity, and trial procedure.

The district court denied relief.

Request for a Schwartz Hearing

A Schwartz hearing investigates a prima facie showing of juror misconduct or concealed bias.

The defense relied partly on posttrial information concerning juror participation in a Washington march and safety concerns.

The district court denied the request, and the Court of Appeals affirmed.

Juror 52 Issue

The defense argued that one juror had not adequately disclosed attendance at an August 2020 Washington event.

The appellate court emphasized the voir-dire questions actually asked and defense opportunities to explore the subject.

It held that denial of a Schwartz hearing was within the trial court's discretion.

Alleged Prosecutorial Misconduct

The defense alleged discovery violations, inadequate witness preparation, improper closing argument, and influence over medical-examiner conclusions.

The trial court found no prosecutorial misconduct.

The Court of Appeals found either no misconduct or harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

Discovery Issues

The investigation generated extensive and sometimes duplicative material.

The appellate court accepted the district court's finding that delays and disorganization were not deliberate bad-faith violations.

Good-faith discovery error did not require a new trial.

Untranscribed Sidebar Conferences

Some trial sidebars were not contemporaneously transcribed.

The Court of Appeals criticized the failure to ensure a complete record.

It nevertheless concluded that Chauvin had not shown prejudice requiring a new trial.

Aggravating-Factor Proceeding

After the guilty verdicts, the court considered sentencing aggravators.

Chauvin waived a jury determination and permitted judicial findings beyond a reasonable doubt.

The findings governed whether an upward departure was legally available.

Abuse of Position of Trust and Authority

The court found that Chauvin abused police authority.

Floyd was handcuffed, under officer control, and entitled to lawful treatment.

Misuse of the public power to restrain and protect supported departure.

Particular Cruelty

The court found particular cruelty beyond what was required to commit the offense.

It cited the prolonged restraint, Floyd's pleas, his loss of consciousness, continued pressure after pulselessness, and bystander distress.

The Court of Appeals held that particular cruelty alone could support the departure.

Children Present

Children witnessed the killing.

The court found commission in children's presence as an aggravating fact.

The sentencing memorandum treated their exposure as part of the offense's exceptional circumstances.

Group Participation

The court found that Chauvin actively committed the offense with three or more participants.

Other officers restrained Floyd or controlled the scene.

Their separate criminal responsibility was adjudicated in later state and federal proceedings.

Particular Vulnerability Not Found

The prosecution alleged that Floyd was particularly vulnerable.

The court did not rely on that proposed factor as independently proven.

A sentencing court may rely only on legally valid and sufficiently established aggravators.

Minnesota Sentencing Guidelines

Minnesota guidelines seek proportionality, consistency, and reduced disparity.

For Count One and zero criminal-history points, the presumptive duration was 150 months within a 128-to-180-month range.

Departure required identifiable, substantial, and compelling circumstances.

State's Sentencing Position

The State requested thirty years.

It emphasized abuse of authority, prolonged suffering, children, multiple officers, and the need for accountability.

The request exceeded the sentence ultimately imposed.

Defense Sentencing Position

The defense sought probation or a sentence below the State's request.

It cited Chauvin's history, lack of prior criminal record, legal arguments, and correctional considerations.

The court rejected a probationary disposition.

Victim-Impact Statements

Floyd's family addressed the court through live and recorded statements.

Victim-impact evidence described personal loss and requested accountability.

The judge remained bound by statutes, guidelines, and sentencing law.

Chauvin's Sentencing Statement

Chauvin made a brief statement expressing condolences and referring to future information.

He did not provide a full admission in the state proceeding.

His later federal plea contained detailed admissions.

270-Month Sentence

Judge Cahill imposed 270 months.

The term equals twenty-two years and six months.

It was below the forty-year statutory maximum for Count One and above the presumptive guideline range.

Extent of the Departure

The sentence was 120 months above the 150-month presumptive duration.

It was 90 months above the high end of the presumptive range.

The Court of Appeals held that the extent did not abuse sentencing discretion.

Credit for Time Served

The judgment awarded 199 days of jail credit.

Credit prevents duplicate confinement for qualifying pre-sentence custody.

Correctional authorities administer the remaining term under Minnesota and concurrent federal rules.

Mandatory Surcharge

The judgment imposed the statutory surcharge.

The sentencing order directed collection from prison wages.

The surcharge is distinct from restitution and civil damages.

Firearm Prohibition

The judgment imposed a lifetime prohibition concerning firearms, ammunition, and explosives under applicable law.

The restriction followed the felony conviction.

It is a collateral legal consequence separate from imprisonment.

DNA Collection

The judgment required a DNA sample.

Minnesota law authorizes collection for qualifying convictions.

DNA collection does not add another criminal count.

Registration Provision

The sentencing judgment included a registration directive under the court's stated statutory authority.

The exact current administrative application should be checked in the judgment and correctional records.

The directive was not a finding that the homicide was a sexual offense.

Direct Appeal

Chauvin appealed the conviction and sentence to the Minnesota Court of Appeals.

Direct appeal reviews preserved trial and sentencing issues from the existing record.

It is different from post-conviction litigation based on new claims or evidence.

Appellate Standards of Review

Different issues received different review: de novo for statutory interpretation, abuse of discretion for venue and evidence, plain error for some unpreserved instructions, and sufficiency review for elements.

Appellate courts do not retry witness credibility from a blank slate.

The applicable standard influenced each holding.

Issues Raised on Direct Appeal

The appeal challenged venue, continuance, sequestration, juror misconduct, third-degree murder, felony-murder applicability, instructions, force evidence, excluded defense evidence, prosecutorial conduct, sidebars, cumulative error, and sentencing.

The published opinion addressed the issues in twelve principal sections.

The court affirmed the judgment.

Venue, Continuance, and Sequestration Holding

The Court of Appeals found that the district court used extensive voir dire, instructions, juror removal, and security measures.

It found no actual or presumed juror prejudice.

The refusal to move or delay the trial was not an abuse of discretion.

Schwartz-Hearing Holding

The appellate court held that Chauvin had not established a basis requiring a juror-misconduct hearing.

The defense had opportunities to question jurors and use strikes.

Posttrial press accounts did not compel a new inquiry.

Third-Degree-Murder Holding

The court declined to adjudicate the Noor challenge.

No formal conviction or sentence existed on Count Two.

The appellate judgment therefore left the unadjudicated verdict without deciding its independent validity.

Felony-Murder Holding

The court held that a police officer can commit second-degree unintentional felony murder during a lawful arrest.

Once force becomes unreasonable, it is no longer authorized and can constitute assault.

The court found the State proved all elements of Count One beyond a reasonable doubt.

Strict-Liability Argument Rejected

Chauvin argued that assault-based felony murder imposed strict liability.

The court explained that assault-harm requires intentional application of force.

Death need not be intended, but the underlying physical act must be intentional.

Jury-Instruction Holding

The appellate court found no plain or reversible error in the challenged instructions.

It upheld the explanation that intent to inflict substantial bodily harm was unnecessary.

Any alleged omission concerning special danger to human life was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Use-of-Force Evidence Holding

The defense argued that seven State witnesses created cumulative and prejudicial force testimony.

The appellate court held that witnesses addressed different training, policy, supervision, and expert perspectives.

Admission was within the district court's discretion.

Excluded Training Slide

The defense challenged exclusion of a Minneapolis training slide concerning prone restraint.

The appellate court found no reversible abuse.

The larger record contained extensive evidence concerning restraint and recovery position.

Excluded Recorded Statement

The defense sought admission of a recorded statement by an unavailable witness.

The Court of Appeals held the statement did not satisfy the asserted statement-against-interest exception.

Exclusion did not require reversal.

Prosecutorial-Misconduct Holding

The Court of Appeals upheld denial of a new trial.

It found no deliberate discovery misconduct, no prejudicial failure to prepare witnesses, and no reversible argument or medical-examiner interference.

Any assumed error was harmless under the applicable standards.

Sidebar-Record Holding

The court recognized that sidebars should have been recorded.

It nevertheless found the record sufficient for review and no demonstrated prejudice.

The omission did not entitle Chauvin to a new trial.

Cumulative-Error Holding

Cumulative error can require reversal when multiple errors collectively deny a fair trial.

The court considered the alleged errors and strength of the State's case.

It concluded that the trial remained fair.

Sentencing Holding

The appellate court held that particular cruelty independently supported departure.

Abuse of police authority supplied another substantial basis.

The 270-month duration was within the district court's discretion.

Court of Appeals Conclusion

The published opinion concluded with one word: Affirmed.

The entered Count One conviction and sentence remained intact.

The opinion became binding published Minnesota appellate authority unless modified by a higher court.

Minnesota Supreme Court Review

Chauvin petitioned the Minnesota Supreme Court for discretionary review.

The court denied review on July 18, 2023.

A denial leaves the Court of Appeals judgment in place without creating a separate merits opinion.

United States Supreme Court Petition

Chauvin petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court for certiorari.

The petition emphasized publicity, venue, security, and fair-trial concerns.

The Supreme Court denied the petition on November 20, 2023.

Meaning of Certiorari Denial

Certiorari denial is not an endorsement or rejection of the petition's merits.

It means the Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

The Minnesota judgment remained final for direct-review purposes.

Minnesota Post-Conviction Relief

Post-conviction procedure allows a person convicted of a crime to seek relief on qualifying constitutional, jurisdictional, evidentiary, or legal grounds.

It is not an unrestricted second direct appeal.

Procedural bars, limitations, waiver, evidentiary standards, and prejudice rules apply.

The Knaffla Rule

Under State v. Knaffla, claims decided on direct appeal generally cannot be relitigated in post-conviction proceedings.

Claims known or that should have been known but not raised are also generally barred.

Minnesota recognizes narrow fairness and novel-legal-basis exceptions.

2024–2025 Initial Post-Conviction Filing

Chauvin initiated a state post-conviction proceeding in 2024.

The district court dismissed that petition in April 2025 without overturning the conviction.

The dismissal did not establish the truth of the asserted medical theories.

November 2025 Petition

A later counseled petition sought a new trial, vacatur, or an evidentiary hearing.

It alleged defects involving medical evidence, video interpretation, police testimony, instructions, prosecutorial conduct, and related matters.

Those assertions were claims by the petitioner rather than judicial findings.

May 1, 2026 Denial

The district court denied the later petition in a forty-one-page order.

The court concluded that none of the claims warranted relief.

It repeatedly applied Knaffla because claims had been decided or could have been raised earlier.

Procedural Bar and Merits

A procedural-bar ruling can prevent reconsideration without conducting a new trial of every factual dispute.

Courts may also address lack of prejudice or substantive weakness as alternative reasons.

Denial means the conviction remains operative unless reversed on appeal.

June 29, 2026 Post-Conviction Appeal

Chauvin filed an appeal from the May 2026 denial.

The appeal placed the post-conviction ruling before Minnesota's appellate system.

Filing an appeal did not stay or vacate the conviction automatically.

Pending 2026 Appellate Status

No official appellate merits decision resolving the 2026 post-conviction appeal was located for this fact sheet as of August 1, 2026.

The proper description is pending rather than affirmed or reversed.

The district-court conviction and sentence remain controlling unless a court grants relief.

Separate Federal Civil-Rights Prosecution

A federal grand jury charged Chauvin under 18 U.S.C. § 242.

He pleaded guilty to using unreasonable force against Floyd and a fourteen-year-old in a 2017 incident.

The federal case produced two convictions and a concurrent federal sentence.

State and Federal Elements

Minnesota proved state homicide and assault elements.

The United States required color of law, a federal constitutional right, willfulness, and aggravating results.

The proceedings vindicated distinct sovereign interests.

Concurrent State and Federal Sentences

The state term is 270 months.

The operative amended federal judgment imposed 240 months.

The terms run concurrently, meaning they are served at the same time rather than consecutively.

Presidential Pardon Limitation

The President of the United States can pardon federal offenses but cannot pardon a Minnesota state conviction.

Relief from the state judgment must come through Minnesota judicial or state clemency processes.

The federal and state judgments must therefore be analyzed separately.

Civil Settlement

The City of Minneapolis reached a civil settlement with Floyd's family.

A civil settlement compensates claims and commonly includes no criminal adjudication.

It did not determine Chauvin's guilt and was not evidence admitted to prove the criminal counts.

State Cases Against Other Officers

Minnesota separately prosecuted Thao, Kueng, and Lane for aiding and abetting offenses.

Their proceedings involved different roles, pleas, verdicts, and sentences.

Chauvin's guilt did not eliminate the State's burden in those cases.

Rarity of Police Murder Convictions

Police officers have historically been difficult to convict for deaths occurring during arrests.

Lawful-force authority, rapidly developing circumstances, jury deference, evidentiary uncertainty, and prosecutorial choices contribute to that history.

The Chauvin conviction became nationally significant because the jury found murder beyond a reasonable doubt.

African American Historical Context

Black Americans have long demanded criminal accountability for killings by officials.

From Reconstruction through lynching, civil-rights-era violence, and modern police cases, local legal systems often failed to convict.

State v. Chauvin became a major counterexample while not resolving broader systemic inequality.

Video and Public Evidence

Multiple recordings reduced dependence on police reports alone.

The jury could observe duration, body position, bystander warnings, and loss of responsiveness.

Video did not eliminate the need for legal instructions, medical evidence, and contextual testimony.

Police Testimony Against Police Conduct

Senior officers and experts testified that Chauvin's continued force departed from training and reasonable practice.

Their testimony challenged a perceived institutional code of silence.

The jury still had to decide criminal liability independently.

Causation Significance

The case demonstrated that criminal causation does not require one exclusive physiological cause.

A defendant remains responsible when conduct materially contributes to death despite drugs or disease.

The jury rejected the defense theory that alternative conditions created reasonable doubt.

Felony-Murder Significance

The published appeal confirmed that assault by unreasonable police force can support Minnesota felony murder.

Police authority protects reasonable force, not force exceeding lawful bounds.

The holding has significance beyond Chauvin for state prosecution of officer-caused deaths.

Sentencing Significance

The upward departure treated abuse of official trust and prolonged cruelty as more serious than an ordinary felony-murder case.

The sentence remained below the statutory maximum and State request.

Appellate affirmance confirmed the departure was legally supportable.

Fair-Trial Significance

The case tested whether a fair jury could be selected amid extraordinary publicity and civil unrest.

The appellate court held that careful voir dire and mitigation were sufficient.

The ruling does not mean publicity can never require venue change; it applies the record-specific abuse-of-discretion standard.

Limits of the Precedent

The appellate holding concerns Minnesota statutes and the particular evidence.

It does not make every police-caused death felony murder.

Future prosecutions must prove unreasonable force, predicate felony, causation, and all other elements.

Current Status Through August 1, 2026

The Count One second-degree unintentional felony-murder conviction remained entered.

The 270-month sentence remained operative and concurrent with the federal sentence.

A 2026 appeal from denial of post-conviction relief was pending without a located merits decision.

Misinformation and Common Errors

“The Case Was Federal”

The murder prosecution was a Minnesota state case. A separate federal civil-rights case followed.

“Chauvin Was Formally Convicted and Sentenced on Three State Counts”

The jury returned three guilty verdicts, but judgment and sentence were entered only on Count One.

“The State Had to Prove Intent to Kill”

Second-degree unintentional felony murder did not require intent to kill.

“Felony Murder Required an Independent Crime Unrelated to Force”

The predicate felony was third-degree assault created by unreasonable force.

“A Lawful Arrest Made All Force Lawful”

Police authority extends only to reasonable force.

“The Jury Had to Find Chauvin Intended Substantial Bodily Harm”

It had to find intentional application of force and resulting substantial bodily harm, not intent to cause the precise injury level.

“Drugs Had to Be Absent for Murder Liability”

Other contributing conditions did not defeat liability if Chauvin's conduct remained a substantial causal factor.

“Homicide on a Death Certificate Automatically Means Murder”

Medical homicide classification and criminal guilt are different determinations.

“The Video Alone Was the Entire Case”

The record included body cameras, surveillance, civilian testimony, police policy, training, medical evidence, toxicology, and experts.

“Chauvin Testified and the Jury Rejected His Story”

He exercised his right not to testify.

“The Defense Had to Prove Reasonable Force”

The State had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the force was unauthorized.

“The Jury Was Completely Sequestered From the Beginning”

The court used instructions and exposure controls before deliberations and sequestration during deliberations.

“The Court of Appeals Upheld a Third-Degree-Murder Conviction”

It declined to decide that count because no judgment of conviction or sentence had been entered.

“The Minnesota Supreme Court Issued a Merits Opinion”

It denied discretionary review.

“The U.S. Supreme Court Affirmed the Conviction in an Opinion”

It denied certiorari without deciding the merits.

“The Sentence Was the Forty-Year Maximum”

The imposed sentence was 270 months.

“The Sentence Was Within the Ordinary Presumptive Range”

It was an upward durational departure from a 128-to-180-month range.

“All Five Proposed Aggravating Factors Were Found”

The court did not find particular vulnerability as a separate proven factor.

“The Federal Sentence Replaced the State Sentence”

The two judgments remain distinct and run concurrently.

“A President Can Pardon the Minnesota Conviction”

Presidential pardon power extends to federal offenses, not state convictions.

“The May 2026 Post-Conviction Denial Ended All Possible Appeal”

Chauvin filed an appeal from that denial in June 2026.

“Filing the 2026 Appeal Vacated the Conviction”

The conviction remains operative unless a court grants relief.

Historical and Continuing Significance

State of Minnesota v. Chauvin produced a state murder conviction for the killing that generated the largest American police-accountability protest movement of the early twenty-first century.

The case established that lawful arrest authority does not protect an officer who intentionally applies unreasonable force constituting assault and causes death.

The published appellate opinion confirmed that Minnesota felony-murder doctrine can apply to police force when the force exceeds lawful authorization.

The trial demonstrated the combined evidentiary power of civilian video, body cameras, police testimony, medical science, dispatch records, and time-stamped chronology.

The sentencing decision treated abuse of public authority and prolonged cruelty as aggravating circumstances warranting a substantial upward departure.

For African American history, the judgment represents a rare instance in which a state criminal court imposed a lengthy murder sentence upon a police officer for killing a Black man during an arrest.

The conviction did not end police violence, racial disparities, or institutional reform debates, but it changed national expectations concerning video evidence, intervention, medical aid, prosecution, and official accountability.

Summary

State of Minnesota v. Chauvin was the Minnesota murder prosecution arising from George Floyd's death on May 25, 2020.

The amended complaint charged second-degree unintentional felony murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

Count One alleged that Chauvin caused Floyd's death without intent to kill while committing third-degree assault through unreasonable police force.

The State presented bystander and body-camera video, civilian testimony, police policy and training, medical evidence, toxicology, surveillance, dispatch records, and expert analysis.

The defense argued that Chauvin's force was reasonable and that heart disease, fentanyl, and other factors created reasonable doubt about causation.

On April 20, 2021, the jury returned guilty verdicts on all three counts.

Under Minnesota's included-offense rule, the district court entered judgment of conviction only on second-degree unintentional felony murder and left the other verdicts unadjudicated.

Judge Peter Cahill imposed 270 months—twenty-two years and six months—after finding substantial aggravating circumstances including abuse of police authority and particular cruelty.

The Minnesota Court of Appeals affirmed in 2023, holding that an officer can commit felony murder by using unreasonable force constituting assault during a lawful arrest and that the trial and sentence did not require reversal.

The Minnesota Supreme Court denied review, and the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari.

A later post-conviction petition was denied on May 1, 2026, and Chauvin appealed that denial on June 29, 2026. No merits decision on that appeal was located as of August 1, 2026.

The entered Count One conviction and 270-month sentence therefore remained the controlling state judgment, running concurrently with the separate federal civil-rights sentence.
