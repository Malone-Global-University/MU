Dred Scott v. Sandford

The 1857 United States Supreme Court decision that denied federal citizenship to Black Americans, rejected Dred Scott's claim to freedom, and declared that Congress could not prohibit slavery in federal territories.

What Was Dred Scott v. Sandford?

Dred Scott v. Sandford was an 1857 United States Supreme Court case arising from an enslaved man's effort to obtain legal recognition of freedom for himself and his family. Dred Scott argued that his residence with his enslaver in Illinois and in the federal Wisconsin Territory had made him free because slavery was prohibited in those jurisdictions.

The Supreme Court rejected Scott's claim. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney's principal opinion declared that Black people descended from Africans brought to the United States and enslaved were not part of the national political community recognized by the Constitution and therefore could not sue as citizens in federal court. The opinion went further and declared that Congress could not prohibit slavery in federal territories because doing so would unconstitutionally deprive slaveholders of property.

The case transformed an individual freedom suit into a sweeping constitutional ruling about race, citizenship, slavery, federal power, property, and the political future of the western territories.

Case Name and Spelling

The case is conventionally titled Dred Scott v. Sandford. The defendant's surname was John F. A. Sanford, without the second letter d. The official reporter's caption preserved the spelling “Sandford,” and that form became the standard legal name of the Supreme Court case.

The difference in spelling does not identify a second person. “John F. A. Sanford” and the “Sandford” named in the case title refer to the same defendant.

Dred and Harriet Scott

Dred Scott was born into slavery in Virginia around the turn of the nineteenth century and was later taken to Missouri. He eventually came under the control of Dr. John Emerson, a United States Army surgeon whose military assignments brought Scott into jurisdictions where slavery was prohibited.

Scott lived for periods at Fort Armstrong in Illinois and at Fort Snelling in the federal Wisconsin Territory. Illinois prohibited slavery under its state constitution, and the Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery in the relevant part of the federal territory.

At Fort Snelling, Scott married Harriet Robinson, an enslaved woman who also became legally associated with Emerson's household. Dred and Harriet Scott later had two daughters. Their freedom litigation concerned the legal status of the entire family, although Dred Scott's federal case became the reported Supreme Court decision.

Freedom Suits and the “Once Free, Always Free” Rule

A freedom suit was a civil action in which an enslaved person asked a court to recognize that he or she was legally free. Plaintiffs relied on grounds such as free ancestry, unlawful enslavement, an agreement for emancipation, or residence in a jurisdiction where slavery was prohibited.

Missouri courts had previously applied a principle commonly summarized as “once free, always free.” Under that approach, an enslaver who voluntarily took an enslaved person to live in a free state or territory could lose the legal right to hold that person in slavery, even after returning to Missouri.

Dred and Harriet Scott's claims followed this established form of litigation. Their argument did not depend on a general judicial power to abolish slavery. It depended on courts enforcing legal consequences that prior Missouri decisions had attached to residence in free jurisdictions.

Missouri State-Court Litigation

Dred and Harriet Scott filed freedom suits in the St. Louis Circuit Court in 1846. Their cases were supported by members of the Blow family, who had previously enslaved Dred Scott and later assisted the litigation.

Scott lost his first trial because the evidence did not adequately establish the legal chain of ownership. The court granted a new trial. In 1850, a jury found that Dred and Harriet Scott were free based on their residence in free territory.

The Missouri Supreme Court reversed that judgment in 1852 in Scott v. Emerson. The court declined to continue applying Missouri's earlier freedom-suit precedents and held that Scott remained enslaved after returning to Missouri.

This reversal reflected a changing political and judicial environment. As national conflict over slavery intensified, the Missouri court abandoned a body of state precedent that had previously recognized freedom acquired through residence elsewhere.

The Federal Case

After losing in the Missouri Supreme Court, Scott brought a new action in federal court against John F. A. Sanford, a New York citizen connected to the Emerson family. Scott alleged that he was a citizen of Missouri and that Sanford was a citizen of New York, creating diversity jurisdiction between citizens of different states.

Sanford denied that Scott was a citizen capable of suing in federal court and also denied that Scott was free. The federal trial court allowed the case to proceed but ultimately entered judgment against Scott after a jury found for Sanford in 1854.

Scott sought review in the United States Supreme Court. The case was argued in 1856, reargued later that year, and decided on March 6, 1857, shortly after James Buchanan became president.

Questions Before the Supreme Court

The litigation presented several connected but legally distinct questions:

  • Could Dred Scott be considered a citizen of Missouri for purposes of suing a New York citizen in federal court?
  • Did Scott's residence in Illinois make him free under Illinois law?
  • Did his residence in the federal Wisconsin Territory make him free under the Missouri Compromise?
  • Was Missouri constitutionally required to recognize a free status allegedly acquired in another jurisdiction?
  • Did Congress possess authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories?
  • Did a federal prohibition on slavery violate the Fifth Amendment rights of slaveholders?

The case therefore involved federal jurisdiction, state conflict-of-laws principles, territorial governance, the legal status of slavery, and the constitutional meaning of citizenship.

The Supreme Court Decision

The Supreme Court ruled against Dred Scott by a vote commonly described as 7-2. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney delivered the principal opinion. Justices John McLean and Benjamin R. Curtis dissented. The justices issued multiple opinions, and not every justice adopted every part of Taney's reasoning, but a majority agreed that Scott could not obtain the judgment he sought.

Taney's opinion addressed more than the minimum jurisdictional issue. It announced broad constitutional conclusions concerning Black citizenship, congressional authority over slavery in the territories, slaveholders' property rights, and the legal effect of Scott's residence outside Missouri.

Citizenship Holding

Article III allows federal courts to hear certain disputes between citizens of different states. Scott's federal suit therefore depended on his status as a citizen of Missouri.

Taney concluded that Black people whose ancestors had been brought to the United States and enslaved were not included within the national political community formed by the Constitution. Under that reasoning, neither enslaved nor free Black people within that described class could be citizens of the United States for purposes of federal diversity jurisdiction.

The opinion attempted to constitutionalize racial exclusion by treating it as part of the original constitutional settlement. This reasoning denied the political and legal history of free Black communities and disregarded evidence that free Black men had been recognized as citizens and voters in several states when the Constitution was adopted.

Justice Curtis's dissent directly challenged Taney's historical claim. Curtis documented that free Black persons were citizens in several states at the founding and argued that state citizenship could qualify a person for federal jurisdiction.

Rejection of Scott's Freedom Claim

The Court also rejected the argument that Scott's residence in Illinois required recognition of his freedom after he returned to Missouri. Taney treated Scott's present status as governed by Missouri law and relied on the Missouri Supreme Court's determination that he remained enslaved.

The opinion therefore refused to use Scott's earlier residence in a free state as a basis for overturning Missouri's judgment. This approach limited the ability of free-state law to protect an enslaved person once that person had been returned to a slave state.

Congressional Power and the Territories

The decision's most sweeping constitutional pronouncement concerned congressional power over slavery in federal territories. The Missouri Compromise of 1820 had prohibited slavery in portions of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36 degrees 30 minutes, except Missouri.

Taney's opinion declared that Congress could not constitutionally exclude slavery from federal territories in the manner attempted by the Missouri Compromise. The opinion reasoned that enslaved people were legally treated as property and that Congress could not deprive a slaveholder of that property merely because the owner entered a federal territory.

The Kansas-Nebraska Act had already repealed the Missouri Compromise restriction in 1854. The Court's ruling nevertheless had broader importance because it announced that Congress lacked constitutional authority to enact such a prohibition at all. That reasoning threatened efforts to prevent slavery's expansion into western territories.

Fifth Amendment Property Reasoning

The principal opinion relied on the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause, which prohibits the federal government from depriving a person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.

Taney treated the slaveholder's claimed property interest in an enslaved person as constitutionally protected and concluded that a territorial prohibition could not automatically destroy that interest. The decision therefore used constitutional due process to protect slavery while denying constitutional citizenship to the person held in slavery.

This asymmetry was central to the decision's legal and political meaning. The Court recognized an expansive constitutional claim for slaveholders while excluding Black people from the national citizenship on which access to federal courts depended.

Jurisdiction and the Broader Merits

The Court's treatment of jurisdiction created a major structural problem. If Scott was not a citizen capable of invoking federal diversity jurisdiction, the Court could have dismissed the case without deciding the constitutionality of the Missouri Compromise or the full merits of his freedom claim.

Taney nevertheless addressed those broader questions. Critics at the time and later legal commentators argued that the territorial-slavery ruling was unnecessary once the Court denied jurisdiction. The decision itself presented the territorial conclusion as constitutional law, and political actors treated it as a major ruling on the power of Congress.

The distinction matters because a court's authority to decide a case is separate from the correctness or necessity of every legal proposition stated in its opinions.

The Dissents

Justice John McLean

Justice McLean rejected the majority's citizenship reasoning and argued that free Black people could be citizens. He also defended congressional power to govern federal territories and regulate slavery within them.

Justice Benjamin R. Curtis

Justice Curtis presented a detailed historical argument showing that free Black men were citizens and voters in several states when the Constitution was adopted. He maintained that a person recognized as a citizen by a state could invoke federal diversity jurisdiction.

Curtis also argued that Congress possessed longstanding authority to legislate for federal territories. His dissent challenged both the historical premises and the constitutional structure of Taney's opinion.

How the Decision Worked

The ruling operated at several levels simultaneously:

  • Federal access: It denied Scott the status needed to sue as a citizen in federal court.
  • State status: It accepted Missouri's refusal to recognize freedom allegedly acquired through residence elsewhere.
  • Territorial policy: It invalidated the principle that Congress could close federal territories to slavery.
  • Property protection: It framed slaveholding as a property interest protected against federal territorial restrictions.
  • National politics: It attempted to remove major questions about slavery's expansion from ordinary congressional control.

The decision did not merely resolve ownership of one family. It altered the constitutional position of all Black Americans and attempted to establish a national rule favorable to the expansion and protection of slavery.

Immediate Public and Political Response

Proslavery political leaders welcomed the decision as confirmation that slaveholders could enter federal territories with enslaved people. President James Buchanan expressed hope that the Court's decision would settle the territorial dispute.

The ruling instead intensified political conflict. Republicans condemned the decision as evidence that the federal government was being organized to protect slavery nationally. Antislavery writers, Black leaders, lawyers, and activists attacked its historical claims and its denial of Black citizenship.

The decision also deepened divisions within the Democratic Party. The Court's conclusion that Congress could not prohibit territorial slavery weakened popular-sovereignty arguments that territorial settlers could decide the issue for themselves.

Abraham Lincoln and the Sectional Crisis

Abraham Lincoln made the Dred Scott decision a central subject of Republican political argument. He accepted that the judgment bound the immediate parties but rejected the proposition that one decision should permanently settle national policy for all branches and all citizens.

During the Lincoln-Douglas debates of 1858, the decision became part of a broader dispute over slavery's expansion, popular sovereignty, and the future of national citizenship. Lincoln warned that the reasoning could support further judicial protection for slavery beyond the territories.

The ruling did not cause the Civil War by itself. It became one major component of the sectional crisis by convincing many Northerners that ordinary political compromises could no longer contain the expansion of slavery.

Freedom After the Decision

The Supreme Court's ruling left Dred and Harriet Scott legally enslaved. Ownership was subsequently transferred to members of the Blow family, who arranged for the Scotts' emancipation in 1857.

Dred Scott lived as a free man in St. Louis for only a short period. He died in 1858. Harriet Scott lived for many years afterward and died in 1876.

Their eventual private emancipation did not undo the Court's constitutional ruling or the nationwide injury it imposed on Black citizenship and antislavery law.

Civil War and Reconstruction Reversal

The Civil War destroyed the political and legal order on which the decision depended. Federal wartime measures attacked slavery, and the Reconstruction Amendments replaced the constitutional premises of Dred Scott.

The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for crime following conviction. It eliminated the legal property system that formed the basis of Scott's enslavement and the Court's Fifth Amendment reasoning.

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared persons born in the United States and not subject to a foreign power to be citizens, with a limited exception concerning certain Native peoples under the law of the period.

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, constitutionalized national and state citizenship for persons born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. Its Citizenship Clause directly repudiated the central citizenship holding of Dred Scott.

The Fifteenth Amendment later prohibited federal and state denial of voting rights on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Together, the Reconstruction Amendments established a constitutional order fundamentally incompatible with the Dred Scott decision.

Historical and Constitutional Significance

Dred Scott v. Sandford is significant because it attempted to place the protection and expansion of slavery beyond democratic restriction while excluding Black Americans from the constitutional community.

The decision demonstrated how judicial review could be used not only to protect liberty but also to entrench an oppressive system. It invalidated federal legislation, narrowed access to federal courts, elevated slaveholders' property claims, and denied the historical citizenship of free Black people.

Politically, the ruling failed to settle the slavery controversy. It increased distrust between sections, strengthened the Republican argument against a national proslavery power, and made compromise more difficult.

Constitutionally, the decision became a negative landmark: a case studied as an example of historical distortion, racial exclusion, overreaching constitutional adjudication, and the danger of using judicial authority to foreclose political conflict without a defensible constitutional foundation.

Relationship to Related Subjects

Missouri Compromise

The Missouri Compromise was federal legislation regulating slavery in territories acquired through the Louisiana Purchase. Dred Scott declared its territorial prohibition unconstitutional, although the Kansas-Nebraska Act had already repealed that restriction.

Kansas-Nebraska Act

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 replaced the Missouri Compromise restriction with popular sovereignty in the Kansas and Nebraska territories. The Dred Scott reasoning destabilized that approach by suggesting that neither Congress nor a territorial government could constitutionally exclude slavery before statehood.

Fugitive Slave Clause and Fugitive Slave Laws

Fugitive-slave law concerned the return of people who escaped from slavery into another jurisdiction. Scott's claim differed because his enslaver had voluntarily taken him to live in free jurisdictions. Both subjects involved conflict between slave-state claims and free-state law.

Reconstruction Amendments

The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments overturned the legal foundations of the decision by abolishing slavery and establishing constitutional citizenship. The Fifteenth Amendment further protected political participation against racial exclusion.

Common Misconceptions

“Dred Scott Was the Only Person Seeking Freedom”

Dred Scott's name became the title of the Supreme Court case, but Harriet Scott pursued a parallel freedom claim, and the legal status of their daughters was also at stake.

“The Case Began in the United States Supreme Court”

The litigation began in Missouri state court in 1846. It passed through trials, a Missouri Supreme Court reversal, and a separate federal action before reaching the United States Supreme Court.

“The Court Ruled Only That Scott Remained Enslaved”

The decision also denied federal citizenship to a broad class of Black Americans and declared that Congress could not prohibit slavery in federal territories.

“The Court Held That No Black Person Had Ever Been a State Citizen”

Taney's national-citizenship reasoning rested on that exclusionary premise, but Justice Curtis demonstrated that free Black people had been recognized as citizens and voters in several states at the founding.

“The Decision Permanently Settled the Meaning of Citizenship”

The Civil War and Reconstruction Amendments displaced the decision. The Fourteenth Amendment established a constitutional rule of birthright citizenship incompatible with Taney's holding.

“The Decision Alone Caused the Civil War”

The Civil War resulted from a long sectional conflict centered on slavery. Dred Scott was a major escalation within that conflict, not a single sufficient cause.

Why Dred Scott v. Sandford Matters

The decision matters because it placed the Supreme Court's authority behind an expansive proslavery constitutional order. It denied Black Americans membership in the national political community, protected claims of property in human beings, and attempted to prevent Congress from restricting slavery's territorial expansion.

It also demonstrates the limits of claims that courts operate outside politics. The justices decided the case during an intense sectional conflict, and the broad ruling altered party competition, territorial policy, public trust, and constitutional debate.

The case remains essential to understanding why the Citizenship Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment was necessary. Reconstruction did not merely add new rights to an otherwise neutral constitutional system; it expressly rejected a Supreme Court decision that had excluded Black Americans from national citizenship.

Summary

Dred Scott v. Sandford was the 1857 Supreme Court decision arising from Dred and Harriet Scott's effort to obtain legal recognition of freedom based on residence in Illinois and the federal Wisconsin Territory. The Court ruled against Scott and denied that Black people descended from enslaved Africans could be citizens within the constitutional meaning required to sue in federal court.

Chief Justice Roger B. Taney's principal opinion also rejected Scott's freedom claim and declared that Congress could not prohibit slavery in federal territories. By treating slaveholding as constitutionally protected property while excluding Black Americans from national citizenship, the decision attempted to establish a national proslavery constitutional rule.

The ruling intensified rather than settled sectional conflict. Dred and Harriet Scott were privately emancipated later in 1857, but the decision remained in force until the Civil War and Reconstruction transformed the Constitution. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, and the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause directly repudiated the decision's central citizenship holding.
