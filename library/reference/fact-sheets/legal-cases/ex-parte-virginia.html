Ex parte Virginia

The 1880 United States Supreme Court decision upholding federal power to prosecute a state official accused of excluding qualified Black citizens from jury service because of race.

What Was Ex parte Virginia?

Ex parte Virginia was an 1880 Supreme Court case concerning racial discrimination in the selection of state jurors and the federal government's authority to enforce the Fourteenth Amendment. J. D. Coles, a Virginia county judge charged with preparing jury lists, was federally indicted for allegedly excluding qualified Black citizens because of race.

Coles and the State of Virginia asked the Supreme Court to release him from federal custody. They argued that Congress lacked constitutional authority to criminalize the performance of a state officer's duties and that the federal court therefore lacked jurisdiction over the prosecution.

The Supreme Court rejected that argument. It held that the Fourteenth Amendment applies to every state agency and every state officer through whom state power is exercised. When a public official uses authority granted by the state to deny equal protection, the official's conduct is state action, and Congress may use appropriate legislation to prevent and punish that violation.

Meaning of the Case Name

The Latin phrase ex parte means that a matter is brought by or on behalf of one party without the conventional opposing-party format used in an ordinary lawsuit. The case arose from petitions asking the Supreme Court to issue extraordinary writs and release Coles from federal detention.

The official reporter uses the heading Ex parte Virginia. The opinion explains that Coles submitted one petition and the State of Virginia submitted a similar petition. The Court considered the two requests together as one case.

The case was not a civil-rights lawsuit filed by excluded jurors and was not an appeal from Coles's conviction. It was a pretrial challenge to federal jurisdiction brought while he was held under a federal indictment.

Reconstruction and Federal Civil-Rights Enforcement

The case developed from the constitutional changes following the Civil War. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, the Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and prohibited states from denying equal protection, and the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting.

Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment gave Congress power to enforce the amendment through appropriate legislation. Congress used that authority to enact civil-rights and enforcement laws intended to protect formerly enslaved people and other Black citizens from discriminatory state laws, public officials, and organized resistance.

Jury service became an important test of equal citizenship. A jury was not merely a group of private observers. Jurors exercised governmental authority by participating in criminal accusation, criminal adjudication, and civil dispute resolution. Excluding Black citizens from that function denied them participation in public institutions and affected defendants whose cases were judged by bodies from which their race had been removed.

Section 4 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875

The Civil Rights Act of 1875 addressed several forms of racial discrimination. Its public-accommodations provisions prohibited specified discrimination in inns, public conveyances, theaters, and other places of public amusement. A separate jury-service provision prohibited racial exclusion from juries.

Section 4 declared that no citizen possessing the other qualifications required by law could be disqualified from service as a grand or petit juror in a federal or state court because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

The section also made it a misdemeanor for an officer or other person charged with selecting or summoning jurors to exclude or fail to summon a citizen for one of those prohibited reasons. The maximum statutory fine stated in the opinion was $5,000.

The jury provision was materially different from the public-accommodations provisions later invalidated in the Civil Rights Cases of 1883. Jury selection is a governmental function, and Coles was alleged to have acted through public authority rather than as a private business owner.

Virginia's Jury-Selection System

Virginia law assigned county and corporation court judges responsibility for preparing annual lists of persons considered qualified for jury service. The dissent described the judge as selecting between one hundred and three hundred inhabitants who were of sound judgment, free from legal exception, and otherwise qualified under state law.

Each selected name was placed on a separate ballot and deposited in a jury box. Names for grand and petit juries were then drawn from that pool. The preparation of the annual list therefore controlled who could realistically be summoned for jury duty.

Unlike the West Virginia statute invalidated in Strauder v. West Virginia, the Virginia law did not expressly state that only white citizens could serve. The constitutional problem alleged in Ex parte Virginia concerned discriminatory administration of a facially neutral selection system.

J. D. Coles and Pittsylvania County

J. D. Coles served as judge of the County Court of Pittsylvania County, Virginia. State law charged him with preparing the jury list used in the county and circuit courts for the year 1878.

A federal indictment alleged that Coles excluded and failed to select certain Black citizens who possessed the other qualifications required by law. According to the charge, he excluded them because of their African race, Black color, and previous condition of servitude, and for no other reason.

The case concerned the power to prosecute that alleged conduct. The Supreme Court treated the allegations in the indictment as allegations to be tested at trial; it did not itself make a final factual finding that every allegation was true.

Federal Indictment and Arrest

A federal grand jury in the United States District Court for the Western District of Virginia indicted Coles under Section 4 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875. Federal process was issued, and a United States marshal arrested and detained him.

Coles argued that the conduct charged was not a federal offense and that Congress could not subject a Virginia judge to federal criminal prosecution for the performance of duties assigned by state law.

Virginia supported the challenge. Its petition asserted an institutional interest in protecting the authority and independence of its judicial officers from what the state regarded as unconstitutional federal interference.

The prosecution presented the opposite theory: the Fourteenth Amendment had changed the constitutional relationship between the federal government and state officials by giving Congress authority to prevent state denials of equal protection.

The Habeas Corpus Petition

Habeas corpus is a legal procedure used to challenge unlawful custody. Coles asked the Supreme Court to issue a writ of habeas corpus directing that he be brought before the Court and discharged. He also requested certiorari so the record of the district court proceedings could be examined.

A habeas petition ordinarily does not operate as a substitute for an appeal or writ of error. The Court nevertheless explained that it could use habeas corpus in its appellate capacity to determine whether an inferior federal court had acted without jurisdiction and was unlawfully restraining a person's liberty.

The jurisdictional inquiry required the Court to decide whether the federal indictment charged a valid federal offense. That, in turn, required examination of the constitutionality of the jury provision of the Civil Rights Act of 1875.

Questions Before the Supreme Court

The petitions raised several connected questions:

  • Could the Supreme Court use habeas corpus and certiorari to examine whether the lower federal court had jurisdiction?
  • Did Section 4 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 validly prohibit racial exclusion from state juries?
  • Could Congress impose criminal liability on a state officer who violated that prohibition?
  • Was discriminatory jury selection by a county judge an act of the state within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment?
  • Was preparation of the jury list a judicial act protected from federal control or a ministerial public duty governed by federal constitutional law?
  • Did the indictment allege enough to permit the district court to proceed?

The Court's Holding

The Supreme Court denied the requested discharge and allowed the federal prosecution to continue. Justice William Strong wrote that the jury provision of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 was authorized by the Reconstruction Amendments and that the federal district court had jurisdiction over the charge.

The Court reasoned that the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits state denial of equal protection. A state can act only through legislators, executives, judges, administrators, and other agents. A constitutional prohibition directed at the state would be ineffective if every officer could violate it while claiming that the conduct was merely personal.

Congress therefore could enact legislation directed at the individuals through whom state power was exercised. Penalizing an officer who used official authority to exclude citizens from juries because of race was an appropriate means of enforcing equal protection.

State Action Through Public Officers

One of the decision's most important principles was its explanation of state action. The Fourteenth Amendment speaks to states, but states operate through people who hold public authority.

The Court stated in substance that whoever, by virtue of a public position under state government, denies another person due process or equal protection violates the amendment. Because the officer acts in the state's name and is clothed with the state's power, the officer's act is treated as the state's act.

This rule prevented a state from escaping constitutional responsibility by adopting a formally neutral law and then allowing an official to administer it discriminatorily. The constitutional inquiry focused not only on the words of a statute but also on how public power was actually exercised.

The decision did not make every private wrong a Fourteenth Amendment violation. Its reasoning depended on Coles's public office, his legal responsibility for the jury list, and his alleged use of state-conferred authority.

Congressional Enforcement Power

Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment authorizes Congress to enforce the amendment by appropriate legislation. The Court interpreted this power as permitting laws that prevent, remedy, and punish state denials of the rights protected by the amendment.

Congress could not prosecute an abstract entity called a state. Enforcement legislation necessarily operated on the people who carried state policy into effect. A criminal prohibition directed at officials responsible for discriminatory jury selection was therefore connected to the constitutional wrong Congress was empowered to address.

The Court also referred to the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolished slavery and authorized congressional enforcement. Racial exclusion based partly on previous condition of servitude arose directly from the legal and social order that the Reconstruction Amendments were designed to transform.

Later Supreme Court decisions developed additional tests governing Congress's Section 5 power. Ex parte Virginia nevertheless remains an early and influential statement that Congress may regulate state officials when their use of governmental authority violates Fourteenth Amendment rights.

Why Jury Selection Was Treated as Ministerial

Coles held judicial office, but the Court distinguished the character of a specific task from the title of the person performing it. Preparing a list of eligible jurors was not the adjudication of a case between parties.

The Court described the duty as ministerial: an administrative function carried out under legal standards. Similar tasks could be assigned to commissioners, supervisors, assessors, sheriffs, or other officers without becoming judicial decisions.

This classification mattered because the petitioners argued that federal prosecution interfered with the independence of state courts. The majority concluded that a judge receives no constitutional privilege to violate federal law while carrying out an administrative selection duty.

The broader principle was that official status does not determine whether conduct is constitutionally protected. The nature of the act, the source of the authority, and the right affected control the analysis.

Whose Rights Were Protected?

Racial jury exclusion affected at least two related sets of rights. First, qualified Black citizens had an equal right not to be disqualified from public jury service because of race. Jury service was a form of participation in government and the administration of justice.

Second, litigants and criminal defendants had a right to legal processes not structured through purposeful racial exclusion. A state could not present a jury as an impartial public institution while categorically removing qualified citizens of one race from the pool.

The decision did not establish a right to proportional racial representation on every jury. It prohibited exclusion for a constitutionally forbidden reason. Selection could still depend on lawful qualifications and neutral procedures.

Sufficiency of the Indictment

The majority concluded that the indictment alleged the essential elements of the statutory offense sufficiently to establish federal jurisdiction. It identified Coles's official responsibility, the exclusion of qualified Black citizens, and the prohibited racial reason for that exclusion.

The Court was not deciding whether the government could prove those allegations beyond a reasonable doubt. Its immediate task was to determine whether the charge described conduct that federal law could constitutionally punish.

The dissent strongly disagreed. Justice Field argued that the indictment was too general because it did not name the excluded citizens, identify particular acts, or provide enough factual detail for Coles to prepare a defense.

Justice Field's Dissent

Justice Stephen J. Field dissented, joined by Justice Nathan Clifford. The dissent raised both criminal-procedure and federalism objections.

Indictment objection

Field argued that the indictment merely repeated general statutory language without naming the citizens allegedly excluded or describing specific acts. In his view, a criminal charge required greater particularity before federal process could lawfully restrain the accused.

State sovereignty objection

Field maintained that jury administration in state courts was a reserved state function. He warned that allowing Congress to punish state judges for the way they performed state-law duties would subject state institutions to federal control and weaken the constitutional autonomy of the states.

Judicial-function objection

The dissent viewed selection of qualified jurors as a judicial function involving judgment and discretion. On that premise, federal criminal supervision threatened judicial independence rather than merely regulating an administrative officer.

View of the Fourteenth Amendment

Field accepted that the amendment restricted state action but interpreted congressional enforcement power more narrowly. He did not believe Section 5 authorized direct federal coercion of state judicial officers performing duties under a facially valid state law.

How the Decision Worked

The ruling created a direct enforcement pathway. Federal prosecutors did not have to wait for Virginia to invalidate its own jury lists or discipline the responsible officer. When evidence supported a charge of intentional racial exclusion by an official, the federal government could indict the officer under federal law.

The prosecution still had to prove the statutory elements. The law did not require a state official to select an unqualified person or to guarantee that every jury contain Black members. It prohibited exclusion of an otherwise qualified citizen for race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

The ruling also addressed evasion. A state could not preserve racial exclusion by leaving broad discretion in a neutral statute and expecting the responsible official to exercise that discretion against Black citizens. Discriminatory administration by a public officer could itself supply the required state action.

The Three Jury Cases of 1880

Ex parte Virginia, Strauder v. West Virginia, and Virginia v. Rives were decided on the same day and addressed different enforcement problems arising from racial exclusion in jury selection.

Strauder v. West Virginia

West Virginia law expressly limited jury eligibility to white male citizens. The Court held that the racial qualification violated equal protection and that a Black defendant could invoke a federal removal statute when discriminatory state law made enforcement of his federal rights impossible in state court.

Virginia v. Rives

Virginia law did not expressly exclude Black citizens, but defendants alleged discriminatory selection in practice. The Court denied pretrial removal because the denial of rights did not appear in the state's constitution or statutes and could still be corrected through state proceedings and later federal review.

Ex parte Virginia

This case supplied a different remedy for discriminatory administration under a neutral law: direct federal prosecution of the responsible state officer. Together, the decisions distinguished facially discriminatory law, discriminatory official conduct, removal jurisdiction, and federal criminal enforcement.

Relationship to United States v. Cruikshank

United States v. Cruikshank limited federal prosecution of private individuals under the Enforcement Act after the Colfax Massacre. The Court emphasized that much of the Fourteenth Amendment restricts state action rather than purely private conduct and also found defects in the indictments.

Ex parte Virginia involved the state-action element that was missing from important parts of Cruikshank. Coles was a public officer alleged to have used state authority in administering a state jury system.

The two decisions therefore illustrate the distinction between private racial violence and discrimination carried out through governmental power. That distinction became central to later Fourteenth Amendment doctrine.

Relationship to the Civil Rights Cases

In 1883, the Supreme Court invalidated the major public-accommodations provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875. The Court concluded that those provisions attempted to regulate private discrimination without a sufficient connection to state action.

The Civil Rights Cases did not treat the jury-service provision at issue in Ex parte Virginia as equivalent. State jury selection was a public function, and officers who controlled it exercised state authority.

The contrast demonstrates that the Civil Rights Act of 1875 contained legally distinct provisions. A judgment concerning private inns, theaters, or conveyances did not automatically resolve the constitutionality of federal protection against racial exclusion in state courts.

Later Jury-Discrimination Doctrine

Explicit white-only jury statutes became less common, but discriminatory exclusion continued through administrative practices, voter-registration systems, discretionary selection, and peremptory challenges.

In Neal v. Delaware in 1881, the Supreme Court applied equal-protection principles where state law was formally neutral but Black citizens had been excluded in administration. In Norris v. Alabama in 1935, the Court rejected systematic exclusion supported by evidence of long-term absence of Black citizens from jury rolls.

In Hernandez v. Texas in 1954, the Court held that equal-protection principles were not limited to a two-race classification and applied them to the exclusion of Mexican Americans from juries. In Batson v. Kentucky in 1986, the Court established a procedure for contesting purposeful racial discrimination in prosecutors' peremptory challenges.

These later cases addressed mechanisms different from the annual jury-list selection involved in Ex parte Virginia, but they developed the same foundational principle: public officials may not use jury selection to impose purposeful racial exclusion.

Continuing Federal Statutory Protection

Federal law continues to prohibit exclusion from jury service because of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. The modern criminal provision appears at 18 U.S.C. § 243.

Modern constitutional doctrine also protects prospective jurors, criminal defendants, and the integrity of the judicial system from purposeful racial discrimination. Enforcement may occur through reversal of convictions, civil litigation, federal criminal law, judicial supervision, and statutory jury-selection requirements, depending on the facts and jurisdiction.

The exact procedural remedy varies. Ex parte Virginia is specifically important because it approved federal prosecution of the official responsible for discriminatory state jury administration.

Historical and Constitutional Significance

Ex parte Virginia gave practical meaning to the Fourteenth Amendment's command that no state deny equal protection. A constitutional rule directed at states would have little force if officials could violate it without federal consequence.

The decision confirmed that state action includes unconstitutional conduct by officers who misuse authority supplied by state law. This principle became important far beyond jury selection, influencing cases involving policing, courts, administration, education, voting, and other governmental functions.

The case also confirmed a direct role for Congress. Section 5 was not limited to declaring abstract rights or asking states to correct themselves. Congress could create enforceable duties and penalties aimed at the agents who carried out unconstitutional discrimination.

At the same time, the dissent showed the continuing conflict over Reconstruction. The constitutional question was tied to a larger institutional dispute: whether federal protection of equal citizenship could reach into functions historically administered by the states.

Limits of the Decision

The ruling did not guarantee racially representative juries. It prohibited intentional disqualification because of race while preserving lawful qualifications and neutral selection procedures.

It did not conclude that every jury list lacking Black names was automatically unconstitutional. A violation required proof that qualified citizens were excluded for a prohibited reason.

It did not decide Coles's guilt. The Supreme Court resolved the jurisdictional and constitutional basis for prosecution and left factual adjudication to the trial process.

It also did not eliminate jury discrimination. Officials could conceal discriminatory choices behind claims of qualification, discretion, or local practice. Later decisions were required to establish evidentiary methods for proving systematic exclusion.

Key Distinctions

Facially discriminatory law
A law that expressly classifies citizens by race, as in the white-only jury statute invalidated in Strauder v. West Virginia.
Discriminatory administration
Unequal treatment produced by an official applying a facially neutral law, as alleged against Coles.
State action
Conduct attributable to government because it is performed by the state, an agency, or an officer using public authority.
Private conduct
Conduct not fairly attributable to government. The Fourteenth Amendment generally does not directly regulate purely private action, although other constitutional powers and statutes may apply.
Ministerial act
An administrative duty carried out under legal authority, even when the responsible official must exercise some judgment.
Judicial act
An act involving adjudication or the exercise of judicial power in deciding cases. The majority held that preparing jury lists was not judicial in the relevant sense.
Habeas corpus
A proceeding testing the legality of custody. Coles used habeas to argue that the federal court had no lawful authority to hold him.
Appeal
Review of a lower court's final or appealable decision for legal error. Coles's petition arose before trial and was not an ordinary appeal from conviction.
Removal
Transfer of a case from state court to federal court. Removal was central to Strauder and Rives, but Ex parte Virginia concerned an original federal prosecution.
Federal prosecution
A criminal case initiated by the United States for violation of federal law. The Court held that the charge against Coles could proceed in federal district court.
Right to nondiscriminatory selection
The right not to have citizens excluded from the jury process for prohibited racial reasons.
Right to a particular jury composition
A claimed entitlement to a fixed racial makeup on a specific jury. The case did not establish such a right.

Why Ex parte Virginia Matters

The case matters because it identified the person through whom government acts as constitutionally accountable. A state officer cannot transform governmental discrimination into private conduct merely by departing from the wording of state law.

It also matters because it connected constitutional rights to enforcement architecture. The Fourteenth Amendment supplied the governing prohibition; Congress supplied a criminal statute; federal prosecutors brought the charge; and federal courts determined whether the official could be held to answer.

Finally, the case showed why facial neutrality is not always sufficient. Virginia's written jury law did not expressly exclude Black citizens, but a discretionary selection system could still be operated to produce racial exclusion. Constitutional analysis therefore had to examine administration as well as text.

Summary

Ex parte Virginia was the 1880 Supreme Court decision allowing federal prosecution of J. D. Coles, a Virginia county judge accused of excluding qualified Black citizens from jury lists because of race. Coles and Virginia sought his release through habeas corpus, arguing that Congress could not criminalize a state officer's performance of state jury-selection duties.

The Court held that discriminatory action by a state officer using public authority is action by the state. Because the Fourteenth Amendment prohibited that denial of equal protection and authorized congressional enforcement, Section 4 of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 could impose criminal penalties on the responsible officer. The Court also treated preparation of the jury list as a ministerial duty rather than protected judicial adjudication.

The ruling did not determine Coles's guilt or guarantee a particular racial composition on every jury. Its larger significance was institutional: federal constitutional rights could be enforced against state agents who used governmental power to preserve racial exclusion, even when the written state law appeared neutral.
