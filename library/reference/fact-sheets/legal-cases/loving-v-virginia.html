Loving v. Virginia

The unanimous 1967 United States Supreme Court decision holding that state laws prohibiting interracial marriage violated the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment.

What Was Loving v. Virginia?

Loving v. Virginia was a 1967 Supreme Court case arising from the criminal prosecution of Richard and Mildred Loving for living in Virginia as a married interracial couple. Virginia treated their District of Columbia marriage as unlawful because state law classified Richard as white and Mildred as colored.

The Supreme Court held that Virginia could not make race a legal barrier to marriage. The Court rejected the argument that the statutes were constitutionally neutral because they punished both spouses. The statutes drew their operative distinctions entirely by race and were designed to preserve white racial separation.

The decision rested on two constitutional principles. Equal protection prohibited the state from imposing invidious racial classifications, and due process protected the individual freedom to choose whether and whom to marry without an arbitrary racial restriction imposed by government.

Meaning of the Case Name

The full caption used the phrase et ux., an abbreviation of the Latin et uxor, meaning “and wife.” The caption therefore referred to Richard Perry Loving and his wife, Mildred Loving, as the appellants challenging the judgment of the Commonwealth of Virginia.

The case should not be confused with later cases involving other people named Loving. The canonical reference is Loving v. Virginia, 388 U.S. 1 (1967).

Richard and Mildred Loving

Richard Perry Loving and Mildred Delores Jeter were residents of Caroline County, Virginia. Richard was classified as white under Virginia law. Mildred was classified as colored, and the Supreme Court's opinion used the racial terminology then found in the record. Historical accounts also discuss her family connections to African American and Native communities, but the constitutional case turned on the categories Virginia imposed rather than on a modern determination of her ancestry.

The Lovings did not seek to create a general social campaign when they married. Their immediate objective was to live together as a married family in their home community. Their prosecution transformed that personal objective into a constitutional challenge with nationwide consequences.

Historical Context

Laws restricting marriage across racial categories existed in Virginia from the colonial period. They developed alongside slavery, hereditary racial status, restrictions on free Black people, and legal rules intended to preserve a white-controlled social order.

After the Civil War, states retained or expanded many restrictions on interracial relationships. These laws became part of the broader Jim Crow system, which used racial classification to regulate schools, transportation, housing, voting, public accommodations, employment, family status, and other areas of life.

Marriage restrictions carried consequences beyond the wedding ceremony. They affected the legal validity of families, inheritance, legitimacy, property, residence, criminal liability, marital privileges, and the ability of spouses and children to receive recognition across state lines.

Anti-Miscegenation Laws

Anti-miscegenation laws were statutes prohibiting marriage, sexual relationships, or cohabitation between people assigned to different racial categories. The term miscegenation entered political usage during the Civil War era and was later used in legislation, court decisions, and public debate.

The laws varied by jurisdiction. Some prohibited only marriages involving a person classified as white. Others applied different definitions to Black, Asian, Native, or other populations. Some states imposed criminal penalties, while others declared marriages void, denied licenses, refused recognition, or combined several enforcement mechanisms.

These laws were not neutral family regulations. They used government authority to determine which racial combinations could form legally recognized families and thereby reinforced racial boundaries established by segregation and white supremacy.

Virginia's Colonial and State-Law Background

Virginia enacted restrictions on interracial relationships during the colonial period. A 1691 law imposed severe consequences on certain interracial marriages and relationships, and later laws continued to regulate family formation through racial categories.

After independence and emancipation, Virginia maintained prohibitions against marriages between white and Black people. State courts treated marriage as an institution subject to extensive legislative control and repeatedly upheld racial restrictions as exercises of the state's police power.

By the twentieth century, these earlier rules had been consolidated into a more systematic racial-classification regime. The state did not merely prohibit particular marriages; it created administrative records and legal definitions intended to determine each person's race for marriage and other official purposes.

The Racial Integrity Act of 1924

Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924 strengthened the state's system for classifying residents by race and restricting marriage. It prohibited a person legally classified as white from marrying anyone not legally classified as white, subject to a narrow statutory exception involving limited American Indian ancestry.

The act required racial information in vital records and gave state and local registrars authority to review or challenge classifications. Marriage-license officials were expected to withhold licenses when they believed the applicants' racial statements were inaccurate or the proposed marriage violated state law.

This administrative system made racial classification a condition of legal family formation. It also affected birth certificates, death records, tribal identity, genealogy, and other official documents. The Supreme Court later described Virginia's scheme as a set of measures designed to maintain white supremacy.

Racial Classification Under Virginia Law

Virginia law defined “white persons,” “colored persons,” and “Indians” through ancestry rules. The definitions treated even traceable ancestry as legally significant and gave officials power to determine whether a person qualified for the privileges attached to whiteness.

The statutory exception allowing limited American Indian ancestry for people otherwise classified as white was commonly associated with elite Virginians who claimed descent from Pocahontas and John Rolfe. The exception exposed the political nature of the classification system: ancestry rules were adjusted to preserve preferred white identities while restricting others.

Loving did not require the Court to resolve race as a biological or scientific category. The constitutional issue was that Virginia used its own racial labels to decide who could marry and to make violation of those labels a felony.

Virginia's Statutory Scheme

The Lovings' prosecution rested within a larger group of connected statutes. Together, the provisions attempted to prevent interracial marriages from being formed, recognized, or maintained within Virginia.

Prohibition on Intermarriage

Virginia prohibited a white person from marrying a person classified as colored and imposed corresponding restrictions on a colored person marrying a white person.

Marriage-License Restrictions

License officials were required to examine racial information before issuing a marriage license. The licensing system therefore operated as a preventive barrier before a ceremony could occur.

Automatic Invalidity

State law declared prohibited interracial marriages void without requiring a separate divorce or annulment proceeding. This provision denied legal recognition to the marital status itself.

Leaving the State to Evade the Law

Virginia made it a crime for a white person and a colored person to leave the state for the purpose of marrying, return to Virginia, and live together as husband and wife. This provision attempted to prevent residents from obtaining a valid marriage elsewhere and then claiming its protection at home.

Criminal Penalty

Interracial marriage was classified as a felony punishable by imprisonment. The criminal law reinforced the licensing and invalidity provisions through the threat of arrest, prosecution, conviction, and confinement.

The Lovings' Marriage

Richard Loving and Mildred Jeter married in the District of Columbia on June 2, 1958. The District permitted their marriage, and the National Archives preserves the marriage license documenting the union.

After the ceremony, they returned to Caroline County, Virginia, and lived together as husband and wife. Their return brought them within the Virginia statute aimed at residents who married outside the state and then resumed married life in Virginia.

The legal conflict therefore involved both marriage formation and interstate recognition. Virginia sought to apply its racial policy to residents even though their marriage had been lawfully celebrated in another jurisdiction.

Arrest and Indictment

Local law-enforcement officers entered the Lovings' home during the night and arrested them. The circumstances of the arrest demonstrated that Virginia's marriage restrictions were not merely symbolic provisions; police and prosecutors used criminal process to enforce them against a family living together in a private residence.

During the October 1958 term, a Caroline County grand jury indicted the Lovings for leaving Virginia to marry and then returning to reside together as husband and wife. The indictment treated their marriage and cohabitation as the elements of a felony offense.

The charge did not allege fraud, force, incapacity, bigamy, or lack of consent. The prohibited characteristic was the racial combination assigned to the spouses by state law.

Conviction and Suspended Sentence

On January 6, 1959, the Lovings pleaded guilty in the Circuit Court of Caroline County. Judge Leon M. Bazile sentenced each spouse to one year in jail but suspended the sentences for twenty-five years on the condition that they leave Virginia and not return together during that period.

In explaining the judgment, the trial judge invoked a religious theory that different races had been placed separately and were not intended to mix. That rationale treated a contested theological assertion as support for criminal enforcement of racial separation.

The suspension avoided immediate imprisonment only by forcing the couple to choose between incarceration and separation from their home state as a married pair. The sentence therefore used geographic exclusion to enforce the marriage ban.

Life Outside Virginia

The Lovings moved to the District of Columbia and complied with the sentence while seeking a way to return home legally. Their displacement separated them from their rural community and extended the consequences of the conviction into housing, family support, work, and daily life.

The case illustrates how a criminal judgment can operate beyond confinement. The suspended sentence controlled where the Lovings could live together, under threat that returning as a married couple could activate imprisonment or produce new prosecution.

Beginning the Legal Challenge

On November 6, 1963, the Lovings filed a motion in the Caroline County trial court asking that the conviction and sentence be vacated. They argued that Virginia's statutes violated the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantees of equal protection and due process.

When the state court did not act promptly, the Lovings filed a federal class action on October 28, 1964, in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia. They sought a declaration that the statutes were unconstitutional and an injunction against continued enforcement of the convictions.

Bernard S. Cohen and Philip J. Hirschkop represented the Lovings. Their litigation strategy preserved both state and federal avenues while moving the constitutional issue toward appellate review.

State Trial Court Ruling

On January 22, 1965, the state trial judge denied the motion to vacate. The Lovings then appealed to the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia, the state's highest court at the time.

The federal three-judge court continued the federal action so that the Lovings could first present their constitutional claims through the state appellate process. This procedural choice allowed the Virginia court to address the state statutes before the federal case proceeded further.

Loving v. Commonwealth in Virginia

On March 7, 1966, the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia upheld the anti-miscegenation statutes and the Lovings' convictions. The state court relied heavily on its earlier decision in Naim v. Naim, which had defended the state's power to preserve what it called racial integrity through marriage law.

The Virginia court distinguished school-desegregation and racial-cohabitation cases from marriage. It reasoned that marriage occupied a special area of state authority and that prior federal decisions had not yet expressly invalidated interracial-marriage prohibitions.

The court did, however, reject the original suspension conditions as unreasonable. It vacated the sentence and remanded for resentencing while leaving the felony convictions intact. The practical result was that the Lovings still faced criminal punishment unless the convictions themselves were overturned.

Review by the United States Supreme Court

The Lovings appealed from the Virginia judgment. The United States Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction on December 12, 1966, and heard oral argument on April 10, 1967.

The case reached the Court after major civil-rights decisions had rejected segregation in education, public facilities, transportation, and criminal law. Yet sixteen states still maintained laws prohibiting interracial marriage when the Court considered the case.

The issue required the Court to decide whether marriage remained an area in which a state could preserve explicit racial classifications despite the Fourteenth Amendment.

Questions Before the Supreme Court

  • Did Virginia's racial restrictions on marriage violate the Equal Protection Clause?
  • Could the statutes be treated as nondiscriminatory because both spouses received the same punishment?
  • What level of constitutional scrutiny applied to a criminal law expressly classifying people by race?
  • Did Virginia have a legitimate objective independent of racial discrimination that justified the restrictions?
  • Did the freedom to marry constitute a liberty protected by the Due Process Clause?
  • Could traditional state authority over marriage override the requirements of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Virginia's Arguments

Virginia argued that its laws applied equally because the white and nonwhite participants in a prohibited marriage were subject to the same punishment. Under this theory, the offense was the interracial relationship itself rather than discrimination against one racial group.

The state also argued that marriage had traditionally been regulated under state police power and that courts should defer to legislative judgment. It urged a lower level of review under which the statutes would survive if any rational basis could be imagined.

Virginia further relied on historical arguments about the Fourteenth Amendment and on older precedents, particularly Pace v. Alabama, which had accepted an equal-application defense to a racially defined criminal statute.

The Lovings' Constitutional Position

The Lovings argued that race was the essential element of the offense. Conduct lawful for two people classified within the same permitted racial category became criminal when performed by people assigned to different categories.

They contended that equal punishment did not make the law equal. Each spouse was denied an individual right because of race, and the state used marriage law to enforce white supremacy rather than a legitimate neutral objective.

They also argued that marriage was a fundamental personal decision protected as liberty under the Fourteenth Amendment. State authority to regulate marriage did not include authority to impose an arbitrary racial prohibition.

The Unanimous Decision

On June 12, 1967, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed the Virginia judgment. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for the Court, and every justice agreed that the convictions could not stand.

The opinion treated Virginia's statutory scheme as both a racial-classification system and a direct restraint on personal liberty. The Court did not limit its reasoning to one defective section; it rejected the entire constitutional premise that government could restrict marriage according to race.

Equal Protection Holding

The Court held that Virginia's laws violated the central meaning of equal protection. The statutes expressly made race determine whether a marriage was lawful and whether ordinary conduct became criminal.

The Court explained that the Fourteenth Amendment was intended to eliminate official state sources of invidious racial discrimination. A state could not preserve such discrimination by placing the racial classification inside the definition of an offense.

Because the statutes prohibited marriages involving white people and members of other assigned racial groups, the Court concluded that the scheme was directed toward maintaining white supremacy rather than administering a neutral marriage policy.

Why Equal Punishment Did Not Cure the Discrimination

Virginia's principal defense was that each spouse received the same penalty. The Supreme Court rejected that “equal application” theory because equality of punishment does not eliminate discrimination in the classification that makes conduct criminal.

A person classified as white could marry another white person without committing the offense. A person classified as colored could marry another colored person without committing the offense. The felony existed only when the state combined the spouses' racial identities in a prohibited way.

The Court therefore examined the law's structure rather than merely comparing sentence lengths. The individual constitutional injury was the denial of freedom and legal status because of race.

Racial Classifications and the Most Rigid Scrutiny

The Court stated that racial classifications, especially in criminal statutes, demanded the most rigid constitutional scrutiny. Such classifications could not be upheld through ordinary deference to legislative judgment.

To survive, the state would have needed a permissible objective independent of the racial discrimination itself and a showing that the classification was necessary to accomplish that objective. Virginia offered no such justification.

The state's claimed interest in racial integrity merely restated the discriminatory goal. It did not supply a constitutionally legitimate purpose separate from maintaining racial hierarchy.

White Supremacy and the Structure of the Law

The Court examined whom Virginia's law protected and restricted. The central prohibition prevented white people from marrying nonwhite people, while not imposing the same barrier on every possible marriage between different nonwhite classifications.

This asymmetry showed that the statutes were not designed to preserve all racial groups equally. Their organizing purpose was to guard the legal boundaries of whiteness and maintain the superior status assigned to white citizens.

The decision is therefore significant not only because it invalidated a marriage rule, but because it identified the institutional objective behind the rule: state enforcement of white supremacy through family law.

Due Process Holding

The Court separately held that the statutes deprived the Lovings of liberty without due process of law. Marriage was recognized as one of the vital personal rights protected from arbitrary government interference.

The freedom at issue belonged to individuals. The state could not decide that a person lost the freedom to marry a chosen partner solely because officials assigned the partners to different racial categories.

This due-process analysis complemented equal protection. Equal protection addressed the racial discrimination; due process addressed the deprivation of the fundamental personal choice itself.

Marriage as a Fundamental Right

The Court described marriage as a basic civil right and a central personal freedom. That language placed the decision within a line of cases recognizing constitutional protection for family formation, personal choice, and intimate association.

The holding did not mean that every marriage regulation was unconstitutional. States retained authority over matters such as consent, age, capacity, licensing, and procedures, provided those regulations complied with constitutional protections.

Loving established that race could not be used as an exclusionary qualification for marriage. State police power ended where it conflicted with the Fourteenth Amendment.

Justice Stewart's Concurrence

Justice Potter Stewart filed a brief concurring opinion. He reiterated the principle he had expressed in McLaughlin v. Florida: a state law cannot constitutionally make the criminality of conduct depend on the race of the actor.

Stewart agreed with the judgment but emphasized a direct rule against race-defined criminality. His concurrence reinforced the Court's rejection of statutes that transform otherwise lawful behavior into a crime solely because of racial identity.

Immediate Legal Effect

The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia. The Lovings' convictions could no longer support punishment, resentencing, or restrictions on their married life in Virginia.

The decision also invalidated the constitutional basis of similar laws in the remaining states that prohibited interracial marriage. Those states could not enforce criminal penalties, license restrictions, automatic invalidity provisions, or nonrecognition rules based on the spouses' races.

Some obsolete language remained in state constitutions or codes until later formal repeal, but after Loving such provisions were unenforceable under the United States Constitution.

National Scope

The Court noted that Virginia was one of sixteen states still prohibiting and punishing interracial marriage when the case was decided. Other states had repealed their restrictions during the preceding years, while California's highest court had invalidated its ban in Perez v. Sharp in 1948.

Because Loving interpreted the Fourteenth Amendment, its rule applied nationally. No state could preserve a contrary racial-marriage policy by invoking local tradition, popular preference, or exclusive authority over domestic relations.

Relationship to Pace v. Alabama

In Pace v. Alabama in 1883, the Supreme Court had accepted the argument that a racially defined criminal law did not violate equal protection when it imposed the same punishment on both participants. That reasoning became an important defense of anti-miscegenation statutes.

By the 1960s, the Court had rejected that narrow approach. McLaughlin v. Florida repudiated the reasoning in Pace when reviewing a racial cohabitation statute, and Loving applied the broader equal-protection rule directly to marriage.

Loving therefore eliminated the equal-application theory as a constitutional defense for state marriage laws expressly based on race.

Relationship to Plessy v. Ferguson

Plessy v. Ferguson upheld racial segregation in railroad accommodations and included language treating marriage restrictions as familiar exercises of state power. Virginia courts later cited that legal tradition when defending anti-miscegenation laws.

Brown v. Board of Education rejected separate-but-equal doctrine in public education, but Virginia argued that marriage remained different. Loving closed that asserted exception by holding that state authority over marriage could not sustain explicit racial discrimination.

Relationship to Naim v. Naim

Naim v. Naim was a 1955 Virginia case upholding the state's interracial-marriage restrictions. The Virginia court described preservation of racial integrity as a legitimate state interest and treated marriage as an area primarily controlled by state law.

The Supreme Court did not decide the constitutional merits of Naim at that time. In the Lovings' case, the Virginia Supreme Court continued to treat Naim as binding state precedent.

Loving rejected the constitutional premises underlying Naim. Racial integrity was not a permissible independent objective, and state control of marriage remained subordinate to the Fourteenth Amendment.

Relationship to McLaughlin v. Florida

McLaughlin v. Florida in 1964 invalidated a statute imposing special criminal restrictions on interracial cohabitation. The Court held that racial classifications could not be insulated by the claim that both participants were punished.

McLaughlin did not decide the validity of marriage bans, but it weakened the principal reasoning used to defend them. Loving completed the doctrinal step by applying the equal-protection principle to interracial marriage itself.

Relationship to Perez v. Sharp

In Perez v. Sharp in 1948, the Supreme Court of California became the first state high court to invalidate a statutory ban on interracial marriage under constitutional equality and liberty principles.

Perez applied within California, while Loving established a federal constitutional rule binding every state. The two cases form important stages in the judicial dismantling of marriage restrictions based on race.

Influence on Later Marriage-Rights Cases

Later Supreme Court decisions cited Loving when considering restrictions on marriage. Zablocki v. Redhail addressed a Wisconsin law limiting marriage for certain people with child-support obligations, and Turner v. Safley addressed marriage restrictions imposed on prisoners.

Obergefell v. Hodges relied in part on Loving's recognition that marriage involves both individual liberty and equal protection. The later case applied constitutional marriage principles in a different context involving same-sex couples.

These later uses do not change the specific subject of Loving. Its direct holding concerned racial restrictions on marriage, but its reasoning became an important part of broader constitutional doctrine concerning marriage and personal choice.

How the Marriage Ban Was Enforced

  • Vital-records officials assigned and recorded racial classifications.
  • Marriage-license clerks reviewed the racial eligibility of applicants.
  • State statutes declared prohibited marriages void.
  • Police investigated and arrested couples suspected of violating the law.
  • Grand juries issued indictments.
  • Prosecutors pursued felony charges.
  • Trial courts imposed imprisonment or suspended sentences.
  • State courts defended the laws as exercises of police power.
  • Interstate-evasion provisions extended Virginia's policy to marriages performed elsewhere.

This combination of administrative, civil, and criminal mechanisms made the system comprehensive. It attempted to stop the marriage before licensing, deny recognition after formation, and punish the spouses if they returned to Virginia.

Institutional Significance

Loving demonstrated that domestic-relations law is not constitutionally isolated. Although states possess broad authority over marriage, family status, and licensing, they must exercise that authority consistently with federal constitutional rights.

The case also exposed the institutional depth of racial classification. Legislatures created categories, registrars recorded them, clerks enforced them, police investigated violations, prosecutors charged couples, and courts imposed punishment. The constitutional violation resulted from coordinated governmental action rather than from one private prejudice.

By invalidating the scheme, the Court removed race as a lawful qualification for civil marriage and restricted the state's ability to use family law as an instrument of racial hierarchy.

Social and Historical Significance

The decision allowed interracial couples to marry and live as legally recognized families without state punishment based solely on race. It affected existing couples, future marriages, children, inheritance, property, residence, and public recognition of family status.

The case also became a major civil-rights symbol because it joined personal freedom with structural equality. The dispute involved an ordinary family's desire to live at home, but the laws confronting them represented centuries of racial control.

June 12 later became informally observed as Loving Day in recognition of the decision. That commemoration is cultural rather than a component of the Court's holding.

Important Limitations

The case did not invalidate every state marriage regulation. It invalidated restrictions based on invidious racial classification and recognized constitutional protection for the freedom to marry.

The case did not decide questions of racial identity in a scientific or genealogical sense. It addressed the legal use of classifications created and enforced by the state.

The decision did not award damages to the Lovings or create a general compensation system for people previously prosecuted under anti-miscegenation laws. Its immediate judicial remedy was reversal of the convictions.

The invalidation of marriage bans also did not automatically eliminate private prejudice, family opposition, housing discrimination, violence, or other social barriers faced by interracial couples.

Key Distinctions

Interracial marriage
A marriage between people whom a legal or social system assigns to different racial categories.
Anti-miscegenation law
A historical law restricting marriage, cohabitation, or sexual relationships across racial categories. The term should be used with awareness of its discriminatory historical context.
Equal Protection Clause
The Fourteenth Amendment guarantee prohibiting states from denying any person equal protection of the laws. In Loving, it invalidated the racial classification used to define the crime.
Due Process Clause
The Fourteenth Amendment guarantee protecting life, liberty, and property from unlawful state deprivation. In Loving, it protected the fundamental freedom to marry.
Equal application
The claim that a law is constitutional because each participant receives the same punishment. Loving held that equal penalties do not cure an invidious racial classification.
Facial racial classification
A law that expressly assigns legal consequences according to race. Virginia's marriage scheme used racial terms in the statutory rules themselves.
Discriminatory administration
Unequal enforcement of a law whose text may appear neutral. That was not the principal problem in Loving, because the statutes openly classified by race.
Marriage regulation
State rules concerning licensing, consent, capacity, age, procedure, and legal status. Such authority remains subject to constitutional limits.
Marriage recognition
The legal acceptance of a marital status formed in another jurisdiction. Virginia attempted to deny recognition to the Lovings' District of Columbia marriage.
Void marriage
A marriage treated by law as legally invalid. Virginia declared prohibited interracial marriages void in addition to imposing criminal punishment.
Suspended sentence
A criminal sentence whose execution is delayed or withheld under conditions. The Lovings' original sentence was suspended only if they left Virginia and did not return together.
Reversal
An appellate decision setting aside a lower-court judgment. The Supreme Court reversed the Virginia judgment rather than merely modifying the sentence.

Why Loving v. Virginia Matters

Loving matters because it removed a central legal boundary of the Jim Crow order. The state could no longer use racial classification to decide which consenting adults could form a legally recognized marriage.

It also matters because the Court examined mechanism rather than accepting a formally symmetrical penalty. Both spouses could be punished equally while both were still denied liberty through a discriminatory rule. The decision therefore clarified that equal protection protects individuals from racial classification, not merely groups from unequal sentence lengths.

Finally, the case established a durable constitutional relationship between equality and personal freedom. The state may regulate marriage, but it may not convert white supremacy into a marriage qualification or override fundamental choice through invidious racial discrimination.

Summary

Loving v. Virginia arose after Virginia prosecuted Richard and Mildred Loving for marrying in the District of Columbia and returning home as an interracial married couple. They pleaded guilty in 1959 and received one-year sentences suspended on the condition that they leave Virginia and not return together for twenty-five years.

The Supreme Court unanimously held in 1967 that Virginia's anti-miscegenation statutes violated the Fourteenth Amendment. Equal punishment of the two spouses did not cure the racial discrimination because race defined the offense. The statutes also deprived the Lovings of the fundamental liberty to marry without a constitutionally valid justification.

The decision reversed the convictions, invalidated state bans on interracial marriage nationwide, and established that state authority over marriage cannot be used to maintain white supremacy or deny individual freedom through invidious racial classification.
