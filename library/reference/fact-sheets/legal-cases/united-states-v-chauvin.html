United States v. Chauvin

The federal criminal civil-rights prosecution in which former Minneapolis police officer Derek Michael Chauvin pleaded guilty to willfully using unreasonable force against George Floyd in 2020 and a 14-year-old child in 2017, resulting in two convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 242 and a concurrent federal prison sentence.

What Was United States v. Chauvin?

United States v. Chauvin was a federal criminal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 242.

The United States alleged that Chauvin misused police authority to willfully deprive two people of Fourth Amendment rights.

The prosecution ended in a binding guilty plea rather than a federal jury trial against Chauvin.

A Family of Related Federal Proceedings

The commonly used case name can refer to the four-officer Floyd indictment, Chauvin's separate juvenile indictment, the plea information, sentencing proceedings, and later post-conviction litigation.

The principal Floyd docket was 21-cr-108.

The separate juvenile indictment was filed as 21-cr-109 and was resolved through the plea arrangement.

Not the Minnesota Murder Case

United States v. Chauvin was not the proceeding in which a Minnesota jury convicted Chauvin of murder and manslaughter.

The state case applied Minnesota criminal law.

The federal case applied federal constitutional and civil-rights law.

Federal Court

The prosecution was filed in the United States District Court for the District of Minnesota.

Federal district courts conduct trials, accept guilty pleas, impose sentences, and decide post-conviction motions under federal law.

The case was not an original proceeding in the Supreme Court or the Eighth Circuit.

Judge Paul A. Magnuson

Senior United States District Judge Paul A. Magnuson presided over Chauvin's plea and sentence in the principal federal case.

A federal judge must determine whether a guilty plea is knowing, voluntary, supported by facts, and legally valid.

The judge also evaluates the plea agreement, Sentencing Guidelines, statutory factors, and final judgment.

Magistrate-Judge Role

United States Magistrate Judge Tony N. Leung handled assigned pretrial matters in the principal docket.

Magistrate judges assist with detention, discovery, warrants, scheduling, and other proceedings within statutory authority.

The district judge retained responsibility for the guilty plea, sentence, and dispositive post-conviction rulings.

Derek Michael Chauvin

Chauvin served as a Minneapolis police officer for more than eighteen years by the time of the Floyd indictment.

He was acting as an on-duty patrol officer during both charged encounters.

His official authority supplied the color-of-law element.

George Perry Floyd Jr.

Floyd was a 46-year-old Black man living in Minnesota.

Police detained him on May 25, 2020 after a report involving a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

Chauvin's restraint caused Floyd's death and produced worldwide protest and demands for police reform.

Juvenile 1

The second victim was fourteen years old during a September 2017 domestic-call response.

Federal filings protected the child's identity by using Juvenile 1.

The juvenile count made the federal plea address a prior episode of excessive force independent of Floyd's death.

18 U.S.C. § 242

Section 242 criminalizes willful deprivation of constitutional or federal rights under color of law.

It applies to police officers and other government actors who misuse official authority.

The statutory maximum rises when bodily injury, dangerous weapons, kidnapping, sexual abuse, attempted killing, or death is involved.

18 U.S.C. § 2

The Floyd indictment cited the federal aiding-and-abetting statute along with Section 242.

A person who intentionally aids or causes a federal offense can be punished as a principal.

Chauvin's guilty plea rested upon his own direct use of force and did not depend upon treating him only as an aider and abettor.

Fourth Amendment Right

The counts of conviction protected the right to be free from unreasonable seizure, including unreasonable police force.

The constitutional question concerns the objective reasonableness of force under the circumstances.

Section 242 additionally required proof that the deprivation was willful.

Due-Process Medical-Care Right

The Floyd indictment also alleged a due-process right to be free from deliberate indifference to serious medical needs while under arrest.

This theory appeared in Count Three.

Chauvin admitted the medical-aid facts in the plea, but Count Three was dismissed as a separate count of conviction.

Color of Law

Chauvin acted under color of law because he used on-duty police authority, uniform, restraint powers, and control over people in custody.

An officer can act under color of law while violating department policy or state law.

The doctrine reaches misuse of official power rather than only lawful action.

Willfulness

Section 242 required the United States to prove a willful constitutional deprivation.

Chauvin admitted that the unreasonable force against both victims was willful.

The guilty plea therefore resolved the criminal mental-state element rather than leaving it for a federal jury.

Bodily Injury

Both counts of conviction included bodily injury.

The Floyd count also alleged and admitted that death resulted.

The juvenile count included injuries from flashlight strikes and prolonged restraint.

Dangerous Weapon

The juvenile offense involved a police-issued flashlight used to strike the child's head.

The plea treated the flashlight as a dangerous weapon for statutory and guideline purposes.

An ordinary object can become a dangerous weapon through the manner of use.

Death-Resulting Offense

Floyd's death placed the Section 242 offense within the statute's highest penalty tier.

The charge did not require proof of a separate federal homicide statute because death was an aggravating result of the civil-rights offense.

Causation remained part of the factual basis and sentencing analysis.

May 25, 2020 Encounter

Chauvin and Tou Thao responded after Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng had begun detaining Floyd.

Floyd was placed prone on the street, handcuffed, and restrained by multiple officers.

Chauvin maintained pressure with his knees for approximately ten minutes.

Prone Restraint

Floyd remained face-down with his hands cuffed behind him.

Chauvin admitted that Minneapolis training warned that prone restraint can make breathing more difficult.

The policy called for moving a restrained person to a recovery or seated position when possible.

Floyd Was Unresisting

The indictment and plea described Floyd as handcuffed and unresisting during the continued force.

Chauvin admitted he knew Floyd had stopped resisting, talking, and moving.

Continued force after control was achieved was central to the constitutional violation.

Loss of Consciousness and Pulse

Chauvin admitted that Floyd became unconscious and lost a pulse while restraint continued.

Kueng stated that he could not find a pulse.

The continued restraint and absence of CPR were central to the plea's force and medical-aid admissions.

Bystander Warnings

Bystanders repeatedly expressed concern, requested medical attention, and offered assistance.

Chauvin admitted he did not permit bystanders to approach Floyd to provide aid.

The warnings supported awareness of the medical emergency.

Lane's Requests to Reposition Floyd

Thomas Lane twice asked whether Floyd should be rolled onto his side.

Chauvin did not direct Kueng to discontinue the restraint or move Floyd to recovery position.

These facts became part of the plea's account of awareness and responsibility.

Failure to Provide Medical Aid

Chauvin admitted he was trained and able to provide emergency aid, including CPR when no pulse was present.

He admitted seeing Floyd in serious medical need, unconscious, and pulseless.

No officer began CPR while Floyd remained on the ground.

Admitted Causation

Chauvin admitted that his actions impaired Floyd's ability to obtain and maintain sufficient oxygen.

He admitted that the unreasonable force resulted in bodily injury and death.

These admissions were binding components of the guilty plea.

Callous and Wanton Disregard

The plea stipulated that Chauvin acted in callous and wanton disregard of the consequences to Floyd's life.

That language supported use of the second-degree-murder guideline.

It was not a separate count of conviction.

September 4, 2017 Police Call

Chauvin and an officer he was training responded to a report concerning a mother and her fourteen-year-old son.

The child was lying on a bedroom floor using a telephone when officers entered.

The encounter became the basis of a separate federal indictment.

No Threat From the Juvenile

Chauvin admitted that the child used no force and made no aggressive movement toward officers.

The child initially pulled away while attempting to explain the situation.

The plea treated the later head strikes and prolonged restraint as disproportionate and unjustified.

Flashlight Strikes

Chauvin admitted striking the child multiple times in the head with a police flashlight.

He admitted pinning the child to a wall by the throat and striking him again.

The strikes caused a wound near the child's ear.

Juvenile Neck and Upper-Back Restraint

Chauvin admitted kneeling on the child's neck, shoulders, and upper back for fifteen to sixteen minutes.

The child was face-down, handcuffed, and unresisting during the prolonged restraint.

The child cooperated with directions and cried from pain.

Training and Knowledge

Chauvin admitted familiarity with policies requiring force to remain proportional to resistance.

He knew officers were required to stop force when a person was not resisting.

His experience and training helped establish willfulness.

The Floyd Indictment

The four-officer indictment contained three Section 242 counts.

Count One concerned Chauvin's direct force.

Counts Two and Three addressed intervention and medical-care duties of other officers, with Count Three also naming Chauvin.

Floyd Count One

Count One charged Chauvin with willfully depriving Floyd of the Fourth Amendment right to be free from unreasonable force.

It alleged that he kept his knees on Floyd's neck and body after Floyd became unresponsive.

It charged bodily injury and death.

Floyd Count Two

Count Two charged Thao and Kueng with willfully failing to intervene.

It alleged that they knew Chauvin was maintaining unreasonable pressure on a handcuffed and unresisting person.

Chauvin was not a defendant in Count Two.

Floyd Count Three

Count Three charged Chauvin, Thao, Kueng, and Lane with deliberate indifference to Floyd's serious medical needs.

It alleged that the officers saw Floyd in clear need of medical care and willfully failed to aid him.

The charge alleged bodily injury and death.

The Separate Juvenile Indictment

The second indictment contained two Section 242 counts.

One addressed the throat hold and flashlight strikes.

The other addressed the prolonged knee restraint after the child was handcuffed and unresisting.

Indictment Was Not Proof

The May 2021 indictments were formal accusations returned by a grand jury.

At that stage, Chauvin retained the presumption of innocence in federal court.

The later guilty plea—not the indictment—established federal guilt.

Timing Relative to the State Trial

The federal indictments were returned after the Minnesota jury convicted Chauvin but before state sentencing.

The United States proceeded under separate federal laws.

The federal case did not overturn, review, or replace the state verdict.

Federal Investigation

The FBI and Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension participated in the investigation.

The Minneapolis Police Department provided cooperation.

Federal civil-rights prosecutors and the United States Attorney's Office jointly handled the case.

Prosecution Team

The prosecution involved attorneys from the Civil Rights Division and District of Minnesota.

The team included national civil-rights specialists and local federal prosecutors.

The federal case was separate from the civil pattern-or-practice investigation of Minneapolis policing.

Initial Not-Guilty Pleas

Chauvin initially contested the federal charges.

He pleaded not guilty to the juvenile indictment before later changing his plea.

A later guilty plea superseded the need for a federal trial against him.

Plea Agreement

On December 15, 2021, Chauvin signed a detailed written plea agreement.

He admitted facts establishing two Section 242 offenses beyond a reasonable doubt.

The agreement resolved both the Floyd and juvenile federal cases.

Rule 11(c)(1)(C)

The agreement used Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(c)(1)(C).

The parties agreed to a binding imprisonment range of 240 to 300 months if the court accepted the plea.

If the court rejected the agreed disposition, either party could withdraw.

Factual Basis

A guilty plea requires facts establishing every element.

Chauvin agreed that the factual recitation was true and represented only a partial summary of the government's evidence.

The plea converted contested allegations into admitted facts for the federal convictions.

Trial Rights Waived

Chauvin waived the right to a public jury trial, confrontation, compulsory process, silence at trial, and the presumption of innocence.

He retained the right to counsel.

The court was required to determine that the waiver was knowing and voluntary.

Count One of the Indictment

Chauvin pleaded guilty to the Floyd unreasonable-force count.

The offense carried a maximum of life imprisonment because death resulted.

The statutory maximum exceeded the agreed plea range.

Count One of the Information

Chauvin waived indictment and pleaded guilty to a replacement information concerning the juvenile.

The information combined conduct underlying the two original juvenile counts into one conviction.

The offense carried a ten-year maximum.

Why an Information Was Used

A defendant may waive grand-jury indictment and plead to a criminal information.

The information allowed the parties to structure one juvenile count covering the admitted force.

The original two-count juvenile indictment was then dismissed under the agreement.

Dismissed Counts

The government agreed to dismiss Floyd Count Three as to Chauvin.

It also agreed to dismiss the two original juvenile-indictment counts.

Dismissal did not erase the medical-aid or juvenile facts admitted as relevant conduct.

Medical-Aid Count Versus Medical-Aid Admission

Chauvin was not convicted of a separate deliberate-indifference count.

He nevertheless admitted that he willfully failed to provide aid and that the failure caused bodily injury and death.

The distinction matters when accurately stating the number and nature of convictions.

Two Federal Convictions

The final federal conviction record consists of two Section 242 counts.

One protects Floyd's Fourth Amendment right against unreasonable force.

One protects the juvenile's Fourth Amendment right against unreasonable force.

Why the Case Did Not Produce Three Chauvin Convictions

Count Three in the Floyd indictment was dismissed as to Chauvin.

The juvenile case was resolved through one information count rather than convictions on both original counts.

The plea therefore produced two—not three or four—federal convictions.

Court Acceptance of the Plea

The court reviewed the agreement and accepted the binding disposition.

Acceptance made the 240-to-300-month range controlling.

The court retained authority over the exact term within the range and other lawful sentencing components.

Statutory Penalties

The Floyd count carried life imprisonment, up to five years of supervised release, a fine, costs, and a special assessment.

The juvenile count carried ten years, up to three years of supervised release, a fine, costs, and a special assessment.

The total statutory ceiling did not dictate the actual sentence.

Civil-Rights Guideline

U.S.S.G. § 2H1.1 governs offenses involving individual rights.

It directs the court to use the greater of a specified civil-rights level or the level for the underlying offense.

It also adds six levels when the defendant was a public official or acted under color of law.

Second-Degree-Murder Guideline

The parties agreed that second-degree murder was the underlying offense for the Floyd count.

That agreement set the base offense level at 38.

The guideline classification was used for sentencing and was not an additional count of conviction.

Guideline Murder Reference Versus State Murder Conviction

The federal guideline reference did not transform the federal indictment into a Minnesota murder charge.

It measured offense seriousness under federal sentencing rules.

The separate state murder conviction arose from Minnesota statutes and a jury verdict.

Color-of-Law Enhancement

The plea added six levels because Chauvin was a police officer acting under color of law.

The enhancement recognizes abuse of official authority.

It applies in addition to the underlying-offense calculation.

Restraint of Floyd

The plea added two levels because Floyd was physically restrained.

He was handcuffed and held prone by multiple officers.

The adjustment reflected harm associated with restricting movement during the offense.

Juvenile Guideline Calculation

The juvenile count used aggravated assault as the underlying offense.

The calculation included enhancements for dangerous-weapon use, bodily injury, color of law, and victim vulnerability.

The juvenile count remained substantially lower than the Floyd count in the grouping analysis.

Vulnerable-Victim Adjustment

The parties agreed to a vulnerable-victim adjustment for the fourteen-year-old.

Age and the custodial circumstances increased vulnerability.

The adjustment was part of guideline calculation, not a separate offense.

Combined Offense Level

The parties calculated a combined level of 46 before acceptance of responsibility.

The juvenile unit did not add levels because it was substantially less serious than the Floyd unit under grouping rules.

A three-level acceptance reduction produced level 43.

Advisory Life Range

An offense level of 43 and Criminal History Category I produced an advisory range of life imprisonment.

The binding plea agreement substantially reduced actual sentencing exposure.

The Sentencing Guidelines remained advisory within the accepted Rule 11 agreement.

Acceptance of Responsibility

The government agreed to recommend a three-level reduction if Chauvin remained truthful and cooperative in the presentence process.

Acceptance recognizes a guilty plea and responsibility for conduct.

It does not diminish the legal seriousness of the admitted offenses.

Government Request for Twenty-Five Years

The United States announced that it would advocate for 300 months.

That request was the top of the binding range.

The court selected a lower term.

Defense Sentencing Position

The defense could advocate within the accepted range.

The agreement prevented a request below 240 months.

The court was not required to select either party's preferred point.

Section 3553(a) Factors

Federal sentencing considers offense seriousness, deterrence, punishment, protection of the public, rehabilitation, history, disparities, and the Guidelines.

The court also considered the two victims and abuse of police authority.

The binding plea limited the available imprisonment range.

July 7, 2022 Sentencing

The court publicly announced a 252-month federal sentence.

DOJ described the punishment as more than twenty years.

The sentence ran concurrently with the Minnesota term.

Credit for Discharged State Custody

The plea required an adjustment for qualifying time already served on the related state case.

Court filings later described an intermediate adjusted term of 245 months.

The Bureau of Prisons separately administers federal credit under governing law.

May 9, 2023 Amended Judgment

The court later entered an amended federal judgment imposing 240 months.

The judgment included five years of supervised release.

It imposed no restitution.

Why Sources Refer to Both Twenty-One and Twenty Years

The July 2022 press announcement accurately reported the original 252-month sentence.

The later amended judgment reduced the operative federal term to 240 months.

A current fact sheet should state both and identify the amended judgment as controlling.

Meaning of Concurrent Sentences

Concurrent sentences run at the same time rather than one after the other.

Chauvin's federal term runs concurrently with the 270-month state sentence.

Concurrent does not merge the convictions or erase either sovereign's judgment.

Service in Federal Custody

The plea reflected Chauvin's expectation that he would serve the concurrent sentences in federal custody.

The Bureau of Prisons determines placement and classification under federal authority.

Custody location does not convert the state conviction into a federal conviction.

Five Years of Supervised Release

After the federal prison term, Chauvin is subject to five years of federal supervised release unless lawfully modified.

Supervised release is administered by the federal court and probation system.

Violations can lead to additional proceedings and imprisonment.

No-Contact Provision

The plea required no direct or indirect contact with Juvenile 1 or Floyd's estate and family during custody or supervised release.

No-contact rules protect victims and families.

They remain subject to the judgment and lawful modification.

Permanent Law-Enforcement Disqualification

Chauvin agreed never to work in a law-enforcement capacity.

He agreed to forfeit Minnesota and other law-enforcement or correctional certifications.

The agreement recognised that the convictions were incompatible with future police authority.

Firearm Disability

A felony conviction generally creates a federal firearm prohibition.

The plea identified loss of firearm rights among collateral consequences.

Restoration would require independent legal authority and was not provided by the plea.

Voting and Public-Office Consequences

The plea warned of possible loss of voting and public-office rights.

The precise effect depends on federal and state law.

Collateral consequences are separate from the imprisonment sentence.

Restitution Proceedings

The plea contemplated possible restitution to Floyd's estate and the juvenile.

The original judgment reserved the amount.

The amended judgment ultimately imposed no restitution.

Civil Compensation Was Separate

The absence of federal restitution did not eliminate civil settlements or claims involving the victims.

Minneapolis separately resolved civil litigation with Floyd's family and the juvenile.

Civil payments were not the federal criminal sentence.

Appeal Waiver

Chauvin waived most appeals of guilt, sentence, restitution, statutory constitutionality, and application of the statutes.

The waiver applied so long as the sentence complied with the agreed disposition.

Jurisdictional and limited legally preserved issues could remain.

Collateral-Review Waiver

The plea waived Section 2255 review except ineffective assistance of counsel.

This limitation shaped Chauvin's later motion.

A waiver does not automatically defeat a qualifying ineffective-assistance claim.

No Timely Ordinary Federal Appeal

Chauvin did not pursue a timely merits appeal from the original federal judgment.

He later attempted to appeal the amended judgment.

The Eighth Circuit dismissed that appeal as untimely.

2023 Appeal Proceedings

A pro se notice of appeal was filed in July 2023.

The Eighth Circuit dismissed the appeal on August 14, 2023.

A rehearing request was denied in September 2023.

Section 2255 Motion

On November 13, 2023, Chauvin moved to vacate, set aside, or correct the federal conviction and sentence.

He invoked ineffective assistance of counsel, the category preserved by his waiver.

A Section 2255 motion is collateral review rather than a new direct appeal.

Ineffective-Assistance Claim

Chauvin alleged that counsel failed to provide or pursue information concerning autopsy materials, use-of-force policy, and unsolicited medical opinions.

He claimed he would not have pleaded guilty had counsel handled those subjects differently.

The United States disputed both deficient performance and prejudice.

Government Opposition

The government argued that counsel had access to medical evidence and made reasonable strategic choices.

It emphasized Chauvin's sworn admissions and the state-trial medical record.

The government requested denial without an evidentiary hearing.

Effect of Plea Admissions

A guilty plea carries solemn factual and legal consequences.

Chauvin admitted that his force impaired Floyd's oxygen and caused bodily injury and death.

A post-conviction claim must overcome the plea, waiver, and ineffective-assistance standards.

Post-Conviction Discovery Request

Chauvin sought access to retained biological material associated with Floyd.

He sought testing related to a proposed alternative medical-causation theory.

Discovery in a Section 2255 case requires court authorization and does not itself establish the theory.

Limited Discovery Granted

The district court allowed the defense to examine relevant tissue and fluid samples.

The order was procedural and evidence-gathering in nature.

It did not make a finding that the alternative theory was correct.

Government Motion to Reconsider

The United States asked the court to reverse or narrow the discovery order.

The government argued that the motion lacked the required good cause and was premature.

It alternatively requested reciprocal access to results and expert materials.

Reconsideration Denied

On December 18, 2024, Judge Magnuson denied reconsideration.

The court expected the parties to cooperate and provide the government reasonable access to laboratory reports, test results, and expert disclosures.

The court declined to enter an additional reciprocal-discovery order.

Discovery Was Not Post-Conviction Relief

Allowing testing did not withdraw the guilty plea.

It did not vacate either conviction or reduce the amended sentence.

A final merits ruling would require separate judicial action.

Current Post-Conviction Status

Official public materials reviewed for this fact sheet document the Section 2255 proceeding and discovery orders.

No official final merits order granting or denying the motion was located through August 1, 2026.

The page therefore treats the convictions and amended judgment as operative and does not infer an unlocated ruling.

Minnesota State Convictions

A Minnesota jury convicted Chauvin of unintentional second-degree murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

The state court imposed a 270-month sentence based on the second-degree-murder conviction.

The state judgment supplied punishment under Minnesota law.

State Appellate Proceedings

The Minnesota Court of Appeals affirmed the state conviction.

The Minnesota Supreme Court declined further review.

The United States Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2023.

State Post-Conviction Litigation

Chauvin also pursued post-conviction claims in the Minnesota case.

Those proceedings concern the state judgment and use Minnesota procedural law.

The federal Section 2255 case does not itself vacate the state conviction.

Dual Sovereignty

Minnesota and the United States are separate sovereigns.

The same conduct can violate state homicide law and federal constitutional-rights law.

Concurrent sentences avoided consecutive punishment without merging sovereign interests.

Different Elements

The state murder case required proof of Minnesota homicide elements.

The federal case required color of law, a federal right, and willfulness under Section 242.

Success in one case did not mechanically decide every element in the other.

Two Victims in the Federal Case

The state murder trial concerned Floyd.

The federal plea also produced a conviction for the 2017 assault on a child.

The second conviction broadened the case beyond the death that triggered national attention.

Federal Cases Against the Other Officers

Thao, Kueng, and Lane went to federal trial after Chauvin pleaded guilty.

Thao and Kueng were convicted of failing to intervene and deliberate indifference.

Lane was convicted of deliberate indifference to Floyd's medical needs.

Failure-to-Intervene Significance

The co-defendant verdicts established that an officer can violate federal rights by refusing to stop another officer's unreasonable force.

Rank and seniority do not eliminate the individual duty.

Chauvin's direct-force conviction and the others' omission convictions addressed different conduct.

Medical-Aid Significance

The plea and co-defendant trial emphasized a constitutional duty to respond to obvious medical need.

Officers were trained in CPR and had information showing Floyd was unconscious and pulseless.

The case connected force accountability with custodial medical responsibility.

Not a Precedential Appellate Opinion Against Chauvin

Chauvin's federal guilt resulted from a plea and district-court judgment.

There was no federal appellate merits opinion affirming his guilt after a contested Chauvin trial.

The case is historically important but should not be cited as though it created a new binding appellate test.

How the Case Can Be Cited

The indictment establishes what the grand jury charged.

The plea agreement establishes Chauvin's admissions and negotiated terms.

The judgment establishes convictions and sentence, while later orders establish post-conviction procedure.

No Federal Murder Count

The federal indictment did not charge a standalone homicide offense.

Death increased Section 242 exposure and the murder guideline measured sentencing.

Calling the federal conviction murder without explanation can obscure the actual statute.

Dismissal of Count Three Was Not an Acquittal

The medical-aid count was dismissed under the plea agreement.

Chauvin admitted the underlying deliberate-indifference facts.

Dismissal by agreement is different from a jury's not-guilty verdict.

Dismissal of the Juvenile Indictment Was Not Exoneration

The original two juvenile counts were dismissed because Chauvin pleaded to a replacement information.

The information produced a conviction based on both flashlight and restraint conduct.

The procedural substitution did not absolve him of the 2017 offense.

The Federal Sentence Was Not Life

The guideline range and statutory maximum allowed life imprisonment.

The accepted plea capped imprisonment at 300 months.

The operative amended judgment imposed 240 months.

The Federal Sentence Was Not Consecutive

The federal and state terms run concurrently.

A consecutive sentence would have begun after completion of the state term.

Concurrent service was an express element of the plea disposition.

Supervised Release Is Not Federal Parole

The federal system generally uses supervised release rather than parole for modern offenses.

Supervised release begins after imprisonment.

It is imposed and enforced by the federal court.

Good-Time Credit

The plea estimated possible time actually served assuming federal good-time credit.

The Bureau of Prisons calculates credit under federal law.

An estimate is not a guaranteed release date.

Prison Placement

The Bureau of Prisons controls federal designation and transfers.

Chauvin was initially placed in federal custody after sentencing.

Prison security events and transfers do not alter the validity of the conviction.

2023 Prison Attack

Chauvin was seriously attacked while incarcerated at a federal facility in November 2023.

The assault generated a separate federal criminal investigation and prosecution.

An incarcerated person's conviction does not remove the government's duty to provide lawful custody and protection.

Separate Minneapolis Pattern-or-Practice Investigation

DOJ opened a civil investigation of the City of Minneapolis and Minneapolis Police Department.

That investigation examined systemic practices rather than Chauvin's individual criminal guilt.

The criminal and civil teams operated under different statutes and purposes.

Minneapolis Findings and the Juvenile Incident

DOJ's later Minneapolis findings report discussed the 2017 juvenile assault.

The report used the incident as evidence of supervisory and force-investigation failures.

The report did not create Chauvin's conviction; the guilty plea did.

Proposed Minneapolis Consent Decree

A later federal civil case sought institutional reform of Minneapolis policing.

DOJ dismissed that civil case in 2025 before a continuing federal decree took effect.

The policy reversal did not erase Chauvin's criminal convictions.

Section 242 Significance

The case shows that Section 242 can address deadly police force and prior excessive-force conduct.

It demonstrates that a guilty plea can establish willfulness through admissions to training, awareness, persistence, and causation.

It also illustrates the statute's high sentencing exposure when death results.

Police Training as Evidence

The plea repeatedly referenced Minneapolis policies and training.

Training showed Chauvin knew force must stop when resistance ended and that prone detainees require repositioning and medical care.

Policy knowledge supported willfulness but was not the sole source of constitutional law.

Prone-Restraint Significance

Both victims were held prone after being handcuffed.

The repeated pattern made prolonged neck and upper-body pressure central to the federal case.

The convictions contributed to national scrutiny of neck restraints and positional asphyxia risk.

Protection of Children

The juvenile conviction demonstrates that age and vulnerability matter in force analysis and sentencing.

Police authority over a child does not reduce the child's constitutional protection.

The case also exposed the importance of supervisory review of force involving minors.

African American Historical Significance

Floyd's murder became one of the most consequential police killings in modern African American history.

The federal prosecution placed official constitutional accountability alongside the state murder conviction.

It linked contemporary Section 242 enforcement to the Reconstruction-era national responsibility to protect Black citizenship against abuse of state power.

Global Significance

Video of Floyd's death prompted demonstrations around the world.

The federal guilty plea became part of the official legal record explaining the conduct.

The case influenced debate over force, intervention, medical aid, police culture, and federal reform.

Public Trust

A public guilty plea avoided uncertainty about whether Chauvin admitted the constitutional violation.

The plea provided a detailed factual record beyond the state jury verdict.

Critics and supporters continue to debate sentencing and broader reform, but the federal admissions are legally binding unless vacated.

Finality and Collateral Review

Guilty pleas promote finality by resolving charges without trial.

Federal law nevertheless permits limited review for constitutional defects such as ineffective assistance.

Discovery in collateral proceedings does not itself overcome finality.

Accuracy Caution

Sources should distinguish the original 252-month announcement from the amended 240-month judgment.

They should distinguish the two convictions from dismissed counts and admitted relevant conduct.

They should also separate the federal case from the Minnesota murder case.

Current Status Through August 1, 2026

Chauvin remained convicted federally on two Section 242 counts.

The operative amended federal judgment imposed 240 months and five years of supervised release, concurrent with the state sentence.

The public official materials reviewed did not show that the Section 2255 motion had vacated the convictions or sentence.

Procedural Map

State Arrest and Prosecution

Minnesota arrested and charged Chauvin after Floyd's death, tried him before a state jury, obtained murder and manslaughter convictions, and imposed a 270-month sentence.

Federal Grand-Jury Investigation

Federal investigators examined Floyd's death and the separate 2017 juvenile incident.

Federal Indictments

A grand jury returned a three-count four-officer indictment in 21-cr-108 and a separate two-count juvenile indictment in 21-cr-109.

Guilty Plea

Chauvin pleaded to Floyd Count One and one replacement-information count concerning the juvenile.

Dismissal of Remaining Counts

The separate Floyd medical-aid count and the original juvenile-indictment counts were dismissed as to Chauvin under the agreement.

Federal Sentencing

The court imposed a term within the binding 240-to-300-month range and ordered concurrency with the state sentence.

Amended Judgment

The final amended federal judgment set imprisonment at 240 months, five years of supervised release, and no restitution.

Untimely Appeal

Chauvin's 2023 attempt to appeal the amended judgment was dismissed as untimely.

Chauvin later asserted ineffective assistance, leading to briefing and limited discovery without an identified final merits disposition in the official public materials reviewed.

Misinformation and Common Errors

“United States v. Chauvin Was the Murder Trial”

The murder trial was a Minnesota state case. The federal case concerned constitutional rights under Section 242.

“Chauvin Was Federally Convicted by a Jury”

He pleaded guilty. The other three officers were tried before a federal jury.

“The Federal Case Concerned Only George Floyd”

The plea also produced a conviction for unreasonable force against a 14-year-old in 2017.

“Chauvin Had Three Federal Convictions Involving Floyd”

He was convicted of Floyd Count One. Count Three was dismissed, although he admitted its medical-aid facts.

“The Juvenile Indictment Was Dismissed Because the Allegations Were False”

The original counts were dismissed after Chauvin pleaded guilty to a replacement information covering the conduct.

“Dismissal of the Medical-Aid Count Erased the Admission”

The plea agreement contains a separate admission that he willfully failed to render aid and caused bodily injury and death.

“The Federal Conviction Was for Murder”

The conviction was under Section 242. Second-degree murder supplied the sentencing guideline for the Floyd offense.

“The Federal Sentence Is Still Exactly 252 Months”

The original announcement was 252 months; the amended judgment imposed 240 months.

“The Federal Sentence Runs After the State Sentence”

The sentences run concurrently.

“Concurrent Sentences Mean the State Conviction Disappeared”

Both judgments remain legally distinct.

“The Plea Left Causation Unresolved”

Chauvin admitted that his force impaired Floyd's oxygen and caused bodily injury and death.

“The Discovery Order Vacated the Conviction”

It allowed evidence testing and did not grant Section 2255 relief.

“A Defense Medical Theory Is a Court Finding”

A theory advanced during collateral review is not established merely because discovery is allowed.

“The Untimely Appeal Decided the Merits”

The Eighth Circuit dismissed for timeliness rather than issuing a merits opinion.

“The Case Created a New Supreme Court Rule”

It was a district-court guilty plea and judgment, not a Supreme Court precedent.

“The Federal Case and Minneapolis Pattern-or-Practice Case Were the Same”

The criminal case targeted individual conduct; the civil investigation examined institutional systems.

“The 2025 Dismissal of the Minneapolis Civil Case Erased Chauvin's Convictions”

The civil policy decision did not vacate the criminal judgments.

“Qualified Immunity Applied to Chauvin's Federal Charges”

Qualified immunity is a civil damages doctrine, not a defense to Section 242 prosecution.

“Policy Violation Alone Produced the Conviction”

Chauvin admitted a willful constitutional deprivation, bodily injury, and—in Floyd's case—death.

“The Fact That a Section 2255 Motion Was Filed Means the Conviction Is No Longer Final”

A conviction remains operative unless a court grants relief.

Historical and Legal Significance

United States v. Chauvin created a federal criminal judgment that independently recognized the willful violation of George Floyd's constitutional rights.

The case supplemented the Minnesota murder conviction by vindicating the national constitutional interest protected by Section 242.

The juvenile conviction established that Chauvin's federal liability extended to a separate episode of prolonged and dangerous force three years before Floyd's death.

The plea's medical-aid admissions reinforced the principle that officers who control a person in custody must respond to obvious and life-threatening medical need.

The related convictions of Thao, Kueng, and Lane demonstrated that federal liability can reach officers who fail to intervene or provide aid even when another officer applies the principal force.

For African American history, the prosecution stands within the Reconstruction tradition of federal intervention when abuse of state authority deprives Black people of rights protected by national law.

The case also illustrates limits of criminal adjudication: individual convictions do not by themselves reform department policy, training, supervision, collective bargaining, civilian oversight, or racial inequality.

Summary

United States v. Chauvin was the federal criminal civil-rights prosecution of former Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin.

A federal grand jury charged Chauvin and three other former officers in connection with George Floyd's death and separately charged Chauvin for force against a 14-year-old in 2017.

Chauvin pleaded guilty to two violations of 18 U.S.C. § 242: unreasonable force causing Floyd's bodily injury and death and unreasonable force causing bodily injury to the juvenile.

He admitted that he continued restraining Floyd after Floyd stopped resisting and became unconscious and pulseless, failed to render required medical aid, and caused Floyd's inability to maintain sufficient oxygen.

He also admitted striking the child in the head with a flashlight, holding the child by the throat, and kneeling on the child's neck, shoulders, and upper back while the child was prone, handcuffed, and unresisting.

The separate Floyd medical-aid count and original juvenile-indictment counts were dismissed under the plea agreement, but the underlying medical and juvenile facts remained formally admitted.

The plea used a binding 240-to-300-month range. The original sentencing announcement was 252 months, while the May 2023 amended judgment imposed 240 months, five years of supervised release, and no restitution.

The federal term runs concurrently with Chauvin's 270-month Minnesota murder sentence, and the convictions remain legally distinct.

A 2023 federal appeal was dismissed as untimely. Chauvin later filed a Section 2255 ineffective-assistance motion, and the district court permitted limited discovery without vacating the plea or judgment.

No final merits order resolving that collateral motion was located in the public official materials reviewed through August 1, 2026; the federal convictions and amended judgment therefore remain the operative legal status reported here.

The case is historically significant because it supplied a national criminal judgment for the willful deprivation of Floyd's constitutional rights and separately documented an earlier unconstitutional assault on a child.
