Defund the Police

A decentralized political demand calling for money, responsibilities, personnel, or institutional authority to be shifted away from conventional police departments and toward community services, nonpolice emergency response, violence prevention, and other approaches to public safety.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Political slogan, decentralized policy demand, protest framework, and public-budget strategy.
Principal period of national prominence
2020 and the years immediately following the murder of George Floyd, although related divestment and police-abolition campaigns existed earlier.
Immediate national catalyst
The May 25, 2020, murder of George Floyd by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin and the nationwide protests that followed.
Principal advocates
Black Lives Matter organizers, Movement for Black Lives coalitions, police- and prison-abolition organizations, racial-justice groups, some local elected officials, community organizations, and independent protesters.
Organizational structure
No single organization, leadership body, platform, or legislative proposal controlled the slogan nationwide.
Core budget demand
Reduce, freeze, redirect, or reorganize some police expenditures and invest the resulting resources in services intended to prevent harm or respond without armed police.
Common proposed investments
Mental-health care, housing, education, youth programs, employment, substance-use treatment, domestic-violence services, violence interruption, emergency medical response, unarmed crisis teams, restorative justice, and neighborhood infrastructure.
Common proposed reductions
Police staffing growth, overtime, military equipment, school policing, low-level enforcement, traffic enforcement, homelessness response, behavioral-health calls, surveillance, specialized units, and other functions advocates believed could be narrowed or reassigned.
Range of meanings
Meanings ranged from modest budget reallocation and civilianization to long-term replacement of existing police institutions.
Relationship to police abolition
Some abolitionist advocates treated defunding as an incremental strategy toward ending conventional policing. Other supporters favored reductions or reallocation while retaining police departments.
Relationship to police reform
Defunding focused on institutional scale, duties, and resource allocation, while many reform proposals focused on training, policy, accountability, supervision, and professional standards within existing departments.
Primary governmental arena
Municipal and county budgeting, because most general-purpose policing in the United States is organized and financed by state and local governments.
Major political opposition
Police organizations, Republican officials, many Democratic officials, public-safety advocates, and voters who argued that reductions could weaken emergency response, investigations, deterrence, staffing, and neighborhood security.
Public opinion
National surveys found substantial support for several police reforms but minority support for reducing local police spending; support for reductions declined between 2020 and 2021.
Implementation pattern
Some cities announced reductions, transferred functions, or created alternative-response programs, but implementation varied greatly and many police budgets later increased, were restored, or were reorganized rather than permanently eliminated.
Legal status
Defund the Police is not a federal law or uniform national program. State legislatures in several jurisdictions restricted local authority to reduce police funding.
Historical significance
The demand shifted national debate from officer conduct alone to the size, purpose, jurisdiction, financing, and institutional boundaries of policing.

What Does “Defund the Police” Mean?

“Defund the Police” is a political slogan and policy framework calling for public resources or public-safety responsibilities to be moved away from conventional police departments. The redirected resources may be assigned to social services, civilian agencies, community organizations, nonpolice crisis teams, violence-prevention programs, or other institutions intended to reduce the conditions that produce harm and decrease the number of situations handled by armed officers.

The phrase does not have one universally accepted definition. Some advocates used it to demand limited budget reductions or cancellation of proposed police increases. Others called for substantial cuts, removal of particular functions, or permanent reductions in police personnel. Police-abolition organizations used defunding as part of a longer strategy to replace policing and incarceration with different systems of community safety and social provision.

Because the slogan covered several different objectives, a statement supporting or opposing “defunding” does not by itself identify a specific budget amount, institutional design, implementation schedule, or public-safety model. The underlying proposal must be examined separately.

Terminology and Scope

In ordinary budget language, to defund an institution means to withdraw or reduce funding. In political debate after 2020, however, the phrase often carried a broader meaning: reduce reliance on police by moving money and responsibilities to other institutions.

The movement was decentralized. Local campaigns used different names, targets, and demands. Some organizers preferred “divest and invest,” “reimagine public safety,” “care not cops,” “fund communities,” or “nonpolice crisis response.” These phrases overlapped but were not always identical.

This fact sheet focuses on the United States, especially the national debate that followed George Floyd's murder. It explains the range of meanings, operational mechanisms, municipal examples, opposition, public opinion, and policy consequences. It does not treat every police-budget reduction as part of the movement, because budgets can decline for unrelated reasons such as recession, vacancies, pension accounting, departmental reorganization, or expiration of temporary grants.

Historical Background

Demands to reduce police power and redirect public resources did not begin in 2020. African American communities, labor organizers, civil-rights groups, antiwar activists, feminist organizations, disability advocates, and prison-abolition movements had long criticized police violence, surveillance, racial profiling, political repression, and the use of criminal enforcement to manage poverty and social disorder.

During the twentieth century, some Black political organizations demanded community control of police, civilian review, removal of abusive officers, an end to police brutality, and investment in housing, employment, education, health, and neighborhood institutions. Later abolitionist organizations argued that policing and incarceration formed part of a broader prison-industrial complex that could not be corrected through procedural reform alone.

The phrase “divest and invest” became especially important during the Black Lives Matter era. The Movement for Black Lives launched its Vision for Black Lives platform in 2016 and promoted campaigns to divest from policing and incarceration while investing in community needs and Black political power. The 2020 slogan therefore drew on an established organizing framework, even though its national visibility expanded dramatically after George Floyd's death.

Ferguson and Pre-2020 Organizing

The 2014 Ferguson protests brought sustained national attention to police use of force, racial disparities, municipal fines, warrants, militarized protest response, and distrust between Black communities and local government. Organizers in Ferguson and other cities increasingly connected police encounters to municipal budgets, court revenue, housing, schools, employment, and political representation.

Campaigns before 2020 sought to remove police from schools, end contracts between police agencies and public institutions, reduce jail construction, restrict surveillance, halt purchases of military equipment, and redirect funds toward community programs. These campaigns did not always use the exact phrase “Defund the Police,” but they helped establish the policy logic later associated with it.

George Floyd's Murder and Nationalization of the Demand

Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd on May 25, 2020, while other officers participated in or failed to stop the restraint. Video of Floyd's death circulated nationally and internationally, producing one of the largest protest waves in modern United States history.

Protesters initially demanded prosecution and accountability, but many also argued that repeated police killings showed the limits of reform. The phrase “Defund the Police” appeared on protest signs, murals, petitions, organizational platforms, social-media campaigns, and statements directed at city councils.

Local budget hearings became major sites of political conflict because many governments were adopting fiscal-year budgets during the protest wave. The COVID-19 pandemic had also produced unemployment, public-health emergencies, municipal revenue losses, school disruptions, housing instability, and increased demand for social services. Advocates questioned why police budgets often appeared institutionally protected while other programs faced cuts.

A Decentralized Movement

Defund the Police was not one organization and did not operate through a national chain of command. It functioned as a broad demand adopted by different coalitions.

The Movement for Black Lives, Black Lives Matter organizations, Critical Resistance, local abolitionist groups, student organizations, neighborhood coalitions, public defenders, social workers, health professionals, elected officials, and unaffiliated protesters sometimes used the same slogan while proposing different end states.

This decentralized structure helped the phrase spread rapidly but also produced ambiguity. Supporters could treat the slogan as a flexible coalition framework, while opponents could interpret it according to its most far-reaching abolitionist meaning. Political debate often proceeded without agreement about which proposal was actually under discussion.

Range of Policy Meanings

Budget Freeze

Some proposals sought to freeze a police budget at its existing level, reject a requested increase, cancel an expansion, or prevent replacement of vacant positions. A department could therefore describe a budget as a cut even when its total appropriation remained close to or above the previous year's level.

Targeted Reduction

Targeted reductions focused on particular line items such as overtime, military equipment, surveillance technology, mounted units, school-resource officers, specialized enforcement teams, recruitment campaigns, weapons, or planned staffing growth.

Reallocation

Reallocation moved money from police departments to housing, health care, education, youth services, violence prevention, legal assistance, substance-use treatment, or neighborhood programs. Under this model, police remained responsible for serious crime and emergency response but no longer received the same share of discretionary public resources.

Civilianization

Civilianization transferred administrative or operational functions from sworn officers to civilian employees or other agencies. Examples included dispatch, traffic control, crash reporting, records, mental-health response, homelessness outreach, public information, human resources, and facility management.

Reduction of Police Responsibilities

Some advocates focused less on the total dollar amount than on the range of situations assigned to police. Their objective was to prevent armed officers from serving as the default response to behavioral-health crises, school discipline, homelessness, substance use, welfare checks, traffic violations, neighborhood disputes, and other nonviolent calls.

Institutional Replacement

Abolitionist proposals treated defunding as a transitional strategy toward dismantling existing police departments and constructing different public-safety institutions. These proposals did not necessarily deny that violence or emergency response would exist. They questioned whether the present model of armed patrol, arrest, surveillance, prosecution, and incarceration should remain the central institutional response.

How Defunding Would Work

Most practical proposals required action through local government. A mayor, city manager, county executive, city council, county board, budget office, police chief, labor relations office, and state government could all influence the outcome.

A city could reduce appropriations, eliminate authorized positions, leave vacancies unfilled, cancel a proposed academy class, cap overtime, transfer a unit, revise collective-bargaining agreements, create a civilian department, contract with community providers, or change dispatch protocols. Each mechanism had different legal and operational consequences.

Budget reductions did not automatically create alternative services. Effective reallocation required agencies or organizations capable of receiving funds, hiring personnel, establishing eligibility rules, coordinating with dispatch, operating during required hours, collecting data, and responding to emergencies. A transfer announced during a budget vote could therefore take months or years to become operational.

Why Police Budgets Are Difficult to Compare

Police spending is not recorded uniformly across governments. One city may include pensions, building costs, emergency communications, school policing, benefits, legal settlements, and capital equipment inside the police budget, while another may place those costs in separate departments.

Municipalities also receive state and federal grants, asset-forfeiture revenue, private police-foundation support, and emergency appropriations. County sheriffs, transit police, campus police, state police, federal law enforcement, and private security may operate in the same geographic area.

A reported “cut” may therefore represent a real reduction in police capacity, an accounting transfer, cancellation of proposed growth, removal of vacant positions, or a temporary recession response. Evaluating a policy requires examination of total spending, staffing, duties, grants, benefits, capital costs, and the services created in place of police activity.

Common Reinvestment Areas

Behavioral-Health Response

Advocates proposed dispatching clinicians, emergency medical personnel, peer-support workers, or mobile crisis teams to appropriate mental-health and substance-use calls. The objective was to reduce unnecessary arrest, force, hospitalization, and escalation while connecting people to treatment and follow-up services.

Housing and Homelessness

Reinvestment proposals included permanent supportive housing, emergency shelter, eviction prevention, outreach, sanitation, and case management. Supporters argued that police repeatedly respond to homelessness without possessing the authority or resources to provide housing.

Community Violence Prevention

Some cities funded violence interrupters, hospital-based intervention, street outreach, victim services, youth employment, conflict mediation, and programs for people at high risk of shooting or being shot. These programs sought to prevent retaliation and address violence outside conventional patrol and prosecution systems.

Education and Youth Services

Campaigns to remove police from schools were often linked to demands for counselors, nurses, social workers, restorative-justice coordinators, after-school programs, recreation, and student support.

Traffic Safety

Some proposals separated traffic enforcement from armed policing or limited police stops for low-level equipment and registration violations. Alternatives included automated systems, civilian traffic agencies, street redesign, unarmed responders, and mailed notices, each raising separate questions of safety, equity, privacy, and due process.

Economic and Neighborhood Investment

Advocates connected public safety to jobs, public transportation, parks, libraries, street lighting, vacant-property remediation, food access, health care, and neighborhood stability. The underlying claim was that safety depends partly on material conditions and social institutions rather than enforcement alone.

Arguments Made by Advocates

Defund advocates did not all use the same reasoning, but several arguments appeared repeatedly.

  • Police are assigned many social problems they are not trained or institutionally designed to solve.
  • Armed response can escalate encounters involving mental illness, disability, intoxication, homelessness, or minor disorder.
  • Black communities experience disproportionate stops, searches, arrests, force, surveillance, and incarceration.
  • Repeated reforms have not eliminated serious misconduct or prevented highly publicized killings.
  • Police budgets can consume large portions of locally controlled general funds even when other services remain underfunded.
  • Prevention, housing, health care, education, and employment can reduce conditions associated with violence and repeated emergency calls.
  • Removing noncriminal duties can allow police, where retained, to focus on serious violence, investigations, and emergencies.
  • Community organizations may possess greater trust and specialized knowledge than police agencies in some situations.

Arguments Made by Critics

Critics also advanced several different objections rather than one unified position.

  • Reducing staffing or overtime could increase response times, reduce investigative capacity, and weaken deterrence.
  • Violence and apparently minor calls can develop unpredictably, making it difficult for dispatchers to determine when an armed response is unnecessary.
  • Many high-crime neighborhoods want both better police protection and protection from abusive policing.
  • Alternative programs may lack scale, continuous coverage, legal authority, secure funding, or capacity to respond to dangerous situations.
  • Police reform, better supervision, improved training, more qualified staffing, and accountability may require additional rather than reduced funding.
  • The slogan's ambiguity allowed opponents to interpret every proposal as elimination of emergency law enforcement.
  • Rapid reductions during a period of rising homicide and social disruption could create unacceptable operational risk.
  • Budget cuts may fall first on training, civilian employees, community programs, and newer officers rather than on the functions advocates intended to reduce.

Public Safety and Evidence

The central empirical question was not simply whether police or social programs “work.” Different police activities, staffing levels, prevention programs, and civilian-response systems produce different outcomes under different conditions.

Research has found that additional police presence can deter some crime and that experienced officers may be more effective than inexperienced officers under comparable conditions. Other research has documented racial disparities, unnecessary low-level arrests, harmful encounters, and large differences among departments in the use of force.

Evidence on nonpolice alternatives has grown but remains uneven. Programs such as Denver's Support Team Assisted Response demonstrated that selected behavioral-health and welfare calls could be handled by medical and clinical teams when dispatch information indicated no violence, weapon, serious injury, or active crime. Such models do not answer how every emergency should be handled, but they show that police need not be the only response to every call.

It is difficult to attribute short-term changes in crime to one budget vote. The year 2020 included a pandemic, school and business closures, economic disruption, changes in police activity, court interruptions, firearm purchasing increases, protest, and shifts in social behavior. Claims that Defund the Police either caused or prevented a national crime trend require more evidence than simple timing.

Representative Example: Minneapolis

Minneapolis became the symbolic center of the debate because George Floyd was murdered there. In June 2020, a majority of City Council members publicly supported replacing the Minneapolis Police Department with a different public-safety system. Converting that political pledge into law required decisions about the city charter, staffing, labor obligations, state law, public administration, and voter approval.

In 2021, Minneapolis voters considered a charter amendment that would have removed the Police Department from the charter and replaced it with a Department of Public Safety using a comprehensive public-health approach. The proposed department could have included licensed peace officers, but the charter's minimum police-funding requirement would have been eliminated.

The proposal failed. Official election results recorded 43.83 percent voting yes and 56.17 percent voting no. The vote demonstrated that public support for structural change did not necessarily produce majority support for that particular institutional model.

Representative Example: Austin

Austin, Texas, adopted a broad “Reimagining Public Safety” process in 2020. The city reduced or reorganized police funding, separated some functions from the Austin Police Department, created transitional funds, examined racial disparities, expanded oversight, and considered alternative mental-health and public-safety responses.

Some changes represented genuine policy redirection, while others transferred functions and employees to other city departments rather than eliminating the services. This distinction became important in later arguments over whether Austin had actually “defunded” police.

In 2021, Texas enacted House Bill 1900. The law restricted large municipalities from reducing police appropriations below a prescribed comparison with previous budget cycles and authorized state penalties against a municipality designated as defunding its police. Austin subsequently restored full police funding in compliance with state law while continuing parts of its broader public-safety reform initiative.

Representative Example: Los Angeles

In 2020, the Los Angeles City Council approved changes reducing the Los Angeles Police Department budget by approximately $150 million relative to the previously proposed plan. The action included reductions to sworn overtime and authorized staffing growth, with officials proposing reinvestment in disadvantaged communities and communities of color.

The Los Angeles example illustrates why baseline selection matters. A reduction from a mayor's proposed increase is different from eliminating $150 million of already existing service. Later budgets and hiring policies also changed, so the 2020 vote did not establish a permanent fixed funding level.

Representative Alternative: Denver STAR

Denver's Support Team Assisted Response program began operations in 2020 as an alternative response for selected low-risk calls. A team including medical and behavioral-health personnel could respond when dispatch information indicated no weapon, violence, serious injury, active crime, or life-threatening emergency.

During its first six months, STAR responded to 748 calls. The city's pilot evaluation estimated that STAR-eligible calls could represent approximately 2.8 percent of Denver Police Department calls for service. The program provided a concrete example of role substitution rather than complete police elimination.

Alternative-response systems require careful screening, coordination, worker safety, escalation procedures, follow-up care, operating capacity, and stable funding. They may reduce unnecessary police contact for appropriate calls without replacing police response to armed violence or serious emergencies.

State and Federal Political Response

Republican leaders strongly opposed Defund the Police and used the slogan to argue that Democratic officials were weakening public safety. Many Democratic officials also rejected the phrase while supporting selected reforms, alternative responders, community investment, or limits on police misconduct.

State legislatures considered or enacted laws restricting local police-budget reductions, withholding revenue, limiting local authority, or establishing penalties for cities classified as defunding police. These laws converted a municipal budget dispute into a conflict over state preemption and local self-government.

At the federal level, officials debated police grants, accountability legislation, qualified immunity, use-of-force standards, data collection, training, and community investment. No federal law imposed a nationwide Defund the Police program, and presidents of both parties supported continued federal assistance to law-enforcement agencies while disagreeing over accountability and reform.

Public Opinion

Public opinion depended heavily on wording. Respondents often expressed greater support for specific measures—such as mental-health responders, misconduct accountability, de-escalation, body cameras, or restrictions on certain tactics—than for the phrase “Defund the Police” or a general reduction in local police spending.

Pew Research Center found in June 2020 that 25 percent of adults favored decreasing spending on policing in their area, while 42 percent favored keeping spending about the same and 31 percent favored an increase. Support for reductions was higher among Black adults, younger adults, and Democrats than among white adults, older adults, and Republicans, but it did not constitute a national majority.

By September 2021, the share favoring reduced local police spending had fallen to 15 percent, while 47 percent favored increased spending. Concern about violent crime, partisan conflict, the slogan's ambiguity, and political opposition contributed to the shift.

Race and Community Opinion

Black public opinion was not uniform. Black respondents were more likely than white respondents to support reduced police funding, but many Black residents also wanted effective protection from homicide, assault, robbery, domestic violence, and other crime.

The apparent conflict often reflected two simultaneous demands: protection from abusive, discriminatory, or unnecessary police action and reliable public protection against violence. Political debate sometimes presented these demands as mutually exclusive even when residents sought both.

Local conditions mattered. A neighborhood experiencing repeated shootings, slow response, and low clearance rates could evaluate police resources differently from a neighborhood experiencing aggressive low-level enforcement and frequent stops. National slogans could not fully represent these differences.

Implementation Results

The immediate policy record was mixed. Some cities reduced proposed police spending, shifted functions, created civilian offices, expanded violence prevention, removed police from schools, or established alternative responders. Other cities made no reductions, restored previous funding, increased police pay, expanded recruitment, or transferred funds back after staffing shortages and rising public concern about crime.

A 2025 study examining budget information for 264 major United States cities found no evidence that the size of local Black Lives Matter protests generally caused reductions in police budgets. In cities with larger Republican vote shares, protest was associated with increases in police budgets, which the authors interpreted as evidence of political backlash. The study also found that strongly Democratic cities did not, as a group, produce a general reduction attributable to protest.

The absence of broad budget defunding does not mean the movement had no effect. Its influence also appeared in public hearings, alternative-response programs, school-police debates, violence-prevention offices, civilianization, public data, budget transparency, language, organizational growth, and the political agendas of both supporters and opponents.

The Crime Debate

Homicide rose sharply in many United States cities during 2020. Opponents associated the increase with anti-police protest, reduced enforcement, declining morale, staffing losses, or the Defund movement. Advocates responded that most cities did not make major police cuts and that the increase occurred amid pandemic disruption, economic instability, firearm violence, court closures, and changes affecting many jurisdictions regardless of local budget policy.

A responsible analysis must separate several questions: whether a city reduced spending; whether staffing or police activity changed; whether the change affected particular crimes; whether alternative services were operational; and whether broader national conditions better explain the outcome. One city, one budget year, or one crime trend cannot establish a universal conclusion.

Relationship to Black Lives Matter

Defund the Police became one of the most visible policy demands associated with the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests. Black Lives Matter organizations promoted investment in communities and divestment from policing, while the Movement for Black Lives placed the demand within a broader platform concerning state violence, economic justice, reparations, community control, and political power.

Black Lives Matter was broader than Defund the Police. The movement also included demands concerning prosecution, voting, protest rights, housing, education, health, employment, incarceration, immigration, gender, disability, and economic inequality. A person could support Black Lives Matter while opposing police-budget cuts, and a person could support shifting selected police functions without adopting every Black Lives Matter position.

Relationship to Police Abolition

Police abolition is the position that conventional policing should ultimately be dismantled and replaced by different institutions and social arrangements. Abolitionist organizations generally describe abolition as a long-term process rather than a single act of closing police stations without replacement systems.

For abolitionists, defunding reduces the resources, legitimacy, reach, and social functions of policing while building nonpolice capacity. Critical Resistance and similar organizations explicitly described defunding as a strategy toward abolition.

Not every defund supporter was an abolitionist. Some sought a smaller, more specialized police role within a continuing law-enforcement system. Treating defunding and abolition as automatically identical obscures important differences in objective and scale.

Relationship to Police Reform

Police reform changes the operation of existing departments through policies, training, supervision, discipline, transparency, oversight, hiring, accreditation, data collection, and legal accountability.

Defund advocates often criticized reform for adding requirements without reducing police power or preventing repeated abuse. Reform advocates argued that abolition or major cuts underestimated the need for emergency law enforcement and ignored improvements possible through professional standards and accountability.

In practice, many governments combined approaches. A city might expand training and civilian oversight while also transferring mental-health calls, reducing low-level stops, and funding violence prevention. The categories describe strategic emphasis rather than mutually exclusive administrative choices.

Relationship to Community Policing

Community policing generally seeks closer relationships, problem solving, legitimacy, and cooperation between police departments and residents. It assumes the continued existence of police and attempts to improve how officers work with communities.

Defund proposals question whether police should retain their present budget, duties, and institutional reach. Some supporters viewed community policing as insufficient because it could expand police contact without changing enforcement power. Others saw community policing, role reduction, and civilian alternatives as compatible parts of a redesigned public-safety system.

Communication and the Slogan Debate

Supporters praised the slogan for making a direct demand, rejecting symbolic reform, and forcing public officials to discuss budgets and institutional power. They argued that softer language had failed to produce structural change.

Critics, including some officials who supported police reform, argued that the slogan was strategically damaging because ordinary language suggested eliminating police funding altogether. The phrase became an effective opposition message in elections and was used to characterize candidates who had not proposed abolition.

The dispute illustrates a broader problem in decentralized movements: language can mobilize a committed constituency while reducing support among people who understand the phrase differently. The slogan's political impact cannot be separated from this ambiguity.

Institutional and Administrative Challenges

Moving responsibilities from police to other institutions required more than an appropriation. Governments had to establish authority, dispatch rules, training, insurance, labor classifications, records systems, data-sharing, emergency backup, worker protection, operating hours, complaint procedures, and performance measures.

Collective-bargaining agreements and civil-service rules could limit layoffs, transfers, or changes in duties. State law could mandate staffing, define police powers, control pensions, restrict local budgets, or preempt civilian enforcement systems.

Community organizations faced a different challenge. Accepting government funds could increase capacity but also create reporting obligations, political dependence, professionalization, or pressure to perform quasi-police functions. Organizers disagreed over whether public money should flow through government agencies or directly to independent community institutions.

Measurement and Accountability

Evaluating a defund or reallocation policy requires clearly defined outcomes. Possible measures include emergency response time, homicide and victimization, case clearance, use of force, arrests, complaints, racial disparities, crisis resolution, repeat calls, hospitalization, service connection, resident trust, cost, worker safety, and client outcomes.

No single measure is sufficient. A program could reduce arrests while failing to connect people with services, or improve service connections while operating during limited hours. A police department could reduce response time while increasing unnecessary force. Transparent evaluation should identify both benefits and failures.

Comparisons also require a credible baseline. Changes during the pandemic should not automatically be attributed to a local policy adopted at the same time. Long-term evaluation must account for population, economic conditions, staffing, reporting behavior, firearm violence, service availability, and changes in other parts of the justice system.

Continuing Status

By the middle of the 2020s, the exact phrase “Defund the Police” had less support in mainstream electoral politics than it held during the peak protest period. Many candidates and public officials replaced it with language such as public-safety reform, alternative response, violence prevention, accountable policing, community investment, or right responder–right call.

The underlying policy questions remained active. Cities continued to decide which calls require police, which functions can be civilianized, how violence-prevention programs should be funded, how behavioral-health crises should be handled, and how much public money should support police staffing, technology, settlements, and oversight.

Supporters continued to organize around divestment and abolition, while opponents continued to use the slogan as a warning against reductions in law enforcement. The movement therefore remained influential both as an organizing framework and as a political symbol contested by its critics.

Historical Significance

Defund the Police changed the scale of debate over policing. Earlier national discussions often centered on individual misconduct, officer training, body cameras, prosecution, or departmental policy. The defund demand asked prior questions: Why are police assigned particular duties? Who decides the size of a department? Which institutions receive public money? What conditions produce repeated police contact? What should count as public safety?

The slogan also exposed differences within civil-rights politics. Some organizations prioritized reform and enforceable professional standards. Others argued that policing was structurally connected to racial control and could not be repaired through better administration. Black communities themselves expressed differing assessments based on local crime, police conduct, political ideology, age, and experience.

Although large-scale police defunding did not become a uniform national outcome, the movement produced durable institutional experiments and forced public budgets into civil-rights debate. Its long-term significance may therefore lie less in one national spending reduction than in redefining which institutions are responsible for safety and how their performance should be judged.

Key Distinctions

Defund the Police
A broad demand to reduce or redirect police money, duties, personnel, or authority. It may describe modest reallocation or serve as a strategy toward abolition.
Divest and Invest
A budget framework emphasizing both withdrawal of resources from policing or incarceration and investment in community institutions.
Police abolition
A long-term objective of dismantling conventional policing and building different systems for safety, conflict, prevention, and accountability.
Police reform
Changes intended to improve existing police institutions through rules, training, accountability, supervision, oversight, and professional standards.
Reimagining public safety
A broader and often less confrontational term for redesigning emergency response, prevention, policing, social services, and public-health systems.
Budget cut
A reduction in an appropriation measured against a specified baseline. A cut from a proposed increase is not necessarily a reduction below the prior year's actual spending.
Accounting transfer
Movement of a function and its cost from the police budget to another department. It changes departmental organization but may not reduce total government spending.
Civilian alternative response
Dispatch of nonpolice personnel to selected calls according to defined safety and eligibility criteria.
Community policing
A strategy for improving cooperation and problem solving between residents and an existing police department.

Summary

Defund the Police is a decentralized political demand that became nationally prominent after the 2020 murder of George Floyd. It called for reductions or redirection of police money, duties, personnel, or authority and for investment in services such as mental-health care, housing, education, youth programs, violence prevention, and civilian emergency response.

The slogan did not identify one program. Some supporters sought modest reallocation or removal of selected duties, while abolitionist organizations treated defunding as a step toward replacing conventional policing. Critics argued that reductions could weaken emergency response, investigations, deterrence, and neighborhood protection, and national polling showed minority support for general police-spending cuts.

Some cities reduced proposed budgets, transferred functions, or created alternative-response systems, but widespread permanent defunding did not occur. Many police budgets were later restored or increased, and state governments restricted local reductions. The movement nevertheless changed public debate by making budgets, institutional duties, community investment, and nonpolice public-safety systems central questions in modern civil-rights and criminal-justice policy.
