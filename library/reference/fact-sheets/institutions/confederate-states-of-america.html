Confederate States of America

The self-declared slaveholding republic formed by seceding Southern states in 1861, never formally recognized by a foreign government, and defeated by the United States in 1865.

What Was the Confederate States of America?

The Confederate States of America was the government formed by Southern states that claimed to secede from the United States during the crisis of 1860–1861.

Confederate leaders described the new government as an independent federal republic composed of sovereign states.

The United States rejected unilateral secession, treated the Confederate government as an unlawful rebellion, and never recognized it as a separate nation.

The Confederacy nevertheless exercised substantial governmental authority within territory it controlled, raised armies, collected taxes, issued money, administered courts and mail, negotiated abroad, and fought a four-year war.

Founding Purpose

The Confederacy was formed principally to protect slavery from perceived restriction by the Republican Party and the federal government.

Secession declarations and commissioners emphasized slave property, racial hierarchy, fugitive-slave enforcement, abolitionist activity, and fear that restricting slavery's expansion would threaten its survival.

Confederate leaders also invoked state sovereignty, constitutional compact theory, local self-government, honor, property rights, and resistance to sectional political control.

Those arguments were organized around preservation of a slaveholding society rather than a general rejection of federal power in all circumstances.

Slavery and Confederate National Identity

Slavery was not an incidental institution carried into the Confederacy from the United States.

The permanent constitution explicitly protected property in enslaved people, prohibited laws impairing that right, and required protection of slavery in Confederate territories.

Confederate political leaders publicly described racial inequality and slaveholding as foundations of the new government.

The state depended on enslaved labor for agriculture, military construction, transportation, hospitals, mining, manufacturing, domestic service, and support of armies.

Secession Crisis

Abraham Lincoln won the presidential election on November 6, 1860, as the candidate of a party opposed to the expansion of slavery.

South Carolina seceded on December 20, followed by six Deep South states before Lincoln's inauguration.

Secession conventions claimed that states retained sovereign authority to withdraw from the constitutional union.

Unionists argued that the Constitution created a perpetual national government and supplied no unilateral process for departure.

Kentucky and Missouri Claims

Kentucky and Missouri remained represented in the United States and never passed fully effective statewide secession ordinances through governments recognized by Washington.

Pro-Confederate factions established rival governments claiming authority for each state.

The Confederate Congress admitted Missouri on November 28, 1861, and Kentucky on December 10, 1861.

Confederate flags commonly displayed thirteen stars to represent the eleven seceded states plus the claimed Kentucky and Missouri governments.

Slave States Remaining in the United States

Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained within the United States despite slavery and intense internal division.

Their continued Union status weakened Confederate claims that all slaveholding states formed one separate nation.

Montgomery Convention

Delegates from the seceding Deep South states met in Montgomery beginning February 4, 1861.

They organized a provisional congress, drafted a constitution, created departments, selected national officers, assumed control over military questions, and prepared to negotiate with the United States.

The convention acted quickly because federal forts, customs houses, arsenals, debts, and diplomatic relations required immediate administration.

Provisional Constitution

The provisional constitution was adopted on February 8, 1861.

It created a temporary government based largely on the United States Constitution while delegates drafted a permanent charter.

The provisional government used a unicameral congress in which states voted through delegations.

Permanent Confederate Constitution

The permanent constitution was adopted on March 11, 1861.

It borrowed extensive language and structure from the United States Constitution, including separate executive, legislative, and judicial branches.

It added explicit slavery protections, emphasized the sovereign character of the states, restricted certain spending, modified presidential powers, and made several procedural changes.

The permanent government began operating in February 1862.

Preamble and Sovereignty

The Confederate preamble stated that each state acted in its sovereign and independent character and invoked the favor and guidance of Almighty God.

The language reflected compact theory: the claim that sovereign states had created the central government and retained fundamental authority.

Wartime necessity nevertheless pushed the Confederate government toward conscription, taxation, impressment, suspension of habeas corpus, and national control that often conflicted with state claims.

Constitutional Protection of Slavery

The Confederate Constitution prohibited Congress from passing any law denying or impairing the right of property in enslaved people.

It protected movement of enslaved people and other property among Confederate states and required Confederate territories to recognize and protect slavery.

The document prohibited the overseas African slave trade but preserved the interstate and territorial slave system.

These clauses made slave property more explicit in the Confederate charter than in the United States Constitution.

Presidential Design

The president and vice president received six-year terms, and the president could not be reelected.

The president possessed a line-item veto over individual appropriation provisions.

The design sought to reduce continuous reelection politics while strengthening executive control over spending.

Jefferson Davis was the Confederacy's only president, so normal transfer of executive power was never tested.

Fiscal and Legislative Restrictions

The constitution restricted protective tariffs and stated that duties should serve revenue rather than foster particular industries.

It limited federal spending on internal improvements, with specified exceptions connected to navigation and commerce.

Appropriations not requested by executive departments generally faced heightened voting requirements, and bills were expected to address one subject expressed in the title.

Montgomery as the First Capital

Montgomery served as the capital while the first seven states organized the government.

Jefferson Davis was inaugurated as provisional president outside the Alabama State Capitol on February 18, 1861.

The first cabinet, congress, departments, diplomacy, military planning, and constitution developed there.

Richmond as the Principal Capital

The Confederate capital moved to Richmond in May 1861.

Richmond contained the Virginia State Capitol, Tredegar Iron Works, rail connections, banks, warehouses, hospitals, printing offices, and a large urban workforce.

Its location close to Washington gave symbolic importance but also exposed the government to repeated military threat.

Confederate White House

Jefferson Davis and his family lived in an executive residence in Richmond commonly called the White House of the Confederacy.

The building also served ceremonial and political functions.

President Abraham Lincoln visited the residence after United States forces entered Richmond in April 1865.

Danville and the Government in Flight

Davis and senior officials evacuated Richmond on April 2, 1865, and reached Danville on April 3.

Davis issued his final major public proclamation there while still predicting continued resistance.

The government moved again as Union forces advanced, becoming an increasingly mobile group without secure territory or functioning departments.

President Jefferson Davis

Jefferson Davis was a Mississippi planter, enslaver, West Point graduate, Mexican War veteran, former United States senator, and former secretary of war.

The provisional congress elected him unanimously on February 9, 1861.

He was inaugurated for the permanent six-year term in Richmond on February 22, 1862.

Davis directed military appointments, diplomacy, administration, and relations with Congress and governors but faced criticism over strategy, favoritism, civil liberties, and central power.

Vice President Alexander H. Stephens

Alexander Stephens was a Georgia politician, enslaver, former United States representative, and prewar Unionist who accepted secession after Georgia acted.

He publicly described racial inequality and slavery as foundations of the new government in his 1861 Cornerstone Speech.

He later criticized Davis over conscription, suspension of habeas corpus, military policy, and centralization.

Executive Cabinet

The Confederate executive departments resembled those of the United States.

  • Department of State;
  • Department of the Treasury;
  • War Department;
  • Navy Department;
  • Post Office Department; and
  • legal administration under the attorney general.

Cabinet turnover was frequent, especially within the War and State Departments.

Department of State

The Department of State managed foreign commissioners, diplomatic correspondence, treaties, passports, consular matters, and efforts to secure recognition.

Robert Toombs, Robert M. T. Hunter, and Judah P. Benjamin served successively as secretary of state.

The department failed in its central objective because no foreign government recognized Confederate sovereignty.

Treasury Department

The Treasury collected customs and taxes, issued bonds and treasury notes, borrowed money, administered financial agents, and attempted to fund war.

Blockade and loss of ports reduced customs income, while reluctance to impose direct taxation increased dependence on borrowing and paper currency.

Inflation and military collapse destroyed the value of Confederate money.

War Department

The War Department administered the Army, conscription, supplies, ordnance, engineers, hospitals, prisoners, transportation, military justice, and appointments.

It became the government's largest and most powerful department.

Secretaries included Leroy Pope Walker, Judah P. Benjamin, George W. Randolph, James A. Seddon, and John C. Breckinridge.

Navy Department

Secretary Stephen R. Mallory led the Navy Department throughout the Confederacy's existence.

The department developed ironclads, commerce raiders, torpedoes, submarines, naval yards, river craft, and coastal defenses despite severe industrial limits.

These innovations imposed costs but could not break the Union blockade.

Post Office Department

Postmaster General John H. Reagan organized the Confederate postal service after separation from the United States system.

The department issued stamps, established routes, negotiated contracts, and attempted to cover costs through postal revenue.

Military disruption, railroad damage, occupation, shortages, and inflation progressively weakened service.

Attorney General and Justice Administration

The attorney general advised the president and departments, issued legal opinions, supervised litigation, and supported organization of federal courts.

Confederate district attorneys, marshals, judges, and courts administered national law within accessible territory.

Provisional Confederate Congress

The provisional congress governed from February 1861 through February 1862 as a single chamber composed of state delegations.

It drafted constitutions, created departments, authorized military forces, adopted a flag and seal, enacted financial measures, and transferred the capital.

Much of its work occurred in closed sessions because members considered military and diplomatic matters confidential.

Permanent Confederate Congress

The permanent congress first assembled in Richmond on February 18, 1862.

It contained a Senate with two members from each claimed state and a House apportioned principally by population.

It enacted conscription, taxation, impressment, military appropriations, suspension authority, postal laws, and economic regulation.

Elections and Representation

White male voters meeting state qualifications elected representatives and presidential electors.

Women, enslaved people, and nearly all Black people possessed no national voting rights.

Military conditions disrupted elections in occupied regions, and Kentucky and Missouri representation came from disputed Confederate-aligned political structures.

Confederate Judiciary

The constitutions authorized a supreme court and inferior courts.

Congress created district courts using much of the former United States judicial geography and personnel structure.

Confederate district courts handled seizures, treason, admiralty, debts, sequestration, and national legal questions.

Congress never successfully established a Confederate Supreme Court because of disputes over appellate authority and state sovereignty.

Sequestration Act

Confederate sequestration legislation targeted property and debts belonging to citizens of the United States.

Courts and receivers collected or attempted to confiscate enemy-owned property.

The law served as retaliation for United States confiscation policy and as a source of revenue.

Relationship With State Governments

Confederate states retained governors, legislatures, courts, militias, taxation, and substantial local administration.

States supplied troops, weapons, clothing, food, transportation, and officers.

Governors resisted Richmond over conscription, troop transfers, impressment, habeas corpus, taxation, and exemptions.

Opposition From Governors

Georgia governor Joseph E. Brown criticized conscription and national interference with state authority.

North Carolina governor Zebulon B. Vance defended state supplies, civil liberties, and local control while supporting the war effort.

Such opposition usually sought a more decentralized Confederacy rather than reunion with the United States.

Confederate States Army

The Confederacy organized a small regular army and a much larger Provisional Army composed principally of volunteers and conscripts.

States transferred regiments and officers into Confederate service while retaining some state forces.

The Army defended Confederate territory, invaded Union states, protected transportation, guarded prisoners, enforced conscription, and supported civil authority.

Military Leadership

Prominent commanders included Robert E. Lee, Joseph E. Johnston, Albert Sidney Johnston, P. G. T. Beauregard, Braxton Bragg, Edmund Kirby Smith, John Bell Hood, and Thomas J. Jackson.

Davis retained close control over appointments and strategy and often preferred officers he knew or trusted.

Conflict among Davis, generals, governors, and Congress weakened strategic coordination.

Lee became general-in-chief only in January 1865.

Conscription

Volunteer enlistment proved insufficient as casualties and expiring terms reduced armies.

The Confederate Congress enacted compulsory military service in April 1862, the first national conscription law in American history.

Later laws expanded age ranges and administrative control.

Conscription provoked resistance because the central government removed men from state and family authority and gave exemptions unevenly.

Twenty-Slave Law

An exemption enacted in 1862 allowed one white man to remain on plantations with twenty or more enslaved people, subject to changing terms.

Supporters argued that the exemption protected food production and prevented slave uprisings.

Critics saw it as proof that wealthy enslavers shifted military burdens onto poorer white families.

Desertion and Conscription Resistance

Desertion increased as armies suffered defeat, families lacked food, pay lost value, and confidence declined.

Deserters formed hidden communities, joined guerrilla bands, returned home, crossed Union lines, or enlisted in United States forces after capture.

Confederate authorities used patrols, home guards, executions, amnesties, and family pressure to restore soldiers.

Southern Unionism

Significant numbers of white Southerners opposed secession, resisted Confederate authority, or transferred loyalty to the United States.

Unionism was especially strong in parts of Appalachia, western Virginia, eastern Tennessee, northern Alabama, western North Carolina, and the Texas Hill Country.

Confederate authorities arrested, executed, expelled, or intimidated suspected Unionists.

Confederate States Navy

The Navy attempted to challenge United States maritime superiority through technology and asymmetric operations.

Ironclads, commerce raiders, torpedoes, submarines, gunboats, privateers, and blockade runners became central tools.

Confederate naval innovation imposed costs but could not break the blockade.

Confederate States Marine Corps

The Confederate Marine Corps served aboard vessels, at naval installations, in coastal defenses, and in selected land operations.

It remained small because naval resources and manpower were limited.

Partisan and Irregular Forces

Confederate law authorized partisan rangers operating with military status in enemy-occupied territory.

John S. Mosby's command became the best-known formal partisan unit.

Other guerrillas and bushwhackers operated with uncertain authority and committed raids, assassinations, theft, and retaliation.

Enslaved Labor for the Confederate State

Confederate armies and governments impressed or hired enslaved people from owners.

Enslaved laborers built fortifications, repaired railroads, drove wagons, cooked, nursed, worked in mines and factories, handled animals, and served officers.

National Archives slave payrolls document payments made to owners rather than wages establishing freedom for the laborers.

Enslaved people resisted through escape, work slowdowns, sabotage, intelligence, family protection, and movement toward United States lines.

Free Black People

Free African Americans lived throughout Confederate territory under restrictive state and local laws.

Authorities imposed registration, movement controls, labor requirements, weapon restrictions, taxation, and suspicion of disloyalty.

Free status did not provide security against impressment, kidnapping, or re-enslavement.

Debate Over Arming Enslaved Men

Confederate leaders resisted arming enslaved men for most of the war because military service challenged the ideology and property system the state defended.

Severe manpower shortages produced debate during late 1864 and early 1865.

Congress enacted a limited enlistment law on March 13, 1865.

The statute did not automatically free recruits, and the government collapsed before a substantial Black Confederate combat force entered field service.

Women and the Confederate State

White women could not vote or hold national political office, but they maintained farms, plantations, businesses, hospitals, schools, factories, relief associations, and households.

Women served as nurses, clerks, spies, smugglers, textile workers, munitions workers, and fundraisers.

Black women experienced enslavement, forced labor, family separation, sexual exploitation, escape, refugee life, and participation in emancipation.

Civil Liberties and Habeas Corpus

Confederate authorities arrested suspected Unionists, deserters, spies, draft resisters, and political opponents.

Congress authorized suspension of habeas corpus in specified circumstances and locations.

Governors, judges, legislators, editors, and citizens argued that centralized detention violated the constitutional principles the Confederacy claimed to defend.

Religion and National Legitimacy

Confederate leaders invoked divine favor in the constitution, seal, proclamations, public fasts, and thanksgiving days.

Protestant ministers and denominational institutions defended secession and slavery through religious argument.

Black Christianity sustained resistance, family life, and hopes for emancipation.

Economic Base

Confederate territory produced cotton, tobacco, rice, sugar, food crops, livestock, timber, minerals, and naval stores.

Enslaved labor underpinned plantation wealth and agricultural production.

The region possessed fewer factories, rail connections, shipyards, machine shops, and financial resources than the United States.

Cotton Diplomacy

Confederate leaders expected European dependence on cotton to encourage recognition or intervention.

Export restrictions, blockade, existing foreign stockpiles, alternative suppliers, economic adjustment, and European antislavery opinion weakened that expectation.

Cotton remained valuable for foreign loans and blockade running but never secured diplomatic independence.

War Finance

The government financed war through bonds, loans, customs duties, taxes, treasury notes, produce loans, foreign credit, and seizures.

Initial resistance to heavy direct taxation reflected constitutional ideology and state interests.

Printing currency became the easiest immediate method of payment, producing severe inflation as military prospects and public confidence declined.

Tax in Kind

Congress adopted a tax-in-kind system in 1863 requiring agricultural producers to deliver a portion of specified crops and livestock products.

The policy supplied armies more directly than depreciating money could.

Assessment, collection, transportation, spoilage, exemptions, and local shortages produced conflict.

Impressment

Confederate authorities seized or compelled sale of food, animals, wagons, materials, labor, and other supplies for military use.

Inflation made official payment less valuable than open-market prices.

Impressment intensified conflict between armies, farmers, state governments, civilians, and enslavers.

Currency and Banking

The Treasury issued numerous designs and series of Confederate notes, while state, local, bank, railroad, and private currencies also circulated.

Counterfeiting, inconsistent redemption, shortages of coin, and rapid depreciation complicated commerce.

By 1865, Confederate paper currency had lost nearly all ordinary purchasing power.

Railroads and Transportation

Railroads transported troops, food, ammunition, animals, prisoners, and officials.

Differing track gauges, limited rolling stock, damaged bridges, worn rails, weak central coordination, and Union raids reduced efficiency.

Loss of Chattanooga, Atlanta, Petersburg, and other junctions damaged the national military system.

War Industry

Confederate arsenals and factories produced rifles, cannon, ammunition, uniforms, shoes, locomotives, ironclads, wagons, medicine, and military equipment.

Tredegar Iron Works in Richmond was the most important heavy industrial complex.

Women, children, free Black workers, enslaved laborers, immigrants, and soldiers detailed from units worked in production.

Union Blockade

The United States blockade restricted Confederate exports, imports, customs revenue, weapons, medicine, machinery, and consumer goods.

The blockade was incomplete at first but strengthened as United States naval forces captured ports and coastal bases.

Confederate recognition as a belligerent allowed neutral governments to apply wartime neutrality law without recognizing independence.

Blockade Running

Fast steamships moved between Confederate ports and neutral ports in the Caribbean, Mexico, and Bermuda.

They imported weapons, medicine, cloth, machinery, luxury goods, and government supplies while exporting cotton.

Capture of Mobile Bay, Wilmington, Charleston, and other ports progressively ended the system.

Foreign Recognition

The Confederacy sought formal recognition from Britain, France, Spain, and other governments.

Britain and France recognized Confederate belligerency but not Confederate sovereignty.

No foreign government formally recognized the Confederate States as an independent nation.

United States diplomacy, uncertain Confederate military prospects, economic interests, European political calculations, and slavery all influenced the result.

Confederate Diplomacy

The government appointed commissioners, envoys, purchasing agents, and representatives abroad.

James Mason served in Britain and John Slidell in France.

Their seizure from the British mail steamer Trent by a United States officer created an international crisis in 1861.

The United States released them, preventing war with Britain.

Foreign Arms, Loans, and Ships

Confederate agents purchased rifles, cannon, uniforms, medicine, machinery, and ships abroad.

European financiers issued loans secured partly by cotton.

British-built commerce raiders damaged United States shipping and later produced the Alabama Claims dispute.

Mexico and the Southwestern Border

Texas trade through Mexico supplied an outlet for cotton and an entry point for imported goods.

Confederate officials monitored the French intervention in Mexico, but no durable alliance or recognition emerged.

After defeat, some Confederates fled into Mexico rather than surrender immediately.

Indian Territory and Native Nations

Confederate commissioners negotiated treaties with governments or factions among the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee Creek, Seminole, and other Native peoples.

Native nations were internally divided over slavery, removal history, sovereignty, and alliance.

Native soldiers served both the Confederacy and the United States.

Stand Watie surrendered his Confederate Cherokee command on June 23, 1865.

Confederate Arizona Territory

Confederate-aligned settlers and officials claimed a southern portion of New Mexico Territory as Confederate Arizona.

The Confederate government formally organized the claimed territory in 1862.

Union victory at Glorieta Pass and later occupation ended effective Confederate control.

Territorial Control

Confederate legal claims and actual military control were not identical.

The United States retained forts, coastal enclaves, mountain regions, occupied cities, and large portions of some claimed states.

By 1865, the Confederate government claimed states whose capitals, populations, and transportation systems it could no longer govern effectively.

Self-Emancipation and Loss of Labor Control

Enslaved people escaped to United States lines, supplied intelligence, withdrew labor, protected relatives, and joined Union service.

Their actions weakened plantations, transportation, fortification work, military households, and food production.

Union occupation and the Emancipation Proclamation progressively destroyed the property system the Confederacy had been created to protect.

Food Crisis and Civilian Relief

Military demand, labor disruption, transportation failure, inflation, occupation, drought, and impressment produced severe shortages.

State and local governments created relief systems for soldiers' families.

Women petitioned officials, seized food, protested prices, and demanded discharge of family members.

Richmond Bread Riot

On April 2, 1863, women and other residents marched through Richmond demanding food and affordable necessities.

Participants entered stores and seized goods before authorities restored order.

The riot exposed inflation, unequal access to supplies, and burdens placed on soldiers' families and urban workers.

Class Conflict

Wealth influenced exemptions, food access, substitution, property protection, and ability to survive inflation.

Poor and nonslaveholding white families carried heavy military and economic burdens despite possessing less direct wealth in slavery.

Class resentment weakened legitimacy but did not erase the broader racial and political investment of many white Southerners in Confederate victory.

Confederate Nationalism

The government attempted to create loyalty through flags, currency, stamps, military service, songs, textbooks, holidays, speeches, newspapers, religion, and public ceremonies.

Citizens identified variously with nation, state, county, community, army, race, and family.

Confederate nationalism sustained a long war but never eliminated state rivalry, class division, Unionism, or localism.

National and Military Flags

The first national flag, the Stars and Bars, contained red and white horizontal bars and a blue canton with stars.

The second national flag, adopted in 1863, used a large white field and a battle-flag canton; a red vertical bar was added in 1865 to create the third national flag.

The familiar Confederate battle flag was a military emblem and was never the Confederacy's sole national flag.

Great Seal and Motto

The Great Seal depicted George Washington on horseback surrounded by Southern agricultural products.

The motto was Deo Vindice, commonly translated as “God will vindicate” or “Under God as our vindicator.”

The seal connected the Confederacy to the American founding while asserting divine legitimacy for separation.

Citizenship and Allegiance

Confederate law treated white residents and former United States citizens within the seceding states as owing allegiance to the new government.

Loyalty rules affected property, military duty, travel, courts, and relations with people remaining United States citizens.

The government's racial and slaveholding structure denied equal national membership to African Americans.

Enemy Aliens and Property

Confederate law classified citizens of the United States as alien enemies under specified conditions.

Enemy property could be registered, seized, administered, or sold under sequestration laws.

Citizenship status became difficult where families, firms, estates, and debts crossed the wartime boundary.

Prisoners of War

Confederate authorities operated prisons, camps, hospitals, parole systems, and exchange offices.

Andersonville became the largest and most notorious Confederate prison, where nearly 13,000 United States prisoners died.

The exchange system collapsed partly because the Confederacy refused equal treatment for captured Black United States soldiers and their white officers.

Treatment of Black United States Troops

Confederate policy denied that Black soldiers serving the United States were lawful military equals.

Captured soldiers faced execution, re-enslavement, forced labor, or transfer to state authorities.

The policy defended slavery but damaged prisoner exchange and strengthened the meaning of Black military service as a war against the Confederate order.

Administrative Achievements

Despite beginning without an established national bureaucracy, the Confederacy created a constitution, executive, congress, courts, departments, military forces, treasury, currency, taxation, mail, diplomacy, supply systems, flags, seal, and archives.

Institutional capacity does not establish legal legitimacy or moral neutrality; it demonstrates that the Confederacy functioned as more than a temporary military alliance.

Structural Weaknesses

The Confederacy had a smaller population and industrial base, vulnerable transportation, limited taxation, excessive paper-money finance, state resistance, diplomatic isolation, loss of enslaved labor, and military casualties it could not replace.

Progressive loss of territory, food regions, ports, rail hubs, and public confidence reduced government capacity.

Why Confederate Centralization Increased

War required coordinated manpower, money, food, transportation, weapons, diplomacy, and strategy.

Confederate leaders therefore adopted conscription, national taxation, impressment, passport controls, military detention, railroad regulation, and centralized supply.

The result exposed a contradiction: a state founded partly through states' rights arguments needed stronger central power to survive.

Why Foreign Recognition Failed

The Confederacy never demonstrated a sufficiently secure path to independence.

Britain and France risked war with the United States and domestic opposition if they intervened.

Union diplomacy, battlefield reverses, economic adaptation, and emancipation changed foreign calculations.

The Confederacy's explicit commitment to slavery made formal alliance politically difficult in major antislavery societies.

Why the Confederacy Was Defeated

United States armies captured rivers, ports, cities, rail centers, farmland, and industrial districts.

The blockade restricted trade while the United States retained foreign recognition and superior finance.

Black self-emancipation and military service reduced Confederate labor and increased United States manpower.

Casualties, desertion, inflation, scarcity, internal conflict, and transportation collapse made continued war impossible.

Evacuation and Burning of Richmond

Union breakthroughs around Petersburg forced the government and Army of Northern Virginia to abandon Richmond on April 2, 1865.

Confederate authorities ordered destruction of military supplies, tobacco, bridges, and selected facilities.

Fires spread through the commercial district as officials and soldiers departed.

United States forces entered on April 3.

Dissolution of the Government

The Confederate government did not end through one formal constitutional act.

It lost its capital, territory, treasury, communications, military protection, and ability to enforce law.

On May 5, 1865, the cabinet met near Washington, Georgia, and effectively dispersed.

Davis was captured near Irwinville, Georgia, on May 10.

Military Surrenders

Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia on April 9, 1865.

Joseph E. Johnston surrendered major forces on April 26, and other commanders surrendered through May and June.

Stand Watie surrendered his Confederate Cherokee command on June 23.

These separate events explain why no single date ended all Confederate military activity.

Capture and Imprisonment of Jefferson Davis

United States cavalry captured Davis in Georgia on May 10, 1865.

He was imprisoned at Fort Monroe for approximately two years.

Federal authorities indicted him for treason but never completed a trial.

The prosecution was abandoned in 1869 amid amnesty, constitutional questions, and political calculation.

Legal Status Under United States Law

The United States did not accept Confederate secession as legally effective.

It treated Confederate forces as belligerents for practical rules of war while maintaining that the states remained within the Union.

Reconstruction governments replaced or reorganized former Confederate state governments.

Texas v. White

The United States Supreme Court decided Texas v. White in 1869.

Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase described the Union as indestructible and held that Texas's secession ordinance and related acts intended to separate the state were legally null.

The decision supplied the leading postwar judicial statement rejecting the Confederacy's legal theory.

Confederate Debt and the Fourteenth Amendment

Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment declared debts incurred in aid of insurrection or rebellion illegal and void.

It also prohibited compensation for emancipation or loss of enslaved people.

Confederate bondholders and currency holders therefore had no valid claim requiring United States payment.

Confederate States During Reconstruction

The eleven states remained legally part of the United States but lacked fully recognized republican governments and congressional representation after defeat.

Presidential and Congressional Reconstruction established conditions for reorganizing state governments.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, while the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments addressed citizenship, equal protection, and racial voting discrimination.

Confederate Government Records

National Archives Record Group 109 contains the War Department Collection of Confederate Records.

Captured, surrendered, purchased, and donated materials include records of:

  • the Confederate Congress and executive offices;
  • the Departments of State, Treasury, War, Navy, and Post Office;
  • the attorney general and judiciary;
  • armies, military units, officers, soldiers, hospitals, prisons, engineers, ordnance, and quartermasters;
  • conscription, military justice, slave payrolls, and impressed labor;
  • diplomatic and purchasing agents;
  • sequestration and enemy property;
  • currency, bonds, taxes, and financial administration; and
  • ships, naval stations, marine units, states, and local governments.

Preservation of the Archives

Government officials attempted to remove records from Richmond during evacuation.

Secretary of War John C. Breckinridge helped prevent destruction of important archives.

United States forces captured large bodies of records, and the War Department established a Rebel Archives operation in 1865.

The United States later published Confederate congressional journals and incorporated military documents into the Official Records.

Limitations of the Historical Record

Records were burned, abandoned, captured, hidden, or dispersed during collapse.

Military files survive more extensively than some civilian departmental records.

Official documents often omit the experiences of enslaved people, poor civilians, dissenters, women, and local communities.

Postwar memoirs were influenced by legal defense, reputation, reconciliation, and Lost Cause interpretation.

The Confederacy and the Lost Cause

The Lost Cause portrayed the Confederacy as defending constitutional liberty, state sovereignty, and honorable civilization against overwhelming force.

It minimized slavery's central role, romanticized plantation society, portrayed enslaved people as loyal, and blamed defeat principally on population and industry.

Confederate constitutional provisions, secession declarations, political speeches, and slave policies contradict claims that slavery was incidental.

Veterans and Descendant Organizations

Confederate veterans created local camps and later the United Confederate Veterans.

Women's memorial associations and the United Daughters of the Confederacy organized cemeteries, monuments, ceremonies, textbooks, and preservation.

These organizations preserved records and graves while also advancing interpretations that often minimized slavery and Reconstruction.

Monuments and Public Names

Confederate monuments appeared in cemeteries, courthouse squares, capitols, parks, schools, military bases, roads, and public buildings.

Many major monuments were erected decades after the war during segregation, disfranchisement, and Lost Cause institution building.

Removal, relocation, reinterpretation, and preservation remain public-policy questions.

Confederate Flag Legacy

The battle flag became more familiar than any Confederate national flag.

It was used in veterans' commemoration, commercial culture, military units, political campaigns, segregationist resistance, white-supremacist movements, and regional identity.

Personal intention, institutional origin, later appropriation, and public impact should be distinguished rather than collapsed.

Institutional Responsibility

The Confederacy's policies were produced through interaction among conventions, voters, governors, legislators, judges, cabinet officials, military officers, newspapers, churches, businesses, enslavers, and local authorities.

Responsibility cannot be limited to Jefferson Davis or battlefield commanders.

Individual motives varied, but the government's institutional purpose and constitutional structure remained tied to slavery and independence.

Individual Soldiers and Government Purpose

Confederate soldiers served for varied reasons, including defense of home, coercion, community pressure, patriotism, slavery, racial order, pay, comradeship, and conscription.

A soldier's personal reason does not redefine the founding purpose, constitution, or policies of the government served.

Government-level analysis and individual biography answer different historical questions.

Was the Confederacy a Nation?

The Confederacy claimed nationhood and possessed many practical characteristics of a state: government, territory, population, laws, military forces, currency, and diplomacy.

It lacked formal foreign recognition and never secured uncontested sovereignty over all territory claimed.

It is historically accurate to describe it as a self-declared or de facto belligerent government without treating its independence as legally recognized.

Was It a States' Rights Government?

The Confederate Constitution emphasized state sovereignty and restricted selected national powers.

The government nevertheless enacted conscription, taxation, impressment, sequestration, military detention, railroad intervention, and suspension of habeas corpus.

The Confederacy therefore combined states' rights ideology with rapid wartime centralization.

Common Misconceptions

“Thirteen States Seceded”

Eleven states passed secession measures; the Confederacy additionally admitted rival governments claiming Kentucky and Missouri.

“The Confederacy Was Formed Only After Fort Sumter”

The provisional government, president, constitution, departments, and diplomacy were created during February 1861.

“Its Constitution Was Completely Different From the United States Constitution”

It copied much of the federal structure but made important changes, including explicit slavery protection and a single presidential term.

“The Foreign Slave-Trade Ban Made the Constitution Antislavery”

It prohibited overseas importation while explicitly protecting slavery, slave property, interstate movement, and territorial expansion.

“States' Rights Was Unrelated to Slavery”

State sovereignty was the principal constitutional argument used to protect slavery and slaveholding political power.

“Every Slave State Joined”

Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the United States.

“The Confederacy Had No Functioning Government”

It operated departments, congress, courts, military forces, taxation, currency, mail, diplomacy, and industry for four years.

“The Confederacy Consistently Protected State Sovereignty”

Conscription, impressment, taxation, detention, and regulation generated major disputes with state governments.

“Foreign Belligerent Recognition Made It a Recognized Nation”

Belligerent status allowed neutral wartime rules; no foreign government recognized Confederate sovereignty.

“Large Black Confederate Combat Forces Defended the Government”

Black people performed extensive coerced and civilian labor, but the March 1865 enlistment measure came too late to create a substantial field force.

“The Government Ended at Appomattox”

Lee surrendered one major army; the cabinet continued fleeing and other commands surrendered later.

“The Battle Flag Was Its Only National Flag”

The Confederacy adopted three national flags, while multiple armies and units used battle flags.

“The Supreme Court Approved Secession”

The postwar Supreme Court rejected unilateral secession in Texas v. White.

Key Distinctions

Confederate States of America
The self-declared national government formed by seceding states.
Confederate state
One of the eleven states that seceded and participated in the government.
Claimed state
Kentucky or Missouri as represented by rival Confederate-aligned governments.
Confederate Army
The military forces serving the government, distinct from the entire civilian Confederacy.
Secession
A state's claimed withdrawal from the United States.
Rebellion
The United States legal and political classification of Confederate resistance.
Belligerent recognition
Foreign acceptance that organized war existed, without recognition of independence.
Diplomatic recognition
Formal acceptance as a sovereign state, which no foreign government granted.
De facto government
A government exercising actual control without necessarily possessing lawful or international recognition.
Slaveholding republic
A republican constitutional government that legally protected human slavery.
Lost Cause
A postwar interpretation romanticizing the Confederacy and minimizing slavery.
Redeemer Government
A later white Democratic Southern state government, not the same institution as the wartime Confederacy.
Reconstruction
The postwar process replacing Confederate authority and defining restoration, citizenship, and freedom.

Why the Confederate States of America Matters

The Confederacy was the institutional expression of secession and the government against which the United States fought the Civil War.

Its constitution and founding statements make protection of slavery explicit, allowing historical interpretation to distinguish primary evidence from later claims.

Its wartime experience demonstrates that states' rights ideology did not prevent rapid centralization when military survival required manpower, taxes, supplies, detention, and regulation.

The government created substantial administrative capacity but could not overcome United States military and economic power, self-emancipation, internal dissent, diplomatic isolation, and loss of territory.

Its defeat preserved the United States and destroyed legal slavery, but the racial and political order defended by the Confederacy continued through resistance to Reconstruction and later Jim Crow institutions.

Understanding the Confederacy as a government—rather than only an army, region, or symbol—is necessary for evaluating institutional purpose, responsibility, legal status, administration, and historical legacy.

Summary

The Confederate States of America was the self-declared government formed by Southern slave states claiming secession from the United States in 1861.

Eleven states seceded. The Confederate Congress also admitted rival governments claiming Kentucky and Missouri, producing the thirteen-star symbolism often associated with the Confederacy.

Delegates created a provisional government at Montgomery in February 1861, elected Jefferson Davis and Alexander Stephens, and adopted a permanent constitution on March 11.

The constitution borrowed extensively from the United States Constitution but explicitly protected slavery, slave property, interstate movement of enslaved people, and slavery in Confederate territories.

The government moved to Richmond and established executive departments, a bicameral congress, district courts, an army, navy, marine corps, treasury, postal system, currency, taxation, diplomacy, and military industry.

A Confederate Supreme Court was authorized but never established. State governors resisted national authority over conscription, impressment, taxation, detention, and military administration.

The Confederacy depended on enslaved labor and attempted to preserve white racial and political supremacy. Enslaved people undermined the state through escape, resistance, intelligence, labor withdrawal, and support for United States forces.

The government financed war through bonds, taxes, paper money, tax in kind, foreign loans, and impressment. Blockade, inflation, transportation collapse, territorial loss, food shortages, and military defeat progressively destroyed capacity.

Britain and France recognized Confederate belligerency, but no foreign government recognized Confederate independence.

The government evacuated Richmond on April 2, 1865, operated briefly from Danville, dispersed in Georgia, and lost its president to capture on May 10.

The United States treated secession as legally ineffective, and the Supreme Court rejected unilateral secession in Texas v. White.

The Confederacy's defeat preserved the United States and destroyed legal slavery, but disputes over its purpose, symbols, monuments, soldiers, and memory have continued to shape American public life.
