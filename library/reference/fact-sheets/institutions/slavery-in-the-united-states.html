Slavery in the United States

The hereditary system of human bondage that treated millions of African-descended people as property, compelled their labor, denied their legal autonomy, shaped the nation's economy and government, and ended formally through Civil War emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Legal institution, labor system, property regime, racial hierarchy, political order, and social system.
Principal form
Hereditary racial chattel slavery imposed primarily on people of African descent and their descendants.

European colonizers enslaved Indigenous people and Africans in territories that later became the United States before the United States existed.

In 1619, captive Africans were brought to English Virginia. This was a major event in English North America, but not the first arrival of Africans or the beginning of all slavery in territories that became the United States.

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century colonial laws gradually made African-descended status presumptively lifelong, inheritable through the mother, racially defined, and transferable as property.

Slavery existed in every region during colonial history and survived legally in fifteen states in 1860, the District of Columbia until 1862, and limited federal territories under changing law.
Principal labor systems
Cotton, tobacco, rice, sugar, hemp, grain, livestock, domestic service, skilled trades, mining, transportation, maritime work, construction, manufacturing, and hired labor.

The Thirteenth Amendment permits involuntary servitude as punishment for crime after conviction, a provision later connected to convict leasing and racially unequal penal labor.
Historical significance
Slavery shaped national wealth, territorial expansion, representation, law, citizenship, race, family formation, religion, political parties, and the Civil War. Its formal abolition ended legal ownership of people but did not erase the institutions, inequalities, or ideologies created by more than two centuries of bondage.

What Was Slavery in the United States?

Slavery was a legally enforced system in which one person or institution claimed ownership and control over another human being.

Enslavers bought, sold, inherited, mortgaged, leased, hired out, punished, transported, and compelled the labor of enslaved people.

The dominant United States system became racial and hereditary: people of African descent were presumed enslaveable, and children generally inherited the legal condition of an enslaved mother.

Enslaved people remained human beings with families, knowledge, beliefs, strategies, and aspirations even when law classified them as property.

Language and Historical Terminology

This fact sheet generally uses “enslaved person” rather than defining a human being only as “a slave.”

“Enslaver” identifies a person who exercised or claimed ownership, while “slaveholder” remains useful when discussing legal and historical classifications.

Original records frequently use dehumanizing racial and property language that should be quoted only when necessary for accuracy.

Chattel Slavery

Chattel slavery treated people as movable and inheritable property.

Ownership claims could be transferred through sale, inheritance, marriage settlements, gifts, debt proceedings, foreclosure, and court order.

Enslavers claimed authority over labor, movement, residence, marriage, children, reproduction, and punishment.

Colonial Origins

The United States inherited systems of bondage from Spanish, French, Dutch, British, and Indigenous societies.

Colonial law and practice differed by empire, region, religion, crop, labor demand, and local population.

The institution later identified as United States slavery developed through these colonial systems and became dominated by hereditary African chattel slavery.

Indigenous Enslavement

European colonizers captured, purchased, transported, and compelled the labor of Native people throughout North America.

Warfare, raiding, debt, criminal punishment, mission systems, household service, and colonial trade produced Indigenous bondage.

Historians should distinguish Indigenous systems of captivity from European commercial chattel slavery while recognizing coercion and exploitation in both.

Spanish Florida

Free and enslaved Africans accompanied the founding of St. Augustine in 1565.

Africans worked in fortification, agriculture, crafts, construction, domestic service, and military activity.

Spanish law recognized slavery but also supplied legal routes to manumission and allowed some enslaved people to purchase freedom, marry, own property, and use courts.

French and Spanish Louisiana

French Louisiana used enslaved African and Indigenous labor in agriculture, households, construction, trade, and military work.

The French Code Noir regulated slavery, Catholic baptism, marriage, punishment, manumission, and racial status.

Louisiana's legal culture later influenced slavery under United States authority after the Louisiana Purchase.

Virginia in 1619

In late August 1619, the privateer White Lion brought captive Africans to Point Comfort in Virginia after the captives had been seized from a Portuguese slave ship.

Additional captives arrived on the Treasurer.

The event marks a foundational moment in English North American slavery but was not the first African presence or first enslavement in territories later incorporated into the United States.

Transition to Hereditary Racial Slavery

Early colonial labor systems included European indentured servants, African servants, enslaved Africans, and Indigenous captives.

Over the seventeenth century, courts and legislatures increasingly associated African ancestry with permanent enslavement.

Laws restricted interracial marriage, movement, weapons, testimony, baptism-based freedom claims, and manumission.

Partus Sequitur Ventrem

Virginia enacted a 1662 law establishing that a child's legal status followed the condition of the mother.

Other colonies adopted similar rules.

The principle made slavery hereditary and gave enslavers a legal ownership claim over children born to enslaved women.

Slave Codes

Slave codes regulated the lives of enslaved people and the authority of enslavers.

They restricted movement, assembly, literacy, weapons, trade, testimony, contracts, marriage, and independent economic activity.

Codes authorized patrols, corporal punishment, capture, imprisonment, sale, and execution under specified circumstances.

Regional Systems of Slavery

Chesapeake slavery centered heavily on tobacco and mixed agriculture; Lowcountry slavery developed around rice and indigo; Louisiana used sugar and other crops; Northern colonies used enslaved labor in farms, ports, households, ships, and trades.

Regional diversity did not change the fundamental denial of freedom and ownership claims over people.

Slavery in Northern Colonies and States

Slavery existed in New England, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and other Northern jurisdictions.

Enslaved people worked in homes, farms, ports, ships, fisheries, shops, printing offices, artisan trades, and commercial enterprises.

Gradual abolition ended legal slavery unevenly, leaving some people in bondage for decades after antislavery laws were enacted.

Urban Slavery

Enslaved people lived and worked in cities including New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington, Richmond, Charleston, Savannah, Mobile, and New Orleans.

They served as domestic workers, artisans, dockworkers, sailors, market sellers, drivers, builders, hotel workers, and factory laborers.

Urban mobility could support communication and escape, while police and enslavers responded with passes, curfews, jails, patrols, and sale.

Slavery and the American Revolution

Revolutionary language of liberty and natural rights exposed the contradiction between independence and human bondage.

Enslaved people petitioned for freedom, sued in courts, joined military forces, escaped, and negotiated with competing governments.

Thousands of Black men served American forces, while many others sought freedom through British lines.

State Abolition and Gradual Emancipation

Vermont's 1777 constitution prohibited adult slavery in principle, Pennsylvania enacted gradual abolition in 1780, and other Northern states used constitutional provisions, statutes, or court decisions.

Gradual laws commonly left adults in bondage and required children to serve for years before becoming free.

The Constitution and Slavery

The Constitution avoided the words “slave” and “slavery” but incorporated compromises that protected the institution.

Federalism left ordinary slave law largely to states while national clauses strengthened slaveholding representation, delayed trade prohibition, and required return of persons held to service.

Three-Fifths Clause

Article I counted three-fifths of “all other Persons” for representation and direct taxation.

The formula did not grant enslaved people partial individual rights; it increased slave-state political power while voting remained in white hands.

Migration or Importation Clause

Article I prevented Congress from banning importation before 1808.

The clause protected the international slave trade from immediate federal prohibition while allowing a limited tax on imported persons.

Fugitive Slave Clause

Article IV required return of a person held to service or labor who escaped into another state.

Congress implemented the clause through the Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850.

The Thirteenth Amendment made its slavery application legally obsolete.

Prohibition of the International Slave Trade

The Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves took effect on January 1, 1808.

It ended legal foreign importation but did not abolish slavery or the domestic trade.

Illegal trafficking continued through Atlantic, Gulf, Caribbean, and land routes.

Domestic Slave Trade

A vast interstate market bought and sold enslaved people within the United States.

Richmond, Alexandria, Washington, Baltimore, Charleston, Savannah, Mobile, Natchez, and New Orleans became important centers.

The trade redistributed labor toward expanding cotton and sugar regions and separated families on a massive scale.

Forced Migration to the Deep South

Historians sometimes call the forced relocation from the Upper South to the Deep South the “Second Middle Passage.”

Enslaved people traveled in chained coffles, wagons, ships, steamboats, and rail transport.

The journey involved confinement, inspection, sale, violence, disease, and separation from communities.

Cotton and Expansion

Efficient cotton processing made short-staple cotton more profitable and increased demand for land and enslaved labor.

Cotton production spread through Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, Texas, and other regions after Indigenous dispossession.

By 1860, cotton was the nation's leading export.

Territorial Expansion and Political Conflict

The Louisiana Purchase, Texas annexation, the Mexican–American War, and western settlement repeatedly raised whether slavery would expand.

The Missouri Compromise, Compromise of 1850, Kansas–Nebraska Act, and popular sovereignty failed to create a lasting settlement.

Dred Scott v. Sandford

Dred and Harriet Scott sued for freedom based on residence in free jurisdictions.

In 1857, the Supreme Court denied federal citizenship to Black people descended from enslaved Africans and held that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories.

The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments later overturned the decision's central principles.

Population Growth

The federal census counted 697,624 enslaved people in 1790, 1,538,022 in 1820, 3,204,313 in 1850, and 3,953,760 in 1860.

After legal international importation ended, growth resulted primarily from births within the United States.

The 1860 enslaved population represented approximately 12.6 percent of the total population.

Geography in 1860

Fifteen states legally recognized slavery in 1860.

The largest enslaved populations were in Virginia, Georgia, Mississippi, Alabama, and South Carolina.

Enslaved people formed a majority of the population in Mississippi and South Carolina.

Labor Systems

  • cotton, tobacco, rice, indigo, sugar, hemp, grain, and livestock production;
  • domestic and household labor;
  • skilled trades such as blacksmithing, carpentry, masonry, tailoring, and coopering;
  • mining, iron production, factories, and tobacco processing;
  • maritime, dock, canal, ferry, and river work;
  • construction, railroads, public works, and military labor; and
  • hiring out to private employers, businesses, and governments.

Plantations and Small Holdings

A plantation was a large market-oriented agricultural enterprise, but not every enslaved person lived on a plantation.

Many enslavers held fewer than ten people, and some held only one.

Small holdings could isolate enslaved people from larger Black communities and place them under close household surveillance.

National Economic Connections

Banks financed plantation and commodity trade; insurers covered ships, cargo, property, and sometimes the claimed value of enslaved people.

Textile factories processed slave-grown cotton, while merchants, ports, railroads, and ships distributed commodities.

Slavery was therefore a national and international economic system, not only a plantation institution.

Family Life

Enslaved people formed marriages, parent-child bonds, extended families, fictive kin, and community networks despite lacking secure legal protection.

Families cared for children, transmitted names and traditions, shared resources, and created emotional refuge.

Ownership divided authority because enslavers claimed control over residence, labor, sale, and children.

Children Under Slavery

Children inherited enslaved status through their mothers.

They performed childcare, household tasks, animal care, field work, errands, and skilled assistance as they aged.

Children faced sale, punishment, disease, family separation, and sexual exploitation.

Housing, Food, Clothing, and Health

Housing ranged from cabins and quarters to urban rooms, work sites, ships, and industrial compounds.

Food and clothing were distributed according to cost and labor requirements, though enslaved people supplemented rations through gardens, hunting, fishing, trade, and community exchange.

Overwork, infectious disease, dangerous childbirth, occupational injury, inadequate housing, and violence damaged health.

Punishment and Coercion

Whipping, beating, confinement, shackling, branding, sale, deprivation, and execution enforced slavery.

Patrols, police, traders, overseers, courts, and jailers connected private ownership claims to public coercion.

The threat of family separation and punishment shaped behavior even when violence was not occurring at every moment.

Sexual Violence and Reproductive Exploitation

Enslaved women and girls faced rape, coercion, harassment, forced relationships, and reproductive exploitation.

Children born to enslaved mothers increased an enslaver's claimed property regardless of paternity.

Law generally failed to recognize meaningful consent within ownership relationships.

Community and Culture

Enslaved communities created systems of mutual care, kinship, worship, food sharing, childcare, storytelling, music, mourning, celebration, and conflict resolution.

Captive Africans brought languages, agricultural knowledge, spiritual traditions, music, craftsmanship, foodways, and healing practices.

African American cultures emerged through preservation, adaptation, exchange, and innovation.

Religion

Enslaved people practiced Christianity, Islam, African-derived traditions, and blended religious forms.

Enslavers used selected religious teachings to demand obedience, while enslaved people emphasized Exodus, divine justice, spiritual equality, liberation, and judgment.

Secret worship, praise meetings, spirituals, prayer, and preaching created autonomous community space.

Literacy and Education

Enslaved people learned through family members, free Black communities, churches, workplaces, white teachers, and self-instruction.

Southern states increasingly restricted literacy instruction.

Literacy supported religion, communication, passes, escape, abolition, and claims to citizenship.

Everyday Resistance

Resistance included slowing work, breaking tools, feigning illness, hiding food, negotiating tasks, protecting family, maintaining independent worship, and creating unauthorized movement.

Not every act sought immediate abolition, but such actions defended autonomy and limited enslaver control.

Escape and Freedom Seeking

Enslaved people escaped temporarily and permanently to cities, free states, Canada, Mexico, Native communities, Spanish Florida, British lines, ships, and military camps.

Escape required knowledge, planning, supplies, disguise, forged passes, navigation, and assistance.

The person seeking freedom—not an assisting network—was the central actor.

Underground Railroad

The Underground Railroad was a changing network of freedom seekers and helpers rather than a centralized organization or literal railroad.

Free Black communities, sailors, religious groups, abolitionists, Indigenous people, and sympathetic white residents provided information, shelter, transportation, and legal aid.

Many escapes occurred without formal assistance.

Armed Resistance and Revolts

Enslaved people planned and conducted revolts, conspiracies, individual self-defense, wartime rebellion, and armed escape.

Major events included the Stono Rebellion of 1739, the German Coast uprising of 1811, Denmark Vesey's alleged 1822 conspiracy, and Nat Turner's revolt of 1831.

Authorities responded with executions, military force, stricter laws, surveillance, and restrictions on assembly and literacy.

Free Black Communities

Free Black people obtained freedom through birth, manumission, purchase, military service, court action, escape, treaty, or gradual-emancipation law.

They formed families, churches, schools, businesses, mutual-aid societies, newspapers, and abolitionist organizations.

Their freedom remained constrained by kidnapping, exclusion, violence, and discriminatory law.

Manumission and Self-Purchase

Manumission released a person from slavery through deed, will, court process, legislative act, purchase agreement, or military policy.

Some enslaved people accumulated money and negotiated purchase of themselves or relatives.

Enslavers could change prices, refuse agreements, confiscate savings, or sell a person elsewhere.

Abolitionism

Abolitionists sought to end slavery but differed over timing, method, racial equality, compensation, colonization, and political action.

Black abolitionists made the movement a demand for citizenship, safety, education, labor rights, and racial justice.

Print, petitions, lectures, churches, conventions, escape assistance, and political parties all contributed.

Abolitionist Leaders

Prominent Black abolitionists included Frederick Douglass, Harriet Tubman, Sojourner Truth, David Walker, Henry Highland Garnet, Maria Stewart, William Wells Brown, Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, and Martin Delany.

Prominent white abolitionists included William Lloyd Garrison, Angelina and Sarah Grimké, Theodore Dwight Weld, Lydia Maria Child, Lucretia Mott, and John Brown.

The movement depended on thousands of local organizers whose work was less fully recorded.

Slave Narratives

Published narratives by formerly enslaved people described labor, violence, family separation, literacy, religion, escape, and abolition.

Works by Frederick Douglass, Harriet Jacobs, William Wells Brown, Solomon Northup, and others reached national and international audiences.

The narratives remain essential first-person evidence and should be compared with other records.

Proslavery Ideology

Defenders described slavery as constitutional, biblical, civilizing, economically beneficial, or a “positive good.”

They promoted claims of Black inferiority and white paternalism while suppressing evidence of violence and resistance.

These arguments became central to Southern politics and Confederate nationalism.

Federal Government and Slavery

Federal institutions enforced fugitive-slave law, regulated territories, admitted states, counted enslaved people for representation, and governed the District of Columbia.

Presidents, Congress, courts, military officers, marshals, customs officials, and commissioners all made decisions affecting slavery.

The national government was not neutral even when ordinary slave law remained under state control.

Sectional Crisis

Repeated compromises attempted to balance slave and free states and regulate slavery in territories.

The Republican Party formed principally in opposition to expansion of slavery, though many members did not initially support immediate nationwide abolition or racial equality.

By 1860, the national party system had broken along sectional lines.

Slavery and Secession

Secession declarations, convention debates, commissioners, and Confederate leaders repeatedly identified slavery and racial hierarchy as central reasons for leaving the Union.

State sovereignty supplied the claimed legal method; protection of slave property and the slaveholding order supplied the principal institutional purpose.

Civil War and the Destruction of Slavery

The United States initially fought officially to suppress rebellion and preserve the Union.

Enslaved people transformed the conflict by escaping to Union lines, withdrawing labor, providing intelligence, and demanding freedom.

Federal policy moved through contraband decisions, confiscation, compensated emancipation, territorial abolition, military enlistment, and proclamation.

Contraband Policy

At Fort Monroe in May 1861, General Benjamin Butler refused to return three men whose labor had supported Confederate forces.

He described them as “contraband of war,” applying seizure principles to claimed property used in rebellion.

Thousands of freedom seekers entered Union lines and created camps and communities.

Wartime Emancipation Measures

  • the First and Second Confiscation Acts;
  • District of Columbia compensated emancipation on April 16, 1862;
  • abolition of slavery in federal territories in June 1862;
  • the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22, 1862;
  • the final Emancipation Proclamation on January 1, 1863;
  • state abolition in Maryland, Missouri, Tennessee, and West Virginia under differing processes; and
  • Black enlistment in the United States Army and Navy.

Emancipation Proclamation

The final proclamation declared enslaved people free in designated states and areas then in rebellion.

It did not apply to loyal slave states or specified Union-controlled regions and depended on military success for enforcement.

It authorized Black military service and made emancipation an official Union war policy.

Self-Emancipation

Enslaved people escaped plantations, guided armies, withheld labor, sheltered soldiers, carried information, protected relatives, and established new communities.

Their actions forced military and political authorities to decide whether the United States would return people to bondage or defend freedom.

Emancipation was therefore both government policy and action by enslaved people themselves.

Black Military Service

More than 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors served the United States during the Civil War.

Military service weakened Confederate labor, increased Union manpower, protected families, and gave freedom armed force.

Black troops faced unequal pay, segregation, restricted commissions, racism, and threatened murder or re-enslavement if captured.

Juneteenth

Major General Gordon Granger issued General Orders No. 3 at Galveston, Texas, on June 19, 1865.

Juneteenth commemorates enforcement and public announcement of emancipation in Texas rather than the single moment every enslaved person in the United States became free.

Thirteenth Amendment

Congress proposed the amendment on January 31, 1865, and required ratification was completed on December 6.

It abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States except as punishment for crime after conviction.

Section 2 authorized Congress to enforce abolition through appropriate legislation.

Post-Emancipation Coercion

Abolition ended legal ownership but did not distribute land, income, education, housing, or political protection automatically.

Black Codes, convict leasing, debt peonage, coercive labor contracts, sharecropping, and racial violence restricted practical freedom.

These systems were not identical to chattel slavery but drew upon its regional institutions and racial ideology.

Historical Records

  • census schedules and statistical reports;
  • plantation books and labor records;
  • wills, probate files, inventories, and estate divisions;
  • deeds, mortgages, insurance policies, and court judgments;
  • bills of sale, auction notices, and trader correspondence;
  • runaway advertisements and jail records;
  • shipping, customs, and illegal-trade cases;
  • freedom suits and fugitive-slave proceedings;
  • church, marriage, baptism, and burial records;
  • letters, diaries, newspapers, and business accounts;
  • slave narratives and oral histories;
  • military, contraband, and emancipation records;
  • Freedmen's Bureau and Reconstruction records; and
  • archaeology, landscapes, buildings, tools, and personal objects.

Federal Census Slave Schedules

The 1850 and 1860 censuses used separate schedules for “slave inhabitants.”

The schedules usually named the enslaver and listed enslaved people by age, sex, color, and other categories rather than by personal name.

Researchers must combine schedules with probate, plantation, military, Freedmen's Bureau, and post-emancipation census records to identify individuals.

Federal Writers' Project Narratives

During the 1930s, interviewers collected more than 2,300 first-person accounts from formerly enslaved people and hundreds of photographs.

Age, memory, interviewer race, local power, transcription, dialect conventions, and editing affected the accounts.

The narratives remain invaluable when interpreted critically and compared with contemporary records.

Historical Memory

Postwar narratives often minimized slavery by describing it as benevolent, destined to disappear, or unrelated to the Civil War.

Plantation nostalgia portrayed enslaved people as loyal and content while excluding sale, punishment, resistance, and sexual violence.

Black families, churches, historians, museums, archaeology, and descendant communities preserved competing memories.

Common Misconceptions

“Slavery in What Became the United States Began in 1619”

Indigenous enslavement and Spanish colonial slavery existed earlier; 1619 marks a foundational event in English Virginia.

“Slavery Existed Only in the South”

It existed throughout colonial North America and remained connected to Northern commerce after gradual abolition.

“Only Plantation Field Labor Counted as Slavery”

Enslaved people worked in households, cities, ports, trades, mines, factories, transportation, and public projects.

“The Constitution Had Nothing to Do With Slavery Because It Avoided the Word”

Representation, importation, fugitive service, and amendment provisions protected slaveholding interests.

“The Three-Fifths Clause Declared Enslaved People Three-Fifths Human”

It was an apportionment formula that increased slave-state political power without granting enslaved people voting rights.

“The 1808 Ban Ended the Slave Trade”

It prohibited legal foreign importation, while illegal trafficking and the domestic interstate trade continued.

“The Cotton Gin Reduced the Need for Enslaved Labor”

Efficient processing made short-staple cotton more profitable and encouraged expansion of plantation slavery.

“Most White Southerners Personally Owned Enslaved People”

Direct ownership belonged to a minority, though many more participated in or benefited from the racial and economic order.

“Enslaved People Had No Families Because Their Marriages Were Not Legally Protected”

They created durable family and kinship systems despite sale and denial of civil recognition.

“Resistance Meant Only Armed Revolt”

Resistance also included escape, work refusal, family protection, literacy, religion, negotiation, sabotage, and cultural independence.

“The Underground Railroad Freed Most Enslaved People”

It was a decentralized network, and most enslaved people remained in bondage until Civil War emancipation.

“The Civil War Was Unrelated to Slavery”

Slavery was the central cause of secession and became the central institution destroyed by Union victory.

“The Emancipation Proclamation Ended Slavery Everywhere on January 1, 1863”

It applied to designated rebellious areas and required military enforcement; nationwide abolition came through the Thirteenth Amendment.

“Juneteenth Was the Date Every Enslaved Person in the Nation Became Free”

It commemorates enforcement and announcement in Texas; legal and practical emancipation occurred through multiple processes.

“Slavery's Effects Ended in 1865”

Abolition ended legal ownership, but economic inequality, racial ideology, political exclusion, penal labor, and institutional legacies continued.

Key Distinctions

Enslaved person
A human being subjected to slavery, not a person whose humanity was defined by that legal condition.
Enslaver
A person or institution claiming ownership and coercive authority over enslaved people.
Chattel slavery
A system treating people as transferable property.
Indentured servitude
Contractual labor for a limited term, legally distinct from hereditary racial chattel slavery.
Slave code
A body of law regulating slavery and racial control.
Manumission
Legal release from slavery by the person claiming ownership or authorized government.
Self-emancipation
Action by enslaved people to secure freedom through escape, resistance, military lines, or other means.
Freedom seeker
A person escaping or attempting to escape slavery; historically called a fugitive slave by enslavers and law.
Domestic slave trade
Sale and transportation of enslaved people within the United States.
International slave trade
Transnational trafficking of captive people, especially across the Atlantic.
Hiring out
Temporary rental of an enslaved person's labor by the enslaver.
Abolition
Legal termination of slavery as an institution.
Emancipation
The process through which an enslaved person or population became free.
Contraband
A Civil War military classification applied to people escaping enslavers supporting rebellion.
Convict leasing
A post-emancipation penal labor system operating under the Thirteenth Amendment's punishment exception.
Debt peonage
Compulsory labor enforced through actual or alleged debt.
Sharecropping
A tenancy arrangement paid through a share of the crop, legally distinct from slavery despite possible coercion.

Why Slavery in the United States Matters

Slavery was central to the development of American agriculture, commerce, territorial expansion, law, political representation, and racial classification.

Enslaved people produced wealth while being denied wages, property rights, legal family protection, education, political participation, and control over their bodies.

Resistance by enslaved and free Black people prevented slavery from operating without challenge and made emancipation a historical process rather than a gift from one individual.

Conflict over slavery's expansion destroyed the national party system, produced secession, and caused the Civil War.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished legal slavery, but freedom required additional constitutional, political, educational, economic, and security institutions.

The institution's legacies remain visible in wealth inequality, landownership, labor systems, criminal justice, segregation, citizenship debates, historical memory, and the continuing recovery of enslaved people's names and lives.

  • Underground Railroad

    The decentralized movement of freedom seekers and people who assisted escape from slavery.

  • Emancipation Proclamation

    The Civil War measure declaring freedom in designated rebellious areas and authorizing Black military service.

  • Thirteenth Amendment

    The constitutional abolition of slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for crime.

  • Reconstruction

    The postwar struggle to define freedom, citizenship, labor, land, education, voting, and federal protection.

  • Convict Leasing

    The post-emancipation penal labor system connected to the Thirteenth Amendment's punishment exception.

  • Debt Peonage

    Compulsory labor enforced through actual or alleged debt after slavery's abolition.

Summary

Slavery in the United States was a hereditary racial chattel system that treated millions of African-descended people as property and compelled their labor through law and violence.

European colonizers also enslaved Indigenous people, and Spanish colonies used enslaved African labor before the 1619 arrival of captive Africans in English Virginia.

Colonial laws gradually made African bondage permanent, hereditary through the mother, racially defined, and unaffected by Christian baptism.

Slavery existed in every colonial region and supported agriculture, households, ports, trades, mining, manufacturing, transportation, and public works.

The Constitution protected slaveholding power through the Three-Fifths Clause, the Migration or Importation Clause, and the Fugitive Slave Clause while avoiding the word “slavery.”

Congress ended legal foreign importation in 1808, but illegal trafficking and a large domestic trade continued. Forced migration carried enslaved people from the Upper South to expanding cotton and sugar regions.

The enslaved population increased from 697,624 in 1790 to 3,953,760 in 1860.

Enslaved people formed families, communities, religions, cultural traditions, and systems of mutual care despite denial of legal autonomy.

Sale, family separation, sexual violence, punishment, patrols, surveillance, and restrictions on movement and literacy enforced the system.

Resistance included daily work opposition, education, religion, family protection, escape, maroon communities, litigation, abolitionism, armed revolt, self-emancipation, and military service.

Conflict over slavery's expansion produced repeated political crises, the Dred Scott decision, sectional party collapse, secession, and Civil War.

Enslaved people's flight to Union lines transformed the war. Confiscation, District of Columbia emancipation, territorial abolition, the Emancipation Proclamation, Black military service, and state action progressively destroyed slavery.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States in 1865 except as punishment for crime after conviction.

Abolition ended legal ownership but did not supply land, wealth, education, political protection, or equality automatically. Black Codes, convict leasing, debt peonage, sharecropping, segregation, and racial violence restricted freedom after slavery.

Slavery remains central to understanding American constitutional government, race, labor, wealth, criminal justice, territorial expansion, family history, Civil War memory, and the continuing recovery of enslaved people's individual names and experiences.
