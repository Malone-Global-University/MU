Chokeholds and Neck Restraints

High-risk restraint tactics involving pressure to the neck, throat, airway, or blood vessels—and the medical, constitutional, historical, and policy reasons many jurisdictions prohibit or restrict them.

Definition

Chokeholds and neck restraints are force techniques involving pressure on the neck or throat.

Some techniques are intended to obstruct breathing, while others are intended to reduce blood flow to the brain.

Because these mechanisms can overlap during a dynamic struggle, the technique's name does not establish what happened medically or legally.

Terminology

Common terms include chokehold, stranglehold, airway restraint, carotid restraint, vascular neck restraint, lateral vascular neck restraint, neck hold, sleeper hold, and headlock.

Statutes and policies define these words differently.

A fact sheet should quote the controlling definition when discussing a particular jurisdiction rather than assume one universal meaning.

Airway Chokehold

An airway chokehold applies pressure to the front of the neck, throat, trachea, or windpipe and interferes with breathing.

Airway obstruction may produce panic, coughing, inability to speak, loss of consciousness, airway injury, oxygen deprivation, and death.

Modern police policies widely treat intentional airway compression as a prohibited or deadly-force-level tactic.

Vascular Neck Restraint

A vascular neck restraint is intended to reduce blood flow through major vessels supplying the brain.

The Justice Department defines a carotid restraint as a technique restricting blood flow to the brain and causing temporary unconsciousness.

Loss of consciousness is not a harmless endpoint; it is evidence that normal brain perfusion has been disrupted.

Mixed and Unintended Mechanisms

A technique taught as vascular may shift during resistance, falling, poor positioning, multiple-officer restraint, or loss of control.

Pressure may reach the airway, jaw, cervical spine, nerves, or soft tissues.

Medical and legal analysis must examine actual body position and force rather than rely exclusively on the officer's intended technique.

Headlocks and Neck Contact

Not every hold involving the head or shoulders is intended to restrict breathing or blood flow.

A headlock, takedown, or stabilizing hold may nevertheless become a neck restraint if pressure compresses the airway or vascular structures.

Policies should define prohibited contact clearly enough to distinguish incidental contact from intentional dangerous compression.

Prone Restraint Is a Related but Distinct Tactic

Prone restraint places a person face down.

It is not automatically a chokehold, but pressure on the neck, back, chest, or torso may impair breathing, especially after a struggle or when the person is handcuffed.

George Floyd's death involved prolonged prone restraint and pressure across the neck, back, and shoulder rather than a conventional arm chokehold.

Why the Neck Is High Risk

The neck contains the airway, carotid arteries, jugular veins, cervical spine, nerves, soft tissue, and structures controlling swallowing and speech.

Relatively small changes in force or position can affect breathing, blood flow, consciousness, or neurological function.

External injury may appear limited even when internal damage is severe.

Blood Flow to the Brain

The carotid arteries are major blood vessels supplying oxygen-rich blood to the brain and head.

Reducing cerebral blood flow can cause lightheadedness, altered awareness, unconsciousness, seizure-like movement, brain injury, or stroke.

A person's medical vulnerability may be unknown to officers at the scene.

Airway Compression

Pressure to the throat can narrow or close the airway and injure the larynx, trachea, hyoid region, or surrounding tissues.

Swelling can develop after pressure ends and may worsen later.

The ability to speak does not conclusively establish that breathing is adequate or that dangerous compression did not occur.

Venous and Cardiovascular Effects

Neck compression may affect both arterial inflow and venous return from the brain.

Pressure can also trigger cardiovascular responses, interact with underlying disease, or contribute to loss of consciousness and cardiac arrest.

Exact causal mechanisms may be disputed in individual deaths and require medical examination.

Arterial Injury and Stroke

Pressure or rapid movement can injure neck arteries, including through dissection or damage to an existing plaque.

Such injury can interfere with blood flow or produce clot formation and stroke.

Neurological symptoms may appear immediately or after an interval.

Medical Vulnerability

Risk may be increased by cardiovascular disease, carotid disease, respiratory illness, intoxication, obesity, age, neck anatomy, prior injury, exhaustion, or prolonged struggle.

Officers usually cannot know every vulnerability before applying force.

That uncertainty supports treating intentional neck compression as a high-risk tactic.

Loss of Consciousness

Unconsciousness is a medical emergency requiring immediate release of pressure, assessment, airway protection, and appropriate emergency response.

An unconscious person cannot communicate worsening distress and may vomit, aspirate, suffer brain injury, or enter cardiac arrest.

A restraint should never be maintained merely because unconsciousness has made the person easier to control.

Warning Signs of Medical Crisis

  • statements that breathing is difficult or impossible;
  • gasping, abnormal sounds, weak speech, or silence after distress;
  • loss of muscle tone or sudden collapse;
  • confusion, seizure-like movement, or altered consciousness;
  • blue, gray, or unusually pale skin;
  • vomiting or inability to protect the airway;
  • absence of normal breathing or pulse; and
  • new neurological symptoms after release.

Medical Response

Officers should release dangerous pressure, control the person through safer means, assess responsiveness and breathing, summon emergency medical services, and provide care consistent with training.

A person who lost consciousness, experienced neck compression, or reports persistent symptoms may require medical evaluation even after appearing to recover.

Reports should document the technique, duration, loss of consciousness, complaints, visible injury, medical care, and transport.

Why Many Policies Treat These Holds as Deadly Force

Chokeholds and carotid restraints can cause death or serious bodily injury rapidly.

Many jurisdictions therefore prohibit them unless the officer would otherwise be legally justified in using deadly force.

This approach does not mean the technique is always fatal; it means the risk is serious enough to require the highest legal justification.

Fourth Amendment Standard

Force during a stop or arrest is generally governed by the Fourth Amendment.

Under Graham v. Connor, courts consider objective reasonableness under the totality of circumstances.

Important factors include the suspected offense, immediate threat, resistance, flight, warnings, restraint status, duration, medical distress, and whether pressure continued after control was achieved.

Tennessee v. Garner

The Supreme Court's 1985 deadly-force decision rejected force used merely to prevent every fleeing felony suspect from escaping.

Deadly force requires a serious threat justification.

Where a jurisdiction classifies a chokehold or carotid restraint as deadly force, the Garner framework becomes particularly important.

Graham v. Connor

Graham v. Connor requires courts to assess force from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene while considering the complete circumstances.

The decision does not authorize automatic deference to any claimed fear.

Neck pressure must be justified by the actual threat and resistance and must end when the justification ends.

Barnes v. Felix

In 2025, the Supreme Court rejected a rule limiting excessive-force review to only the final moment of threat.

Courts must consider all relevant circumstances leading to the force.

For neck-restraint cases, this can include the reason for the stop, earlier tactics, opportunities to de-escalate, the person's restraint status, and how the officer created or responded to danger.

Pretrial Detainees

The Fourteenth Amendment governs force against people detained before conviction.

Under Kingsley v. Hendrickson, the question is whether force was objectively unreasonable.

Custody, handcuffing, number of officers, duration, medical danger, and the detainee's actual behavior are central.

Convicted Prisoners

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment.

Force used against a convicted prisoner is evaluated partly by whether it was applied in a good-faith effort to maintain or restore discipline or maliciously and sadistically to cause harm.

A neck restraint used as punishment or after control is established can violate constitutional and correctional standards.

City of Los Angeles v. Lyons

Adolph Lyons alleged that Los Angeles police applied a chokehold during a traffic stop and rendered him unconscious.

He sought damages and an injunction against the city's chokehold practices.

In 1983, the Supreme Court held that his past injury did not establish a sufficiently real and immediate likelihood that he personally would be choked again, so he lacked standing for the requested injunction.

The decision became a major barrier to prospective federal relief against recurring police practices.

The Los Angeles Chokehold Controversy

The Lyons record arose during intense controversy over LAPD control holds and deaths following their use.

The Supreme Court record noted that sixteen people had died following LAPD chokeholds since 1975 and that twelve were Black men.

The case illustrates how standing rules can prevent a court from reaching the merits of a requested systemic injunction even when a damages claim for past injury remains.

Lombardo v. City of St. Louis

The Supreme Court's 2021 per curiam decision concerned a handcuffed and leg-shackled pretrial detainee held prone by multiple officers.

The Court vacated the lower ruling because restraint cannot be treated as reasonable without careful attention to type, intensity, duration, handcuffing, resistance, and other circumstances.

The case concerns prone restraint rather than a conventional chokehold but is important to neck-and-breathing risk analysis.

Qualified Immunity

Officers sued personally may assert qualified immunity.

A plaintiff must generally show both a constitutional violation and that existing law clearly established the violation under sufficiently similar circumstances.

A court may grant immunity without declaring the tactic lawful, which makes the difference between merits and immunity especially important.

Civil Liability Under Section 1983

42 U.S.C. § 1983 provides the principal federal civil remedy against state and local officials who violate federal rights under color of law.

A plaintiff may seek damages and, when standing and other requirements are met, declaratory or injunctive relief.

Medical evidence, video, policy, training, duration, officer statements, and expert testimony commonly shape neck-restraint litigation.

Municipal Liability

A city does not receive qualified immunity, but it is not automatically liable for every officer's conduct.

Under Monell, a plaintiff must connect the violation to policy, custom, authorized decision, or qualifying failure to train, supervise, investigate, or discipline.

Repeated neck-restraint incidents, inaccurate reports, weak review, and inconsistent discipline can support systemic analysis.

Federal Criminal Civil-Rights Law

18 U.S.C. § 242 prohibits willful deprivation of federal rights under color of law.

Federal prosecution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the officer acted willfully.

Qualified immunity is not a defense to criminal prosecution.

Pattern-or-Practice Enforcement

The Justice Department may investigate whether an agency has a pattern or practice of unconstitutional force.

Investigations can examine neck-restraint incidents, policy definitions, training, reporting, supervision, medical care, racial disparity, discipline, and complaint handling.

DOJ findings in Minneapolis and Memphis documented unjustified or prohibited neck restraints.

Justice Department Definitions

The DOJ Manual defines a chokehold as pressure to the throat or windpipe restricting breathing.

It defines a carotid restraint as a technique restricting blood flow to the brain and causing temporary unconsciousness.

These definitions are useful but do not control every state statute or local policy.

Current Justice Department Policy

The current public DOJ Manual page states that covered Justice Department officers may not use chokeholds or carotid restraints unless deadly force is authorized.

The restriction applies because the department describes the techniques as inherently dangerous.

Separate component policies and training must implement the department-wide rule.

Executive Order 14074

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. issued Executive Order 14074 in 2022, directing federal agencies and grant programs toward use-of-force, chokehold, accreditation, data, and accountability standards.

President Donald J. Trump revoked that order through Executive Order 14148 on January 20, 2025.

The revocation ended the executive order's force but did not automatically repeal state laws or every separate agency policy adopted before or after it.

No Complete Nationwide Statutory Ban

Federal constitutional law can make a particular chokehold unreasonable, and federal criminal law can punish willful rights violations.

Those rules are not the same as a single federal statute prohibiting every chokehold by every state and local officer.

As of August 1, 2026, state law and agency policy remained central for most local policing.

George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025

H.R. 5361 proposes to define chokeholds and carotid holds broadly and treat maneuvers restricting blood or oxygen flow as deadly force within its framework.

It would condition eligibility for specified Byrne and COPS grants on state or local laws prohibiting the techniques.

It also proposes an Eric Garner Excessive Use of Force Prevention Act provision addressing federal criminal civil-rights law.

The bill remained introduced rather than enacted as of August 1, 2026.

State-Law Models

  • Complete prohibition: no chokehold or vascular neck restraint in ordinary police duties.
  • Deadly-force exception: prohibited unless deadly force is legally justified.
  • Criminal-result model: a separate offense applies when a police chokehold causes serious injury or death.
  • Funding or certification model: policy compliance affects grants, accreditation, licensing, or training.
  • Local-policy model: departments restrict the tactic even when state law is silent.

California

California Assembly Bill 1196 prohibited law-enforcement agencies from authorizing carotid restraints.

The law also addressed techniques creating substantial risk of positional asphyxia.

California's approach separates a statewide policy prohibition from the constitutional question arising in an individual case.

Washington

Washington law prohibits peace officers and specified correctional personnel from using chokeholds or neck restraints in the course of their duties.

The statute distinguishes airway pressure from vascular restriction but prohibits both.

Agency policies must remain consistent with the statewide rule.

Minnesota

Minnesota law defines chokehold to include pressure restricting breathing and pressure to the sides of the neck intended to stop carotid blood flow.

A peace officer may not use the listed restraints unless state law authorizes deadly force to protect the officer or another from death or great bodily harm.

The law was enacted after George Floyd's murder in Minneapolis.

Illinois

Illinois prohibits a chokehold or restraint above the shoulders with risk of asphyxiation unless deadly force is justified.

The law also bars lesser neck contact used to prevent destruction of evidence by ingestion.

Its definition addresses airway pressure and restraint interfering with breathing after the person no longer poses a threat.

New York

New York's Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act created the offense of aggravated strangulation for a police or peace officer who uses specified breathing or blood-circulation obstruction and causes serious physical injury or death.

The statute is a criminal-result model rather than a complete rule governing every neck contact.

NYPD policy had prohibited chokeholds long before the state statute.

Texas Model Policy Requirement

Texas model-policy standards require agencies to prohibit chokeholds, carotid artery holds, or similar neck restraints unless an officer reasonably believes the restraint is necessary to prevent death or serious bodily injury.

The model requires the officer to stop once control is achieved and begin an appropriate medical response when needed.

Local implementation and state criminal law remain separate questions.

Policy Ban Versus Legal Ban

An agency can prohibit a tactic even when state law does not create a separate crime.

Violating policy can lead to discipline, termination, decertification, or evidence in civil litigation.

A policy violation does not automatically prove a constitutional violation, and policy compliance does not automatically establish constitutional reasonableness.

Training

Training historically distinguished airway chokeholds from carotid or vascular restraints.

Some agencies trained vascular restraints as intermediate force, while others classified them as deadly force or banned them entirely.

A prohibition must be reflected in recruit training, in-service training, defensive-tactics instruction, scenarios, supervision, and reporting.

Technique Names and Branding

Programs may use names such as lateral vascular neck restraint, vascular neck restraint, carotid control hold, or sleeper hold.

A branded name does not remove the technique from statutory definitions covering pressure to the neck or restriction of blood flow.

Courts and investigators examine what the officer did rather than the training label alone.

Reporting Requirements

Every application or attempted application of a neck restraint should be documented under a strong accountability system.

Reports should identify the legal objective, threat, resistance, body position, duration, loss of consciousness, medical complaints, warnings, witnesses, camera evidence, and aftercare.

Failure to classify the event accurately can conceal patterns and defeat early intervention.

Supervisory Review

Supervisors should respond to serious neck-restraint incidents, preserve evidence, arrange medical care, identify all participating officers, and verify reports against video and medical records.

Review should determine whether the technique was authorized, whether force stopped promptly, whether intervention occurred, and whether medical aid was adequate.

A serious injury or death may require an external criminal investigation.

Body-Worn and Civilian Video

Video can reveal hand placement, body position, duration, statements, loss of consciousness, and whether officers continued force after control.

Camera angle may not show the amount or exact location of pressure.

Medical and forensic evidence remain necessary even when video appears clear.

Duty to Intervene

Officers may have a duty to stop another officer's unlawful neck pressure when they have a realistic opportunity.

Intervention can include verbal direction, physically removing pressure, repositioning the person, summoning a supervisor, and obtaining medical aid.

Standing nearby without acting can produce civil, criminal, disciplinary, or ethical consequences depending on the facts.

Release and Recovery Position

Once the person is controlled and the threat ends, continued neck or torso compression loses justification.

Policies commonly direct officers to move a restrained person from prone positioning into a side recovery or seated position when safe.

Repositioning does not replace medical evaluation after loss of consciousness or significant neck pressure.

Evidence-Ingestion Incidents

Officers may encounter people attempting to swallow drugs or other evidence.

Neck pressure can create airway obstruction, aspiration, internal injury, or death and is prohibited for this purpose in several jurisdictions.

Emergency medical care and lawful evidence-preservation methods should replace dangerous throat compression.

Behavioral and Medical Crises

People in mental-health crisis, intoxication, delirium, panic, or neurological distress may struggle, pull away, or fail to follow commands.

Neck restraint can worsen oxygen demand, fear, confusion, and physiological stress.

Crisis response, distance, coordinated control, and medical assistance can reduce reliance on high-risk restraint.

Children and Adolescents

Children have smaller airways, different anatomy, and developmental differences affecting communication and compliance.

Intentional neck compression against a child presents exceptional risk and requires the highest legal and policy scrutiny.

DOJ findings have documented unjustified neck pressure against youth in police encounters.

Jails, Prisons, and Detention Facilities

Neck restraints can occur during cell extractions, fights, transport, medical crises, and efforts to enforce orders.

Custody increases government responsibility because detained people cannot leave and depend on staff for medical care.

The governing constitutional standard differs for pretrial detainees and convicted prisoners.

Anthony Baez

NYPD officer Francis Livoti placed Anthony Baez in a prohibited chokehold in the Bronx in 1994.

Baez died, and Livoti was later convicted in federal court of violating his civil rights.

The case demonstrated that a departmental ban did not ensure compliance or accountability.

Eric Garner

NYPD officer Daniel Pantaleo wrapped an arm around Eric Garner's neck during a July 17, 2014 arrest encounter.

Garner died after the chokehold, takedown, prone restraint, and related physical stress.

The medical examiner classified the death as homicide, while federal prosecutors concluded they could not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Pantaleo acted willfully under Section 242.

The case made “I can't breathe” a central protest phrase and led to New York's later anti-chokehold statute.

George Floyd

Derek Chauvin held his knee across George Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder while Floyd was handcuffed and prone.

Chauvin continued after Floyd stopped resisting, became unconscious, and lost a pulse.

Chauvin admitted in federal court that the restraint impaired Floyd's ability to obtain and maintain sufficient oxygen and that the force was unreasonable.

The incident was not a conventional arm chokehold but is central to the history of neck pressure and prone-restraint reform.

Minneapolis Police Department

Before George Floyd's murder, Minneapolis policy distinguished conscious and unconscious neck restraints and permitted them under specified resistance levels.

The city later prohibited all neck restraints.

The Justice Department's 2023 findings documented earlier unjustified neck-restraint incidents and failures involving reporting, supervision, and First Amendment retaliation.

Memphis Police Department Findings

The Justice Department's 2024 Memphis findings described chokeholds, strangleholds, and other maneuvers limiting air or blood flow as neck restraints capable of causing brain injury, stroke, cognitive impairment, or death.

The report found that officers used unlawful restraints despite Tennessee law and department policy.

The findings demonstrate the gap that can exist between written restrictions and field practice.

African American Historical Significance

Black people have been disproportionately represented among prominent chokehold deaths, complaints, protests, and civil-rights litigation.

The issue is connected to racial profiling, low-level enforcement, unequal perceptions of threat, police protection of segregated public order, and unequal access to legal remedies.

Historical analysis must examine both individual decisions and institutional patterns.

Low-Level Enforcement

Several major chokehold controversies began with traffic, street-vending, disorder, protest, or other relatively low-level encounters.

The severity of the suspected offense matters because extreme restraint may be disproportionate to the governmental objective.

A minor offense does not eliminate officer-safety concerns, but it weighs against unnecessary deadly-force-level tactics.

Speech and Retaliation

Officers may not use neck restraints to punish profanity, criticism, recording, or perceived disrespect.

Protected speech can coexist with lawful arrest duties, but force must respond to actual threat or resistance rather than contempt.

DOJ investigations have documented neck restraint used after verbal criticism of police.

Data and Measurement

National force databases do not consistently isolate chokeholds, carotid restraints, neck pressure, and prone restraint as separate categories.

Agencies may classify the same tactic as physical control, neck restraint, takedown, deadly force, or other force.

Reliable measurement requires standardized definitions, mandatory reporting, medical outcomes, demographic data, and public access.

Discipline and Decertification

Using a prohibited neck restraint can lead to retraining, suspension, termination, or loss of officer certification.

Disciplinary systems must distinguish accidental contact from intentional application while preserving evidence and evaluating credibility.

Arbitration, civil-service rules, contracts, and state law can affect the final sanction.

State Criminal Liability

State homicide, assault, strangulation, official-misconduct, reckless-endangerment, or evidence-tampering laws may apply.

Some states created a police-specific strangulation offense or expressly limited chokeholds through their use-of-force statutes.

Criminal conviction requires proof of each statutory element beyond a reasonable doubt.

Civilian Oversight

Civilian review bodies can examine complaints, policy, video, medical evidence, training, and discipline involving neck restraints.

Effective oversight requires access to evidence, qualified investigators, public reporting, and authority to recommend or impose meaningful action.

A policy ban without independent review may remain largely symbolic.

Reform Approaches

  • complete prohibition of airway and vascular neck restraints;
  • classification as deadly force with a deadly-threat exception;
  • clear definitions covering knees, arms, objects, and body weight;
  • prohibition on neck pressure to prevent evidence ingestion;
  • mandatory intervention and immediate pressure release;
  • medical evaluation after application or loss of consciousness;
  • mandatory supervisor and external review;
  • body-camera preservation and public reporting;
  • officer certification consequences;
  • scenario training in safer control and de-escalation;
  • standardized state reporting; and
  • civil and criminal remedies for violations.

Evaluating a Ban

A formal ban should be evaluated by incident reports, body-camera review, complaints, medical outcomes, disciplinary action, and whether officers substitute other dangerous techniques.

Training must explain prohibited pressure and safer alternatives.

A successful policy changes field behavior rather than merely changing terminology in reports.

Research and Interpretation

A reliable account should distinguish intended technique, actual pressure, body position, duration, medical mechanism, legal standard, and final adjudication.

Terms used by witnesses, police reports, autopsies, prosecutors, and courts may differ.

A finding that criminal willfulness was not proven does not establish that the force was lawful, medically harmless, or consistent with policy.

Common Misconceptions

“Every Neck Restraint Is the Same Technique”

Airway chokeholds, vascular restraints, headlocks, prone pressure, and incidental neck contact differ, although real-world mechanisms can overlap.

“A Vascular Restraint Does Not Affect Breathing”

A technique intended to compress blood vessels may shift, compress other structures, or combine with body position and struggle to impair breathing.

“Temporary Unconsciousness Is Harmless”

Loss of consciousness reflects disruption of normal brain blood flow or oxygenation and requires immediate medical attention.

“If a Person Can Speak, the Person Can Breathe Adequately”

Speech does not prove that ventilation is normal or that dangerous airway, vascular, or positional compression is absent.

“No Visible Neck Injury Means No Serious Harm”

Internal airway, vascular, neurological, or soft-tissue injury may exist with limited external marks.

“A Departmental Ban Makes Further Use Impossible”

Anthony Baez and Eric Garner died after NYPD had prohibited chokeholds. Training, supervision, intervention, reporting, and discipline determine whether a ban is effective.

“Every Chokehold Is Automatically a Federal Crime”

Federal prosecution under Section 242 requires proof of a willful constitutional violation beyond a reasonable doubt.

“A Policy Violation Automatically Proves Excessive Force”

Policy and constitutional law are distinct, although policy evidence may be highly relevant.

“Policy Compliance Automatically Proves the Force Was Constitutional”

An agency cannot authorize force that violates the Constitution.

“George Floyd Was Killed by a Traditional Arm Chokehold”

His death involved prolonged knee and body-weight pressure across the neck, back, and shoulder while he was prone and handcuffed.

“City of Los Angeles v. Lyons Approved Police Chokeholds”

The Supreme Court decided standing for prospective injunctive relief, not that every chokehold was constitutional.

“Executive Order 14074 Is Still in Force”

Executive Order 14148 revoked it in January 2025. Separate DOJ policy, state law, and local rules must be examined independently.

“Revoking the Executive Order Made Chokeholds Legal Everywhere”

Constitutional law, criminal law, department policy, and state or local prohibitions continued to apply.

“H.R. 5361 Is Current Federal Law”

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025 remained a proposed bill rather than enacted law as of August 1, 2026.

“Every State Uses the Same Ban”

States differ on definitions, exceptions, criminal penalties, correctional coverage, and enforcement.

Key Distinctions

Airway chokehold
Pressure to the throat or windpipe that restricts breathing.
Vascular neck restraint
Pressure intended to restrict blood flow to the brain.
Carotid restraint
A common term for a vascular neck restraint, though legal definitions vary.
Stranglehold
A general term often used for neck compression affecting breathing or blood circulation.
Headlock
A hold controlling the head or upper body that may or may not compress the neck.
Prone restraint
Holding a person face down; distinct from a chokehold but capable of combining with neck or torso compression.
Positional asphyxia
Breathing impairment associated with body position and restraint.
Deadly force
Force presenting a substantial risk of death or serious bodily harm.
Policy prohibition
An agency employment rule that may be stricter than constitutional or statutory law.
Statutory prohibition
A rule enacted by a legislature and enforceable according to its terms.
Constitutional excessive force
Force that is objectively unreasonable under the governing constitutional standard.
Criminal civil-rights violation
A willful deprivation of federal rights under color of law proven beyond a reasonable doubt.
Loss of consciousness
A medical emergency showing serious disruption of normal neurological function.
Recovery position
A side or seated position intended to reduce breathing risk after control, when medically and tactically appropriate.

Historical and Continuing Significance

Chokeholds and neck restraints place government force against one of the human body's most vulnerable regions.

Their history demonstrates how a tactic presented as temporary control can produce unconsciousness, catastrophic injury, or death within a low-level encounter.

The deaths of Anthony Baez, Eric Garner, George Floyd, and others made neck pressure central to African American protest against police brutality and unequal enforcement.

City of Los Angeles v. Lyons also made chokeholds important to constitutional procedure by showing how standing doctrine can limit systemic injunctions.

Modern reform increasingly treats airway and vascular restraints as prohibited or deadly-force-level tactics, but legal rules remain divided among federal policy, constitutional law, state statutes, local policy, criminal law, and civil remedies.

Effective prevention requires more than relabeling a technique. It requires clear definitions, safer tactics, intervention, medical care, accurate reporting, external review, discipline, and public accountability.

Summary

Chokeholds and neck restraints are high-risk force techniques involving pressure to the airway, throat, neck, or blood vessels supplying the brain.

Airway chokeholds restrict breathing. Vascular or carotid restraints are intended to reduce cerebral blood flow and can produce unconsciousness. In real encounters, the mechanisms can overlap, and a technique's name does not determine its actual medical effect.

Risks include oxygen deprivation, airway injury, unconsciousness, aspiration, arterial damage, stroke, brain injury, cardiac arrest, and death. External marks may be limited despite severe internal harm.

Constitutional analysis depends on the person's threat and resistance, the reason for police contact, restraint status, duration, medical distress, and whether pressure continued after control. City of Los Angeles v. Lyons concerns standing for systemic injunctive relief, while Graham, Garner, Kingsley, and Lombardo supply important force standards.

The Justice Department's published manual prohibits covered officers from using chokeholds or carotid restraints unless deadly force is authorized. Executive Order 14074 was revoked in January 2025, but separate agency policies and state laws continue independently.

States differ: some prohibit all airway and vascular neck restraints, while others permit them only when deadly force is justified or impose criminal penalties when serious injury or death results.

Anthony Baez, Eric Garner, and George Floyd became central to the modern history of neck-restraint reform. Their deaths demonstrated that a policy prohibition alone is insufficient without training, intervention, medical response, accurate reporting, supervision, and accountability.

H.R. 5361 proposes broader federal chokehold and carotid-hold restrictions but remained unenacted as of August 1, 2026.
