Segregation

The legally, institutionally, economically, and socially enforced separation of racial groups that restricted African American access to schools, housing, transportation, employment, public accommodations, political power, and other areas of American life.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Racial classification system, legal regime, institutional practice, spatial arrangement, and social order.
Primary United States meaning
The separation of people by race, especially the enforced separation of African Americans from white Americans.
Principal historical period
Segregation existed before the Civil War, but its most comprehensive post-emancipation form developed after Reconstruction and became identified with the Jim Crow era.
Principal geographic reach
Nationwide, although laws, enforcement methods, institutions, and racial classifications varied by state, region, city, and federal policy.
Principal targets
African Americans were the principal targets of the Black-white segregation system. Native Americans, Asian Americans, Mexican Americans, Puerto Ricans, and other groups also faced racial separation under different laws and local practices.
Principal forms
Legal segregation, administrative segregation, residential segregation, educational segregation, employment segregation, social segregation, and separation enforced by private institutions or violence.
Common term for legal form
De jure segregation: separation required, authorized, or intentionally created through law or official government action.
Common term for nonstatutory form
De facto segregation: separation existing without an explicit segregation statute, often resulting from private discrimination, housing markets, institutional decisions, economic inequality, demographic patterns, or government policies written in formally race-neutral terms.
Principal ideology
White supremacy: the belief and institutional order that assigned greater status, power, resources, and public authority to white people.
Principal legal doctrine
“Separate but equal,” upheld in Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896 and rejected in public education by Brown v. Board of Education in 1954.
Commonly segregated areas
Schools, colleges, neighborhoods, transportation, restaurants, hotels, theaters, parks, hospitals, workplaces, unions, military units, government offices, prisons, cemeteries, libraries, beaches, swimming pools, and other public or private facilities.
Primary enforcement methods
Statutes, ordinances, administrative rules, contracts, property covenants, licensing, police action, school assignments, transportation regulations, employment practices, intimidation, economic retaliation, and racial violence.
Political relationship
Segregation operated alongside disfranchisement, unequal taxation, exclusion from juries and public office, racial terror, and suppression of Black political organization.
Economic relationship
Segregation limited access to property, credit, education, employment, markets, transportation, public services, and wealth-building opportunities.
Major constitutional basis for challenge
The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, together with federal power under the Thirteenth and Fifteenth Amendments and the Commerce Clause.
Major federal turning points
Executive Orders 9980 and 9981, Brown v. Board of Education, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the Fair Housing Act of 1968.
Legal status
Government-mandated racial segregation is unconstitutional, and federal civil-rights laws prohibit racial discrimination in major areas including public accommodations, employment, education, voting, and housing.
Important limitation
The invalidation of segregation laws did not automatically eliminate residential separation, unequal institutions, discriminatory practices, or resistance to integration.
Historical significance
Segregation converted racial hierarchy into a durable system governing access to space, institutions, opportunity, citizenship, and public resources after slavery's abolition.

What Was Segregation?

Segregation was the separation of people into different spaces, institutions, services, or legal categories according to race.

In United States history, the term most commonly refers to the system that separated African Americans from white Americans and assigned Black people inferior access to public institutions, private markets, political authority, and social resources.

Segregation could be stated directly in law, created through administrative decisions, imposed through private contracts, maintained through business practices, or enforced by intimidation and violence.

Physical separation was only one part of the system. Segregation also governed who could enter a profession, purchase property, receive public services, attend a school, use transportation, obtain medical care, participate in politics, or live in a particular community.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet focuses primarily on Black-white racial segregation in the United States because that system was central to African American history and the development of Jim Crow.

Other racial and ethnic groups also experienced separation, exclusion, confinement, and unequal institutions. Native American reservation and boarding-school policies, Asian exclusion and school segregation, and the segregation of Mexican American and Puerto Rican communities developed through related but distinct legal histories.

Those histories should not be treated as identical. Each requires attention to its own laws, citizenship rules, labor systems, territorial history, racial classifications, and institutional structures.

Racial Classification

Segregation depended on government and private institutions assigning people to racial categories.

Laws used terms such as “white,” “colored,” “Negro,” “Indian,” “Mongolian,” or other classifications that changed across time and jurisdiction.

Officials, employers, school boards, courts, conductors, landlords, registrars, and business owners often decided who belonged in each category.

Classification disputes exposed the instability of race as a legal concept while demonstrating the power attached to racial labels.

The purpose of classification was not merely descriptive. It determined access, status, movement, family recognition, property rights, schooling, and public treatment.

Historical Origins

Racial separation in North America developed before the United States existed.

Colonial laws separated enslaved Africans, free Black people, Native people, and Europeans through differences in legal status, labor obligations, movement, testimony, marriage, landholding, militia service, worship, and punishment.

Slavery was more than segregation because it treated people as property and compelled their labor. Nevertheless, slavery created racial classifications, policing systems, residential arrangements, educational restrictions, and social practices later adapted to segregation.

Northern colonies and states also used segregated schools, churches, neighborhoods, transportation, cemeteries, and public institutions before the Civil War.

Slavery and Segregation

Slavery and segregation were related but distinct systems.

Slavery claimed ownership of human beings, controlled their labor and movement, and made status hereditary. Segregation separated people by race within a formally free society.

Emancipation ended legal ownership, but it did not eliminate racial hierarchy, accumulated wealth differences, white control of government, or beliefs developed to justify slavery.

Segregation became one of the principal methods through which white institutions restricted the freedom and citizenship of African Americans after slavery.

Reconstruction and the Contest Over Equal Citizenship

Reconstruction created constitutional and statutory foundations for freedom and national citizenship.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, the Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and equal protection, and the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting.

Congress enacted civil-rights laws, established the Freedmen's Bureau, imposed military Reconstruction, and required former Confederate states to create new governments.

African Americans voted, held public office, established schools, formed political organizations, purchased property, and demanded equal access to public life.

White resistance used political organization, economic pressure, fraud, racial terror, and violence to destroy Reconstruction governments and recover control of Southern institutions.

Black Codes and Early Postwar Separation

Black Codes enacted after the Civil War attempted to regulate freedpeople's labor, movement, residence, contracts, family life, weapons, and access to courts.

Some laws explicitly separated Black people; others created special rules applying only to freedpeople.

Federal civil-rights legislation and Reconstruction governments invalidated or displaced many Black Code provisions.

The underlying objective of maintaining white control survived and later appeared in disfranchisement, segregated facilities, vagrancy enforcement, labor coercion, and Jim Crow laws.

Collapse of Reconstruction

Federal commitment to Reconstruction weakened during the 1870s.

Supreme Court decisions narrowed federal protections, white Democratic “Redeemer” governments regained control across the South, and federal troops were withdrawn from remaining political disputes in 1877.

State and local officials dismantled many interracial institutions and withdrew protection from Black voters and officeholders.

Segregation did not appear in one act or one year. It expanded through a succession of laws, court decisions, customs, and institutional changes during the late nineteenth century.

Segregation and Jim Crow

Jim Crow was the broader system of laws, institutions, customs, political exclusion, economic control, and racial violence that maintained white supremacy after Reconstruction.

Segregation was one of Jim Crow's principal operating mechanisms.

Jim Crow included more than separate schools, seats, and entrances. It also included disfranchisement, exclusion from juries, unequal policing, labor coercion, racial terror, discriminatory taxation, and restrictions on public authority.

Not every segregated practice was created by a law formally called a Jim Crow law, and racial segregation existed outside the South.

De Jure Segregation

De jure segregation was racial separation created, required, authorized, or intentionally maintained through government action.

Examples included statutes requiring separate railroad cars, school-board assignments based on race, municipal ordinances separating neighborhoods, rules designating separate parks, and state licensing requirements that imposed racial separation on businesses.

Official segregation could also exist without a statute using the word “segregation.” A school board, housing authority, police department, or other agency could create separate systems through intentional administrative decisions.

Constitutional remedies generally required proof of government responsibility or purposeful state action.

De Facto Segregation

De facto segregation describes racial separation not directly required by an explicit segregation law.

It can arise through private discrimination, unequal income and wealth, housing costs, real-estate steering, lending practices, employer decisions, social pressure, violence, migration, or demographic patterns.

The distinction between de jure and de facto segregation can be difficult because government policies concerning zoning, highways, public housing, school boundaries, credit, and municipal services may contribute to separation without openly naming race.

A formally race-neutral rule may therefore preserve or intensify segregation even when it does not classify individuals explicitly by race.

How Segregation Worked

Segregation divided institutions, restricted movement, and assigned unequal access to resources.

Government created racial rules; private institutions adopted racial policies; markets distributed property and employment unequally; and police, courts, employers, lenders, landlords, and white citizens enforced boundaries.

Separate facilities commonly received unequal funding, equipment, staffing, maintenance, transportation, and political attention.

Even when Black institutions developed strength and independence, their creation often reflected exclusion from institutions supported by greater public or private resources.

Segregation also made inequality appear natural by embedding racial separation in everyday routines.

Law, Custom, and Private Action

Legal rules and private customs reinforced one another.

A business might segregate because a statute required it, because licensing officials expected it, because customers demanded it, because police enforced local custom, or because the owner independently chose exclusion.

State-action doctrine became important because the Fourteenth Amendment directly regulates government conduct rather than every private decision.

Courts therefore examined whether government had required, encouraged, enforced, approved, subsidized, or become sufficiently involved in a discriminatory practice.

Congress later relied on additional constitutional powers, including regulation of interstate commerce, to prohibit discrimination in private businesses serving the public.

Geographic Reach

The South developed the most comprehensive statutory system of Black-white segregation after Reconstruction.

Southern states and municipalities separated schools, transportation, public accommodations, hospitals, prisons, parks, and other facilities through law and official policy.

Northern and Western states generally had fewer comprehensive segregation statutes, but racial separation remained widespread through housing discrimination, school boundaries, employment exclusion, private businesses, neighborhood violence, and institutional practices.

Washington, D.C., included segregated federal workplaces, schools, restaurants, theaters, recreation, and other facilities despite being the national capital.

Segregation was therefore national, although its legal form, visibility, and enforcement differed substantially by region.

Public Accommodations

Public accommodations are businesses and facilities offering goods, lodging, entertainment, transportation, food, or services to the public.

Segregated accommodations included hotels, restaurants, theaters, lunch counters, stores, amusement parks, waiting rooms, restrooms, drinking fountains, libraries, and swimming pools.

Some facilities excluded Black customers entirely. Others admitted them only through separate entrances, balconies, service windows, hours, sections, or inferior facilities.

Denial of access restricted travel, commerce, recreation, personal safety, and participation in civic life.

Signs, Entrances, and Controlled Space

Signs labeled “White,” “Colored,” “White Only,” or similar terms made racial classification visible in daily life.

The sign itself did not create the entire system. It communicated rules backed by property control, law, police power, business policy, and the threat of punishment.

Separate entrances and waiting areas forced Black people to navigate buildings through spaces designed to mark inferior status.

In some locations segregation operated without signs because residents understood the boundary through custom, reputation, or previous enforcement.

Transportation Segregation

Railroads, streetcars, buses, stations, terminals, ferries, and other transportation systems became major sites of segregation and protest.

Laws and carrier rules assigned Black passengers to separate cars, seats, waiting rooms, ticket windows, restrooms, and dining facilities.

Conductors and drivers possessed substantial power to classify passengers, order them to move, remove them from vehicles, or request arrest.

Transportation segregation burdened interstate travel, employment, commerce, family visits, migration, and access to public services.

Challenges by Black passengers produced important legal cases and mass movements, including Plessy v. Ferguson, Morgan v. Virginia, Henderson v. United States, the Montgomery Bus Boycott, Gayle v. Browder, Boynton v. Virginia, and the Freedom Rides.

School Segregation

Public-school segregation assigned children to different schools according to race.

Southern states generally required separate schools. Some Northern and Western communities created separation through district boundaries, neighborhood assignment, administrative decisions, separate buildings, or local practice.

Black schools commonly received less public funding, shorter terms, older buildings, overcrowded classrooms, used textbooks, limited transportation, and fewer specialized facilities.

Black teachers, families, churches, and communities nevertheless built educational institutions, raised money, demanded equal salaries, challenged discrimination, and created traditions of scholarship and leadership.

The existence of strong Black schools did not make compulsory segregation equal; it demonstrated community achievement under unequal conditions.

Higher Education

States excluded Black students from white public colleges and professional schools or directed them toward separate institutions.

Historically Black colleges and universities became essential centers of education, professional training, cultural development, and political organization.

Their importance did not excuse states from equal-protection obligations or justify exclusion from other public institutions.

Legal challenges including Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada, Sipuel v. Board of Regents, Sweatt v. Painter, and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents weakened segregation before Brown.

Residential Segregation

Residential segregation separated racial groups into different neighborhoods and restricted where African Americans could rent, purchase, build, or occupy housing.

Methods included racial zoning ordinances, restrictive covenants, real-estate steering, discriminatory lending, insurance practices, public-housing placement, zoning, exclusionary development, intimidation, and violence.

Neighborhood location affected school assignment, transportation, employment, public services, property appreciation, environmental conditions, policing, and political representation.

Residential segregation therefore connected spatial separation to education, wealth, health, safety, and municipal power.

Racial Zoning

Some cities enacted ordinances preventing Black people from moving onto blocks occupied mainly by white residents and preventing white people from moving onto blocks occupied mainly by Black residents.

In Buchanan v. Warley in 1917, the Supreme Court invalidated a Louisville racial-zoning ordinance.

The decision limited explicit municipal racial zoning but did not end residential segregation.

Cities, property owners, real-estate organizations, lenders, and neighborhood associations shifted toward other mechanisms.

Restrictive Covenants

Racially restrictive covenants were provisions in deeds, contracts, or neighborhood agreements intended to prevent property ownership or occupancy by people identified as members of specified racial groups.

In Shelley v. Kraemer in 1948, the Supreme Court held that state-court enforcement of racial covenants constituted unconstitutional state action.

The ruling prevented judicial enforcement but did not immediately remove covenants from property records or eliminate voluntary compliance, discrimination, steering, or neighborhood hostility.

Redlining and Mortgage Discrimination

Redlining refers to denying, restricting, or pricing credit and related services according to the racial composition or perceived risk of a neighborhood.

Federal, state, and private housing systems helped channel mortgage credit and investment toward white communities while excluding many Black neighborhoods and borrowers.

Lending discrimination limited home purchase, repair, business formation, property appreciation, and intergenerational wealth.

Redlining was not the only cause of residential segregation, but it became an important institutional mechanism reinforcing it.

Violence and Residential Boundaries

Black families who moved into white neighborhoods sometimes faced threats, bombings, arson, vandalism, mob gatherings, harassment, and refusal of police protection.

Such violence communicated racial boundaries even where no segregation ordinance existed.

Local authorities could reinforce segregation by failing to protect residents, arresting Black victims, restricting assemblies unevenly, or treating white violence as a private neighborhood dispute.

Employment Segregation

Employment segregation concentrated workers of different races in different occupations, departments, job classifications, pay scales, promotion tracks, and workplaces.

Black workers were frequently excluded from skilled trades, professional positions, supervisory roles, civil-service classifications, apprenticeships, and higher-paying industrial jobs.

Employers assigned Black workers to dangerous, insecure, physically demanding, service, agricultural, or domestic labor while reserving preferred positions for white workers.

Workplace segregation could continue even when Black and white employees worked for the same employer.

Labor Unions

Some unions excluded Black workers, organized separate auxiliaries, restricted apprenticeships, or tolerated segregated job structures.

Other unions organized interracially and challenged employer discrimination, though internal conflict remained.

Exclusion from unions could deny Black workers bargaining power, seniority, training, job referrals, benefits, and access to occupations controlled through union membership.

Black workers created independent labor organizations and used strikes, litigation, federal complaints, political pressure, and mass protest to challenge these restrictions.

Federal Workforce Segregation

The federal government was not consistently integrated.

During the Woodrow Wilson administration, several federal departments expanded racial separation in offices, restrooms, cafeterias, and employment assignments.

Black federal employees protested demotion, exclusion, screens dividing workspaces, and segregated facilities.

Executive Order 9980, issued by President Harry S. Truman in 1948, directed equal treatment and opportunity in the federal establishment.

Military Segregation

African Americans served in the United States military while being assigned to segregated units, facilities, training sites, and occupations.

Black service members often received inferior equipment, restricted assignments, fewer commissions, discriminatory discipline, and limited opportunities for advancement.

More than one million Black men and women served during World War II, largely within segregated structures.

President Truman issued Executive Order 9981 on July 26, 1948, establishing equality of treatment and opportunity in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion, or national origin.

Implementation met resistance, but the armed services moved toward integration during and after the Korean War.

Healthcare Segregation

Hospitals, clinics, medical schools, nursing programs, blood systems, professional associations, and public-health facilities practiced racial separation.

Black patients could be refused admission, assigned to separate wards, transferred to distant facilities, or treated in underfunded hospitals.

Black physicians and nurses faced exclusion from white institutions and professional networks.

Black communities established hospitals, clinics, nursing schools, medical associations, and mutual-aid systems to provide care denied elsewhere.

Federal civil-rights enforcement and Medicare funding became important tools in hospital desegregation during the 1960s.

Parks, Recreation, and Public Space

Cities and states segregated parks, playgrounds, beaches, swimming pools, golf courses, athletic fields, fairgrounds, zoos, and cultural facilities.

Black residents might be excluded, assigned separate facilities, limited to particular days, or required to travel long distances.

White officials sometimes closed public facilities rather than integrate them.

Segregation in recreation affected health, childhood development, community life, tourism, and public ownership of civic space.

Religion and Segregated Institutions

Churches and denominations participated in segregation through separate congregations, conferences, seminaries, missions, seating, and governance structures.

White churches sometimes defended segregation, avoided civil-rights conflict, or treated racial separation as a social custom beyond religious responsibility.

Independent Black churches developed partly because white congregations imposed discrimination and control.

Black churches became major institutions for worship, education, mutual aid, leadership development, political meetings, and civil-rights organizing.

Criminal Justice and Confinement

Police, courts, jails, prisons, juvenile institutions, and penal labor systems reflected racial segregation and inequality.

Black defendants faced exclusion from juries, unequal legal representation, discriminatory prosecution, racial violence, and harsher treatment.

Prisons and jails separated people by race, while convict leasing and chain gangs used penal labor under conditions connected to the post-emancipation racial labor order.

Segregation within confinement did not necessarily protect prisoners; it often distributed safety, work assignments, discipline, medical care, and facilities unequally.

Death, Burial, and Memory

Segregation extended to funeral homes, cemeteries, burial associations, veterans' institutions, and memorial spaces.

Black families could be denied burial plots, required to use separate cemeteries, or excluded from commemorative institutions.

African American communities established cemeteries and burial societies that preserved family, military, religious, and community history.

Unequal preservation and development have placed many historically Black burial grounds at risk.

Political Segregation and Disfranchisement

Segregation operated alongside political exclusion.

Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries, registration fraud, economic retaliation, and violence removed many African Americans from voting.

Exclusion from voting reduced Black influence over schools, police, courts, taxation, transportation, public works, housing, and other institutions enforcing segregation.

Segregation was therefore not simply a social preference. It was supported by a political system in which many of the people subjected to it had been denied effective power to change it.

Economic Effects

Segregation shaped access to land, housing, credit, employment, education, transportation, insurance, markets, and government contracts.

Separate institutions often required Black communities to finance services through taxes while also raising private funds for facilities denied adequate public support.

Housing restrictions limited access to appreciating property and placed Black buyers and renters in constrained markets.

Employment barriers restricted wages, occupational mobility, retirement benefits, business capital, and intergenerational wealth.

The economic consequences accumulated across families and communities rather than ending when a particular segregation law was repealed.

Enforcement

Segregation was enforced through both public and private authority.

  • Legislatures enacted segregation statutes.
  • Municipal governments adopted ordinances and facility rules.
  • School boards assigned students, teachers, and resources by race.
  • Police arrested people who challenged seating, entry, residence, or local custom.
  • Courts upheld convictions, enforced contracts, and rejected civil-rights claims.
  • Employers, landlords, lenders, unions, and businesses controlled access to jobs, property, credit, and services.
  • White citizens used boycotts, firing, eviction, threats, assault, and mob violence to punish resistance.
  • Newspapers, churches, political organizations, and civic associations defended racial separation and stigmatized integration.

Violence and Racial Terror

Segregation depended partly on the threat that crossing racial boundaries could produce violence.

African Americans faced assault, lynching, bombing, murder, destruction of property, police brutality, and mob attacks for voting, organizing, entering white spaces, purchasing homes, seeking education, or challenging employment discrimination.

Violence did more than punish individuals. It warned entire communities about the consequences of resistance.

When officials participated, failed to intervene, acquitted perpetrators, or prosecuted victims, private violence became connected to public authority.

Effects on Daily Life

Segregation required constant attention to racial boundaries.

Black people had to know where they could travel, eat, sleep, receive medical care, use a restroom, attend school, seek employment, or obtain assistance without risking humiliation, arrest, or violence.

Parents taught children rules necessary for physical safety while also resisting the idea that separation reflected inferiority.

The system consumed time, increased costs, restricted mobility, and imposed emotional and practical burdens not borne equally by white citizens.

Black Institutions Under Segregation

African Americans created schools, colleges, churches, businesses, newspapers, hospitals, banks, insurance companies, professional associations, neighborhoods, cultural institutions, and mutual-aid societies.

These institutions were sources of autonomy, leadership, employment, protection, knowledge, culture, and political organization.

Their success should not be interpreted as proof that segregation was beneficial or equal.

Some institutions developed from voluntary community organization; others became necessary because white institutions excluded Black people or because public systems refused equal service.

Desegregation later created opportunities while also producing closures, mergers, displacement, and loss of some Black-controlled institutions.

Resistance to Segregation

African Americans resisted segregation throughout its existence.

Resistance included litigation, petitions, journalism, migration, boycotts, strikes, armed self-defense, institution building, voting campaigns, direct action, sit-ins, freedom rides, marches, student organizing, economic pressure, and federal advocacy.

Black women frequently organized transportation boycotts, schools, clubs, voter campaigns, legal cases, and community networks while receiving less public recognition than male leaders.

Resistance was local and national, individual and collective, legal and extralegal, gradual and confrontational.

Civil Rights Act of 1875

The Civil Rights Act of 1875 attempted to guarantee equal access to inns, public transportation, theaters, and other public accommodations.

Congress relied on its authority to enforce the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

The statute represented a Reconstruction-era effort to prevent racial exclusion in public life.

Its effectiveness was limited by resistance, weak enforcement, and subsequent Supreme Court invalidation of major provisions.

Civil Rights Cases of 1883

In the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, the Supreme Court invalidated major public-accommodations provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875.

The Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment prohibited state action rather than purely private discrimination and concluded that Congress had exceeded its enforcement authority.

The decision narrowed federal protection against exclusion by privately owned businesses and helped create legal space for expanding segregation.

Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented, arguing for broader federal power to protect equal civil rights.

Plessy v. Ferguson

Louisiana enacted a law requiring separate railway accommodations for white and Black passengers.

Homer Plessy challenged the law after entering a railway car designated for white passengers.

In 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law and accepted the principle that racial separation did not violate equal protection when facilities were legally characterized as equal.

The decision supplied constitutional legitimacy to state-required segregation even though facilities and public power were rarely equal in practice.

Justice Harlan dissented and argued that the Constitution did not permit a legally dominant racial caste.

“Separate but Equal”

“Separate but equal” was the legal doctrine used to defend racial separation as compatible with equal protection.

The doctrine assumed that formal separation did not itself create inequality if the government claimed to provide comparable facilities.

In practice, Black facilities were commonly inferior in funding, location, equipment, staffing, transportation, and political support.

The deeper inequality came from compulsory racial classification and exclusion from institutions controlled by the dominant group.

Brown v. Board of Education rejected the doctrine in public education, and later constitutional and statutory developments dismantled its application in other fields.

The Legal Campaign Against Segregation

Black plaintiffs, lawyers, organizations, and communities brought cases challenging exclusion and unequal facilities.

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People developed a coordinated strategy attacking inequality in graduate and professional education before directly challenging segregated elementary and secondary schools.

Lawyers including Charles Hamilton Houston, Thurgood Marshall, Spottswood Robinson, Oliver Hill, Constance Baker Motley, Robert L. Carter, and many local attorneys built records demonstrating that separate institutions were unequal in both resources and status.

Plaintiffs and their families accepted economic retaliation, threats, harassment, and personal risk to create the cases.

Brown v. Board of Education

Brown v. Board of Education combined public-school segregation cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and the District of Columbia.

On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously held that state-imposed segregation in public education violated equal protection and that separate educational facilities were inherently unequal.

A companion decision applied the principle to the District of Columbia under the Fifth Amendment.

In 1955, Brown II directed lower federal courts to supervise desegregation under the phrase “with all deliberate speed.”

The decisions invalidated the legal foundation of segregated public schools but did not create immediate nationwide integration.

Massive Resistance

Southern political leaders organized “Massive Resistance” to prevent implementation of Brown.

States enacted pupil-placement laws, tuition grants, school-closing measures, private-academy programs, and procedural barriers.

Officials delayed assignments, harassed civil-rights organizations, threatened employment, and encouraged local defiance.

Some districts closed public schools rather than permit integration, shifting educational burdens onto Black children and families.

Federal courts, legislation, executive action, and continued community organizing were required to dismantle these systems.

Little Rock Central High School

In 1957, nine African American students attempted to enter Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas.

Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to block their entry, while white crowds threatened the students.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower federalized the Guard and deployed soldiers from the 101st Airborne Division to enforce federal court orders.

The crisis demonstrated that constitutional rulings required effective federal enforcement when state officials resisted.

Desegregating Transportation

Legal cases and protest campaigns progressively weakened transportation segregation.

Morgan v. Virginia invalidated state-required segregation on interstate buses; Henderson v. United States challenged unequal railroad dining service; and Keys v. Carolina Coach Company helped establish federal restrictions on segregation in interstate bus travel.

The Montgomery Bus Boycott led to Gayle v. Browder, which invalidated bus segregation in Montgomery.

Boynton v. Virginia extended protection against segregation in facilities serving interstate bus passengers.

Freedom Riders then tested compliance and forced stronger federal enforcement of desegregation orders.

Executive Orders 9980 and 9981

On July 26, 1948, President Truman issued Executive Orders 9980 and 9981.

Executive Order 9980 established a policy of fair employment and equal opportunity within the federal government.

Executive Order 9981 established equality of treatment and opportunity in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion, or national origin.

These orders did not instantly integrate every office and unit, but they made desegregation an official federal executive policy and created mechanisms for implementation.

Civil Rights Act of 1964

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 attacked segregation and discrimination across several institutional fields.

Title II prohibited discrimination in covered public accommodations; Title III authorized federal action concerning public facilities; Title IV addressed public-school desegregation; Title VI prohibited discrimination in federally assisted programs; and Title VII prohibited specified employment discrimination.

Congress relied substantially on its power to regulate interstate commerce as well as constitutional civil-rights authority.

The act provided federal agencies and courts with stronger tools than had existed under earlier civil-rights legislation.

Voting Rights Act of 1965

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 targeted discriminatory voting practices that had protected segregationist political control.

It prohibited literacy tests and authorized federal oversight and registration mechanisms in covered jurisdictions under the original statutory framework.

Increased Black voter registration and political participation altered the institutions responsible for schools, policing, public facilities, taxation, and local government.

Voting rights did not automatically desegregate every institution, but political power was essential to enforcing civil-rights guarantees.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, commonly called the Fair Housing Act, prohibited specified discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing.

The law addressed a field in which private discrimination, government policy, lending, real-estate practices, and neighborhood violence had created durable segregation.

Later amendments expanded protected classes and enforcement mechanisms.

Fair-housing law prohibited discriminatory conduct but did not automatically undo established residential patterns, wealth differences, zoning systems, or unequal local resources.

Desegregation

Desegregation is the removal of laws, rules, assignments, and practices requiring or maintaining racial separation.

It may involve admitting excluded people, redrawing assignments, opening facilities, changing staffing, revising transportation, eliminating racial classifications, or placing institutions under court supervision.

Desegregation is not completed merely when a discriminatory rule is deleted. Institutions may need affirmative changes to eliminate the effects of an officially created dual system.

Courts used remedies including reassignment plans, transportation, faculty desegregation, facility equalization, reporting requirements, and continuing jurisdiction.

Integration

Integration generally refers to meaningful participation in common institutions rather than mere removal of a legal barrier.

A school may be legally desegregated while remaining racially isolated because of residential patterns, attendance boundaries, enrollment choices, or unequal access.

An employer may stop using separate departments while retaining discriminatory hiring and promotion systems.

Integration therefore concerns participation, institutional culture, access to authority, and distribution of resources as well as physical presence.

Implementation and Federal Enforcement

Desegregation required cooperation among courts, federal agencies, presidents, Congress, state officials, local governments, school districts, businesses, and civil-rights organizations.

Federal funding conditions became especially important because institutions risked losing funds if they maintained prohibited discrimination.

Litigation established obligations, but plaintiffs and communities still had to document noncompliance and seek enforcement.

Progress varied by jurisdiction, institution, political leadership, and the willingness of federal authorities to act.

Limits of Legal Desegregation

Civil-rights victories ended the lawful authority to require racial segregation, but they did not erase the structures produced by earlier policy.

Residential separation continued to shape school enrollment, municipal tax bases, transportation, employment access, environmental conditions, and public services.

Private institutions could adopt new criteria that appeared race-neutral while preserving earlier patterns.

Court remedies also faced legal limitations, particularly when plaintiffs could not establish intentional government responsibility across jurisdictional boundaries.

The distinction between ending a discriminatory rule and repairing its accumulated consequences remains central to debates about segregation.

Resegregation

Resegregation describes the return or increase of racial separation after a period of desegregation or integration.

It may result from residential change, district boundaries, withdrawal from metropolitan systems, school choice structures, institutional closure, unequal resources, private discrimination, or the termination of court supervision.

Resegregation does not necessarily involve restoration of an explicit Jim Crow statute.

Analysis must distinguish between demographic separation, intentional state-created segregation, and unlawful discriminatory conduct.

Present Legal Status

Government may not require racial segregation in public institutions.

Federal law prohibits racial discrimination in covered public accommodations, employment, federally assisted programs, voting, housing, lending, and other fields.

Courts may remedy intentional government segregation and unlawful discrimination, subject to constitutional doctrine, statutory requirements, evidence, jurisdiction, and available remedies.

Racial concentration or separation alone does not always establish a legal violation. Legal responsibility depends on the source of the condition, governmental intent where required, statutory coverage, and the conduct being challenged.

Common Misconceptions

“Segregation Existed Only in the South”

Southern states built the most comprehensive legal Jim Crow system, but schools, neighborhoods, employment, businesses, recreation, and other institutions were segregated throughout the United States.

“Segregation Began After the Civil War”

Racial separation existed during colonial history and before emancipation. The post-Reconstruction system reorganized and expanded segregation within a formally free society.

“Segregation and Slavery Were the Same System”

Slavery claimed ownership of people and compelled labor; segregation separated people by race and distributed rights and resources unequally. They were distinct but historically connected.

“Jim Crow Meant Only Separate Facilities”

Jim Crow also included disfranchisement, racial violence, labor control, unequal law enforcement, exclusion from juries and office, and institutional white supremacy.

“Separate Facilities Were Usually Equal”

Black facilities were commonly underfunded, distant, overcrowded, poorly maintained, or entirely absent. Compulsory racial classification also denied equal citizenship regardless of material comparison.

“Segregation Was Merely a Private Social Choice”

Government statutes, ordinances, courts, police, school boards, public funding, licensing, transportation rules, and housing policy actively created and maintained separation.

“De Facto Means Accidental”

De facto segregation means separation not directly imposed by an explicit legal racial classification. It may still result from deliberate private discrimination or from policies with predictable segregating effects.

“Black Institutions Proved Segregation Worked”

Black institutions demonstrated community achievement and autonomy under exclusion. Their success did not make compulsory separation or unequal public support legitimate.

“Brown Immediately Integrated American Schools”

Brown invalidated state-imposed public-school segregation, but resistance, delay, school closings, litigation, and federal enforcement shaped implementation for decades.

“The Civil Rights Act of 1964 Ended Every Form of Segregation”

The act prohibited major forms of discrimination and strengthened enforcement, but housing patterns, institutional inequality, resistance, and practices outside particular statutory provisions continued.

“Desegregation and Integration Mean Exactly the Same Thing”

Desegregation removes enforced separation. Integration concerns meaningful common participation and may require additional institutional change.

“Any Racial Imbalance Automatically Proves Illegal Segregation”

Historical analysis may identify serious racial separation, but a legal violation depends on applicable law, evidence of prohibited conduct, government responsibility, intent where required, and available remedies.

Key Distinctions

Segregation
Separation of people into different spaces, institutions, or systems according to race.
De jure segregation
Segregation created or intentionally maintained through law or official government action.
De facto segregation
Segregation existing without an explicit legal racial mandate, though it may reflect private discrimination, institutional practice, economic structure, or formally race-neutral policy.
Jim Crow
The broader post-Reconstruction system of white supremacy that included segregation, disfranchisement, economic control, unequal justice, and racial terror.
Discrimination
Unequal treatment based on race or another protected or socially defined characteristic; discrimination may occur without physical separation.
Exclusion
Complete denial of entry, participation, membership, service, residence, or opportunity.
Separation
Physical or institutional division; separation becomes racial segregation when race determines the boundary.
Separate but equal
The discredited legal doctrine that racial separation could satisfy equal protection if separate facilities were characterized as equal.
Desegregation
Removal of laws, rules, assignments, and institutional practices maintaining racial separation.
Integration
Meaningful participation of previously separated groups in common institutions and access to their opportunities and authority.
Resegregation
An increase or return of racial separation after desegregation or integration.
Residential segregation
Racial separation among neighborhoods or housing markets.
Redlining
Restriction or denial of credit, insurance, investment, or services based on neighborhood racial composition or location.
Restrictive covenant
A property agreement intended to restrict sale or occupancy according to race or another classification.
Racial zoning
Government regulation assigning residential areas or property occupancy according to race.
White supremacy
An ideology and institutional order assigning superior status, power, and authority to white people.
Apartheid
The formal system of racial classification and minority rule historically imposed in South Africa; it may be compared with American segregation, but the two systems had different constitutional, demographic, and administrative structures.

Boynton v. Virginia invalidates racial segregation in a bus-terminal restaurant serving interstate passengers.

Legal segregation remains prohibited, while residential separation, unequal institutions, litigation over remedies, and patterns of resegregation continue to shape public debate.

Why Segregation Matters

Segregation was a governing system that organized space, institutions, public resources, and citizenship according to race.

It enabled white officials and institutions to maintain political and economic control after slavery's abolition without restoring chattel ownership.

Segregation limited African American access to education, housing, transportation, employment, healthcare, public accommodations, political participation, and protection under law.

The system was national rather than exclusively Southern, although the statutory Jim Crow order was most comprehensive in the South.

African Americans resisted segregation through institution building, law, journalism, migration, labor organization, political action, boycotts, direct protest, military service, and federal advocacy.

Court decisions and civil-rights legislation dismantled the legal authority for racial separation, but the accumulated spatial, economic, educational, and institutional consequences did not disappear automatically.

Understanding segregation is necessary for interpreting Jim Crow, the Civil Rights Movement, constitutional equal protection, housing inequality, school policy, federalism, and the continuing relationship between race and place in the United States.

Summary

Segregation was the legally, institutionally, economically, and socially enforced separation of racial groups. In United States history, it most commonly refers to the system that separated African Americans from white Americans and restricted Black access to schools, housing, transportation, employment, public accommodations, healthcare, political authority, and other institutions.

The system developed from colonial racial classifications and slavery but became especially comprehensive after Reconstruction. Jim Crow combined segregation with disfranchisement, labor control, unequal justice, and racial terror. Southern governments established the most visible statutory system, while Northern and Western communities maintained separation through housing, schools, employment, businesses, administrative policy, and private discrimination.

Segregation operated through statutes, ordinances, school assignments, transportation rules, property covenants, lending, zoning, employment practices, police action, economic retaliation, and violence. Separate Black institutions often demonstrated community strength and autonomy, but their achievements did not make compulsory separation equal.

The Civil Rights Cases of 1883 narrowed federal protection, and Plessy v. Ferguson upheld state-required separation under the “separate but equal” doctrine. Black plaintiffs, lawyers, communities, and organizations progressively challenged the system. Brown v. Board of Education rejected separate-but-equal public schooling, and executive action and federal legislation dismantled segregation in the military, public accommodations, employment, voting, federally assisted programs, and housing.

Legal desegregation prohibited government-mandated racial separation but did not automatically eliminate residential patterns, unequal institutions, accumulated economic disadvantage, or resistance to integration. Segregation remains essential to understanding Jim Crow, the Civil Rights Movement, equal-protection law, housing and education policy, and the historical relationship between race, place, power, and public resources in the United States.
