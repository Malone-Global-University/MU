Duty to Intervene

The obligation of law-enforcement officers to take reasonable action when they know another officer is violating a person's rights and they have a realistic opportunity and ability to prevent or stop the harm.

Fact Sheet Overview

Term
Duty to intervene.
Basic meaning
An officer who knows another officer is violating a person's rights must take reasonable steps to prevent or stop the violation when the officer has a realistic opportunity and ability to act.
Principal context
Excessive force by another law-enforcement or correctional officer.
Federal civil doctrine
Often called bystander liability or failure-to-intervene liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.
Primary civil elements
An underlying constitutional violation, the bystander officer's knowledge or reason to know of the violation, a realistic or reasonable opportunity to prevent or stop it, and failure to take reasonable action.
Underlying violation required
A derivative civil claim ordinarily fails when the principal officer's conduct was constitutional.
Most clearly established application
Failure to stop another officer's excessive force when the bystander observed the force and could intervene.
Scope beyond excessive force
Some federal circuits recognize intervention duties involving unlawful arrest, unlawful entry, or other constitutional violations. The scope varies by jurisdiction and facts.
Supreme Court status
The modern civil doctrine has developed principally through federal appellate decisions rather than one comprehensive Supreme Court test governing every type of police misconduct.
Common civil test
Knowledge of the violation, reasonable opportunity to prevent the harm, and a deliberate choice not to act.
Time requirement
An officer cannot ordinarily be liable for failing to stop instantaneous conduct when there was no realistic time to react, but prolonged or repeated misconduct can create an opportunity.
Forms of intervention
Verbal orders, warning the officer, physically stopping or separating the officer, removing a weapon or restraint, calling a supervisor, summoning assistance, repositioning the person, and obtaining medical aid.
Rank
The duty can apply regardless of rank. A subordinate may need to intervene against a supervisor, and a supervisor may have heightened authority and opportunity to act.
Duty to report
A separate obligation to report misconduct after or during the event according to law and policy.
Duty to render aid
A separate obligation to request or provide appropriate medical aid when a person is injured or in distress.
Duty of truthful documentation
Officers must not conceal misconduct through false reports, material omissions, evidence destruction, coordinated stories, or retaliation against witnesses.
Current Justice Department policy
Covered DOJ officers must recognize and act upon an affirmative duty to intervene to prevent or stop unconstitutional or otherwise prohibited force and an affirmative duty to request or render medical aid.
Principal civil remedy
42 U.S.C. § 1983 for state or local officers acting under color of law.
Principal federal criminal statute
18 U.S.C. § 242, which prohibits willful deprivation of federal rights under color of law.
Qualified immunity
An officer may receive immunity from personal civil damages unless the underlying right and duty to intervene were clearly established in the relevant circumstances.
Municipal liability
Cities do not receive qualified immunity, but liability under Monell requires a policy, custom, authorized decision, or qualifying institutional failure that caused the constitutional violation.
George Floyd federal convictions
J. Alexander Kueng and Tou Thao were convicted under federal civil-rights law for willfully failing to intervene in Derek Chauvin's unreasonable force. Kueng, Thao, and Thomas Lane were also convicted for failing to provide medical aid.
State-law variation
States differ on who must intervene, what misconduct is covered, whether verbal intervention is sufficient, reporting deadlines, retaliation protection, criminal penalties, discipline, and decertification.
State examples
Colorado, Minnesota, Washington, Maryland, and other jurisdictions enacted statutory intervention duties after 2020.
Local model
Buffalo, New York, enacted Cariol's Law, combining intervention, reporting, and anti-retaliation provisions.
Current federal proposal
H.R. 5361, the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025, proposes a clear federal duty and training program but remained introduced rather than enacted as of August 1, 2026.
Historical significance
The doctrine rejects the idea that only the officer directly applying force bears responsibility when other officers knowingly permit preventable constitutional harm.

Definition

The duty to intervene requires a law-enforcement officer to take reasonable action when another officer is committing or attempting to commit unlawful misconduct and intervention is realistically possible.

The duty is most firmly established for excessive force.

It is not a demand that officers predict every mistake or physically enter every encounter regardless of danger.

Four Related but Distinct Obligations

  • Intervene: prevent or stop ongoing misconduct.
  • Report: notify the proper authority and document the misconduct truthfully.
  • Render aid: request or provide appropriate medical assistance.
  • Preserve evidence: protect video, reports, medical records, witness information, and other evidence from alteration or destruction.

Intervention Is Not the Same as Reporting

Intervention addresses harm that is occurring or about to occur.

Reporting creates an official record and permits investigation after the event.

An officer who stops misconduct but conceals it afterward may still violate reporting or truthfulness rules.

An officer who files a report after watching preventable harm may still have violated the duty to intervene.

Intervention Is Not the Same as Medical Aid

Stopping force ends or reduces the harmful conduct.

Medical aid addresses injury or physiological distress caused by the encounter.

A complete policy requires both because ending pressure does not revive an unconscious person, control bleeding, or summon emergency treatment.

Sources of the Duty

  • federal constitutional law applied through Section 1983;
  • federal criminal civil-rights law;
  • state statutes and regulations;
  • municipal ordinances;
  • agency use-of-force and conduct policies;
  • consent decrees and court orders;
  • officer-certification standards; and
  • employment, ethics, and professional-duty rules.

Constitutional Foundation

The underlying constitutional right depends on the misconduct being observed.

Excessive force during arrest generally implicates the Fourth Amendment; force against pretrial detainees implicates the Fourteenth Amendment; force against convicted prisoners implicates the Eighth Amendment.

The bystander officer's liability derives from failure to protect the person from the known constitutional violation.

42 U.S.C. § 1983

Section 1983 authorizes civil actions against persons who, under color of state law, deprive others of federal rights.

A bystander officer can be personally liable for nonfeasance when the legal elements of failure to intervene are satisfied.

The statute supplies a remedy; the underlying right comes from the Constitution or another federal law.

Bystander Liability

Federal courts often call failure-to-intervene responsibility bystander liability.

The term refers to an officer who did not directly inflict the principal force but was present, knew of the violation, and could have acted.

Bystander status does not mean legal neutrality when official authority and a realistic opportunity to protect the person exist.

Underlying Constitutional Violation

A civil failure-to-intervene claim ordinarily requires proof that another officer committed a constitutional violation.

If the primary force, arrest, search, or detention was lawful, there is generally no derivative duty to stop that lawful act.

Policy may nevertheless require officers to prevent unsafe or improper conduct before it becomes unconstitutional.

Knowledge or Reason to Know

The officer must know, observe, or have reason to understand that unlawful conduct is occurring or imminent.

Knowledge can arise from direct sight, sound, radio communication, repeated complaints, visible injury, orders, or the officer's participation in the event.

An officer cannot avoid responsibility merely by looking away from obvious prolonged misconduct.

Presence

Physical presence is strong evidence that an officer could perceive and affect the event.

Modern communications can also create knowledge and opportunity for a supervisor or nearby officer who is not standing directly beside the person.

Civil doctrine varies on how far liability extends to officials who are remote from the scene.

Realistic Opportunity to Intervene

The officer must have enough time and practical ability to take reasonable action.

Courts examine duration, distance, officer position, number of participants, weapons, communication, rank, physical ability, and the speed of events.

A realistic opportunity can be verbal, physical, tactical, or medical rather than limited to physically restraining another officer.

Ability and Safety

The duty does not require an intervention that is physically impossible or certain to cause greater immediate harm.

State statutes and policies often include language such as when in a position to act, physically or verbally able, or when a realistic opportunity exists safely.

Safety cannot be used as a blanket excuse when a warning, radio call, command, or request for assistance was feasible.

Reasonable Steps

The law usually requires reasonable intervention rather than guaranteed success.

An officer may comply by taking proportionate action capable of preventing or ending the violation.

Whether the response was reasonable depends on the danger, authority, timing, available options, and whether misconduct continued.

Verbal Intervention

An officer can order another officer to stop, release pressure, discontinue strikes, reposition a restrained person, or summon medical aid.

A clear command from a peer or supervisor may end misconduct without additional force.

Weak or ambiguous remarks may be insufficient when the violation is obvious and continues.

Physical Intervention

Physical intervention may include pulling an officer away, removing a knee or arm, separating participants, taking control of a weapon, opening restraints, or placing the victim in a safer position.

The response must account for weapons and active threats.

Training should prepare officers to intervene physically without creating avoidable crossfire or confusion.

Summoning Help

An officer may request a supervisor, additional unit, emergency medical service, crisis team, or specialized response.

Calling for help can be an important part of intervention but may not be enough when immediate action is possible and serious harm is ongoing.

The response should match the urgency of the danger.

Timing

The duty begins when the officer knows or reasonably should know that the conduct is unlawful and intervention is possible.

It continues while preventable harm remains ongoing.

Once force ends, duties to render aid, report, preserve evidence, and prevent retaliation remain.

Instantaneous Conduct

Courts may reject liability when unlawful force occurred too quickly for another officer to perceive and stop it.

A single unexpected blow or discharge can occur before a bystander can react.

The absence of time must be evaluated from evidence rather than assumed because the event was tense.

Prolonged or Repeated Conduct

Repeated strikes, extended restraint, multiple Taser cycles, a sustained canine bite, continued pressure after handcuffing, or delayed aid can create clear intervention opportunities.

Every additional second may increase the ability of other officers to recognize the violation and act.

Prolonged conduct weakens claims that intervention was impossible.

Multiple-Officer Encounters

Responsibility does not disappear because several officers are present.

Courts examine each officer's location, observation, authority, conduct, and opportunity.

An officer who assists the unconstitutional force may face direct liability rather than only bystander liability.

Rank and Chain of Command

The duty can require a junior officer to challenge a senior officer.

Supervisors ordinarily have greater command authority and may have a stronger practical ability to stop misconduct.

A lawful intervention rule must make clear that obedience to rank does not require participation in unconstitutional conduct.

Supervisory Liability Is Distinct

A supervisor may be directly liable for personally ordering, approving, participating in, or failing to stop misconduct at the scene.

Separate supervisory-liability doctrines may apply when a supervisor knew of a pervasive risk and responded with deliberate indifference.

Title alone does not create automatic liability for every subordinate's act.

Officers From Different Agencies

Misconduct can involve city police, sheriffs, state officers, federal officers, transit police, campus police, or correctional personnel at the same scene.

Some state statutes expressly define whether the duty extends across agencies.

A strong policy does not permit an officer to ignore obvious excessive force merely because the offending officer wears a different uniform.

Off-Duty Conduct

State law and agency policy vary on whether an off-duty officer must intervene or report.

Factors include whether the officer invokes official authority, carries agency equipment, is identifiable as law enforcement, or encounters a serious crime.

On-duty statutory duties should not automatically be described as applying off duty without checking the controlling text.

Scope Beyond Excessive Force

Federal civil liability is most consistently recognized for excessive force.

Some appellate decisions extend intervention duties to unlawful arrest, unlawful entry, or other constitutional violations.

Other courts limit clearly established liability outside force or require more specific precedent.

The governing federal circuit and date of the event are therefore important.

Unlawful Arrest

An officer who knows another officer is making an arrest without legal justification may have a duty to prevent or end the seizure in some jurisdictions.

The analysis can turn on probable cause, the officer's knowledge, authority over the arrest, and clearly established circuit law.

The use of force accompanying an unlawful arrest may create separate direct or intervention claims.

Unlawful Search or Entry

Some courts recognize potential bystander liability when an officer knowingly permits an unlawful home entry or search and can prevent it.

The officer must have knowledge and a realistic ability to affect the operation.

Mere presence during a search does not automatically establish responsibility for every legal defect.

Fabricated Evidence and False Reports

Whether the federal intervention doctrine reaches evidence fabrication or false reports varies.

Officers nevertheless have independent duties not to fabricate evidence, sign false reports, obstruct justice, or conspire to violate rights.

Participation in a cover-up can create direct liability or criminal exposure even when failure-to-intervene law is uncertain.

Jails, Prisons, and Detention

Correctional officers may be liable for failing to stop unconstitutional force against prisoners or detainees.

Custody increases the government's control over the person's safety, witnesses, restraints, and medical access.

The underlying standard differs for pretrial detainees and convicted prisoners.

Duty to Render or Request Medical Aid

Officers must respond appropriately when a person is injured, loses consciousness, cannot breathe, suffers a seizure, bleeds, or otherwise shows medical distress.

Intervention may require ending restraint, repositioning the person, calling emergency services, beginning trained first aid, or communicating critical facts to medical personnel.

Standing by while a known medical emergency worsens can create separate constitutional and criminal liability.

Truthful Documentation

Reports should identify who used force, who observed it, commands given, intervention attempts, injuries, medical aid, and evidence.

Officers must not omit another officer's conduct to protect the officer or agency.

Body-camera footage, dispatch records, civilian video, medical evidence, and witness statements should be preserved.

Duty to Report

Reporting duties may require immediate notice to a supervisor, chief, prosecutor, inspector general, certification board, or external agency.

Some laws require written reports within a defined period.

Reporting only to the offending officer or participating in a coordinated account does not satisfy an independent reporting requirement.

Retaliation Against Intervening Officers

Officers may fear ostracism, undesirable assignments, discipline, denial of promotion, threats, or termination after intervening.

Anti-retaliation provisions protect both the officer and the public purpose of the duty.

A policy that commands intervention but punishes those who act is institutionally self-defeating.

Cariol Horne and Cariol's Law

Cariol Horne, a Buffalo police officer, said she intervened when a fellow officer used a chokehold against a handcuffed man and was later terminated after disciplinary proceedings.

Buffalo enacted Cariol's Law in 2020, creating a duty to intervene when an officer has a realistic opportunity to stop clearly excessive force.

The ordinance also contains reporting and anti-retaliation protections and became a prominent model for officer-intervention reform.

Active Bystandership

Active bystandership treats intervention as a professional skill rather than personal disloyalty.

Training teaches officers to recognize risk, use graded verbal intervention, receive correction, and escalate intervention when necessary.

The goal is prevention before conduct becomes a constitutional violation or medical emergency.

Peer Intervention

Peers may notice escalating emotion, tunnel vision, fatigue, tactical error, or disproportionate force before a supervisor arrives.

Early intervention can protect the public, the officer using force, other officers, and the agency.

A culture of mutual correction should distinguish safety intervention from bad-faith interference.

Duty to Accept Intervention

Policies can require officers to obey a lawful order to stop force and to accept peer intervention without retaliation.

Training should normalize statements such as step back, release pressure, hand me the cuffs, or I have control.

The intervention system fails when the offending officer may punish the intervening officer informally.

Training

Training should cover legal standards, recognition of excessive force, realistic opportunity, verbal and physical techniques, medical response, documentation, reporting, and retaliation.

Scenario training should include intervention across rank, agency, race, gender, and assignment.

Annual repetition is more useful than a one-time lecture.

Scenario-Based Training

Scenarios can require recruits and experienced officers to stop prolonged restraint, repeated strikes, unsafe weapon handling, delayed aid, false reporting, and supervisor misconduct.

Evaluation should measure whether the officer acted, how quickly, and whether the response was safe and effective.

Training must also teach officers how to receive intervention without escalating conflict.

Effective Policy Design

  • a clear affirmative duty rather than permission to intervene;
  • coverage of attempted and ongoing excessive force;
  • verbal and physical intervention options;
  • application regardless of rank or agency when legally possible;
  • duty to report and document independently;
  • duty to render medical aid;
  • anti-retaliation protections;
  • supervisory review and external referral for serious harm;
  • discipline for failure and protection for good-faith intervention; and
  • training, data collection, and periodic public reporting.

Administrative Discipline

Failure to intervene may lead to counseling, retraining, suspension, demotion, termination, or other discipline.

The sanction should consider knowledge, opportunity, seriousness, prior training, reporting, concealment, and resulting harm.

Discipline should be consistent across rank and assignment.

Officer Certification

State licensing or certification boards may investigate serious intervention failures.

Decertification can prevent an officer removed for misconduct from moving immediately to another department within the state.

Reporting to the certification body must be timely and accurate for the system to work.

18 U.S.C. § 242

Section 242 makes it a federal crime for a person acting under color of law to willfully deprive another person of federal rights.

A failure to act can support prosecution when the officer had a legal duty, knew of the violation, had the ability to intervene or provide aid, and willfully chose not to do so.

Federal prosecutors must prove willfulness beyond a reasonable doubt.

Criminal and Civil Standards Differ

Civil liability under Section 1983 ordinarily uses a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard and may be limited by qualified immunity.

Federal criminal liability requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt and proof of willfulness.

An officer may avoid criminal conviction yet face civil or administrative consequences, or may be criminally convicted even when no civil trial occurs.

George Floyd

Derek Chauvin kept George Floyd handcuffed and restrained prone while Floyd repeatedly said he could not breathe, became unresponsive, and lost a pulse.

J. Alexander Kueng and Thomas Lane restrained Floyd's body, while Tou Thao remained nearby and managed bystanders.

The prolonged encounter provided repeated opportunities for officers to stop the restraint, reposition Floyd, and obtain effective medical care.

Kueng and Thao: Failure to Intervene

A federal jury convicted Kueng and Thao of willfully depriving Floyd of his right to be free from unreasonable force by failing to intervene to stop Chauvin.

The Justice Department reported that both officers had been trained in the duty to intervene and disregarded that training.

Thao received a forty-two-month federal sentence and Kueng a thirty-six-month federal sentence.

Lane, Kueng, and Thao: Failure to Provide Aid

The federal jury also convicted Lane, Kueng, and Thao for deliberate indifference to Floyd's serious medical needs.

Lane was not convicted on the separate failure-to-intervene count charged against Kueng and Thao.

Lane received a thirty-month federal sentence.

The distinction demonstrates why intervention and medical aid should be analyzed separately.

O'Neill v. Krzeminski

The Second Circuit considered officers' responsibility during an arrest involving rapid blows and later dragging of the plaintiff.

The court concluded that three blows delivered in rapid succession may have left no realistic opportunity to prevent them.

A later, more extended act could permit a finding that the observing officer had time to intervene.

Yang v. Hardin

The Seventh Circuit recognized that an officer who knew another person was impersonating police and violating constitutional rights could be liable for failing to act.

The decision is often cited for a broader formulation of an officer's duty to protect people from constitutional violations committed by fellow officers.

Its application beyond force depends on jurisdiction and factual similarity.

Randall v. Prince George's County

The Fourth Circuit described bystander liability as arising from an officer's duty to uphold the law and protect the public from illegal acts regardless of who commits them.

The court identified knowledge of a fellow officer's constitutional violation, a reasonable opportunity to prevent the harm, and a choice not to act.

The formulation became influential in Fourth Circuit cases.

Priester v. City of Riviera Beach

The Eleventh Circuit denied qualified immunity to an officer who watched a police dog attack a compliant person for an extended period and remained close enough to intervene.

The case illustrates how duration, proximity, observation, and communication create a realistic opportunity.

The circuit treats the excessive-force intervention duty as clearly established when the officer witnesses unlawful force and can act.

Short-Duration Cases

Federal courts often reject intervention claims when the force occurred in seconds and the bystander arrived afterward or lacked warning.

Body-camera footage may show that an officer did not witness the act or could not respond in time.

The analysis must be individualized rather than assuming every officer at the scene had the same opportunity.

Qualified Immunity

Qualified immunity may shield an individual officer from civil damages unless the intervention duty was clearly established in the relevant context.

The duty to stop observed excessive force is well established in many circuits, but extension to other misconduct can be less clear.

Qualified immunity does not protect municipalities and does not bar criminal prosecution or agency discipline.

Municipal Liability

A city or county may be liable when a policy, custom, authorized decision, or qualifying failure to train or supervise causes constitutional harm.

Evidence can include a code of silence, retaliation against interveners, ignored complaints, repeated reporting failures, deficient training, or discipline that rewards silence.

Municipal liability is not automatic merely because the city employed the officers.

Pattern-or-Practice Enforcement

The Justice Department can investigate systemic unconstitutional policing under 34 U.S.C. § 12601.

Investigations may examine whether officers intervene, report, render aid, document force accurately, and face retaliation.

Consent decrees can require policies, training, supervision, data, discipline, and independent monitoring.

Current Justice Department Policy

The Justice Manual states that covered DOJ officers must recognize and act upon an affirmative duty to intervene.

The duty applies to excessive force and other force violating the Constitution, federal law, or Department policy on reasonable force.

The manual separately requires officers to request or render medical aid as appropriate.

Colorado

Colorado's 2020 Law Enforcement Integrity Act requires a peace officer to intervene when another officer uses unlawful physical force.

The intervening officer must also report the event according to the statute.

The law demonstrates a model combining prevention and post-incident accountability.

Minnesota

Minnesota law requires a peace officer to intercede when present and observing another officer using force beyond what is lawful or objectively reasonable and the officer is physically or verbally able to act.

The officer must report excessive force to the chief law-enforcement officer.

The statute applies regardless of tenure or rank.

Washington

Washington law requires an identifiable on-duty peace officer who witnesses another officer using or attempting excessive force to intervene when in a position to do so.

The officer must also render aid at the earliest safe opportunity to a person injured by force.

A separate provision requires reporting observed or reasonably believed wrongdoing.

Maryland

Maryland's statewide use-of-force statute requires officers to intervene to prevent or terminate force beyond what the statute authorizes.

It also requires basic first aid, prompt medical assistance, and full documentation of observed or personally involved force incidents.

The law places intervention within a broader necessity, proportionality, de-escalation, aid, and supervisory-review framework.

Buffalo's Cariol's Law

Buffalo's ordinance requires an officer present and observing force reasonably believed to be clearly excessive to intervene when a realistic opportunity exists.

It includes reporting obligations and protections for officers who intervene or report.

The ordinance is local law and should not be confused with proposals for a statewide New York statute.

George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025

Section 361 of H.R. 5361 would require the Attorney General to establish a clear duty for federal law-enforcement officers to intervene when another officer uses excessive force against a civilian.

It would also establish federal training and expand grant-authorized training for state and local officers.

The bill was introduced September 15, 2025, and remained unenacted as of August 1, 2026.

Implementation and Evaluation

A written duty should be measured through reports, video, complaints, intervention records, medical outcomes, discipline, surveys, and retaliation complaints.

Agencies should distinguish successful early intervention from misconduct so officers are not punished for preventing harm.

Evaluation should examine whether intervention actually occurs across rank, unit, race, gender, and assignment.

Research and Interpretation

A reliable case summary should identify the underlying constitutional violation, what each officer knew, where each officer stood, how long the conduct lasted, what options existed, and what the officer did.

The terms failure to intervene, failure to protect, deliberate indifference, conspiracy, and aiding and abetting should not be used interchangeably.

A conviction, civil judgment, policy finding, and disciplinary result answer different legal questions.

Representative Scenarios

Sudden Single Strike

An officer unexpectedly strikes a restrained person once before another officer can react. The bystander may have had no realistic opportunity to prevent the initial blow, but must act if additional force appears likely.

Prolonged Prone Restraint

Several officers hold a handcuffed person prone while the person says breathing is difficult and becomes unresponsive. Officers have time to order release, reposition the person, begin aid, or call medical personnel.

Sustained Canine Bite

An officer allows a police dog to continue biting a nonresisting person while another officer watches from several feet away. Duration and proximity can establish a realistic opportunity to intervene.

Unlawful Arrest

An officer knows a colleague lacks lawful grounds to arrest a person and has authority to stop the seizure. Civil liability depends on circuit law, knowledge, opportunity, and whether the unlawfulness was clearly established.

Medical Emergency After Force

The force has ended, but the person is unconscious and not breathing normally. The intervention duty may transition into an independent duty to request and render medical aid.

Supervisor Orders Misconduct

A subordinate is ordered to continue force that is plainly unlawful. Rank does not require obedience to an unconstitutional order, and the subordinate may need to refuse and intervene.

Common Misconceptions

“Only the Officer Applying Force Can Be Liable”

An officer who knowingly permits preventable unconstitutional force can face civil, criminal, or disciplinary consequences.

“Being Present Automatically Creates Liability”

The officer must generally know of an underlying violation and have a realistic opportunity and ability to act.

“The Duty Applies to Every Lawful Use of Force”

Officers do not have a duty to stop force that is objectively reasonable and authorized.

“Reporting Later Satisfies the Duty to Intervene”

Reporting does not excuse failure to stop preventable ongoing harm.

“Intervening Means Physically Fighting Another Officer”

Reasonable intervention may be verbal, physical, supervisory, tactical, or medical.

“A Junior Officer Must Always Obey a Senior Officer”

Chain of command does not authorize unconstitutional force or excuse participation in it.

“An Officer Must Intervene Even When It Is Physically Impossible”

The doctrine requires a realistic opportunity and reasonable action, not impossible conduct.

“A Very Short Incident Always Creates Liability”

A sudden act may occur too quickly to stop. Courts examine actual time and opportunity.

“The Duty Is Identical in Every Federal Circuit”

The excessive-force duty is widely recognized, but tests and application to other constitutional violations vary.

“Duty to Intervene and Duty to Render Aid Are the Same”

Stopping misconduct and treating resulting injury are distinct obligations.

“Qualified Immunity Makes Failure to Intervene Legal”

Qualified immunity concerns personal civil damages and clearly established law; it does not determine policy compliance or criminal responsibility.

“Municipalities Receive Qualified Immunity”

Cities do not receive the doctrine, although municipal liability has separate requirements under Monell.

“All States Have the Same Statutory Duty”

State rules differ in scope, safety language, reporting, penalties, and coverage.

“H.R. 5361 Is Current Federal Law”

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025 remained proposed legislation as of August 1, 2026.

“George Floyd's Three Other Officers Were Convicted of the Same Federal Offense”

Kueng and Thao were convicted for failure to intervene; Kueng, Thao, and Lane were convicted for failure to provide medical aid.

Key Distinctions

Duty to intervene
The obligation to take reasonable action to prevent or stop known misconduct when realistically able.
Bystander liability
The federal civil doctrine imposing responsibility on an officer who fails to intervene in another officer's constitutional violation.
Duty to report
The separate obligation to notify appropriate authorities and document misconduct.
Duty to render aid
The obligation to request or provide appropriate medical assistance.
Failure to protect
A broader constitutional concept often used in custody or known-danger cases; not always identical to police bystander liability.
Direct liability
Responsibility for personally using, ordering, or assisting unlawful force.
Supervisory liability
Responsibility based on a supervisor's own conduct, authorization, or deliberate indifference rather than title alone.
Underlying violation
The constitutional misconduct that the bystander allegedly should have stopped.
Realistic opportunity
Sufficient time and practical ability to take reasonable preventive action.
Qualified immunity
A doctrine that may shield an individual officer from civil damages unless clearly established federal law was violated.
Municipal liability
Local-government liability for a policy-caused constitutional violation under Monell.
Criminal willfulness
The demanding federal intent requirement under 18 U.S.C. § 242.
Active bystandership
A training and organizational approach treating prevention and peer correction as professional duties.
Retaliation
Adverse action against an officer or witness for intervening, reporting, or cooperating with investigation.

Historical and Continuing Significance

The duty to intervene assigns responsibility not only to the officer directly applying unlawful force but also to officers who knowingly permit preventable harm.

For African American communities, the issue is connected to a long history in which officers ignored lynching, mob violence, segregation enforcement, custodial abuse, and brutality by fellow officials.

George Floyd's murder demonstrated that multiple trained officers can remain passive during a prolonged and visible constitutional violation.

Effective intervention protects residents, officers, agencies, and the legitimacy of law enforcement by stopping misconduct before it becomes death, serious injury, criminal prosecution, or institutional crisis.

A written rule is insufficient without realistic training, anti-retaliation protection, truthful reporting, medical response, consistent discipline, and leadership that rewards intervention rather than silence.

Summary

The duty to intervene requires an officer to take reasonable action when another officer is violating a person's rights and the observing officer knows of the violation and has a realistic opportunity to prevent or stop it.

Federal civil courts commonly call the doctrine bystander liability. A claim ordinarily requires an underlying constitutional violation, knowledge, opportunity, ability, and failure to act.

Intervention can be verbal, physical, supervisory, tactical, or medical. It is separate from duties to report misconduct, render aid, document truthfully, and preserve evidence.

Duration matters. An officer may lack time to prevent one unexpected blow but have a clear opportunity during prolonged restraint, repeated strikes, a sustained canine bite, or delayed medical care.

Kueng and Thao were federally convicted for willfully failing to intervene in Chauvin's unreasonable force against George Floyd. Kueng, Thao, and Lane were separately convicted for failing to provide medical aid.

The Justice Department currently requires covered officers to intervene against prohibited force and to request or render medical aid. Colorado, Minnesota, Washington, Maryland, Buffalo, and other jurisdictions enacted additional statutory or local duties.

Effective implementation requires clear policy, realistic training, intervention across rank, anti-retaliation protection, independent reporting, medical response, supervision, discipline, and public accountability.

H.R. 5361 proposes a clear federal intervention duty and training program but remained introduced rather than enacted as of August 1, 2026.
