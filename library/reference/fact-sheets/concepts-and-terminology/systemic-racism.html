Systemic Racism

A society-wide pattern in which interconnected institutions, laws, markets, practices, and distributions of power repeatedly produce or preserve racial inequality.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Social-science concept, historical-analysis framework, civil-rights subject, and public-policy problem.
Basic definition
Systemic racism is racial inequality generated or sustained by the interaction of institutions, laws, markets, social practices, political power, and historical conditions across a society or major social system.
Primary unit of analysis
A network of connected systems rather than one isolated person, decision, organization, or policy.
Central mechanism
Decisions in one domain affect opportunities and risks in other domains, allowing racial advantage and disadvantage to accumulate, reproduce, and reinforce themselves over time.
Possible forms
Explicit racial law, institutional discrimination, segregated markets, unequal resource distribution, inherited wealth differences, discretionary enforcement, exclusionary geography, political underrepresentation, biased data systems, and formally neutral rules operating within unequal conditions.
Intent
Systemic racism may include deliberate discrimination, but a systemic pattern can persist after explicit racial rules are repealed because institutions inherit prior arrangements, incentives, boundaries, assets, and decision practices.
Evidence commonly examined
Historical records, laws, policies, institutional practices, geographic patterns, administrative data, comparative outcomes, resource flows, enforcement patterns, wealth and mobility measures, complaint records, audits, and causal research.
Important evidentiary caution
A racial disparity can identify a condition requiring investigation, but disparity alone does not establish a systemic cause. Reliable analysis must identify mechanisms, institutional connections, historical pathways, comparison groups, and plausible alternative explanations.

Institutional racism focuses on the operation of a particular institution or sector. Systemic racism examines how several institutions and sectors interact to create a durable pattern.
Relationship to structural racism
Many writers use systemic racism and structural racism interchangeably. Others use systemic racism to emphasize interacting processes and feedback loops and structural racism to emphasize the durable architecture of laws, institutions, and social arrangements.
Legal relationship
Systemic racism is an analytical concept rather than one universal legal cause of action. Courts and enforcement agencies address specific constitutional violations, discriminatory policies, patterns or practices, and statutory claims under different legal standards.

The term is widely used in scholarship, public health, civil-rights analysis, organizational review, and political debate, although definitions, evidence requirements, and proposed remedies vary.

The concept explains how racial inequality can remain durable when no single policy, institution, or actor fully controls the overall outcome.

What Is Systemic Racism?

Systemic racism is a pattern of racial inequality produced and reproduced through the interaction of multiple institutions and social systems. It concerns how law, government, housing, education, labor markets, finance, healthcare, criminal justice, transportation, technology, and political power operate together rather than in isolation.

The term does not mean that every person within a society holds the same beliefs, that every institution produces identical outcomes, or that every racial disparity has one cause. It identifies a system-level problem when connected rules and practices repeatedly distribute opportunity, risk, resources, status, and public authority along racial lines.

Systemic racism may contain openly discriminatory laws and conduct. It may also persist through inherited arrangements that no longer use explicit racial language. Residential segregation, school boundaries, property-based financing, employment networks, transportation access, criminal records, lending practices, and wealth accumulation can interact in ways that preserve earlier inequalities.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains systemic racism principally in the United States. It addresses the concept's development, historical foundations, major operating mechanisms, representative examples, methods of evidence and measurement, legal relationships, policy responses, criticisms, and distinctions from related terms.

It does not assume that all unequal outcomes are caused by racism, that race is the only relevant social factor, or that every institution participates in the same way. Economic class, geography, age, disability, family structure, immigration status, education, local policy, and individual behavior may also affect outcomes. Systemic analysis asks how these factors interact with racial classification, historical discrimination, institutional authority, and unequal power.

The concept should therefore be used as an explanatory framework supported by evidence. A sound analysis identifies the relevant systems, their connections, the historical pathway, the mechanism of reproduction, the affected populations, and the evidence linking those elements.

Development of the Concept

Earlier Institutional Analysis

Long before the phrase systemic racism became common, abolitionists, civil-rights organizers, historians, economists, and sociologists described racial oppression as a connected political, legal, economic, and social order. Their work examined slavery, disfranchisement, segregation, labor exploitation, violence, housing exclusion, unequal schooling, and restricted citizenship as mutually reinforcing institutions.

Institutional Racism

Stokely Carmichael, later known as Kwame Ture, and Charles V. Hamilton gave the term institutional racism major public prominence in their 1967 book Black Power: The Politics of Liberation in America. Their distinction between visible individual acts and less visible institutional processes helped move analysis beyond personal prejudice.

Systemic-Racism Theory

Sociologist Joe R. Feagin developed a prominent theory of systemic racism in his 2006 book Systemic Racism: A Theory of Oppression. His approach emphasized the historical foundation of racial hierarchy, the allocation of material resources, institutional power, racial ideology, recurring discriminatory practices, and the capacity of a dominant racial group to reproduce advantage across generations.

Structural-Racism Research

Public-health researchers, historians, economists, legal scholars, and social scientists increasingly used structural racism to examine the totality of interacting systems that shape exposure, opportunity, wealth, health, and political power. In much contemporary scholarship, systemic and structural racism overlap substantially.

Public Use

The phrase entered broad public debate during national controversies over policing, residential segregation, health disparities, voting access, education, employment, and wealth. Its increased use also produced disputes over definition, proof, responsibility, and remedy.

Essential Elements

Multiple Connected Institutions

The pattern extends across more than one organization or policy domain. Housing decisions may shape school access, transportation, employment, tax bases, healthcare, environmental exposure, policing, and wealth.

Durable Racial Pattern

The inequality is not merely a brief fluctuation or an isolated incident. It persists, recurs, or reappears through regular social and institutional operation.

Mechanism of Reproduction

A systemic claim should identify how the pattern continues. Mechanisms may include inheritance, geographic segregation, differential access to credit, cumulative legal penalties, unequal political power, institutional incentives, or feedback loops.

Distribution of Power and Resources

Systems allocate property, income, education, safety, legal protection, public services, political influence, professional authority, and access to institutions. Systemic racism concerns how these allocations become racially patterned.

Historical Continuity

Present conditions may be connected to earlier laws and practices through property ownership, neighborhood boundaries, institutional locations, accumulated wealth, family resources, political representation, and inherited administrative systems.

Institutional Independence and Interaction

No central authority must coordinate every result. Independent institutions can respond to the same incentives, inherit the same historical conditions, use one another's records, and reinforce one another's decisions.

Capacity for Adaptation

Systems may preserve unequal results while changing formal rules. When one exclusionary mechanism becomes unlawful or politically unacceptable, institutions may rely on new criteria, boundaries, technologies, or discretionary practices.

How Systemic Racism Works

Path Dependence

Earlier decisions shape later options. A neighborhood denied credit and public investment may develop lower property values, weaker infrastructure, fewer businesses, and a smaller tax base. Later institutions then make decisions using those inherited conditions as if they were historically neutral.

Cumulative Advantage and Disadvantage

Small differences can compound across a lifetime and across generations. Unequal access to schools, employment, homeownership, credit, healthcare, legal representation, and professional networks can produce larger differences in income, wealth, health, and political influence.

Interinstitutional Transfer

One institution's decision becomes another institution's input. School records affect college and employment opportunities; criminal records affect housing and work; neighborhood location affects insurance and credit; employment affects healthcare; and property ownership affects school finance and family wealth.

Feedback Loops

Institutional outcomes can generate data used to justify future decisions. Concentrated enforcement produces more recorded violations in heavily monitored areas. Those records may be used to justify additional enforcement. Underinvestment can reduce measured performance, which may then be cited as a reason to withhold further investment.

Geographic Concentration

Residential segregation concentrates or separates populations from employment, transportation, quality schools, medical services, safe infrastructure, environmental protections, and political resources. Geography can connect otherwise distinct institutions.

Formal Neutrality Within Unequal Conditions

A rule may apply in the same language to everyone while operating within unequal starting conditions. Property-based school funding, credit requirements, legacy preferences, employment referrals, and location-based eligibility rules may preserve earlier inequalities if their design does not account for the conditions from which they emerged.

Discretion and Unequal Scrutiny

Officials and institutions may have broad authority to stop, search, discipline, admit, hire, promote, insure, lend, diagnose, investigate, or prosecute. Repeated differences in scrutiny or leniency can accumulate into system-level inequality.

Unequal Political Influence

Groups with greater wealth, representation, organizational capacity, access to officials, and control over institutions can shape laws, budgets, boundaries, and public priorities. Political inequality can protect or reproduce inequalities in other systems.

Racial Ideology and Narratives

Beliefs about criminality, intelligence, work, property, citizenship, dependency, neighborhood risk, or cultural fitness can influence institutional decisions. Narratives may legitimize unequal arrangements by presenting them as natural, deserved, efficient, or unrelated to history.

Technology and Historical Data

Automated systems may learn from data generated by unequal institutions. Predictive policing, credit scoring, hiring software, medical algorithms, risk assessments, facial recognition, and fraud detection can reproduce prior patterns unless designers test data quality, proxies, error rates, and unequal effects.

Historical Foundations in the United States

Colonial Racial Classification and Slavery

Colonial and state governments created racial categories through property law, labor law, citizenship, testimony, movement restrictions, taxation, marriage, inheritance, education, policing, and punishment. Slavery linked racial status to labor extraction, property ownership, political power, and hereditary condition.

Native Dispossession and Territorial Expansion

Federal and state expansion displaced Native nations, redistributed land, imposed treaties and reservation systems, and created racialized rules of citizenship and property. These histories differed from Black enslavement but formed part of the broader development of race and state power in the United States.

Reconstruction and Counterrevolution

The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments and Reconstruction legislation attempted to establish freedom, national citizenship, equal protection, and voting rights. Black Codes, political violence, labor coercion, court decisions, administrative obstruction, and the restoration of white-controlled state governments limited those gains.

Jim Crow

Jim Crow connected segregation, disfranchisement, unequal education, labor exclusion, racial violence, discriminatory courts, restricted housing, and unequal public services. The system was sustained through statutes, local ordinances, private practice, police power, political control, and social enforcement.

Federal Housing and Economic Policy

Twentieth-century housing, mortgage, transportation, zoning, and development policies expanded homeownership and suburban growth while many Black households faced exclusion, redlining, racially restrictive covenants, real-estate steering, discriminatory lending, and neighborhood violence. These patterns shaped later differences in property ownership, school access, transportation, and family wealth.

Labor Markets and Social Provision

Occupational segregation, discriminatory unions, unequal access to apprenticeships, agricultural and domestic-work exclusions, segregated workplaces, and local administration limited access to wages, benefits, pensions, and economic security. Employment differences affected housing, healthcare, education, and wealth.

Civil Rights Reform

Brown v. Board of Education, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Fair Housing Act of 1968, and other reforms invalidated major forms of legal segregation and discrimination. Enforcement, implementation, local resistance, private conduct, and inherited inequality nevertheless prevented formal legal change from producing immediate equality.

Post-Civil-Rights-Era Persistence

After explicit racial classifications became less common, debates shifted toward residential segregation, unequal schools, criminal justice, wealth, healthcare, political representation, environmental exposure, and the racial operation of formally neutral policies. Systemic-racism analysis examines how these domains continue to interact.

Major Systems and Institutional Connections

Housing and Land Use

Housing determines access to neighborhood schools, transportation, public services, employment, environmental conditions, political districts, property appreciation, and family stability. Zoning, lending, insurance, appraisal, public housing, infrastructure, taxation, and real-estate practices can reinforce one another.

Education

School assignment, district boundaries, local tax capacity, disciplinary practices, advanced-course access, transportation, teacher distribution, and college admissions affect educational opportunity. Education then influences employment, income, civic participation, health, and wealth.

Employment and Labor Markets

Hiring networks, credential requirements, occupational segregation, apprenticeships, promotion practices, workplace discipline, union access, and geographic job location influence income and benefits. Employment also affects housing eligibility, healthcare, retirement security, and intergenerational resources.

Finance and Wealth

Access to mortgages, business credit, insurance, investment, inheritance, and appreciating assets affects economic security. Wealth differences shape the ability to move, attend school, withstand unemployment, obtain legal assistance, start businesses, and transfer resources to children.

Policing and Criminal Justice

Enforcement concentration, stops, searches, arrests, charging, bail, legal representation, sentencing, incarceration, fines, fees, probation, and criminal records can interact. Criminal-justice contact may affect employment, housing, education, voting, family stability, and debt.

Healthcare and Public Health

Insurance, employment, neighborhood conditions, environmental exposure, transportation, hospital access, provider distribution, medical treatment, stress, and public investment shape health. Health outcomes then affect education, employment, income, and family resources.

Voting and Political Representation

Registration rules, district boundaries, polling access, felony-disfranchisement laws, campaign finance, representation, and administrative capacity influence political power. Political power affects budgets, appointments, enforcement priorities, infrastructure, schools, housing, and public services.

Transportation and Infrastructure

Highway construction, transit routes, road maintenance, pedestrian safety, utility service, broadband, and public investment determine access to employment, schools, healthcare, markets, and civic life. Infrastructure decisions can divide neighborhoods or expose communities to pollution and displacement.

Environmental Regulation

Land use, industrial siting, zoning, permitting, enforcement, disaster response, and infrastructure maintenance can distribute environmental hazards unequally. Environmental exposure may interact with housing constraints, political influence, healthcare access, and wealth.

Technology and Data Systems

Institutions increasingly share data and rely on automated decision systems. Historical inequalities may enter algorithms through training data, proxy variables, missing data, enforcement records, and design assumptions, allowing one system's bias to spread to another.

Representative Example: Housing, Schools, and Wealth

Residential segregation provides a representative example of systemic interaction because housing connects families to many other institutions.

During the twentieth century, Black households often encountered racially restrictive covenants, discriminatory real-estate practices, neighborhood violence, unequal mortgage access, redlining, and exclusion from many suburban developments. Federal, state, local, and private actors participated in different parts of this process.

Residential location then affected school assignment, local tax resources, transportation, job access, public services, environmental exposure, policing, and property appreciation. Families excluded from appreciating housing markets had fewer opportunities to accumulate home equity and transfer wealth.

Later institutions could rely on neighborhood property values, credit history, school performance, inherited wealth, or geographic risk without openly classifying applicants by race. The resulting inequality was therefore maintained through several linked systems rather than one continuing segregation statute.

This example does not establish that every neighborhood difference results from racial discrimination. It demonstrates why system-level analysis must examine historical policy, market behavior, institutional connections, and cumulative consequences together.

Additional Illustrative Patterns

Municipal Revenue and Policing

A municipality may rely on fines and fees, direct police toward revenue-producing enforcement, process cases through local courts, suspend licenses for nonpayment, issue warrants, and impose additional penalties. When these practices concentrate on a racially identifiable population, policing, courts, debt, transportation, and employment can reinforce one another.

School Discipline and Justice Contact

School discipline may remove students from instruction, refer them to law enforcement, produce records, and increase disengagement. Those consequences can affect graduation, employment, and later justice-system contact. A systemic inquiry examines rules, discretion, school resources, policing, disability status, neighborhood conditions, and comparative treatment.

Employment, Insurance, and Health

Occupational segregation can place workers in jobs with lower pay, less stability, greater physical risk, and weaker benefits. Employment-linked insurance then affects access to treatment, while illness can reduce earnings and increase debt. The result crosses labor, healthcare, finance, and family systems.

Criminal Records and Civil Opportunity

Arrest and conviction records may affect hiring, occupational licensing, housing, education, credit, voting, and family stability. When enforcement and sentencing are racially unequal, collateral consequences can spread the effect of criminal justice into other institutions.

Evidence and Measurement

Define the System

Researchers must identify the geographic area, period, population, institutions, policies, and outcomes under study. A claim about one city, state, agency, or decade should not automatically be generalized to the entire nation.

Identify the Mechanism

A sound analysis explains how one institution affects another. Correlation among racial disparities is not enough. The research should trace rules, decisions, resource flows, geographic connections, eligibility requirements, incentives, or feedback processes.

Use Historical Evidence

Historical records can establish how present boundaries, assets, institutions, and distributions developed. Relevant materials include statutes, maps, property records, agency manuals, court decisions, administrative correspondence, budgets, and institutional archives.

Use Comparative Data

Researchers may compare similarly situated people, neighborhoods, schools, institutions, or jurisdictions. Comparisons should account for relevant variables and avoid treating race as a biological cause of social outcomes.

Examine Distribution, Not Only Averages

Average outcomes may conceal differences in exposure, severity, geography, wealth, treatment, or access. Analysis may require distributions, longitudinal data, cohort studies, administrative records, audits, and qualitative evidence.

Test Alternative Explanations

Reliable research considers competing causes and explains why the proposed mechanism fits the evidence. Poverty, age, geography, education, health, family resources, local policy, and institutional selection may be relevant without eliminating the need to examine racial history and discrimination.

Avoid False Precision

Systemic racism is not measured by one universal index. Different studies use segregation, wealth, incarceration, voting, health, education, lending, employment, or institutional-policy indicators. Measures should match the defined mechanism.

Distinguish Disparity from Discrimination

A disparity is a measured difference. Discrimination is unequal treatment or exclusion based on a protected characteristic. Systemic racism is a broader explanatory claim about connected processes. These categories may overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

Relationship to Law

Systemic racism is not itself a single constitutional test or statutory cause of action. Legal claims must identify the government action, employer practice, housing policy, voting rule, law-enforcement pattern, funding recipient, or other conduct governed by a particular law.

Equal Protection

The Equal Protection Clause prohibits government racial classifications and intentional racial discrimination. Under Washington v. Davis, a racially disproportionate effect from a facially neutral government policy does not by itself establish a federal constitutional violation; discriminatory purpose must generally be shown.

Discriminatory Purpose

Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp. identified factors relevant to discriminatory purpose, including impact, historical background, sequence of events, departures from normal procedure, and legislative or administrative history.

Statutory Claims

Federal and state statutes prohibit discrimination in areas including employment, housing, voting, education, credit, public accommodations, federally assisted programs, and law enforcement. The applicable legal standard depends on the statute, claim, jurisdiction, and remedy.

Pattern-or-Practice Enforcement

Civil-rights agencies may investigate a discriminatory policy or regular practice that has broad effects. In legal usage, a pattern or practice generally concerns conduct that is regular rather than isolated. This is narrower and more defined than the full social-science concept of systemic racism.

Legal and Historical Findings

A court may reject a particular claim because the required legal elements were not proved while historians or social scientists continue to examine broader inequality. Conversely, a legal finding of discrimination concerns specified defendants, conduct, and law and should not automatically be generalized beyond the record.

Governmental and Institutional Responses

Constitutional Amendments and Civil-Rights Legislation

The Reconstruction Amendments, federal civil-rights statutes, voting protections, fair-housing laws, and employment-discrimination laws created legal tools against racial exclusion. Their effectiveness has depended on enforcement authority, administrative capacity, judicial interpretation, political support, and access to remedies.

Desegregation and Institutional Reform

Courts, agencies, legislatures, school districts, employers, housing authorities, and local governments have used desegregation orders, consent decrees, compliance plans, revised boundaries, new hiring systems, oversight, and data reporting to change institutional practices.

Pattern-or-Practice Investigations

The Department of Justice and other enforcement agencies may investigate whether discrimination or rights violations reflect regular institutional practice. Remedies can include policy revision, supervision, training, data collection, complaint systems, independent monitoring, and court-enforced agreements.

Targeted Investment

Governments and institutions may direct resources toward schools, neighborhoods, transportation, health, housing, environmental remediation, business development, and infrastructure where historical exclusion or underinvestment produced persistent disadvantage.

Universal Reform with Distributional Review

Some policies apply universally but are designed and evaluated for unequal access or effect. Examples include public-health coverage, transit expansion, school funding reform, wage policy, child benefits, and procedural protections.

Data and Accountability

Institutions may collect demographic data, conduct audits, publish outcomes, test algorithms, examine complaints, compare similarly situated groups, and establish independent review. Data must be interpreted carefully and should not substitute for investigation of mechanisms.

Community Participation

Affected communities may contribute evidence about practical barriers, enforcement, service quality, and unintended consequences. Participation can improve diagnosis, but institutions remain responsible for lawful administration and evidence-based evaluation.

Principles for System-Level Reform

Map Institutional Connections

Reform should identify how decisions move across housing, schools, employment, healthcare, finance, transportation, criminal justice, and public administration. Changing one institution may fail if connected systems reproduce the same barrier.

Address Upstream Rules

Programs that treat consequences without changing eligibility rules, boundaries, incentives, or resource distribution may provide relief while leaving the generating mechanism intact.

Control Discretion

Written standards, documentation, supervision, review, and appeal can reduce unequal treatment in decisions involving discipline, enforcement, hiring, lending, treatment, and access to benefits.

Correct Feedback Loops

Institutions should examine whether their own records reflect unequal prior enforcement or access. Data generated by a biased process should not be treated as neutral evidence for repeating that process.

Measure Outcomes and Mechanisms

Evaluation should determine whether the policy changed the targeted institutional process, whether benefits reached the intended population, and whether new burdens or avoidance strategies emerged.

Preserve Individual Rights

System-level reform must remain consistent with constitutional and statutory limits. Broad social goals do not eliminate requirements concerning equal treatment, due process, evidence, transparency, and lawful authority.

Use Periodic Review

Institutions, markets, technology, and populations change. A reform that succeeds under one set of conditions may become ineffective or create new problems. Periodic review should test results rather than assume permanent success.

Debates and Criticisms

Definition and Scope

Critics argue that the term is sometimes used so broadly that it becomes difficult to test or falsify. Supporters respond that narrow analysis can miss the interaction of institutions and the cumulative nature of racial inequality. The strongest use of the concept defines the system and mechanism precisely.

Intent and Responsibility

Debate often concerns whether racism requires conscious intent. Some definitions reserve the term for purposeful conduct. Others include systems that reproduce racial hierarchy without a present decision maker intending the overall result. Fact sheets and research should state which definition is being used.

Disparity and Causation

Critics warn that unequal outcomes may be attributed to systemic racism without adequate causal evidence. This criticism is valid when analysis relies on disparity alone. A defensible systemic claim requires historical, institutional, and causal evidence.

Agency and Heterogeneity

Broad racial categories may conceal differences in class, geography, ancestry, immigration history, age, family resources, and individual choices. Systemic analysis should not erase personal agency or treat every member of a group as having the same experience.

Policy Tradeoffs

Proposed remedies may involve costs, legal restrictions, administrative complexity, competing public priorities, and unintended consequences. Evaluation should compare alternatives and measure results rather than assume that a policy is effective because its stated purpose is equitable.

Political Use

The term is used in advocacy, scholarship, government, and partisan debate. Institutional reference writing should separate evidence from political messaging and explain the relevant mechanism before applying the label.

Historical and Continuing Significance

Systemic racism matters because it explains why racial inequality can survive the repeal of explicitly discriminatory laws. Institutions may continue to operate through boundaries, assets, records, incentives, and distributions created during earlier racial regimes.

The concept also shows why isolated reforms may have limited effects. Desegregating one school system does not by itself change housing segregation, transportation, wealth, employment, healthcare, or local tax capacity. Reform in one domain may be weakened by conditions in another.

At the same time, system-level language requires disciplined evidence. The larger the claim, the more important it becomes to define scope, identify causal pathways, distinguish institutions, examine counterexamples, and measure change.

Key Distinctions

Systemic racism
A racial pattern reproduced through the interaction of multiple institutions, laws, markets, practices, and distributions of power.
Structural racism
The durable societal architecture through which institutions and historical arrangements distribute opportunity and risk by race. The term often overlaps with systemic racism.
Institutional racism
Racial inequality produced or sustained through the policies, routines, incentives, or decisions of a particular institution or institutional sector.
Interpersonal racism
Racial prejudice, hostility, stereotyping, exclusion, or discriminatory conduct between individuals.
Internalized racism
The acceptance or incorporation of racial hierarchy, stigma, or negative racial beliefs by members of a racialized group or by individuals within society.
Racial discrimination
Unequal treatment, exclusion, or disadvantage imposed because of race, color, ethnicity, or associated characteristics.
Racial disparity
A measurable difference between racial groups. A disparity may result from discrimination, historical inequality, geography, socioeconomic conditions, institutional selection, or several interacting causes.
White supremacy
An ideology or social order assigning greater status, authority, resources, or entitlement to white people. Systemic racism may reproduce white advantage, but the two terms identify different analytical objects.
Systemic discrimination
A legal or administrative term often used for a policy, pattern, practice, or class-wide form of discrimination with broad effect. It may concern race or another protected characteristic.
Racial equity
A policy objective concerned with fair access, treatment, opportunity, and outcomes, often including the correction of avoidable barriers linked to historical or current discrimination.

Relationship to Related Subjects

Systemic racism is broader than institutional racism because it concerns connections among institutions. A discriminatory employer practice is institutional; its interaction with housing, transportation, education, healthcare, and wealth may become systemic.

Systemic racism is closely related to structural racism. In many sources, the terms are functionally interchangeable. Where a distinction is maintained, systemic emphasizes dynamic interaction, reinforcement, and feedback, while structural emphasizes the durable architecture within which those processes operate.

Systemic racism is not identical to segregation. Segregation is the separation of racial groups in space, institutions, or services. Segregation can serve as one major mechanism within a systemic racial order.

It is also not identical to white supremacy. White supremacy identifies an ideology or social order of racial hierarchy. Systemic racism identifies the processes through which racial hierarchy is produced and reproduced across connected systems.

Summary

Systemic racism is a society-wide pattern in which connected institutions, laws, markets, practices, and distributions of power repeatedly produce or preserve racial inequality. It can include explicit discrimination, but it can also persist through inherited boundaries, assets, data, incentives, and institutional relationships after openly racial rules are repealed.

Its principal mechanisms include path dependence, cumulative advantage and disadvantage, geographic segregation, interinstitutional transfer, feedback loops, unequal discretion, political inequality, and the reuse of historical data. Housing, education, employment, finance, criminal justice, healthcare, voting, transportation, and environmental policy can therefore influence one another.

A reliable systemic-racism analysis requires more than identifying a disparity. It must define the system, establish the historical and institutional connections, identify the mechanism, test alternative explanations, and distinguish social-science findings from the elements required by a particular legal claim. Effective reform must address the upstream rules and interactions that reproduce inequality rather than treating each outcome as an isolated event.
