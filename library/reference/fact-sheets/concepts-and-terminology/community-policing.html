Community Policing

A policing philosophy that uses sustained community partnerships, organizational change, and systematic problem solving to address crime, disorder, fear, and other locally defined public-safety concerns.

What Is Community Policing?

Community policing is a philosophy of police organization and public safety that relies on partnerships between law-enforcement agencies and the communities they serve. It seeks to identify recurring local problems, develop coordinated responses, and prevent harm rather than relying exclusively on emergency response, patrol, investigation, citation, and arrest after an incident occurs.

The U.S. Department of Justice COPS Office defines community policing through three connected components: community partnerships, organizational transformation, and problem solving. Under this model, outreach alone is insufficient. A department must align its leadership, personnel, information systems, training, deployment, supervision, and evaluation methods with collaborative and preventive work.

Community policing is not one standardized program. A neighborhood foot-patrol initiative, a citywide beat-meeting system, a tribal partnership, a youth-engagement program, and a recurring problem-solving project may all be described as community policing. Whether those activities amount to a genuine organizational strategy depends on how authority, resources, information, and accountability are structured.

Scope and Terminology

The terms community policing and community-oriented policing are usually used interchangeably in the United States. The word “community” may refer to a geographic neighborhood, a municipality, a tribal nation, a business district, a school population, a faith community, a demographic group, a group sharing a public-safety concern, or another population affected by police activity.

The breadth of the term creates both flexibility and ambiguity. Different groups within the same place may have conflicting priorities. Business owners may seek more visible enforcement, young residents may seek fewer stops, survivors may seek faster protection, unhoused residents may seek protection from displacement, and civil-rights organizations may seek stronger limits on surveillance and force.

A valid community-policing analysis must therefore ask who was consulted, who participated, whose concerns became official priorities, who exercised final authority, what resources were committed, and how the results were measured.

Historical Context

Modern community policing developed partly in response to dissatisfaction with the professional or reform model of policing that became influential during the twentieth century. That model emphasized centralized command, standardized procedures, motorized patrol, rapid response, technical expertise, and separation from local political influence.

Professionalization addressed genuine problems such as patronage and corruption, but it could also distance officers from residents and make police work increasingly reactive. Patrol cars allowed officers to cover larger areas while reducing routine face-to-face contact. Performance measures often emphasized response time, arrests, citations, and reported crime rather than problem resolution, public confidence, or quality of life.

Urban unrest, civil-rights conflict, discriminatory enforcement, police violence, and public distrust during the 1960s and 1970s exposed deep failures in police-community relations. Researchers and practitioners began testing foot patrol, team policing, neighborhood-based deployment, citizen meetings, problem analysis, and other approaches intended to reconnect police agencies with local populations.

Civil Rights and Police Legitimacy

Community policing cannot be understood apart from the history of unequal policing in the United States. African American, Latino, Native American, immigrant, poor, and politically marginalized communities have experienced discriminatory enforcement, exclusion from police employment, unequal protection, surveillance, brutality, and inadequate responses to victimization.

In this context, improved communication is not equivalent to legitimacy. Trust depends on lawful conduct, equal protection, transparency, accountability, fair treatment, reliable service, and the correction of institutional practices that produce unequal harm.

Community-policing programs may create opportunities for residents to identify problems and influence priorities. They may also fail when police agencies expect community cooperation without addressing misconduct, racial disparities, secrecy, or previous institutional harms.

Early Development

Experimental and reform efforts during the 1970s and early 1980s challenged assumptions about conventional patrol. The Kansas City Preventive Patrol Experiment found that changing the level of routine preventive patrol in marked vehicles did not produce the large effects on crime, public fear, or satisfaction that many officials expected. This encouraged greater attention to how police resources were deployed rather than assuming that generalized patrol visibility was sufficient.

The Newark Foot Patrol Experiment, published in 1981, found that added foot patrol did not reduce recorded crime in the study areas. Residents in foot-patrol areas nevertheless tended to feel safer and had more favorable attitudes toward police, while officers gained greater familiarity with local conditions. The experiment became influential because it showed that police presence could affect public perceptions and relationships even when recorded crime did not decline.

These studies did not by themselves create community policing. They contributed to a wider reconsideration of police visibility, neighborhood knowledge, public confidence, and the limits of reactive patrol.

Problem-Oriented Policing

Herman Goldstein's problem-oriented policing framework became one of the most important intellectual foundations of community policing. Goldstein argued that police agencies focused too heavily on the means of police work—such as calls handled, arrests made, and response times—rather than on the recurring problems those activities were supposed to solve.

Under problem-oriented policing, officers identify patterns, analyze causes and conditions, select tailored responses, and assess whether the response worked. A recurring series of assaults near a transit stop, for example, might require lighting changes, schedule adjustments, environmental redesign, youth outreach, business coordination, targeted enforcement, or victim services rather than repeated incident-by-incident response alone.

Problem-oriented policing and community policing overlap but are not identical. Problem-oriented policing can be conducted primarily by police analysts and officers. Community policing requires sustained partnerships and organizational arrangements that allow community knowledge and participation to influence the work.

Federal Expansion During the 1990s

Community policing became a major national policy during the 1990s. Title I of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 authorized large federal investments in police hiring, technology, training, and community-policing implementation.

The Department of Justice created the Office of Community Oriented Policing Services to administer grants and promote the model. Federal policy sought to fund 100,000 community-policing professionals and encourage state, local, tribal, and territorial agencies to adopt partnership and problem-solving strategies.

The hiring initiative expanded federal influence over local policing, but the numerical goal requires careful interpretation. Funding an officer position did not always mean a net increase of one permanent officer, and evaluations distinguished between grants awarded, officers funded, officers actually hired or redeployed, and positions retained after federal support ended.

Three Core Components

The COPS Office organizes community policing around three mutually dependent components. A program that contains only one component may provide useful services, but it does not represent the full institutional model.

Community Partnerships

Police agencies form continuing relationships with residents, community organizations, service providers, businesses, schools, public agencies, and other institutions. The objective is to exchange knowledge, identify priorities, coordinate resources, and develop shared responses.

Organizational Transformation

The agency changes its management, structure, personnel practices, training, deployment, information systems, supervision, internal culture, and performance measures to support partnership and problem solving. Without organizational transformation, community policing may remain an isolated unit or temporary project.

Problem Solving

Police and community partners identify recurring problems, analyze contributing conditions, develop responses appropriate to the problem, and evaluate outcomes. The focus is on reducing the conditions that generate repeated calls, harm, disorder, or fear.

Community Partnerships

Partnerships are intended to make public safety a shared institutional responsibility rather than an exclusive police function. A police department may work with housing officials on abandoned properties, public-health agencies on overdose patterns, schools on safe travel routes, transportation agencies on dangerous locations, service providers on crisis response, and residents on recurring neighborhood concerns.

Effective partnerships require more than attendance at public events. Participants need reliable communication, defined responsibilities, access to relevant information, a method for resolving disagreements, and opportunities to evaluate whether promised actions occurred.

Partnerships are vulnerable to unequal participation. Meetings held at inaccessible times, reliance on established organizations, language barriers, fear of retaliation, distrust of police, digital exclusion, and differences in political influence can allow a small group to be treated as the voice of an entire community.

Organizational Transformation

Community policing requires police organizations to support long-term local work. Officers need time to learn their assigned areas, attend meetings, analyze recurring problems, coordinate with other agencies, and follow through on commitments. Constant reassignment, call overload, short-term deployment, and evaluation based only on enforcement totals can undermine those responsibilities.

Agencies may decentralize some decision-making so that district commanders, supervisors, and officers can tailor responses to local conditions. Decentralization does not eliminate central accountability. Policies governing constitutional rights, force, stops, searches, data, discipline, and equal treatment must remain enforceable throughout the organization.

Training may include communication, conflict management, problem analysis, procedural justice, cultural competence, disability response, youth interaction, public-health coordination, and the use of nonenforcement options. Supervisors must reinforce these priorities through assignments, coaching, promotion, recognition, and review.

Problem-Solving Process

Community policing treats recurring incidents as possible symptoms of broader conditions. Instead of repeatedly responding to the same address, person, location, or conflict without examining the pattern, police and partners seek to identify why the problem persists.

Analysis may include calls for service, incident reports, victim accounts, resident observations, environmental conditions, time patterns, transportation routes, business practices, school schedules, housing conditions, service gaps, and previous interventions. Information should be relevant, lawfully obtained, and interpreted with attention to underreporting and biased enforcement data.

Responses may include enforcement, but they may also involve mediation, environmental design, licensing action, social services, civil remedies, education, youth programs, victim support, transportation changes, public-health intervention, or changes in agency procedure.

The SARA Model

The SARA model is a widely used problem-solving framework associated with problem-oriented and community policing. Its four stages are scanning, analysis, response, and assessment.

Scanning
Identify recurring incidents, harms, locations, populations, or conditions that appear to form a meaningful problem.
Analysis
Study the problem's frequency, location, participants, causes, incentives, environmental conditions, previous responses, and effects on different groups.
Response
Develop and implement a tailored strategy using police, community, governmental, private, and service resources as appropriate.
Assessment
Measure implementation, outcomes, unintended effects, displacement, public experience, and whether the response should continue, change, or end.

Common Practices

Community policing can include many practices. No single activity proves that an agency has adopted the full philosophy.

  • Assigning officers consistently to defined beats or neighborhoods.
  • Using foot, bicycle, or other visible patrol methods that support direct contact.
  • Holding regular beat meetings, listening sessions, town halls, and advisory-board meetings.
  • Creating neighborhood substations or shared public-safety offices.
  • Using officers or civilian specialists as community liaisons.
  • Working with youth, schools, recreation programs, and families.
  • Coordinating with housing, sanitation, transportation, licensing, code-enforcement, and public-health agencies.
  • Conducting joint problem-solving projects around recurring locations or harms.
  • Providing crime-prevention information and victim follow-up.
  • Using surveys, complaint data, meeting records, and community feedback to evaluate service.

How Community Policing Worked

In a functioning model, an agency first establishes stable geographic or community responsibility. Officers and supervisors become familiar with residents, institutions, recurring incidents, vulnerable populations, and local resources. Community members gain identifiable contacts within the department rather than relying only on emergency dispatch or changing personnel.

Police and community partners then identify priorities through meetings, service data, surveys, complaints, direct observation, and analysis. The agency determines which issues require police action, which require another public agency, and which require a coordinated response.

Responsibilities are assigned, resources are committed, and the response is implemented over a defined period. Results are reviewed using crime data where appropriate, but also using measures such as repeat calls, victimization, fear, accessibility, resident satisfaction, complaints, trust, service completion, and unintended consequences.

Representative Example: Newark Foot Patrol

The Newark Foot Patrol Experiment evaluated added and retained foot patrol posts in Newark, New Jersey. The study found no significant reduction in recorded crime attributable to the added foot patrols.

Residents in areas with foot patrol generally reported feeling safer and held more favorable views of police. Officers on foot also acquired detailed knowledge about local people and conditions that was difficult to develop from vehicle patrol alone.

The experiment is important because it separates different public-safety outcomes. A strategy may affect fear, satisfaction, communication, and neighborhood knowledge without producing the same effect on recorded crime. Community-policing evaluations must therefore specify what outcome is being measured.

Representative Example: Chicago Alternative Policing Strategy

Chicago introduced the Chicago Alternative Policing Strategy, commonly called CAPS, as a pilot in five police districts in April 1993 and later expanded the model citywide. CAPS reorganized policing around geographic beats, recurring beat meetings, problem solving, continuity of assignment, community participation, and coordination with other city services.

CAPS became one of the most extensively studied large-city community-policing programs. Its evaluations examined implementation, public awareness, meeting participation, neighborhood problems, satisfaction with police service, crime patterns, management systems, and differences among districts.

The Chicago experience demonstrated both the potential and the complexity of citywide implementation. Formal adoption did not guarantee equal participation, consistent officer commitment, durable problem solving, or identical results across neighborhoods.

The COPS Office

The Office of Community Oriented Policing Services is the principal federal institution associated with community policing in the United States. It administers grants, publishes guidance, funds training and technical assistance, and supports organizational reform and public-safety initiatives.

The office's work has included police hiring, tribal law enforcement, school safety, technology, collaborative reform, officer safety, violence prevention, crisis response, and resources for community partnerships and problem solving.

Federal grants can accelerate adoption, but long-term implementation depends on local law, budgets, leadership, labor agreements, staffing, community relationships, supervision, and institutional accountability after grant periods end.

Who Represents the Community?

Community policing assumes that police can work with “the community,” but communities are internally diverse. Residents differ by race, age, income, immigration status, disability, housing status, occupation, political influence, victimization, and experience with police.

Homeowners, landlords, business associations, clergy, neighborhood organizations, youth, tenants, unhoused people, formerly incarcerated residents, survivors, and people frequently stopped by police may identify different public-safety problems and prefer different solutions.

Representative participation therefore requires deliberate outreach, language access, accessible meeting times and locations, protection against retaliation, multiple forms of input, publication of decisions, and attention to groups that are commonly absent from formal civic processes.

The Community-Policing Officer's Role

Community policing expands the expected role of the patrol officer. Officers may be required to identify patterns, convene partners, communicate with residents, refer people to services, document unresolved conditions, coordinate with government agencies, and evaluate long-term responses.

This expanded role can increase professional discretion. Discretion may permit flexible, nonenforcement responses, but it can also produce inconsistency or bias when standards, supervision, data, and accountability are weak.

Agencies must therefore balance local initiative with constitutional rules, equal protection, clear policy, complaint systems, supervisory review, and transparent evaluation.

Data and Information

Community policing relies on both official data and community knowledge. Calls for service, reported crime, complaints, use-of-force records, traffic stops, response times, repeat locations, victimization surveys, public-health data, environmental conditions, and resident reports may all inform problem analysis.

Police data are not neutral records of all harm. They reflect reporting behavior, enforcement priorities, deployment choices, legal definitions, data quality, and public willingness to contact police. High enforcement in one neighborhood may produce more official incidents even when underlying conduct is similar elsewhere.

Information sharing must also respect privacy, civil rights, due process, confidentiality, and limits on intelligence gathering. Partnership should not become a method for obtaining broad surveillance access without clear public-safety purpose and legal authority.

Relationship to Procedural Justice

Procedural justice focuses on how authorities exercise power. Its commonly identified elements include allowing people a meaningful voice, making decisions neutrally, treating people with dignity and respect, and demonstrating trustworthy motives.

Community policing can create more opportunities for procedurally just interaction, but frequent contact does not automatically produce fair contact. An agency may hold meetings while continuing unlawful stops, disrespectful treatment, secrecy, or unequal enforcement.

Procedural justice is therefore a standard for the quality of police-community interaction. Community policing is a broader organizational framework for partnership, transformation, and problem solving.

Relationship to Broken-Windows and Order-Maintenance Policing

Community policing is sometimes associated with broken-windows or order-maintenance policing because both may address visible disorder and neighborhood conditions. The approaches are not identical.

Order-maintenance strategies may rely heavily on citations, stops, arrests, removals, and enforcement of low-level offenses. Community problem solving may instead use environmental repair, public services, mediation, outreach, housing intervention, business practices, or other noncriminal responses.

A program's label does not determine its actual character. A community-policing initiative that primarily expands low-level enforcement may produce different legal, racial, and public-trust consequences from one that emphasizes services and jointly selected solutions.

Relationship to Community Control of Police

Community policing ordinarily gives police agencies final institutional authority while inviting public participation, advice, information, and partnership. Community control of police seeks stronger civilian power over policy, budgets, discipline, leadership, oversight, or deployment.

A neighborhood advisory board may influence priorities without possessing legal authority to require agency action. A civilian oversight body may investigate complaints or review policy without directing day-to-day operations. These arrangements must be examined according to their actual powers rather than their names.

Community policing can coexist with civilian oversight or community-control proposals, but it does not by itself transfer governing authority from police institutions to residents.

Relationship to Reform, Defunding, and Abolition

Community policing is usually a police-reform strategy. It seeks to improve the legitimacy, responsiveness, knowledge, prevention capacity, and effectiveness of existing law-enforcement agencies.

Defund the Police proposals seek to reduce or redirect police funding and responsibilities. Some defunding proposals retain a smaller police institution and could incorporate community-policing practices. Others shift functions to civilian agencies and reduce the scope of police involvement.

Police abolition seeks the long-term replacement of conventional policing with different institutions for safety and accountability. Abolitionists frequently criticize community policing as an institutional-legitimacy strategy that expands police relationships and intelligence without changing the underlying coercive structure.

Evidence of Effectiveness

Community policing is difficult to evaluate because the term covers many different interventions. Programs vary in intensity, duration, community participation, officer training, organizational change, enforcement practices, and local conditions.

A major systematic review of community-oriented policing found generally positive effects on citizen satisfaction, perceptions of police legitimacy, and perceptions of disorder. It found limited effects on crime and fear of crime. The National Academies similarly concluded that evidence was stronger for improved satisfaction than for broad crime-control effects.

These findings do not mean that no community-policing program can reduce crime. Programs that include effective problem-oriented, place-based, or tailored prevention strategies may reduce particular harms. The evidence does mean that the general label “community policing” should not be treated as proof of crime reduction without program-specific evaluation.

Measuring Success

Evaluation should match the program's stated objectives. A program intended to improve trust should measure trust. A project intended to reduce repeat calls at a location should measure repeat calls and the conditions producing them. A youth-engagement initiative should not be evaluated only through arrest totals.

Relevant measures may include:

  • Victimization and reported crime.
  • Repeat incidents, repeat locations, and calls for service.
  • Public fear and perceptions of disorder.
  • Satisfaction with police service and willingness to report harm.
  • Perceptions of legitimacy, fairness, respect, and responsiveness.
  • Complaints, stops, searches, arrests, force, and racial or geographic disparities.
  • Participation rates and representation in community processes.
  • Completion of promised government or service actions.
  • Officer knowledge, assignment stability, and problem-solving activity.
  • Costs, sustainability, displacement, and unintended consequences.

Implementation Challenges

Community policing often conflicts with existing organizational incentives. Emergency calls, staffing shortages, specialized units, enforcement campaigns, overtime systems, promotion standards, and centralized command may take priority over long-term neighborhood work.

Officers assigned to community roles may lack authority, training, time, data support, or access to other public agencies. Residents may become frustrated when meetings identify problems but government institutions fail to act.

Leadership turnover can produce repeated relaunches under new names. Temporary grants may support positions or projects that disappear when funding ends. A specialized community-policing unit may also isolate the philosophy instead of changing the entire department.

Criticism: Symbolic Participation

One major criticism is that community policing can become a public-relations program. Departments may sponsor events, distribute information, attend meetings, or create advisory groups without allowing public concerns to alter policy, deployment, discipline, or resource allocation.

Symbolic participation can increase frustration because it invites residents to invest time without establishing a process for action or accountability. Agencies should publish priorities, responsibilities, timelines, decisions, and outcomes when possible.

Genuine partnership does not require police to accept every public demand, but it does require transparent explanation of which requests were accepted, rejected, redirected, or limited by law and resources.

Criticism: Surveillance and Intelligence Gathering

Community relationships can provide police with information about local conflicts, individuals, organizations, and activities. Supporters view lawful information sharing as essential to prevention and investigation.

Critics warn that partnership networks can become informal intelligence systems, especially in communities with histories of political surveillance, immigration enforcement, gang databases, counterterrorism investigations, or discriminatory monitoring.

Clear rules are needed concerning collection, retention, sharing, accuracy, access, correction, and use of information. Residents should not have to surrender broad privacy or political freedom as the price of receiving public safety services.

Criticism: Overpolicing and Unequal Priorities

Community demands may sometimes increase enforcement against marginalized people. Complaints about public space, youth, unhoused residents, informal commerce, noise, substance use, or disorder can produce stops, citations, arrests, displacement, and surveillance.

The residents most likely to attend official meetings may not be the people most affected by enforcement. Agencies must examine who benefits, who bears the burden, and whether noncriminal responses are available.

A community-selected policy is not automatically lawful, equitable, or effective. Constitutional rights and equal protection remain controlling standards even when an enforcement demand receives local political support.

Governmental and Institutional Response

Federal, state, local, and tribal governments have used grants, training, technical assistance, consent decrees, strategic plans, and administrative policy to promote community policing.

The President's Task Force on 21st Century Policing was established after the 2014 Ferguson protests. Its 2015 final report treated community policing as one part of a wider framework for trust, oversight, training, technology, crime reduction, and officer wellness.

These reforms presented community policing as a continuing institutional obligation rather than a temporary outreach campaign. Their effectiveness still depended on lawful conduct, accountability, resources, representative participation, and measurable implementation.

Current Status

Community policing remains an active federal, state, local, and tribal policy framework. The COPS Office continues to fund training, technical assistance, hiring, organizational reform, violence prevention, and partnership initiatives.

There is no national licensing rule or single implementation standard for the term. Agencies may use the same label for substantially different practices, from genuine organizational restructuring to limited outreach events.

Public debate after the 2020 murder of George Floyd intensified scrutiny of whether community policing can correct structural problems or merely improve the public image of existing institutions. The model therefore remains influential, contested, and highly dependent on local design.

Summary

Community policing is a policing philosophy built around community partnerships, organizational transformation, and systematic problem solving. It developed in response to the limits of reactive patrol, weak police-community relations, civil-rights conflict, and the need to address recurring public-safety problems through more than incident-by-incident enforcement.

The model may include stable beat assignments, foot patrol, community meetings, local problem analysis, coordination with public agencies, and nonenforcement responses. Its effectiveness depends on whether the police organization changes its incentives, supervision, deployment, information systems, accountability, and decision-making rather than treating community engagement as a separate public-relations function.

Research provides stronger support for improvements in public satisfaction and some perceptions of legitimacy and disorder than for broad, consistent reductions in crime or fear. Community policing remains a major police-reform framework, but it is contested because partnership does not automatically produce representation, fairness, accountability, community power, or structural change.
