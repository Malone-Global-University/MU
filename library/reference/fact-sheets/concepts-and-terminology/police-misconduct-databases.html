Police Misconduct Databases

Governmental and nongovernmental record systems used to document complaints, sustained misconduct, discipline, decertification, credibility problems, civil liability, employment history, and other information relevant to police accountability and hiring.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Police-accountability concept, employment-screening system, public-records issue, officer-certification mechanism, prosecutor-disclosure tool, and data-governance subject.
Basic definition
A police misconduct database is a structured record system containing allegations, findings, discipline, decertification, litigation, credibility information, employment history, or related records concerning law-enforcement officers.
No single national database
The United States does not operate one complete public database containing every complaint, finding, lawsuit, settlement, termination, resignation, certification action, and criminal case involving every federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial officer.
Major database types
Internal complaint systems, disciplinary databases, early-intervention systems, state certification records, decertification indexes, federal employment-accountability databases, Brady–Giglio files, civil-liability databases, and public registries.
Complaint database
A system recording allegations submitted by civilians, officers, supervisors, courts, prosecutors, or other sources.
Disciplinary database
A system recording findings, corrective action, suspension, demotion, termination, retraining, reprimand, or other employment consequences.
Decertification database
A system recording revocation, suspension, surrender, denial, or other action involving the state license or certification required to serve as a peace officer.
Early-intervention system
An internal risk-management system using multiple indicators—such as complaints, force, pursuits, injuries, lawsuits, or missed court appearances—to identify possible need for supervisory review or support.
Brady–Giglio system
A prosecutor or agency record system identifying potential credibility, bias, misconduct, or impeachment information concerning law-enforcement witnesses.
Civil-liability database
A system tracking lawsuits, judgments, settlements, legal fees, indemnification, claim type, involved officers, and corrective action.
Public registry
A website or data portal allowing members of the public to search or obtain misconduct-related records, subject to governing law.
Confidential hiring index
A restricted system allowing authorized agencies to identify that another agency possesses relevant records and request further information.
National Decertification Index
An IADLEST-hosted registry of certificate or license revocation actions reported by participating state government certification agencies.
NDI access
Access is generally controlled by each state's peace-officer standards and training or certifying agency rather than provided as a universal public name-search system.
National Law Enforcement Accountability Database
A Justice Department system launched in December 2023 for authorized federal users and designed principally to improve federal law-enforcement hiring, assignments, promotion, and task-force screening.
NLEAD structure
The system was designed as a pointer database indicating that a source agency holds a qualifying record rather than necessarily storing the full underlying personnel file.
NLEAD scope
The first federal report covered final or adjudicated qualifying federal-officer incidents occurring from 2018 through 2023.
NLEAD 2024 snapshot
As of September 20, 2024, the database contained 4,790 misconduct-incident records involving 4,011 federal law-enforcement officers.
Largest reported NLEAD category
About 63 percent of incidents were sustained complaints or disciplinary records based on findings of serious misconduct.
NLEAD public access
NLEAD was not created as a public officer-name registry; public reporting was aggregate and anonymized, while record-level access was restricted to authorized users.
Current NLEAD status issue
Executive Order 14074, which directed creation and use of NLEAD, was revoked in January 2025. The Justice Department's current systems-of-record page continued to list NLEAD in 2026, but public official materials reviewed for this fact sheet do not clearly establish that every earlier mandatory-use requirement continued unchanged.
Allegation versus finding
An allegation records a claim; it does not by itself establish misconduct. Databases must preserve disposition, evidentiary status, appeal, and correction information.
Decertification versus termination
Termination ends employment with one agency. Decertification can prevent employment as an officer throughout the certifying jurisdiction.
Resignation under investigation
An officer may leave before an investigation or disciplinary process is completed. Whether that event is reported and whether the case continues varies by jurisdiction.
Wandering officer
A commonly used term for an officer who leaves one law-enforcement agency after misconduct or warning signs and obtains employment with another agency.
Primary accountability purpose
Preventing agencies from hiring officers without learning of serious prior misconduct, decertification, dishonesty, force, discrimination, or unresolved investigation.
Primary fairness purpose
Preventing inaccurate, stale, duplicated, mistaken, or unadjudicated information from being treated as proven misconduct without notice or an opportunity to correct the record.
Public-transparency purpose
Allowing communities, courts, journalists, researchers, defendants, policymakers, and oversight bodies to evaluate police conduct and institutional response.
Privacy concerns
Personnel privacy, victim confidentiality, witness safety, medical information, active investigations, officer home information, and identity theft.
Current federal registry proposal
H.R. 5361, the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2025, proposes a publicly searchable National Police Misconduct Registry. It remained introduced rather than enacted as of August 1, 2026.
Current federal cost-reporting proposals
H.R. 7278 and S. 3731, the Cost of Police Misconduct Act of 2026 proposals, would require federal, state, and local reporting of misconduct allegations, judgments, settlements, and related information. Both remained introduced.
Historical significance
Police misconduct databases address a recurring failure of fragmented American policing: serious records can remain hidden within one agency, prosecutor's office, court, insurer, or state while an officer moves elsewhere.

Definition

Police misconduct databases are organized systems for collecting and retrieving information about alleged or established officer misconduct and its consequences.

They may be maintained by police departments, prosecutors, courts, state certification agencies, federal departments, insurers, oversight bodies, journalists, researchers, or nonprofit organizations.

The term describes many different systems rather than one standard national product.

Why These Databases Exist

Police employment is fragmented among thousands of federal, state, local, tribal, territorial, campus, transit, school, park, and special-jurisdiction agencies.

An officer's prior employer may hold important records that are not visible to another agency.

Databases seek to preserve institutional memory, improve hiring, support prosecution disclosure, identify patterns, and provide public accountability.

Fragmented American Policing

Licensing, personnel law, public records, discipline, prosecution, and officer hiring are governed by different state and local systems.

One jurisdiction may decertify an officer for conduct that another state treats only as an employment matter.

National information sharing therefore depends on state participation, common definitions, identity matching, and legal authority.

A Database Entry Is Not Necessarily a Finding

A record may represent an allegation, pending investigation, sustained finding, overturned finding, settlement, resignation, criminal charge, conviction, or decertification.

Users must understand the record type and procedural posture.

Treating every entry as proven misconduct can violate fairness and produce inaccurate decisions.

Complaint Databases

Internal affairs and civilian oversight agencies use case-management systems to record complaints, allegations, officers, witnesses, evidence, findings, discipline, and appeal.

A single complaint may contain multiple allegations with different outcomes.

Public statistics should distinguish complaints, allegations, officers, incidents, and findings rather than treating them as interchangeable counts.

Disciplinary Databases

Disciplinary systems record corrective actions imposed after policy or employment findings.

Possible actions include counseling, retraining, reprimand, suspension, demotion, loss of specialty assignment, termination, or referral for decertification.

A record should identify whether discipline was final, reduced, reversed, settled, or pending appeal.

Personnel Files

Personnel files may contain evaluations, complaints, discipline, commendations, training, attendance, use-of-force reviews, and employment history.

State law determines whether such files are confidential, partly public, or accessible after final discipline.

A misconduct database may point to a personnel file without reproducing the complete file.

Early-Intervention Systems

Early-intervention systems combine indicators such as complaints, force, pursuits, injuries, lawsuits, sick leave, vehicle crashes, and report corrections.

A threshold alert does not establish misconduct.

The system is intended to prompt review, training, wellness support, supervision, or investigation before serious harm recurs.

Use-of-Force Databases

Use-of-force databases record incidents, tactics, injuries, demographic information, circumstances, review, and policy findings.

They may identify officers with repeated force without determining that the force was unlawful.

Force data and misconduct data overlap but should not be treated as identical.

Lawsuit, Judgment, and Settlement Databases

These systems track civil claims, filed lawsuits, judgments, settlements, legal fees, indemnification, claim type, involved officers, and agency response.

Settlement does not necessarily establish liability, and dismissal does not necessarily establish that the alleged conduct did not occur.

Financial records can reveal recurring institutional cost even when personnel records remain confidential.

Criminal Charges and Convictions

An officer may face state or federal criminal investigation for assault, perjury, obstruction, theft, sexual misconduct, homicide, civil-rights violations, or other offenses.

Charges are allegations; convictions are adjudicated outcomes.

Databases should record acquittal, dismissal, diversion, appeal, and vacatur when applicable.

Officer Certification and Licensing

Most states use a peace-officer standards and training agency or similar body to certify or license officers.

Certification is separate from employment with a particular department.

State authority to suspend, revoke, deny, or accept surrender varies in grounds, procedure, and public access.

Decertification

Decertification removes or restricts the legal credential needed to serve as an officer within the jurisdiction.

Grounds may include felony conviction, dishonesty, excessive force, sexual misconduct, civil-rights violations, certification fraud, or other serious misconduct.

Some states historically had limited authority to decertify officers who resigned before discipline.

Termination and Decertification

Termination ends one employment relationship.

A terminated officer may remain certified and eligible for hiring elsewhere unless the state takes separate action.

A robust system links employment separation, misconduct investigation, and state certification review while preserving due process.

Resignation or Retirement Under Investigation

Officers may resign or retire before internal proceedings conclude.

Allowing every investigation to close automatically upon departure can erase facts needed by future employers and certification agencies.

Systems should identify the allegation as unresolved unless the jurisdiction completes the case or reaches a final disposition.

Wandering Officers

The phrase wandering officer refers to movement between agencies after misconduct, warning signs, termination, resignation, or poor performance.

Movement is not proof of misconduct; many officers change agencies for legitimate reasons.

The accountability concern arises when serious adverse information is concealed, omitted, expunged, or never requested.

Preemployment Background Checks

Hiring agencies may review criminal history, driving records, credit where lawful, prior employment, training, litigation, complaints, certification, social media, references, and psychological screening.

A database query should supplement rather than replace direct contact with previous employers and source-record review.

Agencies should document unresolved gaps and attempts to verify adverse information.

Employer Disclosure and Reference Laws

Prior agencies may fear defamation, privacy, labor, or litigation claims when disclosing misconduct.

Some states provide good-faith immunity or require disclosure of specified officer records.

Legal protection should encourage truthful records sharing without shielding knowingly false or retaliatory statements.

National Decertification Index

The National Decertification Index is hosted by the International Association of Directors of Law Enforcement Standards and Training.

It records certification or license revocation actions reported by participating state government agencies.

Its principal purpose is interstate screening rather than public complaint transparency.

Limits of the National Decertification Index

The index depends on state authority, reporting, definitions, timeliness, and participation.

It does not automatically include every complaint, suspension, lawsuit, settlement, resignation, termination, or officer who remains certified.

Access is generally controlled through state certifying agencies.

How an NDI Query Should Be Used

A match should prompt verification with the reporting certification agency.

Hiring agencies should confirm identity and obtain source records rather than rely only on a name match.

No match does not prove that an applicant has no adverse history.

State POST Databases

State peace-officer standards and training agencies maintain certification, training, employment, and disciplinary records.

Some publish searchable decertification lists; others provide only limited public reports or restricted agency access.

State systems may serve as the authoritative source for certification status even when national indexes contain pointers.

Origin of the National Law Enforcement Accountability Database

Executive Order 14074 directed the Attorney General to establish a centralized federal accountability database.

The Justice Department launched NLEAD on December 18, 2023.

The system focused on federal law-enforcement personnel and authorized employment-related use.

NLEAD Pointer-System Design

The NLEAD privacy assessment described a pointer model.

A query identifies that a qualifying record exists and directs the authorized user to the source agency for additional information.

This design reduces central storage of full personnel files but makes source-agency responsiveness essential.

NLEAD Qualifying Categories

  • criminal convictions;
  • suspension of enforcement authority related to misconduct;
  • termination related to misconduct;
  • resignation or retirement while under investigation for serious misconduct;
  • sustained complaints or discipline based on findings of serious misconduct;
  • specified civil judgments connected to official duties; and
  • commendations or awards associated with an officer who has a qualifying adverse record.

NLEAD Finality and Adjudication

The first BJS report stated that only incidents considered final and adjudicated were submitted.

An incident did not need to have exhausted every appeal before submission.

Later outcomes can therefore require correction or supplementation.

NLEAD Time Window

The December 2024 DOJ fact sheet described records of misconduct and related commendations occurring during the preceding seven years.

Retention and lookback rules affect whether older serious misconduct appears.

A hiring investigation should not assume that absence from a defined window means absence of all prior misconduct.

NLEAD First Statistical Report

The BJS report counted 4,790 qualifying incidents involving 4,011 federal officers for events from 2018 through 2023.

The comparison frame included more than 148,000 federal law-enforcement officers.

The report was a database snapshot rather than a complete measure of every allegation or all federal misconduct.

NLEAD Incident Types

Sustained serious-misconduct complaints or disciplinary records made up 63.3 percent of incidents in the first report.

Termination related to misconduct accounted for 15.4 percent, resignation or retirement under serious-misconduct investigation 10 percent, criminal conviction 6.5 percent, and suspension of enforcement authority 4.8 percent.

The categories reflect qualifying records rather than severity rankings.

NLEAD Employment Use

The original federal framework directed authorized agencies to use NLEAD in hiring, assignment, promotion, and participation in federally controlled activities such as task forces.

The system was not designed as an automatic employment ban.

A pointer required the decisionmaker to obtain and assess the underlying record.

NLEAD Was Not a Public Name Registry

Authorized federal users could search record-level information.

Public reporting was aggregate and anonymized to protect officers and sensitive information.

This design served employment screening more directly than public transparency.

Revocation of Executive Order 14074

Executive Order 14148 revoked Executive Order 14074 on January 20, 2025.

That action removed the executive-order directive that created and governed the original NLEAD framework.

Revocation did not automatically erase existing records or the Privacy Act system notice.

NLEAD Status Through August 2026

The Justice Department's public systems-of-record page continued to list DOJ-022, National Law Enforcement Accountability Database, in 2026.

The prominent DOJ NLEAD fact sheet available publicly was archived and described the pre-revocation framework.

Official public sources reviewed for this page do not clearly explain whether every former mandatory query, submission, and reporting rule continued unchanged after revocation.

The page therefore distinguishes continuing record-system evidence from uncertain continuation of the earlier executive mandate.

Brady Information

Under Brady v. Maryland, prosecutors must disclose material exculpatory evidence to the defense.

Officer misconduct can be relevant when it undermines evidence, reveals bias, or supports a defense.

Brady obligations are case-specific and broader than a simple officer list.

Giglio Information

Under Giglio v. United States, material impeachment information concerning government witnesses may require disclosure.

Potential officer information can include dishonesty, prior inconsistent statements, bias, credibility findings, or conduct affecting evidence reliability.

A Giglio record is not the same as decertification or a criminal conviction.

DOJ Giglio Information Files

The Justice Manual establishes procedures for federal investigative agencies to provide potential impeachment information to federal prosecutors.

The policy seeks to support fair trials while protecting legitimate employee privacy.

Prosecutors determine disclosure in the context of the officer's role, the case, defenses, and applicable law.

Local Brady or Giglio Lists

State and local prosecutors may maintain lists or files concerning officers whose credibility could affect criminal cases.

Terminology, inclusion standards, notice, appeal, public access, and employment consequences vary widely.

Placement on a list may limit usefulness as a witness without automatically ending certification or employment.

Credibility Lists Are Not Public Findings of Guilt

A prosecutor's disclosure decision concerns trial fairness and evidentiary obligations.

It may rely on information that was not adjudicated in a criminal or civil trial.

Fair systems provide notice, an opportunity to respond, and procedures to update records when underlying findings change.

Civilian-Oversight Databases

Civilian review boards and inspectors general maintain complaint, investigation, recommendation, and implementation records.

These systems can preserve data separate from police internal affairs.

Public dashboards may show allegation type, officer, unit, outcome, discipline, and case duration subject to law.

Court Records

Civil complaints, criminal indictments, judgments, orders, and appellate decisions are important misconduct-data sources.

Court records can be incomplete because many claims settle, are sealed, are dismissed procedurally, or name agencies rather than individual officers.

A filed complaint is an allegation; a final judgment has a different evidentiary status.

Settlements

Police-misconduct settlements can compensate claimants and resolve litigation without an admission of liability.

Settlement databases should identify that status rather than describe every payment as a judicial finding.

Repeated settlements involving one officer, unit, tactic, or policy can still justify institutional review.

Insurer and Risk-Management Databases

Municipal insurers and risk pools may track claims, payouts, officer histories, training, and department practices.

These records can influence premiums, coverage, settlement strategy, and risk recommendations.

They are often less visible to the public than court or budget records.

Nongovernmental Databases

Journalists, universities, civil-rights organizations, defense attorneys, researchers, and nonprofit groups build databases from public records and reporting.

These projects can fill gaps left by government secrecy or fragmentation.

Their methods, completeness, correction procedures, and legal definitions should be evaluated individually.

Public and Restricted Databases

Public registries support transparency and community research.

Restricted databases can protect privacy and investigative information while supporting hiring or prosecution.

A complete accountability system may need both, with clearly separated purposes and access rules.

Identity Matching

Common names, name changes, aliases, inconsistent middle initials, date-of-birth errors, and agency identifiers can produce false matches or missed matches.

Restricted systems may use sensitive identifiers not suitable for public display.

Every adverse match should be confirmed with the source agency.

Unique Officer Identifiers

A stable identifier can connect an officer's employment and certification records across agencies while avoiding reliance on name alone.

Identifiers must be protected against identity theft and unauthorized use.

Public systems may display a non-sensitive professional identifier rather than Social Security or personal information.

Standardized Definitions

Complaint, allegation, sustained, exonerated, decertified, resigned under investigation, serious misconduct, and final action must be defined.

Without common definitions, national counts combine unlike events.

Data dictionaries should be public even when individual records are restricted.

Allegations

Allegations should be recorded because patterns can be important and investigations require case management.

They should be labeled clearly as allegations and linked to final outcomes.

Publishing unverified allegations without context can cause serious unfairness.

Pending Investigations

Pending cases may be highly relevant to hiring but remain unresolved.

Systems should show filing date, status, responsible agency, delay, and whether the officer resigned.

Long-pending cases should not become permanent unreviewed adverse findings.

Sustained Findings

A sustained finding means the allegation was proven under the administrative standard used by the agency.

It is stronger than a complaint but not equivalent to a criminal conviction.

The record should identify the policy violated, evidence standard, discipline, and appeal status.

Not Sustained, Unfounded, and Exonerated

Not sustained generally means insufficient evidence.

Unfounded generally means the alleged event did not occur or the identified officer was not responsible.

Exonerated generally means the act occurred but was lawful or within policy.

Definitions vary and must accompany the data.

Appeals and Reversals

Discipline may be appealed through civil service, arbitration, administrative hearing, or court.

A database should preserve the original finding and accurately record later reduction, reversal, reinstatement, or settlement.

Silent removal can erase institutional history, while failure to update can misstate current status.

Expungement and Sealing

Collective-bargaining agreements, personnel laws, settlements, court orders, or agency policy may require removal or sealing of records.

Expungement can protect officers from unsupported or obsolete allegations.

It can also prevent future employers from learning of repeated serious conduct if rules are overly broad.

Retention

Retention periods should reflect the database purpose, severity, appeal, litigation, certification, public-record law, and employment relevance.

Short retention can erase patterns; indefinite retention of unsubstantiated allegations can become punitive.

Policies should distinguish underlying records from aggregate statistical history.

Notice and Correction Procedures

Officers should receive notice of qualifying adverse records when law permits.

They should be able to identify mistaken identity, inaccurate dates, overturned findings, duplicate entries, or missing dispositions.

Correction procedures should not permit deletion of accurate final records merely because they are unfavorable.

Due Process

Consequences can include lost employment, inability to testify, decertification, reputational harm, and public exposure.

The required process depends on the legal interest, database use, state law, and whether information is public or restricted.

Fair procedures improve accuracy and legitimacy without preventing accountability.

Federal Privacy Act

The Privacy Act governs federal systems of records retrieved by personal identifiers.

It addresses notice, access, amendment, disclosure, security, and agency responsibilities subject to statutory exceptions.

The NLEAD was established with a published federal system-of-records notice and privacy assessment.

FOIA and State Public-Records Law

Federal FOIA and state open-records statutes determine public access to many misconduct records.

Exemptions may protect personal privacy, active investigations, confidential sources, security, medical information, and compelled statements.

States differ sharply on access to officer names, complaints, discipline, and decertification.

Victim and Witness Privacy

Misconduct records can contain addresses, medical details, sexual-assault information, immigration status, children, confidential sources, and retaliation risks.

Public databases should redact protected information without concealing officer accountability.

Victim privacy is not served by erasing aggregate patterns or final official findings.

Officer and Family Safety

Public records should exclude home addresses, personal telephone numbers, family information, Social Security numbers, and other sensitive identifiers.

Safety protection does not require hiding professional identity in every final misconduct record.

Policy should separate legitimate security from reputational secrecy.

Cybersecurity

Misconduct databases contain sensitive personnel, investigative, victim, and employment information.

Security requires encryption, access controls, multifactor authentication, audit logs, breach response, backups, and vendor oversight.

Unauthorized access can harm officers, victims, witnesses, investigations, and public trust.

Audit Logs

Systems should record who searched, viewed, exported, changed, corrected, or deleted a record.

Audit logs deter misuse and permit investigation of unauthorized access.

Administrators should not be able to alter audit history without detection.

Database Governance

A governing authority should define data categories, submission duties, access, correction, retention, audit, sanctions, and public reporting.

Police agencies should not have unilateral authority to remove accurate adverse records from a shared accountability system.

Independent oversight and periodic quality review are essential.

Submission Compliance

A database cannot reveal records that agencies never submit.

Compliance should be measured by agency, timeliness, completeness, and correction rate.

Grant conditions, certification rules, audits, and penalties can encourage reporting.

Source Records

Summary entries should identify the agency holding the underlying investigation, order, judgment, or certification action.

Decisionmakers should obtain source records before taking major adverse action.

A pointer with no responsive source agency can create an unusable or unfair record.

Data Quality

Quality problems include duplicate records, missing dispositions, inconsistent dates, wrong officers, vague allegations, stale appeals, and unreported reversals.

Automated validation can detect some errors but not resolve legal meaning.

Periodic human review and source-agency certification remain necessary.

False Negatives

A database can miss misconduct because it was never reported, remained pending, was expunged, occurred before the lookback period, or fell outside the database definition.

No result should never be treated as proof of a clean complete history.

Direct background investigation remains essential.

False Positives

A mistaken identity, duplicate, reversed finding, or poorly labeled allegation can create an inaccurate adverse match.

High-consequence employment decisions require verification and an opportunity to respond.

Public systems should publish correction contacts and update histories.

Use in Hiring Decisions

Databases can identify records requiring further investigation.

Hiring agencies should assess seriousness, relevance, credibility, time, rehabilitation, outcome, and legal restrictions.

Automatic exclusion based on any allegation can be unfair; ignoring sustained serious misconduct can endanger the public.

Promotion and Assignment

Federal NLEAD use originally extended beyond hiring to promotion, assignment, and task-force participation.

Other agencies may use internal databases for specialty units, supervision, training, or access to sensitive duties.

The decision rule should be published internally and applied consistently.

Use by Prosecutors

Prosecutors need reliable officer-history information to meet discovery obligations and assess witness credibility.

A prosecutor database should not substitute for continuing disclosure by agencies and officers.

Failure to connect employment records with pending prosecutions can produce wrongful convictions or dismissed cases.

Access by Criminal Defendants

Defendants may seek officer impeachment evidence through constitutional discovery, court orders, subpoenas, public records, and litigation.

A confidential prosecutor file does not eliminate the duty to disclose material information.

Courts may review sensitive records privately to determine what must be produced.

Use by the Public

Residents may use public databases to evaluate officers, departments, candidates for chief, settlements, policy patterns, and oversight performance.

Public records can support journalism, research, civil litigation, and legislative reform.

Interfaces should explain data limitations and avoid presenting unresolved allegations as final facts.

Research Use

Researchers can study officer mobility, repeated complaints, force, discipline, decertification, lawsuits, racial disparities, and institutional response.

Data access must include definitions, coverage, missingness, and changes over time.

Apparent differences among agencies may reflect reporting practices rather than underlying conduct alone.

African American and Racial-Justice Significance

Black communities have often faced police violence while lacking access to complaint, discipline, and officer-history records.

Secret or fragmented systems can hide repeat misconduct affecting Black residents across neighborhoods and jurisdictions.

Public accountability must also protect complainants and witnesses from exposure or retaliation.

Individual and Institutional Accountability

Officer-name databases can reveal repeat conduct but may overemphasize individual wrongdoing.

Agency policy, supervision, training, incentives, prosecutor practice, union rules, and political leadership shape misconduct outcomes.

Databases should permit analysis of units, supervisors, agencies, and policies as well as individual officers.

National Police Misconduct Registry Proposal

H.R. 5361 would require the Attorney General to establish a publicly searchable National Police Misconduct Registry.

The proposed registry would include complaint dispositions, discipline, termination, certification records, lawsuits, settlements, and specified other records concerning federal and local officers.

The proposal remained a bill and did not create an operating registry as of August 1, 2026.

Complaint Categories Proposed in H.R. 5361

The bill distinguishes complaints found credible or resulting in discipline, pending complaints, and complaints ending in exoneration, unfounded findings, or not-sustained findings.

It separately identifies use-of-force and racial-profiling allegations.

This structure illustrates the importance of publishing disposition rather than complaint counts alone.

Cost of Police Misconduct Act of 2026

H.R. 7278 and S. 3731 would require reporting of allegations, misconduct, judgments, settlements, fees, and related agency action to the Attorney General.

The proposals focus strongly on the financial and institutional cost of misconduct.

Both were introduced in late January 2026 and referred to judiciary committees.

Current Federal Law Through August 1, 2026

No enacted federal statute had created the complete public nationwide officer-misconduct registry proposed in H.R. 5361.

The National Decertification Index continued as a state-reported law-enforcement screening registry.

The Justice Department continued to list NLEAD as a federal system of records, while the executive order that established its original mandate had been revoked.

State and local law therefore remained central to public access, certification, discipline, and reporting.

Reform Principles

  • standard definitions and unique professional identifiers;
  • mandatory timely reporting by agencies and certification bodies;
  • clear separation of allegations, findings, appeals, and final outcomes;
  • source-record verification before adverse action;
  • notice and correction procedures;
  • protection of victims, witnesses, officers, and sensitive identifiers;
  • public access to final records and aggregate patterns where lawful;
  • restricted access for legitimate hiring and prosecutorial functions;
  • audit logs, cybersecurity, and independent governance;
  • retention rules that preserve serious institutional history;
  • submission-compliance audits and sanctions; and
  • tracking of agency, supervisory, and policy failures—not only officer names.

Research and Interpretation

A reliable database analysis should identify who maintains the system, who must report, who can search, which events qualify, what time period is covered, and how corrections occur.

Counts should distinguish officers from incidents, complaints from allegations, and allegations from sustained findings.

Archived federal pages should not be described as current policy without checking later executive, statutory, and agency action.

Representative Record Types

Unsubstantiated Complaint

A civilian alleges discourtesy and excessive force. The investigation cannot determine whether the conduct occurred. The database should label the result not sustained rather than misconduct proven.

Sustained Dishonesty Finding

An administrative investigation finds that an officer intentionally falsified a report. The record may affect discipline, Giglio disclosure, certification, promotion, and hiring.

Resignation During Investigation

An officer resigns before a serious force investigation is completed. The system should record the unresolved departure and should not mislabel it either as exoneration or as a final sustained finding.

Decertification

A state licensing body revokes an officer's certification after final proceedings. The action may be entered in the state database and reported to the National Decertification Index.

Civil Settlement

A city pays to settle a lawsuit without admitting liability. The database should record the settlement, amount, allegations, and no-admission status rather than describe it as a judicial misconduct finding.

Overturned Discipline

An officer's termination is reduced to suspension on appeal. The record should preserve the original action and accurately display the final status.

Common Misconceptions

“There Is One Complete National Police Misconduct Database”

The United States has multiple partial federal, state, local, prosecutorial, certification, court, and private systems.

“The National Decertification Index Contains Every Complaint”

The NDI principally records certification or license revocation actions reported by participating state agencies.

“NLEAD Was a Public Search Engine for Officer Names”

NLEAD was designed for authorized federal use, with aggregate anonymized public reporting.

“A Database Entry Proves Misconduct”

An entry may represent an allegation, pending case, settlement, final finding, appeal, or decertification.

“Not Sustained Means the Complaint Was False”

It generally means evidence was insufficient to prove or disprove the allegation.

“Exonerated and Unfounded Mean the Same Thing”

Exonerated generally means the act occurred but was lawful; unfounded generally means it did not occur or the officer was not responsible.

“Termination Automatically Prevents Employment Elsewhere”

An officer may remain certified unless the state takes separate licensing action.

“Decertification and Criminal Conviction Are the Same”

Decertification is an administrative licensing action and may occur without a criminal conviction.

“A Brady or Giglio List Is a Criminal Conviction List”

It concerns potential impeachment or credibility information relevant to prosecution disclosure.

“Every Settlement Proves Liability”

Parties often settle without admission or judicial finding.

“No Database Match Means the Applicant Has No Misconduct History”

Records may be unreported, pending, outside the time window, expunged, or excluded by definition.

“Public Access and Hiring Access Must Be Identical”

Restricted employment systems may use sensitive identifiers and unresolved records that require different safeguards from public registries.

“Due Process Requires All Records to Remain Secret”

Fair notice and correction can coexist with public access to accurate final official actions.

“Executive Order 14074 Is Still in Force”

Executive Order 14148 revoked it in January 2025.

“Revoking the Executive Order Automatically Deleted NLEAD”

DOJ continued to list NLEAD as a system of records in 2026, although the public status of all former operating mandates was unclear.

“H.R. 5361 Created a Public National Registry”

The bill proposed one but remained introduced rather than enacted.

“The Cost of Police Misconduct Act Is Current Law”

The House and Senate 2026 versions remained introduced proposals as of August 1, 2026.

Key Distinctions

Complaint
A report or allegation of possible misconduct.
Allegation
A specific claimed act within a complaint or investigation.
Sustained finding
An administrative determination that evidence proved the allegation under the governing standard.
Not sustained
A determination that evidence was insufficient to prove or disprove the allegation.
Exonerated
A determination that the act occurred but was lawful and within policy.
Unfounded
A determination that the alleged act did not occur or the identified officer was not responsible.
Discipline
An employment consequence imposed by an agency or authorized body.
Decertification
Revocation or loss of the state credential authorizing service as an officer.
Termination
End of employment with one agency.
Brady information
Material exculpatory evidence the prosecution must disclose.
Giglio information

Historical and Continuing Significance

Police misconduct databases determine whether serious official history follows an officer across agencies, states, courts, and prosecutorial districts.

They can protect communities from repeat misconduct, support fair criminal trials, improve hiring, reveal institutional cost, and preserve evidence that otherwise disappears when an officer resigns.

For African American communities, database access is connected to a longer struggle against police brutality, hidden discipline, discriminatory enforcement, and official control over records needed to prove patterns.

Poorly designed systems can also spread unproven allegations, misidentify officers, expose victims, conceal final dispositions, or create secret employment blacklists without meaningful correction.

The strongest model separates allegations from findings, preserves appeals, verifies source records, protects sensitive information, audits agency compliance, and provides both public transparency and legitimate restricted hiring access.

Summary

Police misconduct databases record allegations, findings, discipline, decertification, credibility information, lawsuits, settlements, employment history, and related accountability information.

The United States does not maintain one complete public national database covering every officer and every record type. Instead, information is divided among police departments, civilian oversight bodies, prosecutors, courts, state certification agencies, federal systems, insurers, and nongovernmental projects.

The National Decertification Index records state-reported certification or license revocation actions and is primarily a restricted law-enforcement screening tool. It does not contain every complaint or termination.

NLEAD was launched in 2023 for authorized federal employment and assignment decisions. Its first report counted 4,790 qualifying incidents involving 4,011 federal officers for events from 2018 through 2023.

Executive Order 14074 was revoked in January 2025. DOJ continued to list NLEAD as a federal system of records in 2026, but public official materials did not clearly establish that every former mandatory-use rule remained unchanged.

Brady–Giglio systems serve criminal-discovery and witness-credibility functions, while complaint, discipline, decertification, and civil-liability databases serve different purposes.

Reliable systems distinguish allegations from sustained findings, record appeals and reversals, verify identity, preserve source records, provide correction procedures, protect victims and sensitive information, and audit agency reporting.

H.R. 5361 proposes a public National Police Misconduct Registry. H.R. 7278 and S. 3731 propose broader reporting of allegations, judgments, settlements, costs, and agency action. All remained unenacted as of August 1, 2026.

The central policy challenge is creating enough information sharing to prevent repeat misconduct without turning incomplete or inaccurate allegations into permanent unreviewable blacklists.
