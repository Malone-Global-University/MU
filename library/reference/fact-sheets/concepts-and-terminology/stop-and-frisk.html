Stop-and-Frisk

A limited police authority to detain a person briefly upon individualized reasonable suspicion of criminal activity and, when separate facts support a reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous, conduct a protective pat-down for weapons—an established constitutional doctrine that became nationally controversial when New York City transformed it into a high-volume policing program concentrated upon Black and Latino communities.

Fact Sheet Overview

Canonical subject name
Stop-and-Frisk.
Formal policing term
Stop, Question, and Frisk, commonly abbreviated SQF. A lawful stop does not automatically authorize a frisk, and many stops end without any frisk.
Federal legal term
Investigative detention or Terry stop, named for Terry v. Ohio.
Subject type
Fourth Amendment doctrine, police investigative technique, proactive-policing practice, and public-policy controversy.
Foundational Supreme Court decision
Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, decided June 10, 1968.
Basic stop standard
An officer must possess individualized reasonable suspicion, based upon specific and articulable facts and rational inferences, that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime.
Basic frisk standard
The officer must possess an additional reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.
Purpose of a stop
To investigate suspected criminal activity through a temporary detention shorter and less intrusive than an arrest.
Purpose of a frisk
To protect officers and others by locating weapons or objects capable of causing serious injury, not to search generally for evidence.
Legal level below arrest
Reasonable suspicion is less demanding than probable cause but requires more than an intuition, generalized concern, stereotype, or unparticularized hunch.
Meaning of a stop
A seizure occurs when, considering the total circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to disregard police and leave or when police intentionally restrain the person's movement.
Meaning of a frisk
A carefully limited pat-down of the outer clothing and immediately accessible areas for weapons.
Meaning of a search
A more intrusive examination, such as reaching inside clothing or containers, ordinarily requiring probable cause, consent, a warrant, an arrest-related exception, or another recognized legal basis.
Not automatically unlawful
Stop-and-frisk remains lawful throughout the United States when each intrusion satisfies constitutional and applicable state-law requirements.
Not a universal random-search power
Police may not stop and pat down people merely because they are present in a high-crime neighborhood, belong to a demographic group, avoid eye contact, appear nervous, or are unknown to the officer.
Race and suspect descriptions
Race may form one part of a sufficiently specific description of a known suspect, but race alone or a broad racial category cannot establish reasonable suspicion.
High-crime area
Location may contribute to the totality of circumstances but cannot independently justify a stop or frisk.
Flight
Unprovoked flight upon noticing police may contribute to reasonable suspicion under Illinois v. Wardlow, but refusal to cooperate and mere departure do not automatically justify detention.
Anonymous tip
An uncorroborated anonymous allegation that a person possesses a gun is generally insufficient by itself under Florida v. J. L..
Identification
Federal law does not impose one universal identification rule. State law varies, and any demand for identification must arise from a legally valid encounter.
Consent
A person may voluntarily consent to questioning or a search, but consent must be voluntary and must not be confused with compliance during detention.
Evidence remedy
Evidence obtained through an unconstitutional stop or frisk may be suppressed in a criminal case, subject to exceptions and later doctrinal limitations.
Civil remedy
An affected person may pursue administrative complaints or civil litigation, although standing, causation, qualified immunity, municipal-liability rules, and proof requirements may restrict recovery.
Major state framework
New York Criminal Procedure Law § 140.50 and the four-level encounter framework established in People v. De Bour.
Major policy implementation
New York City's mass stop-question-frisk program, which expanded dramatically under Mayor Michael Bloomberg and Police Commissioner Raymond Kelly.
Peak New York City year
The NYPD recorded 685,724 stops in 2011.
Major historical dataset
The federal court in Floyd v. City of New York reviewed approximately 4.4 million recorded stops from 2004 through June 2012.
Historical racial distribution
In the Floyd dataset, 52 percent of stopped people were Black, 31 percent were Hispanic, and 10 percent were white.
Historical outcome rate
Approximately 88 percent of the 4.4 million stops resulted in no arrest or summons.
Major constitutional ruling
In August 2013, the federal district court held that New York City was deliberately indifferent to a pattern of unconstitutional stops and frisks and had violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments.
Meaning of the ruling
The court did not abolish lawful Terry stops. It condemned a municipal pattern of unsupported and racially discriminatory implementation.
Major remedy
Revised policies and training, stronger documentation and supervision, body-worn-camera use, community participation, disciplinary reforms, data analysis, and continuing oversight by a court-appointed federal monitor.
Current New York City volume
The NYPD's public 2025 dataset contains 26,422 recorded Level III stops, compared with 25,386 in 2024—far below the 2011 peak but higher than the early post-reform years.
Current 2025 racial distribution
Using the NYPD's published categories, approximately 58.8 percent of recorded stops involved people classified as Black, 31.1 percent involved people classified as white Hispanic or Black Hispanic, and 6.4 percent involved people classified as white.
Current 2025 encounter outcomes
The 2025 data record frisks in approximately 52.2 percent of stops, searches in 40.8 percent, arrests in 29.6 percent, and summonses in 3.6 percent.
Current 2025 weapon indicators
The dataset records a weapon-found flag in approximately 11.9 percent of stops and a firearm flag in approximately 3.6 percent. These are stop records, not a count of unique weapons or proof that every stop was lawful.
Current compliance concern
A federal-monitor review of sampled encounters from the first half of 2024 found substantially lower legal-compliance rates than NYPD supervisors reported, especially for frisks and searches.
Expanded reporting
New York City's 2024 How Many Stops Act expanded reporting beyond Level III stops to include lower-level investigative encounters.
Current status through August 2026
The tactic remains lawful when properly supported; New York City continues publishing detailed data, federal monitoring remains part of the reform structure, and disputes continue over rising stop numbers, racial concentration, underreporting, supervision, effectiveness, and officer safety.
Historical significance
Stop-and-frisk is one of the most important examples of the difference between a limited constitutional authority and a large-scale policy that can become unconstitutional through volume, weak supervision, racial targeting, and unsupported intrusion.

What Is Stop-and-Frisk?

Stop-and-frisk is a two-stage police encounter.

First, an officer may stop and briefly detain a person when specific facts create reasonable suspicion of criminal activity.

Second, the officer may frisk the person's outer clothing for weapons only when separate facts create reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.

The phrase is misleading when it suggests that every stop permits an automatic frisk.

A lawful stop may involve questions and observation without physical patting.

A frisk may be unlawful even when the stop itself was justified.

A search inside pockets may be unlawful even when both the stop and outer-clothing frisk were justified.

Legal Doctrine and Policing Program

Stop-and-frisk describes both a legal doctrine and, in public debate, a policing program.

The legal doctrine authorizes individualized investigative detentions and protective weapon pat-downs.

A policing program determines how frequently officers are directed to use that authority, where they are deployed, which conduct is treated as suspicious, how performance is measured, how supervisors review reports, and what happens when officers act unlawfully.

The Supreme Court approved the limited tactic in Terry.

It did not approve suspicionless mass stops, racial quotas, stop productivity targets, or citywide campaigns detached from individual facts.

Why the Terminology Matters

Public discussion often uses “stop-and-frisk” to describe any police questioning, street search, or New York City proactive-policing policy.

The legal distinctions determine what justification is required.

A consensual conversation requires less justification because the person remains free to leave.

A stop requires reasonable suspicion.

A frisk requires reasonable suspicion of armed danger.

A search generally requires a stronger legal basis.

An arrest requires probable cause.

Combining all encounters under one label conceals whether an officer crossed from one legal level to another.

Historical Antecedents

Police officers conducted street questioning and weapon pat-downs before the Supreme Court created the modern doctrine.

American governments also used slave patrols, Black Codes, vagrancy statutes, loitering laws, and public-order enforcement to control the movement and presence of Black people.

These systems were not identical to the modern Terry doctrine.

They are historically relevant because broad street discretion has repeatedly been used against people defined as suspicious by status, race, poverty, unemployment, age, or unfamiliarity.

Modern constitutional standards were intended partly to replace unreviewable street authority with articulable legal limits.

New York's Stop-and-Frisk Statute

New York enacted a statutory stop-and-frisk provision before the Supreme Court decided Terry.

The current provision appears in Criminal Procedure Law § 140.50.

It authorizes a police officer in a public place to stop a person whom the officer reasonably suspects has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a felony or Penal Law misdemeanor.

The officer may demand the person's name, address, and explanation of conduct.

A protective weapon search requires reasonable suspicion that the officer is in danger of physical injury.

The statute supplies state authority but remains subject to the federal and New York constitutions.

Facts of Terry v. Ohio

Cleveland detective Martin McFadden observed John Terry and another man repeatedly walking past a store window, looking inside, and conferring.

McFadden believed the men were preparing an armed robbery.

He approached, asked questions, grabbed Terry, and patted the outside of his clothing.

McFadden felt and recovered a handgun.

Terry was convicted of carrying a concealed weapon and challenged the seizure of the gun.

The Supreme Court treated the street detention and pat-down as Fourth Amendment seizures and searches, even though they were less intrusive than arrest and a full evidentiary search.

Holding of Terry v. Ohio

The Supreme Court held that an officer may conduct a brief investigative stop when observation and experience support a reasonable conclusion that criminal activity may be occurring.

When the officer reasonably believes that the person may be armed and presently dangerous, the officer may conduct a carefully limited pat-down for weapons.

The Court balanced public and officer safety against the personal intrusion.

It rejected both unrestricted police discretion and the argument that probable cause was required for every temporary street detention.

The Caution within Terry

Chief Justice Earl Warren recognized the danger that street stops could be used as harassment.

The decision addressed a specific officer, a specific suspected robbery, observed conduct, and a limited weapon search.

It did not give police routine authority to pat down every person they question.

Later policing programs often cited Terry while operating at a scale and level of managerial direction not before the Court in 1968.

Reasonable Suspicion

Reasonable suspicion is a probability-based judgment drawn from the totality of circumstances.

The officer must identify facts and explain why those facts suggest criminal activity by the particular person.

Innocent conduct may contribute when combined with other circumstances, but common behavior cannot become suspicious merely because an officer labels it so.

The standard permits trained inference while requiring more than a conclusion such as “looked suspicious,” “did not belong,” or “furtive movement” without further description.

Specific and Articulable Facts

Useful facts may include a detailed suspect description, temporal and geographic proximity to a reported crime, observed casing behavior, concealment of an apparent weapon, a reliable witness account, an exchange consistent with an identified offense, or conduct that fits a known crime pattern.

The officer must connect the facts to an actual felony or misdemeanor rather than to generalized unease.

A report should explain what happened, what crime was suspected, what the person did, and why innocent explanations were insufficient at that moment.

Boilerplate language prevents meaningful supervision and judicial review.

A Hunch Is Not Enough

Police experience can inform interpretation.

Experience cannot convert an unsupported intuition into reasonable suspicion.

If an officer cannot describe the observed conduct and its relationship to a suspected offense, the stop may lack a constitutional basis.

Repeated use of vague terms across thousands of reports may indicate a supervisory system accepting conclusions without facts.

Individualized Suspicion

Suspicion must attach to the person stopped.

Being near someone suspected of crime does not automatically justify detention.

Being present in a building, neighborhood, housing development, transit station, or group associated with crime does not make every occupant suspicious.

Group descriptions must contain enough detail to distinguish the suspected person from large numbers of innocent people.

When Does a Stop Begin?

Police may approach a person and ask questions without creating a seizure when the encounter is genuinely voluntary.

A stop begins when physical force restrains the person or when a show of authority would cause a reasonable person to believe departure is not permitted and the person submits.

Relevant circumstances include blocking movement, surrounding the person, displaying weapons, commanding rather than requesting, retaining identification, activating emergency equipment, physical touching, or using accusatory language.

An officer's internal intention does not control when the surrounding conduct communicates detention.

Consensual Encounter

During a consensual encounter, an officer may ask questions and request identification.

The person ordinarily may decline and leave unless state law and the circumstances create a valid detention.

Consent is not genuine when officers communicate that refusal is impossible.

Recording and reporting lower-level encounters helps determine whether supposedly voluntary questioning functioned as an unrecorded stop.

Duration and Scope of a Stop

An investigative detention must remain temporary and reasonably related to the suspected crime.

Officers may ask questions, verify information, consult dispatch records, conduct limited identification procedures, and take reasonable safety precautions.

They must pursue the investigation diligently.

Moving a person long distances, using prolonged handcuffing, placing the person in a cell, or delaying for unrelated investigation may transform the stop into an arrest requiring probable cause.

There is no universal minute limit; courts examine the reason, necessity, diligence, force, location, and total intrusion.

Handcuffs and Force during a Stop

Handcuffs, drawn weapons, prone positioning, and other force do not automatically convert every stop into an arrest.

Officers must possess objective safety reasons proportionate to the situation.

Severe or prolonged restraint without a sufficient threat may become an arrest or unreasonable force.

The label “investigative stop” does not excuse tactics that exceed the justification for the encounter.

The Separate Standard for a Frisk

A frisk is not an automatic part of a lawful stop.

The officer must reasonably suspect that the person is armed and dangerous.

The suspicion may arise from the suspected offense, a visible weapon outline, threats, reliable information, movement toward a waistband, or other particular facts.

Suspicion that the person possesses drugs or committed a nonviolent offense does not by itself establish armed danger.

Departmental practice that frisks most stopped people may indicate that the separate standard has collapsed.

Purpose and Scope of a Frisk

The frisk is a protective search for weapons.

It ordinarily involves running hands over outer clothing to determine whether an object could be a weapon.

Officers may remove an object when its shape, hardness, location, or surrounding facts reasonably indicate a weapon or dangerous instrument.

A frisk is not a license to manipulate soft objects, empty every pocket, inspect a wallet, search a phone, or look generally for drugs.

Plain-Feel Doctrine

In Minnesota v. Dickerson, the Supreme Court recognized that police may seize contraband detected during a lawful frisk when its identity is immediately apparent through lawful touch.

Officers may not continue squeezing, sliding, or manipulating an object after determining that it is not a weapon merely to identify possible contraband.

The doctrine does not transform every weapon pat-down into a narcotics search.

Frisk and Search Are Different

A frisk is limited to protection.

A search seeks evidence or contraband and is more intrusive.

Reaching inside a pocket ordinarily requires a weapon-related basis, consent, probable cause, search incident to arrest, or another exception.

A search cannot be justified retroactively because officers happened to find something illegal.

Legality depends upon what officers knew before and during the intrusion.

Consent Searches

A person may voluntarily authorize a search beyond the scope of a frisk.

Courts evaluate voluntariness under the total circumstances, including police tone, number of officers, detention status, age, language, threats, promises, and whether the person understood the request.

The Constitution generally does not require officers to inform a person of the right to refuse, although local policy may require explanation or documentation.

Recording consent helps distinguish voluntary permission from submission to authority.

“High-Crime Area”

A location associated with crime may contribute to reasonable suspicion.

It cannot justify stopping every person who lives, works, visits, or travels there.

The label may be imprecise when officers do not identify the offense, geographic boundary, time period, or data supporting the designation.

Because residential segregation concentrates Black and Latino residents in heavily policed areas, location-based reasoning can magnify racial disparities even without an explicit racial instruction.

Flight and Avoidance

In Illinois v. Wardlow, the Supreme Court held that unprovoked headlong flight in an area of heavy narcotics trafficking contributed to reasonable suspicion.

The Court did not hold that every refusal to speak, change of direction, or departure from police creates suspicion.

People may avoid police because of fear, prior mistreatment, immigration concerns, outstanding civil obligations, or a desire for privacy.

Courts examine the nature of the movement and the complete circumstances rather than apply a universal flight rule.

Anonymous Tips

Anonymous reports can support a stop when police corroborate predictive or reliable information.

In Florida v. J. L., an anonymous caller described a young Black man at a bus stop and claimed he possessed a gun.

Officers found a person matching the visible description but had no information establishing the tipster's reliability or basis of knowledge.

The Supreme Court held that the tip did not justify the stop and frisk.

A description may identify a person while providing no reliable evidence that the person committed a crime.

Suspect Descriptions

A recent, reliable, and specific description can justify a stop near the time and place of a reported crime.

Useful details may include clothing, age range, height, build, distinctive features, direction of travel, vehicle, companions, property carried, and observed conduct.

A description such as “young Black male” covers too many innocent people to justify a stop by itself.

Race may help identify a known suspect but may not substitute for individualized facts.

Identification and Name Requests

Police may request identification during voluntary contact.

During a lawful Terry stop, states may authorize officers to require a name or identifying information.

In Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, the Supreme Court upheld application of a Nevada stop-and-identify law during a valid stop.

The decision did not create a national rule requiring every person approached by police to carry or display identification.

New York law authorizes an officer conducting a valid § 140.50 stop to demand name, address, and explanation, but the legal consequences of refusal remain governed by the precise statute, constitutional limits, and the facts of the encounter.

Vehicle Stops and Protective Frisks

A traffic stop seizes the driver and ordinarily the passengers for Fourth Amendment purposes.

Officers may order occupants out of a lawfully stopped vehicle under established Supreme Court rules.

A passenger may be frisked only when reasonable suspicion supports a belief that the person is armed and dangerous.

Under Michigan v. Long, officers may conduct a limited protective examination of vehicle areas within reach when specific facts suggest access to a weapon.

A traffic stop is not automatically a license to search the person or vehicle.

Prolonging a Stop

Police may not extend a stop beyond the time reasonably necessary for its lawful mission without additional justification.

The rule applies most visibly to traffic stops but reflects a general principle of investigative detention.

Officers may develop new reasonable suspicion during the encounter.

They may not delay merely to seek unrelated evidence when the original basis has been resolved.

Exclusionary Rule

A criminal defendant may seek suppression of evidence obtained through an unlawful stop, frisk, or search.

Suppression prevents the government from benefiting directly from unconstitutional evidence gathering and encourages police compliance.

The remedy is subject to doctrines concerning attenuation, independent source, inevitable discovery, good faith, standing, and causation.

An unlawful stop does not always result in suppression when a later event legally breaks the connection, but such exceptions do not make the original stop lawful.

Utah v. Strieff

In Utah v. Strieff, an officer unlawfully stopped a man and then discovered a valid outstanding warrant.

The Supreme Court held that the warrant sufficiently attenuated the connection and allowed evidence found during the arrest search.

Critics warned that the rule could reward unsupported stops in communities where minor warrants are common.

The decision illustrates how warrants generated by fines, missed court dates, and low-level cases can interact with street policing.

New York's People v. De Bour Framework

New York's Court of Appeals established a four-level structure for police-initiated street encounters.

Level one permits a request for information supported by an objective, credible reason not necessarily indicating crime.

Level two permits a common-law inquiry supported by founded suspicion that criminal activity is afoot.

Level three permits a forcible stop based upon reasonable suspicion that the person committed, is committing, or is about to commit a felony or misdemeanor.

Level four permits arrest upon probable cause.

The graduated framework gives New York residents protections and analytical distinctions beyond a simple voluntary-contact-versus-Terry-stop model.

New York's Frisk Requirement

A New York Level III stop does not automatically authorize a frisk.

The officer must reasonably suspect danger of physical injury and limit the search to weapons or dangerous instruments.

The state's appellate courts continue applying De Bour, People v. Hollman, and related cases to distinguish requests, accusatory inquiry, forcible detention, frisk, and search.

New York courts may suppress evidence under the state constitution even when federal doctrine might permit a broader intrusion.

New York City Precursors

New York City officers used street stops before the modern high-volume program.

Specialized plainclothes and anti-crime units concentrated upon robberies, guns, drugs, and street offenses.

The Street Crime Unit became especially prominent under the slogan that it sought illegal guns.

Aggressive field interrogation produced complaints concerning race, force, documentation, and unsupported suspicion before stop totals reached their later peak.

Amadou Diallo and the Street Crime Unit

On February 4, 1999, four Street Crime Unit officers approached Amadou Diallo in the vestibule of his Bronx building.

The officers fired forty-one shots, striking him nineteen times and killing him.

Diallo was unarmed.

The officers stated that they believed he was reaching for a gun; the object was his wallet.

The killing intensified scrutiny of racial profiling, aggressive street encounters, plainclothes tactics, and the Street Crime Unit.

The officers were acquitted of criminal charges, but the event became central to the legal and political history of stop-and-frisk.

New York Attorney General's 1999 Report

The New York Attorney General conducted a major analysis of NYPD stop-and-frisk practices after Diallo's death.

The report examined legal standards, UF-250 stop reports, racial patterns, precinct practices, supervision, and outcomes.

It found substantial racial disparities that could not be explained fully by population or reported-crime measures.

The report also emphasized incomplete documentation and the need for stronger legal training and supervision.

It established an empirical foundation later used in litigation and public debate.

Daniels v. City of New York

The Center for Constitutional Rights and plaintiffs filed Daniels after Diallo's killing, challenging Street Crime Unit stops as racially discriminatory and unsupported by reasonable suspicion.

The 2003 settlement required reforms involving policy, training, documentation, supervision, audits, and public data.

The Street Crime Unit had been disbanded in 2002.

The settlement did not end stop-and-frisk.

Citywide stop volume expanded dramatically during the following decade.

Bloomberg–Kelly Era Expansion

Michael Bloomberg became mayor in 2002, and Raymond Kelly served as police commissioner through 2013.

The NYPD integrated stop-question-frisk with CompStat management, Operation Impact deployment, anti-crime units, public-housing trespass enforcement, quality-of-life policing, and gun-suppression strategies.

Officers recorded increasingly large numbers of stops.

Command and productivity pressure became a central disputed issue.

Police leaders argued that the tactic deterred gun carrying, removed weapons, solved crimes, and protected Black and Latino shooting victims.

Critics argued that the department converted an individualized legal tool into a mass program measuring activity rather than suspicion.

Operation Impact

Operation Impact assigned large numbers of officers, including recent academy graduates, to designated high-crime areas.

The program aimed to increase visible presence and suppress shootings, robberies, and other street crime.

Impact Zones generated high stop volume and concentrated encounters in neighborhoods with large Black and Latino populations.

Critics questioned whether inexperienced officers received enough supervision and whether geographic designation became a substitute for individualized suspicion.

Public Housing and Trespass Enforcement

The NYPD conducted vertical patrols in New York City Housing Authority buildings and trespass enforcement in private apartment buildings enrolled in the Trespass Affidavit Program.

Residents and guests reported being stopped in lobbies, stairwells, elevators, roofs, and courtyards and required to prove permission to be present.

A person's presence in or near a residential building did not by itself establish trespass.

Ligon v. City of New York challenged stops outside private buildings, while Davis v. City of New York addressed public-housing practices.

These cases became part of the federal reform structure associated with Floyd.

UF-250 Stop Reports

The UF-250 form documented reasons, suspected crime, location, frisk, search, force, race, age, outcome, and recovered property.

The reports created an unusually large public dataset.

They also reflected officer reporting choices and did not necessarily capture every encounter.

Repeated checked boxes such as “furtive movements,” “high-crime area,” and “fits description” became important evidence concerning whether officers articulated individualized facts.

Modern electronic stop reports contain more detailed fields, but accuracy still depends upon truthful completion and meaningful supervision.

Peak Scale

Recorded NYPD stops rose from tens of thousands annually around 2000 to hundreds of thousands during the following decade.

The department recorded 685,724 stops in 2011.

This averaged more than 1,800 recorded stops per day.

The volume mattered because even a modest percentage of unsupported stops would translate into tens of thousands of constitutional violations.

It also made stop activity a routine experience for many young Black and Latino men rather than an exceptional investigative event.

The 4.4 Million Stops Examined in Floyd

The federal court examined approximately 4.4 million recorded stops from January 2004 through June 2012.

Black people accounted for 52 percent of the stops.

Hispanic people accounted for 31 percent.

White people accounted for 10 percent.

Approximately 88 percent of stops resulted in no arrest or summons.

The court evaluated not only raw disparity but also expert analysis, stop reasons, precinct patterns, outcomes, departmental policy, supervision, testimony, recordings, and statements by officials.

Hit Rates

A hit rate measures how often a stop produces an arrest, summons, weapon, contraband, or other enforcement result.

Low hit rates can indicate weak targeting, but no-result stops are not automatically unlawful because reasonable suspicion can be disproved through investigation.

High hit rates do not establish that every stop was lawful because evidence may be discovered after an unsupported seizure.

Racial comparisons may reveal whether officers apply different suspicion thresholds, but analysis must account for offense, location, time, reporting, and selection into the stopped population.

Gun Recovery

Police leaders defended stop-question-frisk as a weapon-removal and deterrence strategy.

Critics emphasized that only a small fraction of peak-era stops recovered firearms.

Supporters responded that deterrence cannot be measured solely by recovered guns because people may stop carrying weapons when they expect lawful police activity.

The deterrence claim is difficult to isolate from other policing, incarceration, demographic, market, economic, and community changes.

A lawful gun-focused strategy still requires individualized facts; the seriousness of gun violence does not authorize random searches.

Crime-Reduction Debate

New York City's homicide and serious-crime rates fell greatly during years of high stop volume.

Crime also fell before the largest expansion and in other cities using different strategies.

Multiple changes operated simultaneously, including CompStat, focused deployment, investigations, incarceration, demographic change, the decline of crack markets, economic conditions, community action, and medical improvements in trauma care.

Stops fell by more than 90 percent after the peak without a return to early-1990s crime levels.

This undermined claims that hundreds of thousands of annual stops were necessary, while not proving that every properly targeted stop lacks preventive value.

Racial Profiling

Racial profiling occurs when race or ethnicity is used as a basis for suspicion beyond a sufficiently specific suspect description or another lawful and narrowly defined purpose.

It may be explicit, such as an instruction to target a racial group.

It may also operate through vague descriptions, deployment, stereotypes, selective interpretation of behavior, and unequal thresholds.

The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits intentional racial discrimination.

Statistical disparity alone does not answer every intent question, but extreme and persistent disparity combined with policy and supervisory evidence can support a constitutional finding.

Black and Latino Experience

Young Black and Latino men experienced repeated stops while walking, travelling to school or work, visiting friends, entering their homes, or standing in public.

Some were stopped multiple times without arrest or summons.

Encounters could involve wall positioning, handcuffs, waistband searches, commands, insults, force, public exposure, and fear of escalation.

The cumulative effect mattered even when each encounter was brief.

Families adapted behavior, clothing, travel routes, and conversations with children around anticipated police contact.

Children and Youth

Teenagers and young adults were major subjects of high-volume stop activity.

A police encounter can affect school attendance, mental health, trust, identity, willingness to seek help, and relationships with public institutions.

Young people may not understand whether an encounter is voluntary or how to assert rights without appearing defiant.

Youth-focused policy should account for developmental differences, disability, trauma, language, and the risk of unnecessary escalation.

Dignitary and Psychological Harm

A stop may cause fear, humiliation, anger, helplessness, public embarrassment, or a sense of racial exclusion even when no physical injury occurs.

Repeated encounters may contribute to anxiety, hypervigilance, avoidance, and distrust.

These harms are difficult to represent in arrest statistics.

Constitutional analysis recognizes bodily and movement intrusion, while public policy must also consider cumulative social and psychological consequences.

Police Perspective

Officers encounter ambiguous situations in which delay can expose victims or officers to danger.

Reasonable suspicion gives police authority to investigate before probable cause is complete.

A lawful frisk may prevent a weapon from being used during that investigation.

Officers and police organizations argue that abolishing the tactic would prevent response to reliable descriptions, observed weapon concealment, casing behavior, and rapidly developing street crimes.

The central reform issue is therefore not whether police may ever stop or frisk, but how to ensure that officers can act on concrete danger without transforming whole communities into presumptive suspects.

Supervisory and Productivity Pressure

Plaintiffs and officers in New York litigation described pressure to produce stops, summonses, and arrests.

Formal quotas and informal expectations can encourage officers to prioritize numbers rather than legal quality.

Commanders may deny a quota while evaluating officers according to activity comparisons and unexplained numerical expectations.

A performance system should reward lawful identification of serious threats, accurate reports, community problem solving, and restraint—not merely total stops.

Floyd v. City of New York

Black and Latino plaintiffs brought a federal class action challenging New York City's stop-question-frisk practices.

They alleged that officers stopped people without reasonable suspicion in violation of the Fourth Amendment and targeted Black and Latino people in violation of equal protection.

A lengthy bench trial in 2013 included individual testimony, police recordings, stop reports, expert statistical analysis, departmental documents, training evidence, and testimony from officers and officials.

The case evaluated municipal policy and deliberate indifference rather than one isolated stop.

2013 Liability Ruling

Judge Shira Scheindlin held that the City had violated the Fourth Amendment through deliberate indifference to a pattern of stops and frisks without legal justification.

She also found a Fourteenth Amendment violation based upon indirect racial profiling and deliberate indifference to discriminatory practice.

The ruling did not find every one of the millions of stops unlawful.

It found a systemic municipal practice producing repeated constitutional violations.

Remedial Order

The court ordered a reform process rather than ending all investigative stops.

The remedies included revised policies, training, supervision, documentation, audits, discipline, a body-worn-camera pilot, community participation, and appointment of an independent federal monitor.

Related reforms incorporated trespass-enforcement cases involving public and private housing.

The monitor's role is to assess whether the NYPD has implemented reforms and achieved sustained legal compliance.

Appeal and Municipal Settlement

The Bloomberg administration appealed and contested the findings and remedy.

After Bill de Blasio became mayor, the City agreed in 2014 to accept the core reforms and withdraw its appeal.

Police unions attempted to intervene but were not permitted to overturn the City's decision to settle.

The federal judgment and remedial process therefore continued without a merits reversal by the Court of Appeals.

Federal Monitorship

A court-appointed monitor reviews policies, training, stop reports, body-camera footage, supervision, audits, discipline, and data.

The monitor reports publicly to the federal court.

The position continues until the City achieves the required compliance milestones.

Monitoring does not operate the NYPD or replace the police commissioner.

It creates an external method for comparing departmental claims with independent samples and legal standards.

Body-Worn Cameras

The Floyd remedy included a body-camera pilot, and the NYPD later deployed cameras widely.

Video can reveal whether an encounter was consensual, what officers said, when detention began, what facts were visible, whether a frisk occurred, and how supervisors characterize the event.

Cameras do not capture every angle or thought and may be unactivated, obstructed, muted, or unavailable.

They substantially improve the ability of supervisors, monitors, courts, prosecutors, defense attorneys, and complaint investigators to test written reports.

Right to Know Act

New York City's Right to Know Act created identification, explanation, business-card, and consent-search requirements for specified police encounters.

The law was intended to reduce confusion about officer identity and whether a person could refuse a request.

Its application depends upon the encounter category and statutory exceptions.

Disclosure requirements improve transparency but do not cure an encounter that lacked reasonable suspicion from the beginning.

How Many Stops Act

New York City enacted the How Many Stops Act over a mayoral veto in January 2024.

Local Law 43 expanded public reporting of Level I, Level II, and Level III investigative encounters.

Earlier public stop datasets focused mainly upon Level III forcible stops.

Supporters argued that lower-level encounters can become coercive and were often undercounted.

Opponents argued that added reporting burdens consume officer time and discourage useful community contact.

The NYPD began publishing Level I, II, and III encounter reports for the second half of 2024 and continues publishing quarterly data.

Underreporting

Administrative data measure reported stops, not necessarily every stop that occurred.

Officers may misunderstand when a voluntary encounter becomes a detention, fail to complete the report, classify the encounter at a lower level, or omit a frisk or search.

Body-camera review and civilian complaints have identified encounters absent from stop databases.

Underreporting distorts racial analysis, hit rates, officer histories, and supervisory evaluation.

A decline in recorded stops may reflect less stopping, incomplete recording, or both.

Supervisory Compliance Gap

A 2025 New York City Comptroller audit summarized findings from the federal monitor concerning the first half of 2024.

NYPD supervisors concluded that 99 percent of stops, 98 percent of frisks, and 97 percent of searches in their reviews were compliant.

The monitor's independent review found compliance rates of approximately 89 percent for stops, 69 percent for frisks, and 68 percent for searches.

The difference indicated that supervisors were not consistently identifying unsupported frisks and searches.

The figures came from reviewed samples and should not be applied mechanically to every encounter, but they demonstrate continuing weaknesses in internal oversight.

Discipline and Corrective Action

An unlawful stop can lead to retraining, command instruction, monitoring, discipline, suppression of evidence, complaint findings, civil litigation, or early-intervention review.

New York City's disciplinary guidelines identify improper stops, questions, frisks, and searches as abuse-of-authority offenses.

Critics have argued that training without meaningful consequences may not deter repeated violations.

Police managers respond that legal mistakes vary in seriousness and that discipline should account for intent, harm, record, complexity, and whether correction is possible.

Recorded Stops in 2024

The NYPD's individual-level public dataset contains 25,386 stop records for 2024.

New York City's EquityNYC indicator reported that recorded stop-question-frisk encounters increased 53 percent from 2023 to 2024 and approximately 190 percent from 2021 to 2024.

EquityNYC also reported that the 2024 stop rate for Black New Yorkers was 16.2 times the rate for white New Yorkers.

These figures document renewed growth from the early post-reform period while remaining far below the 2011 peak.

Recorded Stops in 2025

The NYPD's 2025 public stop database contains 26,422 records.

This was approximately 4.1 percent more than the 25,386 records in 2024.

The published racial categories identify 15,538 stopped people as Black, 5,594 as white Hispanic, 2,614 as Black Hispanic, 1,692 as white, 590 as Asian or Pacific Islander, 313 as Middle Eastern or Southwest Asian, and 81 as American Indian or Alaska Native.

Black, white-Hispanic, and Black-Hispanic categories together represented approximately 89.9 percent of recorded stops.

The data document who officers reported stopping; they do not by themselves establish whether the distribution was legally justified or discriminatory.

2025 Frisks, Searches, Arrests, and Weapons

The 2025 dataset records 13,784 frisks, approximately 52.2 percent of stops.

It records 10,780 searches, approximately 40.8 percent.

It records 7,819 arrests, approximately 29.6 percent, and 951 summonses, approximately 3.6 percent.

A weapon-found flag appears in 3,144 records, approximately 11.9 percent.

A firearm flag appears in 963 records, approximately 3.6 percent.

These rates differ substantially from peak-era outcomes and may reflect more targeted deployment, different reporting practices, changing suspected offenses, searches incident to arrest, and other institutional changes.

They do not resolve the legality of any particular stop, frisk, or search.

Status through August 2026

Stop-question-frisk remains part of NYPD policy and American constitutional law.

The NYPD publishes full-year 2025 stop data and first-quarter 2026 reports, together with lower-level investigative-encounter reporting.

Federal monitoring remains part of the court-ordered reform structure, and City agencies continue reviewing body-camera access, supervision, early intervention, discipline, and racial disparities.

Recorded stop volume has risen from the lowest post-reform years but remains a small fraction of the Bloomberg-era peak.

The central controversy has shifted from whether hundreds of thousands of stops should occur to whether a smaller and more targeted program is accurately reported, lawfully supervised, effective against weapons and violence, and equitably distributed.

Effectiveness

Stop-and-frisk can be effective in an individual case when an officer lawfully interrupts a robbery, identifies a person matching a reliable description, or safely recovers a weapon.

The policy question is whether a department's aggregate program reduces serious crime enough to justify its costs.

Research has difficulty separating stop activity from hot-spots deployment, CompStat, gun investigations, staffing, economic conditions, community initiatives, and broader crime trends.

Evidence does not support indiscriminate stop volume as an independent measure of safety.

Precision, legal quality, focused place and offense selection, and community legitimacy are more defensible than mass activity.

Deterrence

Supporters argue that people are less likely to carry illegal guns when they believe police may lawfully stop and frisk them.

The deterrence effect cannot be observed directly.

High stop volume may also deter lawful public activity, cooperation with police, and use of public space.

A policy must distinguish deterrence of weapon carrying from deterrence of ordinary movement by innocent residents.

Constitutional rights do not depend solely upon whether an unlawful tactic might discourage crime.

Institutional and Social Costs

Stops consume officer and supervisory time.

Unlawful encounters create suppression litigation, civil claims, complaint investigations, discipline, training costs, and monitor review.

Residents may lose time, miss transportation, experience public humiliation, or suffer physical and psychological injury.

Repeated low-yield encounters may reduce willingness to report shootings, testify, identify suspects, or cooperate with investigations.

Cost analysis should include both direct enforcement benefits and damage to institutional legitimacy.

Officer Safety

A person reasonably suspected of violent crime may present immediate danger before officers possess probable cause to arrest.

The protective frisk permits officers to investigate without being required to ignore objective weapon risk.

Officer safety is not served by unsupported routine pat-downs that normalize weak articulation and generate avoidable resistance.

Accurate threat assessment, cover, communication, positioning, backup, and tactical patience may protect officers more effectively than automatic physical contact.

Community Safety

Black and Latino communities experience disproportionate shares of shootings and homicide in many cities and may demand strong action against illegal guns.

The same communities may oppose being stopped without lawful cause.

These positions are not contradictory.

Residents can seek effective investigation of violence while rejecting indiscriminate surveillance and bodily intrusion.

Policy should measure whether stops reach people connected to specific danger rather than use victim demographics to justify broad suspicion of an entire population.

Immigrants, LGBTQ+ People, and Other Vulnerable Groups

Street encounters can carry consequences beyond the immediate stop.

Immigrants may fear status inquiry, database exposure, or contact with federal authorities.

Transgender and gender-nonconforming people have reported searches, misgendering, harassment, and assumptions concerning prostitution.

Disabled people may have difficulty hearing, understanding, communicating, or complying with rapid commands.

Policy and training must prevent identity from being treated as suspicion and provide accommodations where required.

Interpreting Stop Data

Population share is an important comparison but not the only possible denominator.

Researchers may compare stops with resident population, pedestrian exposure, suspect descriptions, victim reports, arrests, calls for service, crime location, age and sex distributions, or officer deployment.

Each comparison answers a different question and may contain bias.

Arrest-based benchmarks can reproduce earlier discriminatory enforcement.

Suspect-description data may be incomplete or shaped by reporting and crime patterns.

Strong analysis uses multiple measures and remains explicit about what can and cannot be inferred.

Outcome Measures and Selection

Search and weapon hit rates are measured only among people already selected for police contact.

If groups face different thresholds for being stopped, comparing post-stop outcomes may understate or distort discrimination.

Administrative data rarely include comparable people whom officers observed but chose not to stop.

Statistical conclusions should therefore avoid treating the stopped population as a neutral sample of the public.

Procedural Justice

Even a legally justified stop can be conducted respectfully or abusively.

Officers can identify themselves, explain the reason, limit commands, avoid unnecessary force, protect privacy, return documents, state the outcome, and provide a business card where required.

Respectful procedure does not cure missing reasonable suspicion.

It does reduce confusion and may preserve cooperation during a lawful encounter.

Alternatives and Complementary Strategies

Police can focus upon detailed suspect descriptions, shooting investigations, firearm tracing, warrant service, intelligence-led cases, violence interruption, place-based problem solving, and community partnership.

Environmental improvements, lighting, abandoned-building security, youth programs, victim services, and employment may address conditions associated with violence.

Technology and surveillance tools raise their own constitutional and equity questions and should not be assumed harmless alternatives.

A lawful Terry stop remains necessary in some cases, but it should be one tool within a broader public-safety system.

Requirements for a Lawful Departmental Program

Officers must receive accurate legal and scenario-based training.

Policy must state that stops require individualized reasonable suspicion and frisks require armed-danger suspicion.

Reports must contain facts rather than checked conclusions.

Supervisors must review body-camera footage and reject insufficient reports.

Departments must identify underreporting and officers with repeated unlawful encounters.

Performance systems must not reward raw stop volume.

Data must be published and analyzed for race, age, sex, geography, suspected offense, frisk, search, force, outcome, and weapon recovery.

Complaint, discipline, suppression, and civil-liability information must feed back into training and early intervention.

Conditions under Which Stop-and-Frisk Becomes Abusive

The practice becomes abusive when officers stop people based upon generalized demographics or location.

It becomes abusive when a frisk is treated as automatic.

It becomes abusive when officers search for evidence under the name of a protective pat-down.

It becomes abusive when command pressure rewards numbers over facts.

It becomes abusive when reports are not completed or supervisors approve boilerplate.

It becomes abusive when force, humiliation, or retaliation replaces investigation.

It becomes abusive when racial concentration is ignored rather than examined.

It becomes abusive when courts, complaints, and body-camera evidence reveal violations without corrective consequences.

How Success Should Be Measured

Stop totals are an activity measure, not a safety outcome.

Useful measures include lawful-articulation rates, valid frisk and search rates, recovery of weapons connected to actual danger, serious-crime reduction, complaint rates, suppression decisions, force, racial disparities, underreporting, cooperation, and community trust.

A program may recover weapons while still producing too many unsupported stops.

A low-volume program may still be unconstitutional if officers target people by race.

Evaluation must consider both effectiveness and rights.

Major Outcomes

Terry v. Ohio created a durable national doctrine permitting temporary investigative detention below the probable-cause threshold.

The Court separated suspicion of crime from suspicion of armed danger.

New York developed a more detailed four-level framework for police encounters.

New York City expanded stop-question-frisk into a program recording hundreds of thousands of annual stops.

The program concentrated heavily upon Black and Latino people and produced millions of encounters without arrest or summons.

The 2013 Floyd ruling found systemic Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment violations.

Federal monitoring, body cameras, revised training, documentation, public reporting, and community remedies followed.

Recorded stop volume fell dramatically and later increased from the lowest post-reform levels.

Current data show a smaller, higher-yield program that remains racially concentrated and subject to significant frisk, search, supervision, and reporting concerns.

The tactic remains lawful; the mass suspicionless model does not.

Limits and Unresolved Questions

Administrative data do not include every encounter or every fact known to officers.

Weapon and arrest outcomes do not prove the legality of the preceding stop.

No-result stops are not automatically unlawful.

Statistical disparity does not identify the complete cause without additional evidence.

Crime-reduction effects are difficult to isolate from simultaneous strategies and social changes.

Federal-monitor samples do not represent a judicial ruling on every stop.

Body-camera footage may be incomplete and requires legal interpretation.

The appropriate balance between targeted gun enforcement and reducing intrusive police contact remains politically and empirically disputed.

National practice varies widely among states and departments; New York City should not be treated as the only form of stop-and-frisk.

Misinformation and Common Errors

Stop-and-frisk is not categorically unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court did not authorize random pat-downs in Terry v. Ohio.

A stop and a frisk require separate justifications.

Reasonable suspicion is less than probable cause but more than a hunch.

Presence in a high-crime area does not alone justify a stop.

Race alone does not justify a stop.

A broad suspect description does not automatically justify stopping every person who matches race and sex.

Refusing voluntary questions does not automatically create reasonable suspicion.

A frisk is a weapon pat-down, not a general evidence search.

Feeling an ordinary object such as a wallet or phone does not automatically justify reaching into a pocket.

The Floyd court did not ban all lawful stops in New York City.

The 2013 ruling concerned both unsupported stops and racial discrimination.

The 88 percent no-enforcement rate does not mean exactly 88 percent of stops were unconstitutional.

Crime falling during high stop volume does not prove that mass stops caused the decline.

Crime remaining comparatively low after stops fell does not prove that no lawful stop has value.

Current stop totals are far below 2011 but are not zero.

The 2025 firearm flag is not necessarily a count of unique guns and does not establish legality for every stop.

Recorded data may omit unreported encounters.

Stop-and-frisk, broken-windows policing, CompStat, hot-spots policing, and stop-and-identify laws are related but distinct subjects.

Key Distinctions

Consensual encounter
Police contact in which a reasonable person remains free to decline and leave.
Request for information
A lower-level inquiry that may be permitted without reasonable suspicion under applicable state law, provided it does not become detention.
Common-law inquiry
New York's Level II accusatory questioning supported by founded suspicion that criminal activity is afoot.
Stop
A temporary seizure requiring individualized reasonable suspicion of a crime.
Frisk
A limited protective pat-down requiring reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.
Search
A more intrusive examination ordinarily requiring consent, probable cause, a warrant, arrest-related authority, or another exception.
Arrest
A custodial seizure requiring probable cause.
Reasonable suspicion
Specific, articulable, individualized facts supporting a reasonable inference of criminal activity.
Probable cause
A fair probability that a crime occurred and the person committed it, sufficient for arrest or many searches.
Terry stop
The federal term for a brief investigative detention authorized under Terry v. Ohio.
Stop, Question, and Frisk
A police-administration term describing a stop that may include questioning and, when separately justified, a frisk.
Stop-and-identify law
A state statute requiring specified identifying information during a valid detention; it does not authorize a stop by itself.
Plain feel
Seizure of contraband whose identity becomes immediately apparent during a lawful weapons pat-down, without additional manipulation.
Racial profiling
Use of race or ethnicity as a basis for suspicion beyond a sufficiently specific suspect description or other lawful purpose.
Hit rate
The proportion of recorded stops producing an arrest, summons, weapon, contraband, or other defined result.
Underreporting
Failure to document a qualifying encounter or accurate level of intrusion.
Federal monitorship
Court-supervised independent review of reform implementation and sustained compliance.
Exclusionary rule
The suppression of evidence obtained through unconstitutional government conduct, subject to doctrinal exceptions.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Distinguish the Supreme Court's Terry doctrine from New York City's mass policy.

Distinguish a voluntary conversation from a stop.

Distinguish a stop from a frisk.

Distinguish a frisk from a search inside clothing or property.

Distinguish reasonable suspicion from probable cause.

Do not state that high-crime location, race, nervousness, or refusal to cooperate is sufficient alone.

Do not state that officers must frisk every person suspected of a crime.

Do not state that the Floyd court outlawed all stop-and-frisk.

Do not use no-arrest outcome as a complete substitute for legal analysis.

Do not use a weapon recovery as retroactive proof that the stop was justified.

Do not use population share as the only possible racial benchmark or dismiss it as irrelevant.

Identify whether statistics refer to stop records, people, searches, weapons, arrests, summonses, or convictions.

State whether Hispanic categories include Black Hispanic and white Hispanic records.

Treat NYPD datasets as reported administrative records subject to underreporting and classification error.

Distinguish a monitor's sample finding from a ruling that every encounter in that year had the same compliance rate.

Distinguish stop-and-frisk from broken-windows policing, quality-of-life enforcement, CompStat, and hot-spots deployment.

Why Stop-and-Frisk Matters

Stop-and-frisk is a foundational example of constitutional balancing between public safety and personal liberty.

It gives officers authority to investigate danger before probable cause becomes complete.

It also places bodily freedom in the hands of officers making rapid and discretionary judgments.

The doctrine's two separate requirements show that suspicion of crime does not automatically equal suspicion of a weapon.

New York City's experience demonstrated how a limited power can change when managerial systems reward volume and tolerate weak articulation.

The 4.4 million-stop record made racial profiling measurable and transformed individual street encounters into a municipal constitutional case.

The Floyd ruling showed that a city can be liable not because every officer acts unlawfully, but because policies and supervision permit a pattern of violations.

The collapse in stop volume without a return to early-1990s crime levels challenged the claim that mass stopping was indispensable.

Current higher weapon and arrest rates suggest that a smaller program may be more targeted, while continuing racial concentration and compliance gaps show that lower volume does not guarantee fairness.

Body cameras, monitor review, and expanded reporting demonstrate how accountability depends upon evidence beyond officer paperwork.

The debate remains central because communities require protection from shootings and robbery while also requiring protection from suspicionless state intrusion.

The durable principle is that urgency cannot replace individualized facts: police may intervene against concrete danger, but public safety does not permit treating race, neighborhood, or ordinary presence as a crime.

Summary

Stop-and-frisk is a limited constitutional authority, not a general power to search people at random. Under Terry v. Ohio, police may briefly stop a person when specific and articulable facts create individualized reasonable suspicion of crime. A frisk requires a separate reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous and must remain a protective pat-down for weapons. More intrusive searching ordinarily requires additional legal authority.

New York codified stop-and-frisk authority in Criminal Procedure Law § 140.50 and developed a four-level encounter framework in People v. De Bour. New York City later transformed the tactic into a high-volume proactive-policing program. Recorded stops peaked at 685,724 in 2011. The federal court in Floyd v. City of New York examined approximately 4.4 million stops from 2004 through June 2012; 52 percent involved Black people, 31 percent involved Hispanic people, and approximately 88 percent ended without arrest or summons.

In 2013, the Floyd court found systemic Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment violations. It did not ban lawful Terry stops. The remedy created federal monitoring, revised training and policy, documentation review, body-worn-camera use, supervisory reforms, discipline and early-intervention mechanisms, and community participation. Recorded stops fell dramatically.

Stop numbers later rose from the lowest post-reform years. The NYPD's 2025 dataset contains 26,422 recorded stops. Approximately 89.9 percent involved people classified as Black or Hispanic, 52.2 percent included a frisk, 40.8 percent included a search, 29.6 percent resulted in arrest, and 3.6 percent carried a firearm flag. A federal-monitor review of sampled 2024 encounters found continuing legal-compliance problems, especially in frisks and searches, and substantial disagreement with NYPD supervisors' assessments.

As of August 2, 2026, stop-and-frisk remained lawful when supported by individualized facts and limited to its proper purpose. Its history demonstrates that constitutionality depends not only upon written doctrine but upon deployment, racial selection, officer articulation, report accuracy, supervision, discipline, and whether institutions value lawful precision more than enforcement volume.
