School-to-Prison Pipeline

A framework describing how exclusionary school discipline, police involvement, court referral, educational exclusion, disability-service failures, racial bias, poverty, and juvenile-justice practices can move students away from classrooms and toward repeated contact with the criminal-legal system.

What Is the School-to-Prison Pipeline?

The school-to-prison pipeline is a framework for understanding how educational discipline and criminal-justice systems can become connected.

It describes processes through which students are removed from instruction, labelled as disciplinary problems, referred to police, cited or arrested, placed under court supervision, transferred to restrictive educational settings, and exposed to later justice-system contact.

The pipeline is not a physical institution or one mandatory path. Many students who are suspended never enter court, and many court-involved youths were not pushed out of school through discipline.

The concept is most useful when it identifies the particular policies, decisions, and institutional handoffs that make legal involvement more likely.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet addresses public-school discipline, school police, school-related arrest, juvenile justice, racial and disability disparity, educational exclusion, school safety, civil-rights law, and alternatives.

It covers preschool through grade twelve, while recognizing that juvenile courts, alternative schools, justice facilities, and adult criminal courts may become involved at different ages.

It does not claim that schools should ignore violent or dangerous behavior.

It also does not assume that every disciplinary disparity is caused by one form of bias or that every student referral is unlawful.

Canonical Classification

The school-to-prison pipeline belongs canonically under concepts-and-terminology because it names a recurring institutional pattern rather than one organization, statute, or event.

Related pages may belong under laws and legislation, legal cases, government programs, institutions, social movements, or education history.

The concept can appear in criminal-justice, African American history, disability rights, juvenile justice, and public-education indexes.

Cross-linking should preserve one canonical file rather than creating duplicate versions.

The Pipeline Metaphor

The word pipeline suggests movement through connected stages.

Students may pass from classroom discipline to administrative removal, police involvement, juvenile-court processing, probation, alternative placement, dropout, and later criminal-legal contact.

The metaphor draws attention to cumulative institutional effects rather than treating each decision as unrelated.

Critics of the metaphor argue that it can imply inevitability and understate student agency, crime victimization, family circumstances, and variation among cases.

Why the Pipeline Is Not Automatic

No student is mechanically destined for incarceration because of one suspension or referral.

Family support, school reentry, legal representation, diversion, treatment, mentoring, employment, and individual choices can interrupt the sequence.

Many schools use discipline without routinely involving police or courts.

The proper inquiry is whether a policy increases risk and creates avoidable pathways, not whether every student experiences the final outcome.

Direct and Indirect Pathways

A direct pathway occurs when school personnel refer conduct to police or juvenile court.

An indirect pathway occurs when exclusion reduces instruction, weakens school attachment, increases unsupervised time, contributes to academic failure, or makes dropout more likely.

Students may then encounter police outside school or violate court and probation requirements connected to school attendance.

Both pathways can operate together, but they require different evidence and remedies.

School Pushout

School pushout describes formal and informal practices that remove students or make continued attendance difficult.

Examples include repeated suspension, involuntary transfer, pressure to withdraw, shortened school days, exclusion without documentation, failure to provide disability supports, and assignment to unsuitable alternative programs.

Pushout may occur without an arrest.

The term emphasizes institutional contribution while not denying that student conduct can create legitimate safety and educational concerns.

Exclusionary Discipline

Exclusionary discipline removes a student from the ordinary learning environment.

It includes out-of-school suspension, expulsion, some forms of in-school suspension, involuntary alternative placement, and informal removal.

Removal may be necessary in limited circumstances to protect safety or permit investigation.

Overuse reduces instructional time and can deepen the academic, behavioral, and relational problems that contributed to the incident.

In-School Suspension

In-school suspension separates a student from regular classes while keeping the student on campus.

A well-designed program can preserve academic work, supervision, counseling, and reintegration.

A poorly designed program may function as warehousing with little instruction or support.

National statistics count in-school and out-of-school suspension separately because their intensity and educational effects differ.

Out-of-School Suspension

Out-of-school suspension prohibits attendance for a defined period.

It immediately removes instruction and may leave students without structured supervision.

Parents and guardians may lose work or face emergency childcare responsibilities.

Suspension can be proportionate for serious misconduct, but repeated use for subjective or low-level conduct can increase disengagement without improving behavior.

Expulsion

Expulsion removes a student from a school or district for an extended period, sometimes for the remainder of a term or year.

State law may require continued educational services or permit placement in an alternative program.

Expulsion is among the most consequential school sanctions because it disrupts enrollment, peer relationships, coursework, services, and graduation progress.

Procedural protections generally become more important as the duration and severity of exclusion increase.

Informal Removal

Informal removal occurs when a school excludes a student without using the formal suspension or expulsion process.

Examples include repeated early pickup requests, shortened days, sending a child home without documentation, or pressuring a family to keep the student absent.

Informal removal can conceal the true scale of exclusion and bypass notice, data reporting, and appeal rights.

Students with disabilities are especially vulnerable when behavior related to unmet needs is managed through undocumented exclusion.

Alternative Schools

Alternative schools serve students who need a different educational environment or who have been removed for disciplinary reasons.

High-quality programs can provide smaller classes, counseling, flexible scheduling, credit recovery, and reentry planning.

Low-quality placements may offer reduced curricula, weak instruction, isolation, security-oriented control, and limited routes back to the home school.

Alternative placement can interrupt the pipeline or become one of its stages depending upon design and accountability.

Disciplinary Alternative Placement

A disciplinary alternative placement assigns a student to a separate program because of misconduct.

Placement may be mandatory under state law or locally determined.

The educational program should remain meaningful and should address the causes of the behavior rather than merely contain the student.

Duration, due process, disability rights, course access, transportation, and return criteria are essential.

Zero-Tolerance Policies

Zero-tolerance policies prescribe predetermined consequences for defined conduct, often with limited consideration of circumstances.

They expanded during the late twentieth century in response to drugs, weapons, violence, and demands for predictable enforcement.

Supporters argue that clear rules promote safety and fairness.

Critics argue that rigid policies punish accidental, minor, developmentally ordinary, or disability-related conduct too severely and encourage administrators to refer matters outside the school.

Gun-Free Schools Act

The federal Gun-Free Schools Act requires states receiving specified federal education funds to adopt policies requiring at least one year of expulsion for students who bring firearms to school, subject to case-by-case modification by the chief administering officer.

The law addresses firearms rather than every object broadly described as a weapon.

States and districts may enact wider rules.

The case-by-case modification authority is important because federal law does not require completely mechanical treatment of every circumstance.

Weapons Definitions

School codes often regulate firearms, knives, explosives, replicas, ordinary objects used as weapons, and prohibited objects.

Definitions can become overbroad when harmless items, toys, food shapes, tools, or inadvertent possession trigger the same response as intentional armed conduct.

Safety requires prompt assessment of actual capability, intent, context, age, and threat.

Accurate reporting should distinguish a firearm from a broad school-code weapons category.

Subjective Discipline Categories

Terms such as defiance, disrespect, disruption, insubordination, inappropriate behavior, and willful disobedience require interpretation.

Subjective categories allow educators to respond to varied classroom conditions.

They also create greater opportunity for inconsistent enforcement, cultural misunderstanding, disability bias, gender stereotypes, and racial bias.

Clear examples, graduated responses, documentation, and data review can reduce arbitrary application.

More Objective Offenses

Possession of a specified firearm, documented physical injury, sale of a controlled substance, or measurable property damage may be more objectively defined than disrespect.

Even objective conduct can involve disputed facts, self-defense, coercion, disability, mistake, or inconsistent punishment.

Objective definitions improve notice but do not eliminate the need for investigation and proportionality.

Schools should separate the factual finding from the sanction decision.

Dress Codes

Dress codes regulate clothing, grooming, symbols, exposure, safety, and disruption.

Subjective or gendered rules can disproportionately affect girls, Black students, religious minorities, transgender students, and students whose cultural expression differs from dominant expectations.

Discipline that removes a student from instruction for a minor clothing issue can be disproportionate to the educational need.

Rules should be specific, nondiscriminatory, accessible to families, and enforced without humiliation.

Truancy and Chronic Absence

Truancy refers to unexcused absence under state or local law, while chronic absence generally counts missed instructional days regardless of reason.

Absence may result from illness, disability, housing instability, caregiving, transportation, bullying, work, family crisis, or disengagement.

Punitive truancy enforcement can deepen family debt and court contact without addressing the cause.

Attendance intervention is more effective when schools identify barriers and provide transportation, health, housing, academic, and family support.

Status Offenses

Status offenses are acts prohibited because of the person's age, such as truancy, running away, or curfew violations.

They are distinct from conduct that would be criminal for an adult.

Juvenile systems historically used detention and probation to control status behavior.

Federal and state reforms seek to limit secure confinement for status offenses, but court orders and supervision violations can still create custody pathways.

School Misconduct versus Crime

Many school-rule violations are not crimes.

Talking back, classroom disruption, dress-code violations, unauthorized phone use, tardiness, and failure to complete work ordinarily belong within educational discipline.

Assault, weapons, sexual violence, credible threats, theft, and serious property destruction may require law-enforcement involvement.

The pipeline problem arises when schools treat ordinary discipline as criminal conduct or fail to distinguish developmental behavior from genuine public-safety threats.

Criminalization of Student Behavior

Criminalization occurs when conduct traditionally managed by families and educators is handled through police, citations, arrest, prosecution, or court supervision.

The same behavior may produce a conference in one school and an arrest in another.

Institutional presence, state law, school policy, staff discretion, race, disability, and local culture influence the response.

Criminalization can occur without conviction because police contact and court processing themselves impose consequences.

Referral to Law Enforcement

The Civil Rights Data Collection defines and counts school referrals to law-enforcement agencies or officials under federal reporting rules.

A referral may lead to investigation, citation, arrest, diversion, or no further action.

All school-related arrests are counted as referrals, but not all referrals become arrests.

In 2021–22, districts reported about 220,000 K–12 students referred to law enforcement.

School-Related Arrest

A school-related arrest generally concerns an arrest on school grounds, during school activities, or because of conduct connected to school, under the applicable CRDC definition.

Definitions and reporting practices have changed, complicating comparisons across collection years.

GAO warned that Education modified the 2021–22 arrest definition without notifying districts before data collection, creating a data-quality concern.

Arrest data should therefore be interpreted with attention to definitions, underreporting, and local practice.

Citations and Summonses

Police may issue a citation, summons, or complaint instead of making a custodial arrest.

The student may still face court dates, fines, probation, records, and family costs.

Because data systems differ, citation-based criminalization may not appear fully in arrest statistics.

A noncustodial process is less restrictive than detention but can still move school behavior into the justice system.

School Police

School police may be employed directly by a district or assigned by a local law-enforcement agency.

Their authority, training, supervision, collective-bargaining status, and relationship to school administrators vary.

Police can respond rapidly to serious threats and build relationships with students.

Their presence can also increase the likelihood that ordinary student conduct becomes a law-enforcement matter.

School Resource Officers

A school resource officer is generally a sworn law-enforcement officer assigned to one or more schools.

The role may include safety, law enforcement, mentoring, education, emergency planning, and liaison work.

Clear memoranda of understanding should define when officers may intervene and reserve routine discipline for educators.

Training should address adolescent development, disability, de-escalation, civil rights, trauma, and the difference between school rules and criminal law.

Security Officers and Other Safety Personnel

Schools may employ nonsworn security officers, monitors, private guards, safety assistants, or specialists.

Their legal authority is usually narrower than that of police.

Poorly defined roles can create confusion over search, restraint, questioning, use of force, and recordkeeping.

Safety staffing should be matched to actual risks and coordinated with counselors, nurses, administrators, and emergency services.

Police Participation in School Discipline

Police involvement in routine discipline blurs the boundary between educational authority and criminal enforcement.

GAO found that arrests were more common in schools where police participated in discipline.

Officers should not become the default response to noncriminal defiance, disrespect, classroom disruption, or dress-code violations.

Administrators remain responsible for school climate and cannot transfer ordinary behavior management to law enforcement merely because an officer is available.

Research on Police Presence

GAO's 2024 analysis of 2017–18 data found that arrest rates were more than twice as high in schools with police present as in otherwise similar schools without police.

The finding is an association strengthened by statistical comparison, not proof that police presence alone caused every arrest.

Schools with police may differ in size, location, reported safety concerns, student population, and administrative practice.

Evaluation should examine serious-incident response, minor arrests, racial disparities, student perception, and alternative safety investments.

Search and Seizure at School

Public-school officials are governed by the Fourth Amendment but generally operate under a reasonableness standard different from the warrant and probable-cause rules ordinarily applied to police.

Police searches may trigger stricter constitutional requirements depending upon who initiated and controlled the search.

Locker, backpack, phone, vehicle, drug-test, and person searches raise different issues.

Schools should define roles carefully because involving police can change the legal analysis and the consequences for the student.

Questioning Students

Administrators may question students about school misconduct, while police questioning can implicate constitutional protections, state law, and juvenile-specific rules.

Whether a student is in custody for Miranda purposes depends upon the circumstances, including age.

Parents do not possess an identical constitutional right to attend every school or police interview, though state laws and district policies may provide protections.

Students should understand whether the interview concerns discipline, crime, or both.

Use of Force in Schools

Police and school personnel may use reasonable force under applicable law to address immediate threats, fights, weapons, escape, or other dangerous situations.

Force that is unnecessary, excessive, retaliatory, or disproportionate can violate law and school policy.

Adolescent behavior, size, disability, trauma, and available alternatives matter.

Video of force may show only part of the incident, so investigation should include preceding events, commands, witness accounts, injuries, and policy.

Physical and Mechanical Restraint

Physical restraint restricts a student's movement through bodily force; mechanical restraint uses a device.

Restraint may be necessary during an imminent serious safety emergency.

It should not be used as punishment, convenience, routine compliance, or a substitute for adequate staffing and behavioral support.

The 2021–22 CRDC showed substantial overrepresentation of Black students, boys, and students with disabilities in restraint.

Seclusion

Seclusion involuntarily isolates a student alone in a space the student is prevented from leaving.

It differs from a voluntary calming space or ordinary classroom removal with supervision.

Seclusion can create physical and psychological risk and should be tightly restricted, documented, monitored, and reviewed.

Students served under IDEA represented 14 percent of enrollment but 68 percent of students secluded in the 2021–22 CRDC.

Handcuffing Students

Handcuffs are law-enforcement restraints intended to control movement and reduce immediate risk.

Their use on young children, students with disabilities, or nonviolent students has generated litigation and public controversy.

Officer safety may require restraint in particular circumstances, but age, size, behavior, threat, duration, and alternatives must be considered.

Handcuffing can transform a school incident into a traumatic justice-system encounter even when no charge follows.

Behavioral Threat Assessment

Threat assessment evaluates whether a student poses a genuine risk rather than relying solely upon alarming words or zero-tolerance labels.

Teams may consider communication, intent, planning, access to weapons, stressors, protective factors, and the student's movement toward or away from violence.

Assessment is different from prediction and cannot guarantee safety.

It should not become an unreviewed surveillance system or substitute for disability evaluation, mental-health care, and due process.

School Shootings and Safety Demands

School shootings and credible threats create legitimate pressure for security, emergency planning, reporting, and law-enforcement coordination.

The rarity of mass shootings does not diminish their catastrophic harm.

Policies designed for extraordinary violence can become overextended to minor conduct.

A balanced safety system distinguishes targeted prevention and rapid emergency response from routine criminalization of ordinary student behavior.

Bullying and Harassment

Schools must respond to bullying, discriminatory harassment, sexual harassment, threats, and violence.

Failure to protect victims can deny equal educational access and undermine trust.

Automatic exclusion or arrest is not the only possible response; investigation, protection plans, counseling, restorative processes where appropriate, and discipline may all be needed.

Restorative approaches should never force a victim into unsafe contact or replace legal reporting duties.

Fighting and Mutual Conflict

School fights range from brief mutual scuffles to planned group attacks causing serious injury.

Automatic arrest of every participant can ignore self-defense, coercion, disability, bullying, and differences in conduct.

Schools should stop the violence, provide medical care, preserve evidence, notify families, and assess continuing risk.

Serious assault may require police, while lower-level conflict may be handled through school discipline and structured intervention.

Entry into Juvenile Court

A school-related referral can lead to diversion, informal adjustment, a delinquency petition, detention, adjudication, probation, or dismissal.

Juvenile courts are civil or quasi-criminal institutions under state law but can impose significant loss of liberty.

The process is intended to account for youth development and rehabilitation while protecting public safety.

Pipeline analysis asks whether court involvement was necessary and whether school conduct was escalated beyond an educational response.

Juvenile Detention

Detention holds a young person before adjudication, after a court order, or while awaiting placement under state law.

It may be justified by flight risk, immediate danger, or statutory criteria.

Even short detention can interrupt education, medication, family connection, and employment.

Detention should not be used merely because a school lacks services or because a family cannot provide transportation or supervision.

Juvenile Probation

Juvenile probation can require school attendance, curfews, testing, treatment, reporting, restitution, and compliance with family or placement rules.

Supervision may provide structure and services.

Broad or unrealistic conditions can create repeated violations for conduct that would not otherwise justify incarceration.

The pipeline can deepen when school suspension itself becomes a probation violation or when a court order criminalizes ordinary adolescent noncompliance.

Valid Court Order and Status-Offense Detention

Federal juvenile-justice policy has long sought to deinstitutionalize status offenders.

Historically, a valid-court-order exception permitted detention for violating a court order arising from status conduct in qualifying circumstances.

States have adopted different restrictions and reforms.

Pipeline critics argue that transforming truancy or running away into contempt or probation violations recreates incarceration through a procedural detour.

Transfer to Adult Court

Some students accused of serious offenses may be transferred, waived, or directly filed into adult criminal court.

Rules vary by age, offense, prior record, prosecutor authority, and judicial findings.

Adult processing exposes youths to more severe sentences, records, and facilities.

A school-based incident can therefore have consequences extending far beyond educational discipline.

Education in Juvenile Justice Facilities

Young people in detention and residential placement retain educational rights under federal and state law.

Programs must address interrupted credits, special education, language access, reentry, and short or uncertain lengths of stay.

The 2021–22 CRDC reported that justice facilities offered an average of twenty-five hours of educational programming per week, while nineteen percent offered fewer than twenty hours.

Weak facility education can convert temporary custody into long-term academic exclusion.

Reentry from Juvenile Justice to School

Returning students may face delayed enrollment, lost records, incompatible credits, stigma, safety plans, and uncertainty about special education.

Immediate reenrollment and record transfer reduce lost instructional time.

Schools should distinguish the student's legal history from current educational need and risk.

Reentry is a critical point at which the pipeline can be interrupted or reinforced.

Juvenile Records and Confidentiality

Juvenile records may be confidential, sealed, expunged, or accessible under state-specific rules.

School records are separately governed by the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act and other laws.

Information sharing can protect safety and coordinate services but can also spread stigma beyond what is necessary.

Agreements should define purpose, access, retention, accuracy, correction, and redisclosure.

Racial Disparity

Black, American Indian or Alaska Native, and some other student groups experience disproportionate suspension, expulsion, referral, and arrest.

Disparity can arise through differences in school conditions, exposure to recorded conduct, subjective interpretation, resource inequality, policy design, and discrimination.

A statistical gap does not alone establish a legal violation.

It is nevertheless a warning requiring examination of comparable conduct, decision makers, sanctions, school assignment, and opportunity to learn.

Black Students

Black students were fifteen percent of K–12 public-school enrollment in 2021–22.

They accounted for twenty-eight percent of students referred to law enforcement and thirty-three percent of students subjected to school-related arrest.

Black boys and Black girls were also overrepresented in exclusionary discipline.

The disparity appears nationally but varies in size and mechanism across states, districts, schools, grade levels, sex, disability, and offense category.

Black Boys

Black boys were eight percent of K–12 enrollment in 2021–22.

They represented twenty-two percent of students receiving one or more out-of-school suspensions and twenty-one percent of students expelled.

Stereotypes associating Black boys with danger, aggression, maturity, or criminality can affect interpretation of behavior.

Black boys also experience serious victimization and need school environments that provide both protection and fair discipline.

Black Girls

Black girls were seven percent of K–12 enrollment in 2021–22 but thirteen percent of students receiving out-of-school suspension and thirteen percent of students expelled.

GAO found that Black girls received harsher discipline than White girls for similar infractions such as defiance, disrespect, and disruption in 2017–18 data.

Adultification, colorism, gender stereotypes, and punishment for nonconformity can shape discipline.

A male-centered pipeline analysis can overlook girls' experiences with trauma, harassment, self-defense, dress rules, caregiving, and interpersonal conflict.

Adultification Bias

Adultification bias occurs when adults perceive Black children, particularly girls, as older, less innocent, less vulnerable, or more responsible than similarly aged peers.

The perception can reduce empathy and increase punishment.

It does not explain every discipline decision involving a Black student.

Training, structured decision rules, student voice, and comparative data can help institutions identify and reduce its influence.

Colorism

Colorism is unequal treatment based upon skin tone within or across racial groups.

Research reviewed by GAO identified colorism as one factor associated with discipline disparities among girls.

National administrative data rarely contain skin-tone measures, making the mechanism difficult to quantify at scale.

Schools should ensure that appearance, hair, complexion, and racialized beauty standards do not affect behavioral judgments.

American Indian and Alaska Native Students

American Indian or Alaska Native students were about one percent of K–12 enrollment but two percent of referrals and school-related arrests in 2021–22.

National percentages can conceal much higher local exposure in particular states, reservations, border communities, and urban schools.

Colonial history, underfunding, jurisdiction, cultural misunderstanding, boarding-school trauma, and community conditions shape the context.

Tribal consultation and sovereignty are essential to locally appropriate reform.

Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander Students

GAO found elevated arrest rates for Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander students in 2017–18.

Small national population size can cause these students to disappear within aggregate categories.

Local data and community knowledge are necessary to interpret the disparity.

Disaggregation should be balanced against privacy risks in small schools.

Latino Students

Latino students constitute a large and diverse share of public-school enrollment.

National discipline patterns differ by location, sex, disability, immigration experience, English proficiency, and subgroup.

Language barriers and national-origin discrimination can affect communication with families and interpretation of behavior.

Schools should not treat Spanish use, accent, cultural expression, or family immigration status as disciplinary risk.

Asian American Students

Aggregate Asian student discipline rates are generally lower than national averages, but the category includes highly diverse communities.

Model-minority stereotypes can conceal disability, mental-health needs, bullying, poverty, refugee experience, and subgroup disparities.

Low aggregate discipline does not demonstrate equal treatment in every school.

Disaggregated analysis is appropriate where sample size permits.

Students with Disabilities

Students with disabilities are substantially overrepresented in suspension, expulsion, restraint, seclusion, referral, and arrest.

Behavior may communicate unmet disability needs, inaccessible instruction, sensory distress, trauma, communication difficulty, or an ineffective education plan.

Disability does not make all conduct harmless or exempt a student from accountability.

Schools must provide legally required services and avoid using discipline to substitute for evaluation, accommodation, and behavioral support.

Students Served under IDEA

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act provides special education and related services to eligible students.

In 2021–22, students served under IDEA were fourteen percent of K–12 enrollment but twenty-five percent of referrals and school-related arrests.

They were also heavily overrepresented in restraint and seclusion.

The disparity requires attention to identification, individualized education programs, functional behavior, staff capacity, placement, and discipline protections.

Students Protected by Section 504

Section 504 protects qualified students with disabilities in federally funded schools, including students who may not qualify under IDEA.

Schools must provide nondiscriminatory access, reasonable accommodations where required, and appropriate procedural protections.

Discipline may become discriminatory when a school punishes disability-related behavior while failing to provide required supports.

Federal guidance can be revised or rescinded across administrations, but the statute and regulations remain legally significant.

Manifestation Determination

IDEA generally requires a manifestation determination when a disciplinary change of placement reaches statutory conditions.

The team asks whether the conduct was caused by, or had a direct and substantial relationship to, the child's disability, or resulted directly from failure to implement the individualized education program.

A finding that conduct was a manifestation triggers specified corrective and placement protections.

The process is not a declaration that the conduct did not occur or that safety needs must be ignored.

Functional Behavioral Assessment

A functional behavioral assessment examines the purpose or function served by behavior.

Possible functions include escape from difficult work, attention, access to an item, sensory regulation, communication, peer status, or response to trauma.

The assessment should rely upon data rather than stereotypes.

Understanding function helps schools design support that changes conditions and teaches replacement skills rather than repeatedly punishing the symptom.

Behavior Intervention Plan

A behavior intervention plan identifies preventive supports, skills, staff responses, reinforcement, crisis procedures, and data collection.

The plan should be individualized, understandable, feasible, and implemented consistently.

A plan existing only on paper does not protect a student or staff.

Repeated discipline may indicate that the plan requires revision, training, or a different educational placement.

Significant Disproportionality under IDEA

IDEA requires states to identify significant disproportionality based upon race and ethnicity in identification, placement, and discipline under federal regulations.

When identified, districts may be required to review policies and use funds for comprehensive coordinated early intervening services.

State methodologies and thresholds affect which districts are identified.

The framework addresses systemic patterns rather than proving discrimination in every individual case.

Intersectionality

Students experience race, sex, disability, class, language, sexual orientation, gender identity, and age simultaneously.

GAO found that arrest disparities widened when race, gender, and disability status overlapped.

A broad category can conceal extreme risk for a smaller subgroup.

Data systems should support intersectional analysis while protecting privacy and avoiding labels that stigmatize individual students.

Boys

Boys are overrepresented in many forms of school discipline, restraint, referral, and arrest.

Differences may reflect conduct, peer dynamics, developmental expectations, teacher interpretation, school culture, and gender norms.

A pipeline analysis should not treat boys as naturally criminal or disregard the harms experienced by victims.

Schools need positive models of masculinity, emotional regulation, conflict resolution, academic engagement, and help-seeking.

Girls

Girls' discipline experiences can involve fighting, self-defense, trauma responses, dress codes, relationship conflict, sexual harassment, running away, and caregiving burdens.

Girls are not a uniform group, and aggregate female rates can conceal Black, Native, disabled, LGBTQ, and justice-involved girls.

School systems historically designed around male patterns may fail to recognize girls' needs.

Gender-responsive policy should preserve equal standards while addressing distinct pathways and safety concerns.

LGBTQ Students

LGBTQ students may experience bullying, family rejection, homelessness, dress-code enforcement, identity-based conflict, and school disengagement.

Federal education materials have cited research reporting elevated suspension among LGBTQ students, particularly LGBTQ students of color.

Law governing sex discrimination and gender identity has shifted through statutes, court decisions, regulations, and federal-administration policy.

Schools should maintain accurate, current legal guidance while preventing harassment and viewpoint discrimination.

English Learners

English learners were not overrepresented nationally in 2021–22 suspension and expulsion percentages relative to enrollment.

National averages do not rule out local discrimination or communication barriers.

Students and families need understandable notice, interpretation, and meaningful access to discipline proceedings.

Language difference should not be mistaken for defiance, disability, or lack of intelligence.

Poverty

Poverty affects transportation, housing, attendance, healthcare, nutrition, legal representation, technology, and family availability.

High-poverty schools may have fewer counselors, psychologists, social workers, experienced teachers, and program options.

GAO found that racial discipline disparities persisted across levels of school poverty.

Poverty therefore contributes to conditions but does not fully explain racial disparity.

Students Experiencing Homelessness

Homeless students may face transportation failures, lost records, fatigue, mobility, missing uniforms, storage problems, and difficulty completing work.

The McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act provides educational protections, including school stability and immediate enrollment under qualifying circumstances.

Discipline for absence, dress, or missing materials can punish the consequences of homelessness.

Housing and transportation intervention may be more effective than exclusion.

Students in Foster Care

Foster youth may experience school changes, trauma, court obligations, placement instability, medication changes, and gaps in records or services.

Federal law includes educational-stability provisions for qualifying children in foster care.

Behavioral difficulties can be intensified by disrupted relationships and inconsistent supports.

Coordination among schools, child welfare, courts, caregivers, and attorneys is necessary to prevent disciplinary pushout.

Immigrant and Refugee Students

Immigrant and refugee students may bring linguistic, cultural, war, displacement, or family-separation experiences.

Miscommunication can be interpreted as noncompliance or withdrawal.

Schools generally may not deny access to public elementary and secondary education based upon undocumented status under established Supreme Court law.

Discipline systems should not be used to gather or threaten immigration information unrelated to a legitimate legal duty.

Trauma

Trauma can affect attention, trust, emotional regulation, threat perception, memory, and behavior.

It may arise from violence, abuse, neglect, displacement, discrimination, incarceration of a family member, or community conditions.

Trauma-informed practice explains behavior without excusing harm.

Schools should combine safety, predictable boundaries, relationship, treatment referral, and accountability.

Adverse Childhood Experiences

Adverse childhood experiences include forms of abuse, household disruption, violence, and other serious stressors.

Higher exposure is associated with health, educational, and behavioral risks at the population level.

An ACE score is not a diagnosis or destiny and should not be used to predict criminality.

Universal supportive practices can reduce stigma while targeted services address individual need.

Mental Health

Anxiety, depression, psychosis, attention difficulties, mood disorders, and suicidal distress can affect school behavior.

Schools need prevention, counseling, crisis assessment, referral, and emergency procedures.

Police may be necessary when there is an immediate safety threat, but arrest is rarely a treatment plan.

Disciplinary systems should not punish students for symptoms while failing to provide legally required or reasonably available support.

Substance Use

Student possession, use, intoxication, and distribution raise safety, health, and legal concerns.

Responses may include medical care, family notification, assessment, education, treatment, discipline, and law enforcement according to severity and law.

Zero-tolerance removal can interrupt school without addressing substance-use disorder.

Trafficking, coercion, overdose risk, and repeated distribution require a different response from experimental possession.

School Climate

School climate includes safety, relationships, belonging, fairness, expectations, teaching quality, physical conditions, and institutional trust.

A positive climate is associated with engagement, achievement, attendance, and willingness to seek help.

A highly punitive climate can discourage students from reporting threats or victimization.

Fairness and safety reinforce one another when rules are clear, adults are consistent, and students believe concerns will be taken seriously.

School Connectedness

Connectedness is the sense that adults and peers care about the student and that the student belongs in the school community.

Suspension, humiliation, discrimination, academic failure, and repeated conflict can weaken connectedness.

Strong relationships can reduce misconduct and support reentry after discipline.

Connectedness should not be confused with permissiveness; students can experience firm expectations as fair when adults are trustworthy and supportive.

Loss of Instruction

Every exclusion removes time from teaching, assignments, peer collaboration, and support.

Federal education guidance reported that more than 2.5 million students lost over eleven million school days to exclusionary discipline in 2017–18.

Missed work can create frustration and increase the likelihood of further discipline.

Schools should provide assignments, tutoring, credit continuity, disability services, and structured return even when exclusion is justified.

Academic Failure

Discipline and academic difficulty influence one another.

Students struggling with reading, mathematics, language, attention, or unmet disability needs may avoid work or become disruptive.

Removal then reduces opportunity to master the material.

Effective intervention addresses instruction and behavior together rather than interpreting every academic escape behavior as defiance.

Grade Retention

Exclusion and chronic absence can increase the likelihood that a student lacks credits or is retained in grade.

Older students placed with younger peers may experience stigma and disengagement.

Retention can help some students when paired with strong intervention but is not a substitute for targeted teaching.

Repeated failure can contribute indirectly to dropout and justice-system exposure.

Dropout and Disenrollment

Students who leave school without a diploma face reduced employment opportunities and greater exposure to poverty and criminal-legal contact.

Dropout has many causes, including academic failure, work, pregnancy, caregiving, disability, homelessness, safety, and discipline.

Suspension is associated with later dropout, but association does not mean one suspension alone caused the outcome.

Reengagement, alternative pathways, credit recovery, and adult education can interrupt the sequence.

Effects on Families

Suspension and arrest require families to arrange transportation, childcare, legal assistance, court attendance, and missed work.

Families may struggle to understand codes, disability rights, appeals, and juvenile-court procedures.

Repeated conflict can damage trust between families and schools.

Accessible notice, interpretation, conferences, advocates, and timely records improve participation and problem solving.

Effects on Teachers

Teachers need classrooms where students and staff are safe and instruction can continue.

Insufficient support can leave teachers managing severe behavior without training, assistants, counselors, or administrative response.

Pipeline reform that simply prohibits suspension without adding capacity can increase burnout and disorder.

Effective reform protects teacher authority while providing graduated responses and specialized help.

Effects on Victims

Students harmed by bullying, assault, harassment, theft, or sexual misconduct may feel ignored when reform language focuses only upon the disciplined student.

Victims need protection, medical and mental-health care, information, academic support, and freedom from retaliation.

Restorative or informal processes should not be imposed when unsafe or unwanted.

A fair system protects due process for the accused and meaningful support for the harmed student.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits discrimination based upon race, color, or national origin in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

Public school districts and many other educational institutions fall within its coverage.

Discipline can violate Title VI when students are intentionally treated differently because of race or when other legally recognized standards are met under current law.

A disparity alone does not automatically prove a violation, but federal agencies use data, comparative evidence, policies, statements, and institutional practice in investigations.

Title IV of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title IV authorizes the Attorney General to address certain denials of equal protection in public schools based upon race, color, national origin, sex, and religion.

The Justice Department has used Title IV together with other authorities in school-discipline investigations and settlements.

Its remedies can include policy revision, training, data review, nondiscrimination procedures, and limits on police involvement in ordinary discipline.

Title IV should not be confused with Title IV federal student-aid programs.

Title IX

Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in federally funded education programs, subject to statutory and regulatory rules.

Discipline may raise Title IX issues when students are treated differently because of sex, when sexual harassment is mishandled, or when gender stereotypes affect punishment.

The governing regulatory framework has changed across administrations and litigation.

Schools must use current legal requirements while ensuring fair notice, impartial process, safety, and nondiscrimination.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination by recipients of federal financial assistance.

It applies to discipline, services, accommodations, evaluation, placement, and access to educational programs.

Schools may maintain safety and discipline students with disabilities, but they must avoid discriminatory rules and failure to provide required supports.

Federal guidance documents may change status, but statutory and regulatory obligations remain.

Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II prohibits disability discrimination by state and local public entities, including public schools and police departments.

It can require reasonable modification of policies when necessary to avoid discrimination, unless a lawful exception applies.

Disability-related communication, mobility, sensory, behavioral, and mental-health needs can affect school and police encounters.

ADA duties do not require schools to tolerate an immediate direct threat without appropriate safety action.

Individuals with Disabilities Education Act

IDEA requires a free appropriate public education for eligible children with disabilities through special education and related services.

It contains discipline procedures addressing removals, services, manifestation, interim alternative settings, and due process.

Certain weapons, drug, and serious bodily injury cases permit temporary alternative placement under statutory conditions.

IDEA seeks to reconcile school safety with the educational and procedural rights of children whose behavior may be connected to disability.

Due Process in School Discipline

The Fourteenth Amendment protects students from deprivation of protected interests without constitutionally adequate process.

In Goss v. Lopez, the Supreme Court held that students facing short public-school suspensions generally must receive notice of the charges and an opportunity to present their side.

Longer exclusions may require more formal procedures under constitutional and state law.

The exact process depends upon the sanction, emergency, jurisdiction, disability status, and statutory protections.

Equal Protection

The Equal Protection Clause prohibits states from denying equal protection of the laws.

Intentional racial discrimination in discipline, police referral, or juvenile processing can violate the Constitution.

Statistical disparities may support an inference when combined with evidence of inconsistent treatment, departures from policy, discriminatory statements, or foreseeable effects.

Constitutional intent requirements differ from every statutory and administrative standard.

First Amendment

Students retain speech and expression rights at public schools, though those rights operate within the educational environment.

Schools may regulate substantial disruption, threats, harassment, school-sponsored expression, and certain lewd or drug-promoting speech under established doctrine.

Criticism of school officials, political expression, religious expression, and recording can raise different legal questions.

Discipline should identify the applicable standard rather than labelling protected disagreement as defiance.

Fourth Amendment

Students have Fourth Amendment rights against unreasonable searches and seizures.

School officials generally may search on reasonable grounds when the scope is related to the suspected violation.

Police participation can change the legal standard depending upon the relationship and purpose.

Strip searches, invasive searches, phone searches, drug testing, and force require careful legal and factual analysis.

Miranda and Juvenile Questioning

Police must provide Miranda warnings before custodial interrogation under constitutional doctrine.

The Supreme Court has recognized that a child's age can matter when determining whether a reasonable person would feel free to leave.

School setting, officer presence, locked rooms, commands, and administrator involvement can affect custody analysis.

School discipline interviews and police interrogations should be clearly distinguished.

FERPA and Student Records

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act protects the privacy of education records and grants parents or eligible students specified access and correction rights.

FERPA contains exceptions permitting disclosure under defined circumstances, including some health or safety emergencies and law-enforcement-unit records.

A school police unit's records may be treated differently depending upon purpose and maintenance.

Privacy law should not be used to conceal aggregate discipline data or prevent lawful family access.

McKinney-Vento Protections

The McKinney-Vento Act protects educational access and stability for qualifying children and youths experiencing homelessness.

Immediate enrollment, transportation, and dispute procedures can prevent absence from becoming disciplinary exclusion.

Schools should identify housing instability before treating attendance, uniforms, or missing documents as willful noncompliance.

Discipline remains possible for misconduct, but homelessness must not be the basis for exclusion.

Federal and State Discipline Law

Federal law establishes limited national requirements, civil-rights duties, disability protections, and reporting obligations.

States define compulsory attendance, suspension, expulsion, juvenile jurisdiction, school police authority, and many procedural rules.

District codes add local categories, sanctions, appeals, and safety plans.

The pipeline therefore operates differently by jurisdiction, and national terminology should not replace review of the actual law.

Corporal Punishment

Corporal punishment uses physical force as school discipline under jurisdictions where it remains lawful.

The Supreme Court held in Ingraham v. Wright that the Eighth Amendment did not govern public-school corporal punishment in the same manner as criminal punishment, while state tort and due-process protections may apply.

States differ substantially in authorization and restriction.

Corporal punishment is related to punitive school culture but does not by itself involve criminal-justice referral.

Goss v. Lopez

Goss v. Lopez arose from suspensions of public-school students in Ohio.

The Supreme Court recognized protected interests in public education and held that short suspensions generally require notice and an opportunity to respond.

The decision did not prohibit suspension.

It established that administrative efficiency and school authority do not eliminate basic procedural fairness.

New Jersey v. T.L.O.

New Jersey v. T.L.O. established the principal Fourth Amendment framework for searches conducted by public-school officials.

The Court rejected both unrestricted school authority and the ordinary warrant requirement.

A search generally must be justified at its inception and reasonably related in scope to the circumstances.

The case does not automatically govern a search primarily conducted by police for criminal investigation.

Safford Unified School District v. Redding

In Safford Unified School District v. Redding, the Supreme Court held that a strip search of a thirteen-year-old student for suspected possession of common pain medication violated the Fourth Amendment.

The intrusiveness of the search was not justified by evidence concerning danger or concealment.

The case demonstrates that school safety authority has constitutional limits.

Age, suspected item, evidence, and degree of bodily intrusion matter.

J.D.B. v. North Carolina

In J.D.B. v. North Carolina, the Supreme Court held that a child's age can be relevant to the Miranda custody analysis when known or objectively apparent.

The case involved police questioning of a middle-school student at school.

Children may experience authority and freedom to leave differently from adults.

The decision is central to school-based interrogation but does not establish that every student interview is custodial.

Meridian, Mississippi Litigation

The Justice Department's 2012 complaint against Meridian and related defendants became a leading federal example of the school-to-prison pipeline.

The complaint alleged that school referrals, automatic police arrest, juvenile-court processing, probation, and detention operated together without adequate discretion and procedural protection.

It described extreme racial and disability disparities and detention linked to school suspension.

The allegations were case specific and required judicial process; they should not be generalized to every school-police partnership.

Wicomico County Settlement

A federal settlement involving Wicomico County Public Schools required reforms to racial discipline disparities and school-resource-officer practice.

The agreement distinguished school-code conduct from law-enforcement matters and required training, data, policy, and memoranda of understanding.

It illustrates how remedies can preserve school police while limiting involvement in minor misconduct.

Settlements provide models but remain binding principally upon the parties.

Federal School-Discipline Guidance

The Departments of Education and Justice launched the Supportive School Discipline Initiative in 2011.

They issued a major discipline guidance package in 2014 and later resources concerning school police, racial discrimination, disability, and supportive school climate.

Guidance documents have been issued, withdrawn, replaced, restored, revised, or rescinded across administrations.

Schools must distinguish binding statutes, regulations, and court decisions from nonbinding guidance that explains an agency's interpretation or enforcement approach.

Supportive School Discipline Initiative

The Supportive School Discipline Initiative was a federal collaboration addressing discipline practices that push students out of school and into the justice system.

Its resources emphasized safe, inclusive learning environments and reduced unnecessary reliance upon suspension, expulsion, and police.

The initiative helped institutionalize the phrase school-to-prison pipeline within federal policy.

Its historical significance remains even where particular documents later changed status.

Civil Rights Data Collection

The Civil Rights Data Collection gathers school- and district-level data from nearly every public school district in the United States.

It covers enrollment, discipline, referrals, arrests, restraint, seclusion, staffing, courses, and other educational opportunities.

The CRDC enables local, state, and national disparity analysis.

Data quality depends upon definitions, district reporting, validation, timing, and the unusual conditions of each collection year.

The 2021–22 CRDC

The 2021–22 CRDC was released in January 2025.

It reported approximately 220,000 K–12 students referred to law enforcement and found that about seventeen percent of those referrals resulted in school-related arrests.

It also reported approximately 2.3 million students receiving in-school suspension and 2.4 million receiving out-of-school suspension.

The data provide the latest complete national snapshot available for this fact sheet but do not capture every local citation, informal removal, or justice consequence.

Pandemic-Era Data Caution

The 2021–22 school year followed extensive COVID-19 disruption and included varied patterns of remote, hybrid, and in-person instruction.

Discipline opportunities, enrollment, attendance, staffing, and reporting differed from pre-pandemic years.

Comparisons with 2017–18 or 2020–21 require caution.

The persistence of disparity remains important even where total counts changed.

Data Quality and Underreporting

Districts may interpret definitions differently or omit incidents.

Police data and school data may not match because agencies count events, students, charges, or locations differently.

Informal removals and noncustodial citations can be difficult to identify.

A strong local audit compares education, police, court, disability, attendance, and complaint records while protecting privacy.

Disparity versus Discrimination

A disparity is a measurable difference in rate, share, severity, or outcome.

Discrimination is unlawful differential treatment or another prohibited practice under the governing legal standard.

A disparity may result from several mechanisms and does not automatically establish liability.

Ignoring a persistent disparity without examining comparable conduct, discretion, and institutional conditions can allow unlawful or ineffective practices to continue.

Causation

Students who are suspended often differ from nonsuspended students in prior behavior, achievement, trauma, family conditions, and school context.

Research designs attempt to account for those differences, but no observational study removes all uncertainty.

Evidence nevertheless consistently links exclusion with lost instruction, later discipline, disengagement, and elevated justice contact.

The best conclusion is that exclusion can contribute to risk and is also a marker of preexisting risk.

Texas Statewide Study

A major Texas study followed millions of public-school students and found that suspension and expulsion were common and strongly associated with grade repetition, dropout, and juvenile-justice involvement.

The study also documented substantial variation among schools serving similar students.

Variation suggested that policy and institutional practice mattered in addition to student characteristics.

Association should be interpreted carefully, but the study became foundational to pipeline research.

Effects of Suspension

Suspension can provide immediate separation, communicate seriousness, and create time for investigation or planning.

It also removes instruction, reduces belonging, and can expose students to unsupervised environments.

Federal education guidance summarized research finding that suspension does not reliably reduce future disruptive behavior.

The effect depends upon offense, duration, reentry, services, student characteristics, and school practice.

Effects of School Arrest

Arrest can create fingerprints, records, court dates, legal expense, detention risk, probation, and stigma.

It may also protect victims and establish accountability for serious criminal conduct.

An arrest is not a conviction, but the experience can disrupt school and family life immediately.

Alternatives should be considered for lower-level conduct when public safety and law permit.

Labelling and Institutional Expectations

Students identified as troublemakers may receive increased surveillance and reduced benefit of the doubt.

A prior disciplinary record can influence later decisions even when the new incident is ambiguous.

Labelling theory does not mean that misconduct is imaginary.

It asks whether institutional reactions amplify deviance, exclusion, and identity around rule breaking.

Relationship to Mass Incarceration

The school-to-prison pipeline is one mechanism within the wider history of mass incarceration.

It concerns early institutional exposure and educational exclusion rather than the complete adult prison population.

Most disciplined students do not become prisoners, and most adult incarceration cannot be attributed to school discipline alone.

The relationship is cumulative: early records, dropout, court supervision, neighborhood enforcement, and later sentencing can interact.

Relationship to the War on Drugs

Drug-free school zones, zero-tolerance rules, police presence, searches, testing, and mandatory referral grew within the wider War on Drugs.

School drug policy addressed genuine concerns about intoxication, trafficking, addiction, and violence.

Punitive approaches sometimes treated low-level possession and serious distribution without adequate distinction.

Public-health and educational responses can reduce harm while preserving enforcement against coercive or dangerous trafficking.

School Surveillance

Schools use cameras, metal detectors, identification systems, anonymous reporting, device monitoring, social-media review, and access controls.

These tools can deter weapons, document incidents, and support emergency response.

They can also normalize surveillance, generate false positives, and disproportionately scrutinize selected students.

Governance should address necessity, data retention, vendor access, accuracy, appeal, and mission creep.

Metal Detectors

Metal detectors can reduce the likelihood that weapons enter a building and support visible safety procedures.

They require staffing, secondary screening, maintenance, disability accommodation, and protocols for ordinary metal objects.

Use is concentrated in many urban schools serving students of color.

The effect on safety, climate, delay, and dignity should be evaluated locally.

Drug-Detection Dogs

Schools and police may use trained dogs to detect controlled substances under constitutional and state-law rules.

A canine sniff of property raises different legal questions from physical searching of a student.

False alerts, handler influence, residual odor, and broad sweeps can produce contested searches.

Drug-dog operations can communicate deterrence while also creating a prison-like climate.

Social-Media Monitoring

Schools monitor public social-media posts for threats, bullying, weapons, self-harm, and disruption.

Off-campus speech can be regulated only within constitutional limits applicable to public schools.

Automated monitoring can misread humor, lyrics, cultural language, and context.

Threat review should use trained human assessment and avoid treating unpopular expression as criminal intent.

School Hardening

School hardening includes locked entries, barriers, cameras, police, detectors, emergency drills, and tactical design.

Physical security may reduce specific vulnerabilities.

Excessive hardening can increase fear, reduce openness, and make schools resemble correctional institutions.

Security should be proportional to assessed risk and combined with relationships, reporting, mental-health support, and instructional quality.

Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports

Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports is a tiered framework for teaching expectations, reinforcing appropriate behavior, using data, and providing increasing levels of support.

It is not a reward-only system and does not prohibit consequences.

Implementation quality matters: vague slogans without coaching, fidelity, and targeted intervention produce limited change.

PBIS can reduce exclusion when integrated with culturally responsive instruction and disability services.

Multi-Tiered Systems of Support

A multi-tiered system organizes universal instruction, targeted intervention, and intensive individualized support.

Academic, behavioral, attendance, mental-health, and family needs can be coordinated.

The framework helps schools intervene before conduct becomes a crisis.

It requires sufficient staffing, data, time, and referral pathways rather than relying upon teachers to provide every service.

Restorative Practices

Restorative practices build relationships, address harm, require accountability, and reintegrate students.

Tools include circles, conferences, mediation, reflection, restitution, and community agreements.

They should not be reduced to an unstructured conversation or used to pressure victims.

Effective implementation requires training, preparation, voluntariness where appropriate, follow-through, and consequences for repeated serious harm.

Trauma-Informed Schools

Trauma-informed schools recognize how stress can affect behavior, learning, and relationships.

They emphasize predictability, regulation, connection, safety, and referral.

The approach should not diagnose students casually or lower expectations permanently.

Accountability remains necessary, but responses avoid unnecessary escalation and humiliation.

Social and Emotional Learning

Social and emotional learning teaches self-awareness, self-management, relationship skills, decision making, and social awareness.

Programs can support classroom behavior and conflict resolution.

Quality varies, and political controversy has developed over curriculum, values, privacy, and parental authority.

Schools should use transparent, age-appropriate, evidence-informed instruction connected to academic and behavioral goals.

Counselors, Psychologists, and Social Workers

Mental-health and student-support professionals assess needs, provide counseling, coordinate services, and help prevent crisis.

High caseloads limit availability and can cause schools to rely upon administrators or police.

Support staffing does not eliminate the need for security in dangerous incidents.

Resource allocation should be based upon student need, school size, community services, and actual safety data.

School Nurses

School nurses respond to injury, medication, chronic illness, overdose, withdrawal, disability, and mental-health emergencies.

Medical symptoms can be mistaken for misconduct.

Nurses also assist with care plans and family referrals.

Limited nursing coverage can increase emergency-service and police involvement in situations that are principally medical.

De-Escalation

De-escalation uses calm communication, time, distance, reduced stimulation, choices, and coordinated support to lower immediate conflict.

It is not appropriate when delay creates imminent danger.

Staff need practice and clear authority to summon assistance.

De-escalation protects students and employees while reducing force, restraint, removal, and arrest.

Reentry after Suspension

A reentry conference reconnects the student with school after exclusion.

It can review assignments, safety, supports, relationships, expectations, and repair of harm.

The process should not become an additional trial or humiliation.

Successful reentry reduces the likelihood that suspension becomes the beginning of prolonged disengagement.

Graduated Discipline

Graduated discipline uses responses matched to seriousness, repetition, intent, age, harm, and prior intervention.

Possible steps include redirection, conference, skill building, restitution, detention, behavior support, suspension, alternative placement, and law-enforcement referral.

Serious dangerous conduct may justify immediate high-level action.

Graduation should not become endless minor punishment without addressing underlying causes.

School-Based Diversion

Diversion routes eligible student conduct away from formal arrest or court processing.

Programs may use warning, mediation, assessment, services, restorative agreements, or community panels.

Eligibility should be clear and equitable.

Diversion reduces formal records but can widen control if students who would otherwise receive no sanction are placed in intensive programs.

Philadelphia Police School Diversion

Philadelphia developed a prearrest diversion program for qualifying school incidents.

Police and school partners redirected eligible youths toward services rather than formal arrest.

Research associated the program with reduced school-based arrest and lower recidivism among participants.

Local results depend upon eligibility, service quality, data sharing, police discretion, and sustained institutional support.

School–Police Memoranda of Understanding

A memorandum of understanding can define officer authority, school responsibility, information sharing, training, reporting, complaints, and emergency response.

Strong agreements state that routine school discipline remains with educators.

They identify offenses or threats requiring immediate police involvement and permit lawful discretion in less serious cases.

An agreement is effective only if staff understand and follow it.

Discipline Data Audits

Schools should review discipline by race, sex, disability, grade, school, teacher referral, offense, sanction, and repeat involvement.

Rates and risk ratios reveal different aspects of disparity.

Small numbers require caution, and privacy must be protected.

Data should lead to file review, observation, policy change, training, and evaluation rather than accusation based upon numbers alone.

Student and Family Voice

Students and families can identify unwritten practices, humiliation, unsafe areas, language barriers, and barriers to reentry.

Listening sessions and surveys provide evidence not visible in administrative data.

Participation should include victims, disciplined students, students with disabilities, staff, and families with varied viewpoints.

Consultation must be connected to decision making and feedback.

Implementation Capacity

Discipline reform requires more than changing the code of conduct.

Schools need trained administrators, teacher support, counselors, special-education staff, alternative spaces, transportation, family engagement, and timely services.

Removing suspension authority without building capacity can leave staff and students exposed to repeated disruption or danger.

Implementation plans should assign responsibility, funding, timelines, data measures, and corrective action.

Teacher Training and Coaching

Teachers make many initial decisions about whether behavior is managed in class or referred to administration.

Training should address classroom management, adolescent development, disability, cultural interpretation, de-escalation, documentation, and implicit bias.

One-time workshops rarely change practice without coaching, observation, and feedback.

Teachers also need clear procedures for obtaining immediate help during serious incidents.

Administrator Discretion

Administrators interpret school codes, investigate facts, choose sanctions, and decide whether police are contacted.

Discretion permits proportional and individualized judgment.

It can also produce inconsistency and bias when standards are vague or decisions are unreviewed.

Written reasons, comparative data, appeal rights, and supervisory review improve accountability.

Accountability for Staff Conduct

Pipeline prevention requires review of adult conduct as well as student conduct.

Staff may escalate situations through humiliation, unlawful force, discriminatory language, retaliation, failure to implement services, or inaccurate reporting.

Employees also deserve protection from assault, threats, false allegations, and unsafe working conditions.

Fair investigations should examine all participants and preserve due process.

Complaint and Appeal Systems

Students and families need accessible ways to challenge discipline, discrimination, disability-service failure, police conduct, and retaliation.

Procedures should identify deadlines, decision makers, evidence rights, interpretation, and appeal.

A complaint system cannot work if families reasonably fear retaliation or receive no explanation.

Aggregate complaint trends can reveal policy problems even when individual allegations are unsubstantiated.

Independent Oversight

Oversight may be provided by school boards, inspectors general, ombuds offices, civil-rights agencies, courts, police review systems, or community bodies.

Independence can improve credibility when the school or police department investigates itself.

Oversight must still use reliable evidence and respect privacy and employee rights.

Recommendations should be tracked through implementation rather than ending with publication.

Evaluating School Safety

Safety should be measured through victimization, weapons, fights, threats, bullying, climate surveys, emergency response, injuries, and attendance.

Arrest and suspension counts do not by themselves establish safety.

A decline in referrals may indicate improved climate, changed reporting, under-enforcement, or reclassification.

Evaluation should pair quantitative trends with incident review and feedback from students and staff.

Under-Discipline and Institutional Neglect

A school can harm students by punishing too much or by failing to respond to serious misconduct.

Victims may avoid school when bullying, assault, harassment, or weapons are minimized.

Reform that discourages documentation or suppresses legitimate referrals can conceal danger.

The goal is accurate, proportionate, supportive discipline rather than automatic leniency.

Over-Discipline

Over-discipline occurs when sanctions are more severe, frequent, or exclusionary than necessary for safety, accountability, and learning.

It may arise from rigid codes, staff stress, resource scarcity, bias, political pressure, or poor alternatives.

Repeated removal can worsen the conditions that produced the behavior.

Review should examine both the written policy and how it operates in practice.

Serious Violence

Homicide, sexual violence, armed assault, severe beating, credible planned attack, and other serious offenses require immediate safety and investigative responses.

Pipeline analysis does not require schools to treat such conduct as a minor classroom matter.

Students accused of grave misconduct retain due-process and disability rights.

Long-term prevention also requires violence intervention, threat assessment, family support, mental-health care, and secure educational reentry where appropriate.

Weapons and Firearms

Firearms at school create exceptional risk and generally require law-enforcement response.

Schools should secure the weapon, protect students, preserve evidence, and avoid unsafe staff intervention beyond training.

Investigation should distinguish intentional possession, coercion, credible threat, accidental transfer, and false report.

Prevention includes safe storage, reporting, family education, threat assessment, and enforcement against illegal access.

Sexual Violence

Sexual assault and coercion require victim-centered safety, medical options, preservation of evidence, legally required reporting, and fair investigation.

Schools must coordinate Title IX and criminal processes without assuming one automatically controls the other.

Informal resolution may be restricted or inappropriate depending upon law and circumstances.

Pipeline reform should never be used to pressure survivors into accepting unsafe alternatives.

Dating and Family Violence

Students may experience dating violence, stalking, coercive control, or family violence that appears at school.

Discipline focused only upon a visible fight may miss self-defense, retaliation, or continuing danger.

Schools need protective plans, confidential support, appropriate family engagement, and coordination with specialized services.

Criminal referral may be necessary, but it should not replace educational and victim services.

Debate over Removing Police from Schools

Some advocates seek to remove police from routine school settings and redirect funds toward counselors, social workers, nurses, and community programs.

Supporters argue that police presence increases arrest, fear, and racial disparity.

Opponents argue that trained officers improve emergency response, deter violence, and build relationships.

Local decisions should consider incident patterns, response times, student and staff views, officer role, alternative capacity, and measurable outcomes.

Reforming School Police

Reform can retain officers while narrowing duties and strengthening training, supervision, data, complaints, and community accountability.

Officers can focus upon serious crime, threats, emergency response, and safety planning rather than ordinary discipline.

Assignment quality matters more than the title alone.

A school-police program should be evaluated against defined safety and civil-rights objectives.

Funding Priorities

District budgets reflect choices among police, security, counselors, nurses, psychologists, social workers, teachers, and facilities.

These functions are not always substitutes because safety and student support require different expertise.

Federal and state grants can shape local decisions by making one category easier to fund.

Budget review should identify actual costs, vacancies, contracts, outcomes, and opportunity costs.

Criticism of Pipeline Reform

Critics argue that pipeline rhetoric can minimize student violence, weaken teacher authority, discourage reporting, and shift responsibility away from students and families.

They contend that discipline disparities may partly reflect differences in recorded conduct and school conditions.

These concerns are strongest where reform is imposed without staffing, training, or transparent safety data.

A credible reform program answers these objections with clear boundaries, victim protection, evidence, and corrective action.

Criticism of the Pipeline Concept

Some scholars argue that the pipeline metaphor is too linear and treats schools as direct feeders into prison.

Others prefer concepts such as school pushout, criminalization, carceral schooling, or multiple pathways.

The metaphor can obscure protective schools, student resilience, community variation, and the role of serious offending.

Its value lies in exposing institutional connection, provided it is not treated as a complete causal theory.

Carceral Schooling

Carceral schooling describes educational environments organized around surveillance, containment, obedience, police authority, and punishment.

The concept examines architecture, metal detectors, searches, uniforms, cameras, restraint, isolation, and disciplinary language.

Security measures do not automatically make a school carceral.

The term is most useful when institutional control overwhelms teaching, relationship, and developmental purpose.

Criminalization versus Accountability

Accountability requires recognition of harm, responsibility, protection, repair, and changed conduct.

Criminalization places the matter within police and court institutions.

The two overlap but are not identical.

Schools can impose meaningful educational consequences without arrest, while serious crime may require criminal accountability in addition to school action.

Limits of Restorative Justice

Restorative practices require trained facilitation, truthful participation, safety, and follow-through.

They can fail when used as a superficial substitute for consequences or when participants are coerced.

They may be unsuitable in cases of severe power imbalance, ongoing danger, or unwilling victims.

Programs should define eligibility, confidentiality, enforcement, and interaction with legal duties.

Limits of PBIS and Tiered Supports

Tiered supports cannot compensate for unsafe facilities, severe staffing shortages, weak instruction, or unavailable treatment.

Reward systems may feel manipulative or culturally narrow if implemented without student input.

Data systems can reproduce bias if referrals remain subjective.

The framework requires continuous review rather than assumption that adoption alone eliminates disparity.

Community Schools

Community schools coordinate education with health, mental-health, family, food, housing, and after-school services.

They address conditions contributing to absence, crisis, and misconduct.

A coordinator and reliable partnerships are necessary; a label without services has little effect.

Community schools can interrupt indirect pipeline pathways by keeping students connected and families supported.

After-School and Summer Programs

Structured programs provide supervision, tutoring, recreation, mentoring, employment, and safe peer relationships.

They can reduce unsupervised time and support students returning from discipline or court involvement.

Access depends upon transportation, cost, staffing, and neighborhood availability.

Programs should not stigmatize participants as presumed offenders.

Mentoring

Mentoring connects students with reliable adults who provide guidance, advocacy, and encouragement.

Relationship quality and consistency matter more than brief contact.

Mentors should receive screening, training, supervision, and boundaries.

Mentoring supplements rather than replaces family, instruction, disability services, and professional treatment.

Family Engagement

Families hold knowledge about disability, trauma, culture, prior incidents, medication, and effective calming strategies.

Schools should communicate before problems become crises.

Engagement must be accessible to families working irregular hours, speaking other languages, or distrustful because of prior treatment.

Family participation does not eliminate the school's independent duty to protect students and staff.

Student Rights and Responsibilities Education

Students should understand the code of conduct, reporting systems, appeal rights, police role, search rules, and consequences.

Rights education should be paired with responsibilities toward peers, staff, property, and safety.

Clear notice improves compliance and procedural legitimacy.

Rules should be written in age-appropriate and accessible language.

Principles of Sound Discipline Policy

A sound policy defines conduct clearly, distinguishes school violations from crimes, and uses proportionate graduated responses.

It protects victims, provides emergency authority, and preserves due process.

It incorporates disability rights, family notice, data review, reentry, and alternatives.

The code should be reviewed against actual outcomes rather than judged only by its wording.

Evaluation Framework

Districts should measure suspensions, instructional days lost, referrals, arrests, victimization, repeat incidents, attendance, achievement, climate, complaints, restraint, seclusion, and staff injury.

Results should be disaggregated by race, sex, disability, grade, and school.

Reform should be compared with baseline conditions and similar schools where possible.

Unexpected harm should trigger adjustment rather than ideological defense.

Status through August 2026

The school-to-prison pipeline remained an active civil-rights, juvenile-justice, disability, and school-safety concern.

The latest complete national CRDC showed continuing racial and disability overrepresentation in discipline, referral, arrest, restraint, and seclusion.

Federal guidance and enforcement emphasis continued to change across presidential administrations, while statutes, regulations, court decisions, and state law remained controlling.

Local practice ranged from police removal and restorative reform to renewed security, zero-tolerance enforcement, and expanded threat response.

Historical Significance

The pipeline concept connected school discipline to the wider histories of juvenile courts, racial inequality, disability exclusion, the War on Drugs, and mass incarceration.

It changed how researchers and officials interpreted suspension and police referral.

A disciplinary event could no longer be understood only as a temporary classroom-management decision.

The concept made educational access, civil rights, procedural fairness, and long-term justice exposure part of the same policy discussion.

Continuing Significance

Schools must protect students and staff while preserving education as their central purpose.

The strongest response neither criminalizes ordinary childhood behavior nor ignores serious harm.

It uses police for genuine public-safety functions, educators for discipline, clinicians for health needs, and courts when legal intervention is necessary.

The pipeline remains a useful framework when it guides precise institutional reform rather than replacing evidence with a slogan.

Misinformation and Common Errors

The school-to-prison pipeline is not a literal pipe, one program, or a single federal policy.

The concept does not mean that every suspended student will be arrested or incarcerated.

It does not mean that every school arrest, expulsion, or police referral is unjustified.

Schools have legitimate duties to protect students and staff from weapons, assault, sexual violence, threats, and severe disruption.

Ordinary school-rule violations and criminal offenses are not interchangeable categories.

A referral to law enforcement does not always result in arrest, and an arrest does not establish guilt.

The 2021–22 CRDC reported about 220,000 students referred to law enforcement, not 220,000 school arrests.

Approximately 17 percent of those reported referrals resulted in school-related arrests.

CRDC discipline counts identify students receiving an action, not necessarily the total number of disciplinary events.

A statistical disparity alone does not automatically prove unlawful discrimination.

The absence of intentional bias by every individual does not rule out institutional discrimination or unequal effects.

Poverty does not fully explain national racial discipline disparities.

Black boys are highly overrepresented, but Black girls also experience substantial and distinct discipline disparities.

Students with disabilities are not exempt from discipline, but schools must comply with IDEA, Section 504, and the ADA.

A manifestation determination does not declare that misconduct never occurred.

Restraint and seclusion are not ordinary synonyms for detention, timeout, or classroom removal.

School resource officers are sworn police officers; nonsworn security staff ordinarily possess different authority.

Police presence may be associated with higher arrest rates without proving that police presence alone caused every arrest.

Zero tolerance does not always mean that administrators possess no discretion; governing law and policy must be reviewed.

The federal Gun-Free Schools Act concerns firearms and permits case-by-case modification by the chief administering officer.

Exclusionary discipline can be lawful and necessary in particular cases.

Keeping every student in the same classroom regardless of immediate danger is not required by pipeline reform.

Restorative justice is not the absence of accountability and should not be forced upon victims.

PBIS is not merely giving prizes for good behavior.

Trauma-informed practice explains behavior but does not excuse harm.

Threat assessment is not a method for predicting violence with certainty.

An unconvicted student in juvenile or adult court has not been found guilty on the current charge.

Juvenile court is not always identical to adult criminal court, but it can impose serious restrictions and confinement.

School discipline data do not fully capture informal removals, citations, or every police contact.

Annual arrest or referral totals can change because definitions and reporting practices change.

The pipeline is connected to mass incarceration but does not explain the entire adult prison population.

Removing police from schools does not automatically create counseling, mental-health, or safety capacity.

Retaining police does not require assigning officers to enforce dress codes, defiance rules, or ordinary classroom discipline.

A decline in suspensions does not by itself prove that schools became safer or fairer.

A rise in reported incidents may reflect increased misconduct, improved reporting, changed definitions, or several factors.

Victims of student misconduct and disciplined students both possess rights and legitimate needs.

Federal guidance documents are not identical to statutes, regulations, or binding court decisions.

Guidance status can change across administrations; schools should verify current law.

The 2021–22 CRDC was released in January 2025 and remains the latest complete national collection used in this fact sheet.

The term is most accurate when linked to specific institutional mechanisms rather than used as a general accusation.

Summary

The school-to-prison pipeline is a framework describing how exclusionary discipline, school police, court referral, disability-service failures, racial bias, poverty, and educational disengagement can connect schools to juvenile and adult criminal-legal systems. It is not one program or an automatic path followed by every disciplined student.

The latest complete national Civil Rights Data Collection reported about 220,000 K–12 students referred to law enforcement in 2021–22, with approximately seventeen percent of referrals resulting in school-related arrest. Black students and students with disabilities were substantially overrepresented in referrals and arrests, and Black boys, Black girls, and students with disabilities were overrepresented in suspension and expulsion.

Effective reform does not require schools to ignore weapons, violence, harassment, or genuine threats. It requires clear distinctions between educational misconduct and crime, proportionate discipline, disability compliance, due process, victim protection, well-defined police roles, behavioral and mental-health capacity, strong reentry, reliable data, and correction of unequal treatment. The central objective is to keep students safely connected to education while reserving criminal intervention for circumstances in which it is legally and practically necessary.
