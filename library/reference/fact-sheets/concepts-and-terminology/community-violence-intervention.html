Community Violence Intervention

A community-centered public-safety approach that works with people and places at elevated risk of serious interpersonal violence to interrupt imminent conflicts, prevent retaliation, support survivors, and reduce future harm.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Public-safety framework, public-health intervention strategy, violence-prevention practice, and community-service system.
Common abbreviation
CVI, meaning community violence intervention. Federal programs sometimes use CVIPI for Community Based Violence Intervention and Prevention Initiative.
Primary purpose
To prevent shootings, homicides, retaliatory assaults, and other serious interpersonal violence by intervening with people, groups, conflicts, and locations at elevated near-term risk.
Principal violence addressed
Community violence occurring mainly in public places among people who may or may not know one another, including shootings, group-related conflicts, retaliatory violence, and serious assaults.
Principal participants
Community-based organizations, outreach workers, violence interrupters, credible messengers, case managers, victim-service providers, hospitals, public-health agencies, local governments, researchers, and, in some models, law-enforcement and criminal-justice agencies.
Principal intervention models
Street outreach and violence interruption, hospital-based violence intervention, intensive case management, credible-messenger mentoring, group violence intervention or focused deterrence, trauma recovery and victim services, and selected place-based strategies.
Core operating method
Identify a narrowly defined violence problem, engage people closest to the risk through trusted relationships, mediate conflicts, prevent retaliation, connect participants to services and opportunities, and coordinate institutions around measurable safety goals.
Targeting principle
Many CVI programs concentrate resources on a relatively small number of people, groups, disputes, and locations associated with a disproportionate share of serious violence.
Role of lived experience
Some programs employ staff whose community standing or personal experience with violence, gangs, incarceration, victimization, or recovery helps them gain access and credibility. Lived experience is neither universal nor sufficient by itself; programs also require training, supervision, ethical standards, and worker support.
Relationship to police
CVI is not one fixed position on policing. Programs may operate independently of police, receive limited referrals, coordinate around specific threats, participate in formal partnerships, or form part of a focused-deterrence strategy involving law enforcement.
Relationship to public health
CVI commonly treats violence as preventable and addresses risk, exposure, trauma, social conditions, and repeated victimization rather than relying only on arrest and punishment after violence occurs.
Evidence status
Some evaluated programs and models have produced meaningful reductions in shootings, homicides, retaliation, or reinjury. Results are mixed across sites, however, and the broader evidence base remains uneven because programs, populations, measures, and implementation quality differ substantially.
Principal implementation requirements
Accurate local assessment, trusted community leadership, qualified staff, stable funding, worker safety, effective supervision, service capacity, clear partnerships, lawful data practices, and evaluation matched to the program's actual theory of change.
Principal risks
Underfunding, short grant cycles, burnout, weak supervision, loss of community trust, inappropriate information sharing, overly broad eligibility, failure to reach the people at highest risk, poor coordination, and claims of success unsupported by adequate evaluation.
Federal development
The U.S. Department of Justice launched a major Community Based Violence Intervention and Prevention Initiative in fiscal year 2022, expanding federal grants, training, technical assistance, victim services, and research.
Current status
CVI is widely used as an umbrella term for locally designed violence-reduction efforts. Program models, funding structures, police involvement, eligible participants, operating hours, and evidence quality remain highly variable.
Historical significance
CVI broadened modern public-safety policy by establishing community organizations, hospitals, outreach workers, survivors, and public-health institutions as active violence-reduction authorities rather than treating policing and prosecution as the only central response.

What Is Community Violence Intervention?

Community violence intervention is a broad approach to reducing serious interpersonal violence through locally tailored, community-centered action. It concentrates on preventing violence before it occurs, interrupting conflicts that may become lethal, reducing retaliation after a violent event, assisting survivors, and changing the conditions that keep individuals or groups involved in repeated cycles of harm.

CVI is not one standardized program. The term may describe street outreach workers mediating an active dispute, hospital staff connecting a gunshot survivor to long-term support, a credible messenger mentoring a person at high risk of shooting or being shot, a multidisciplinary partnership addressing group violence, or a place-based strategy improving conditions around locations where violence repeatedly occurs.

The defining feature is not the absence of government or law enforcement. It is the deliberate use of community knowledge, trusted relationships, targeted intervention, services, and prevention methods to reduce serious violence. Some CVI models are primarily community-led and nonpolice. Others formally combine community support with law-enforcement deterrence. The label should therefore be interpreted by examining how a specific program actually selects participants, responds to danger, shares information, delivers services, and measures results.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet addresses community violence intervention primarily in the modern United States. It focuses on programs designed to reduce shootings, homicides, retaliatory violence, serious assaults, and related harms in communities experiencing concentrated interpersonal violence.

Community violence is distinct from, though it may overlap with, intimate-partner violence, child abuse, sexual violence, elder abuse, workplace violence, terrorism, self-harm, and armed conflict. Those subjects involve different patterns of relationship, authority, victimization, law, and service provision and should not automatically be grouped under CVI.

CVI also differs from broad social policy. Employment, education, housing, environmental improvement, youth recreation, and economic security may reduce violence risk and are often important to a comprehensive strategy. A program does not become CVI merely because it benefits a neighborhood. CVI ordinarily includes an explicit violence-reduction purpose, a defined intervention population or place, a mechanism connecting activities to violence outcomes, and some method of tracking implementation and results.

What Is Community Violence?

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes community violence as violence that generally occurs outside the home between unrelated people who may or may not know one another. Examples include public assaults, group fights, homicides, and fatal or nonfatal shootings in streets, parks, schools, businesses, and other shared spaces.

Community violence can affect people far beyond the person directly injured. Family members, witnesses, neighbors, classmates, medical personnel, outreach workers, and entire neighborhoods may experience grief, fear, displacement, disrupted schooling, reduced commerce, damaged trust, and repeated exposure to trauma.

In the United States, the burden is not distributed evenly. Youth and young adults, particularly in Black and Latino communities affected by concentrated disadvantage and firearm violence, experience disproportionate exposure and loss. CVI programs must describe these disparities accurately without treating race or residence as a substitute for individualized evidence of risk.

Historical Context

Communities have long used informal mediation, neighborhood leadership, clergy, mutual aid, youth work, victim assistance, and trusted intermediaries to prevent interpersonal violence. Modern CVI developed by formalizing and funding some of these practices while combining them with public-health methods, criminal-justice research, hospital services, and local data analysis.

Hospital-Based Intervention

A major early model emerged in Oakland, California, where Youth ALIVE! launched Caught in the Crossfire at Highland Hospital in 1994. The program sought to reach violently injured young people during hospitalization, connect them with support, and reduce the likelihood of reinjury or retaliation. Hospital-based violence intervention later expanded into a national field linking trauma care, victim services, safety planning, case management, and community organizations.

Boston Operation Ceasefire

The Boston Gun Project began in the mid-1990s as a research-practitioner partnership examining youth homicide. Its Operation Ceasefire strategy, implemented in 1996, combined a focused law-enforcement response to serious group violence with direct communication, community moral voices, and offers of assistance. This approach became a foundation for later group violence intervention and focused-deterrence programs.

Chicago CeaseFire and Violence Interruption

Chicago CeaseFire, later associated with the Cure Violence model, formed in 1999 and began operating at scale around 2000. It applied a public-health framework to shootings and killings, using violence interrupters and outreach workers to identify conflicts, mediate disputes, prevent retaliation, change behavior, and connect people at elevated risk with services. The model made the term violence interrupter widely known.

Expansion of the CVI Umbrella

During the 2000s and 2010s, cities adapted street outreach, focused deterrence, hospital-based intervention, youth mentoring, victim services, and place-based prevention in different combinations. The field increasingly used the umbrella term community violence intervention to distinguish these targeted approaches from both broad prevention programs and conventional enforcement-only strategies.

Federal Investment Beginning in 2022

In fiscal year 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice launched the Community Based Violence Intervention and Prevention Initiative. The initiative supported community organizations, local governments, hospitals, victim-service providers, research, training, and technical assistance. Federal investment helped expand the field while also increasing expectations for grant compliance, evaluation, partnership agreements, and organizational capacity.

Core Principles

Community Leadership

CVI assumes that residents and community organizations possess information, relationships, and practical knowledge that outside institutions may lack. Community participation should influence problem definition, program design, staffing, implementation, oversight, and interpretation of results rather than serving only as public endorsement after decisions have been made.

Focused Attention

Serious violence is often concentrated among a limited number of people, groups, disputes, and places. Many CVI programs therefore direct intensive resources toward the situations most closely connected to near-term risk rather than distributing the same intervention across an entire population.

Trusted Relationships

Outreach workers and credible messengers seek voluntary engagement through trust, consistency, cultural competence, and demonstrated concern. Their influence generally depends less on formal legal authority than on access, reliability, reputation, and the ability to help participants solve immediate problems.

Immediate Safety and Long-Term Change

Effective programs distinguish urgent conflict interruption from long-term stabilization. Preventing a shooting tonight may require mediation, safe relocation, transportation, or separating opposing groups. Reducing future risk may require employment, education, housing, legal assistance, trauma treatment, substance-use care, family support, and sustained case management.

Trauma-Informed Practice

Participants and staff may have experienced repeated violence, grief, incarceration, injury, and institutional mistrust. Trauma-informed practice seeks to avoid unnecessary re-traumatization, provide emotional and practical support, recognize survival responses, and build safety without assuming that trauma alone explains every act of violence.

Data-Informed Adaptation

Programs may use law-enforcement records, hospital data, public-health information, community reports, outreach knowledge, and geographic analysis to understand local patterns. Data should guide action, but programs must examine bias, missing incidents, privacy, consent, and the risk that administrative records reproduce unequal enforcement patterns.

Multi-Sector Responsibility

CVI generally requires coordination among organizations with different authority and expertise. Community groups may lead outreach; hospitals may identify injured participants; public-health agencies may analyze trends; schools and employers may provide opportunity; local government may fund and coordinate; researchers may evaluate; and law enforcement may address imminent threats or serious criminal conduct where the model includes that role.

Major Models and Components

Street Outreach

Street outreach workers build continuing relationships with people and groups at elevated risk of serious violence. They may conduct nighttime outreach, attend community events, visit homes, respond after shootings, help participants navigate services, and maintain contact through periods of crisis. Outreach is broader than mediation because it includes sustained engagement before and after an immediate conflict.

Violence Interruption

Violence interrupters identify disputes that may become violent and attempt to prevent escalation. They may negotiate temporary truces, separate participants, challenge retaliatory plans, arrange safe movement, communicate through respected intermediaries, or respond rapidly after a shooting to discourage revenge. Interruption relies on timely information and access; it does not provide lawful authority to detain, search, threaten, or use force.

Hospital-Based Violence Intervention Programs

Hospital-based violence intervention programs, commonly called HVIPs, identify patients injured by interpersonal violence and offer support beginning during hospitalization or emergency treatment. Programs may provide bedside engagement, risk and needs assessment, trauma-informed care, safety planning, victim compensation assistance, transportation, legal referrals, employment and education support, mental-health care, and long-term case management after discharge.

Some HVIPs are staffed by the hospital, some by community partners with hospital access, and others by mixed teams. Participation ordinarily requires patient consent. The hospital setting may create a meaningful opportunity for engagement, but the existence of a hospital contact does not guarantee that long-term housing, employment, behavioral-health, or safety needs can be met.

Credible-Messenger Mentoring and Intensive Case Management

Credible messengers use relevant life experience, local standing, or demonstrated recovery to mentor participants and support behavior change. Intensive case managers help participants create safety and stability plans, obtain identification, meet court or probation obligations, access education and work, resolve transportation barriers, and maintain contact with service systems.

Credibility is relational and context-specific. A criminal record does not automatically create trust, and programs should not assume that every formerly incarcerated person is suited for intervention work. Hiring should consider judgment, emotional stability, boundaries, safety, communication, community relationships, and the nature of any prior conduct.

Group Violence Intervention and Focused Deterrence

Group violence intervention identifies groups associated with serious violence and communicates a combined message: violence must stop, the community rejects the harm, services and assistance are available, and continued serious violence may trigger focused enforcement. Meetings, custom notifications, or direct contacts may involve community members, service providers, probation or parole personnel, prosecutors, and police.

This model is commonly included within the CVI field, but it differs from nonpolice street outreach because deterrence and criminal-justice consequences are explicit components. Local programs should not blur these differences when seeking participant trust or public consent.

Victim Services and Trauma Recovery

Violence intervention may include grief support, counseling, emergency relocation, protection planning, funeral assistance, compensation applications, legal advocacy, and help returning to school or work. These services address the direct consequences of violence and may also reduce the conditions that contribute to retaliation or repeated victimization.

Place-Based and Environmental Strategies

Some CVI frameworks include improvements to locations where violence repeatedly occurs. Activities may include repairing abandoned property, improving lighting, maintaining vacant lots, redesigning public space, creating safe routes, supporting local businesses, or organizing resident stewardship. These interventions address environmental conditions and opportunities for harm rather than focusing only on individual behavior.

Youth Development and Opportunity

Education, mentoring, summer employment, recreation, life-skills development, and connection to caring adults can form part of a comprehensive violence strategy. Broad youth programs are usually classified as prevention rather than targeted intervention unless they intentionally serve people or places linked to an identified violence problem.

How Community Violence Intervention Works

1. Community Violence Assessment

Program planners first define the violence problem. They examine who is being harmed, where incidents occur, what conflicts or group dynamics are involved, when risk increases, what services already exist, and which institutions or residents possess relevant knowledge. A valid assessment combines quantitative records with community experience rather than relying on one data system.

2. Program Selection and Theory of Change

The community selects an intervention that fits the problem. A neighborhood experiencing recurring retaliatory shootings may need street outreach and conflict mediation. A trauma center treating many repeat victims may need an HVIP. A city facing violence concentrated in identifiable groups may consider group violence intervention. The theory of change should state how program activities are expected to reduce specific forms of violence.

3. Identification and Referral

Participants may be identified through outreach knowledge, hospital admissions, probation or parole referrals, schools, victim-service agencies, community members, social networks, or violence-risk reviews. Eligibility should use defined criteria and should avoid treating race, neighborhood, age, or association alone as proof that a person is dangerous.

4. Engagement

Staff make contact, explain the program, assess immediate safety, and seek voluntary participation where the model permits. Successful engagement may require repeated attempts. People at highest risk may mistrust institutions, have unstable housing or communication, face active threats, or have previously experienced services that did not meet their needs.

5. Immediate Intervention

When violence appears imminent, workers may mediate, separate parties, arrange transportation, communicate through third parties, support temporary relocation, develop a safety plan, or request emergency assistance. Programs need clear procedures for weapons, active threats, medical emergencies, child protection, and circumstances requiring law-enforcement or clinical intervention.

6. Individualized Support

Staff and participants identify practical barriers and goals. Services may include employment, education, housing, identification documents, behavioral-health care, medical follow-up, legal support, family mediation, food assistance, transportation, and help leaving a dangerous group or relationship. A referral list is not the same as service delivery; programs must track whether the participant actually gains access.

7. Continuing Contact

Risk does not end when a single conflict is resolved. Programs may maintain contact for months or longer, monitor changing disputes, support participants after arrests or injuries, help with setbacks, and coordinate transitions between hospital, community, education, employment, and justice systems.

8. Measurement and Adjustment

Programs track activities such as contacts, mediations, referrals, service completion, participant retention, and response time. Outcome evaluation may examine shootings, homicides, reinjury, retaliation, victimization, arrests, employment, education, trauma symptoms, community trust, or safety perceptions. Measures should match the program's authority and should not claim responsibility for changes the program could not reasonably cause.

Credible Messengers and Violence Interrupters

A credible messenger is a person whose experience, relationships, knowledge, or standing enables meaningful communication with people whom conventional institutions may not reach. Credibility can arise from neighborhood roots, prior involvement in violence, recovery from injury, incarceration experience, professional service, family relationships, faith leadership, youth work, or a long record of reliability.

Violence interrupters are specialized conflict mediators. They gather information about developing disputes, make contact with involved parties, identify motives and pressures, and seek a nonviolent resolution. Outreach workers may perform some interruption duties, but not every outreach worker is an interrupter and not every credible messenger conducts active conflict mediation.

These roles can expose workers to serious danger, grief, secondary trauma, retaliation, and accusations from both community members and authorities. Programs require competitive pay, health and mental-health support, clear supervision, communication systems, field safety plans, ethical rules, confidentiality guidance, and procedures for withdrawing when a situation exceeds the worker's lawful role or capacity.

Representative Examples

Caught in the Crossfire

Caught in the Crossfire began at Highland Hospital in Oakland in 1994. It connected violently injured youth with intervention specialists and community services. The program became an influential example of using hospitalization as an entry point for recovery, safety planning, mentoring, and prevention of repeat injury.

Boston Operation Ceasefire

Boston's Operation Ceasefire combined problem analysis, focused deterrence, direct communication with group-involved individuals, service offers, community participation, and targeted enforcement. Evaluations associated the original strategy with significant reductions in youth homicide and other measures of serious violence, though later replications have varied according to design and implementation.

Chicago CeaseFire

Chicago CeaseFire used violence interrupters, outreach workers, community mobilization, and public education in selected high-violence areas. A National Institute of Justice evaluation found declines in shooting hot spots at six of seven evaluated sites, with stronger evidence linking four of those declines to the program. The study also reported reductions in retaliatory killings in several areas while warning that broader citywide declines and differences between comparison areas complicated causal attribution.

Local Adaptation

These examples should not be treated as interchangeable brands. A city may combine hospital responders with street outreach, use credible-messenger case management without adopting the full Cure Violence model, or integrate community organizations into focused deterrence. Accurate evaluation requires describing the actual local program rather than assigning results from a national model name.

Administration, Partnerships, and Funding

Lead Organizations

CVI programs may be led by nonprofit organizations, hospitals, health departments, mayoral offices, violence-prevention offices, county agencies, coalitions, or hybrid partnerships. Community-based leadership can improve trust and local knowledge, while public institutions may provide funding, data access, procurement, facilities, and coordination. Neither structure guarantees quality by itself.

Contracts and Grants

Programs commonly depend on municipal contracts, state appropriations, federal grants, hospital support, philanthropy, or braided funding. Short-term grants can create staffing instability, delayed payment, administrative burden, and abrupt service interruption. Governments should account for indirect costs, insurance, training, evaluation, worker benefits, and organizational infrastructure rather than funding only visible frontline activity.

Service Partnerships

Outreach loses effectiveness when employment, housing, treatment, transportation, and victim services are nominally available but inaccessible in practice. Partnership agreements should specify referral procedures, eligibility, priority access, responsible staff, follow-up, and what happens when a service provider lacks capacity.

Research Partnerships

Researchers may help define measures, build comparison strategies, protect data quality, document implementation, and separate program effects from broader trends. Community partners should participate in deciding what outcomes matter and how findings are interpreted. Evaluation should not expose participants to avoidable surveillance or convert service relationships into undisclosed research activity.

Relationship to Law Enforcement

CVI is often described as an alternative to enforcement, but the actual relationship varies. Some community organizations maintain strict separation from police to preserve trust and participant safety. Others coordinate emergency response, accept referrals, participate in violence reviews, or work within group violence intervention strategies that include police and prosecutors.

Programs should establish boundaries before operations begin. Staff and participants need to know what information is confidential, what must be reported by law, what may be shared during an emergency, whether workers participate in investigations, and whether program records can be subpoenaed or accessed by government partners.

Poorly defined collaboration can compromise both safety and legitimacy. If outreach workers are perceived as police informants, they may lose access to people and conflicts. If programs refuse all emergency coordination without a workable safety plan, workers and residents may face avoidable danger. The appropriate arrangement depends on local law, program design, participant consent, and the specific threat being addressed.

Evidence and Effectiveness

The evidence for CVI must be evaluated by model, site, population, implementation period, and outcome. The term includes substantially different interventions, so no single finding establishes that every CVI program works or fails.

Promising and Effective Findings

Evaluations of the original Boston Operation Ceasefire associated the intervention with significant reductions in youth homicide and other violence measures. CrimeSolutions has rated some focused-deterrence programs effective and the original Chicago Cure Violence program promising. Hospital-based programs have reported lower reinjury, improved service connection, or better recovery outcomes in some studies.

Mixed Results

Other replications have produced limited, statistically uncertain, or unfavorable results. A program may fail because it does not reach the people at highest risk, lacks staff credibility, loses funding, operates inconsistently, cannot provide promised services, or changes the original model without establishing a new theory of change. Local violence may also change because of policing, economic conditions, demographic shifts, firearms markets, social networks, seasonal patterns, or unrelated community activity.

Evaluation Challenges

Random assignment may be impractical or ethically difficult for some interventions. Violence events may be relatively rare, geographically concentrated, and influenced by network effects. Participants may move, disengage, be incarcerated, or receive services from several programs. Police and hospital data may be incomplete, and community trust or trauma recovery may change before homicide statistics do.

State of the Research

A 2025 scoping review of U.S. CVI evaluations concluded that the field is promising but methodologically underdeveloped. Studies use inconsistent definitions, measures, designs, and reporting practices, limiting comparisons across programs. The appropriate conclusion is neither that CVI lacks evidence nor that the label guarantees effectiveness. Programs require model-specific evaluation, transparent limitations, and continuing improvement.

Implementation Challenges and Risks

Reaching the Highest-Risk Population

Programs may serve people with significant needs yet fail to reach those most closely connected to imminent violence. Eligibility rules that are too broad can dilute intensive services. Rules that are too narrow can exclude survivors, family members, or emerging conflicts that require attention.

Workforce Stability

Frontline workers often perform difficult, irregular, and dangerous labor while managing grief and secondary trauma. Low wages, contractor status, delayed reimbursement, inadequate benefits, weak supervision, and limited advancement can produce turnover that destroys the relationships on which the program depends.

Safety and Role Boundaries

Workers may encounter weapons, active retaliation, coercive group dynamics, domestic disputes, or people requiring emergency medical or psychiatric care. Programs must define when staff should disengage, call emergency services, use team response, or decline an assignment. Community credibility does not make a worker invulnerable.

Confidentiality and Surveillance

Program records may contain sensitive information about victimization, conflicts, relationships, housing, health, and illegal conduct. Excessive data sharing can expose participants to prosecution, retaliation, stigma, or immigration consequences. Insufficient information sharing can impede emergency coordination. Policies should distinguish service data, evaluation data, mandatory reports, and immediate safety information.

Service Scarcity

Outreach workers cannot resolve structural barriers through relationship alone. Housing shortages, unstable employment, untreated trauma, inadequate schools, transportation barriers, and limited behavioral-health capacity can undermine participant plans. Programs should not promise opportunities that partner institutions cannot deliver.

Organizational Capacity

Small community organizations may possess exceptional trust but lack accounting systems, procurement staff, insurance, data infrastructure, or experience administering large government grants. Capacity-building should strengthen community control rather than using administrative requirements to exclude the organizations closest to the problem.

Political Pressure

Officials may expect immediate citywide results from a limited pilot or may cut programs after a temporary increase in violence. Programs may also overstate success to preserve funding. Responsible governance distinguishes short-term implementation measures from long-term violence outcomes and reports setbacks directly.

Governmental and Institutional Response

Local governments increasingly created offices of violence prevention, contracted with community organizations, embedded intervention staff in hospitals, and funded outreach or victim services. States developed grant programs and, in some jurisdictions, authorized public-health or victim-service funding streams for violence intervention.

The U.S. Department of Justice's Community Based Violence Intervention and Prevention Initiative, launched in fiscal year 2022, represented a major federal expansion. The initiative supported community-led programs, local government partnerships, victim services, training, technical assistance, and research through several Office of Justice Programs components.

Federal support also increased scrutiny. Grantees must demonstrate financial controls, lawful use of funds, program design, partnerships, performance measurement, and organizational readiness. These requirements can improve accountability but may burden small organizations unless technical assistance and realistic reimbursement systems are provided.

Public Debate

Arguments in Support

Supporters argue that serious violence cannot be reduced through arrest and prosecution alone. Community organizations can identify disputes earlier, reach people who avoid government institutions, reduce retaliation, support survivors, and address practical conditions that sustain risk. CVI can also provide a public-safety role for people with lived experience who are often excluded from formal systems.

Criticisms and Concerns

Critics question whether some programs have sufficient evidence, whether community organizations can safely manage high-risk conflicts, whether funding lacks adequate oversight, and whether broad CVI claims obscure weak local implementation. Others argue that programs may become dependent on police data, function as informal surveillance, or be used politically as a substitute for deeper investment in housing, education, health, and economic opportunity.

Operational Standard

These disputes cannot be resolved by the label alone. A sound assessment should examine the local violence problem, program theory, workforce, participant selection, safety procedures, data boundaries, service capacity, implementation fidelity, cost, and measured outcomes. CVI should be evaluated as an operating system rather than as a slogan for or against policing.

Relationship to Related Subjects

Community Violence Prevention

Community violence prevention is the broader field of policies and programs intended to stop violence before it begins and reduce its harms. It includes economic security, education, family support, youth development, protective environments, firearm policy, victim services, and intervention. CVI usually refers to more targeted efforts directed at elevated risk, active conflicts, survivors, or concentrated places.

Gun Violence Prevention

Gun violence prevention includes firearm laws, safe storage, suicide prevention, trafficking enforcement, domestic-violence interventions, school safety, research, and community programs. CVI frequently focuses on firearm violence but does not encompass the entire gun-policy field.

Community Policing

Community policing is a police organizational philosophy emphasizing partnerships, problem solving, and institutional transformation. CVI may collaborate with police but often places community organizations, hospitals, and public-health actors in independent operational roles. The two approaches can overlap without being identical.

Alternative Crisis Response

Alternative crisis response directs selected behavioral-health, substance-use, homelessness, and welfare calls to civilian or clinical responders. CVI focuses primarily on serious interpersonal violence, retaliation, victimization, and the people or networks connected to those risks. A city may operate both systems with different dispatch, staffing, and safety rules.

Defund the Police

Defund the Police is a political demand to reduce, redirect, or restructure police budgets and responsibilities. Some advocates propose transferring resources to CVI, but CVI existed before the 2020 slogan and can be funded without reducing police appropriations.

Police Abolition

Police abolition seeks the long-term replacement of conventional policing and carceral systems with other institutions of safety, support, and accountability. Abolitionist frameworks may support CVI, but many CVI programs retain formal relationships with police, prosecutors, probation, or local government and do not adopt an abolitionist objective.

Restorative and Transformative Justice

Restorative justice emphasizes accountability, repair, and participation by affected parties. Transformative justice seeks responses to harm outside conventional punishment while changing conditions that produced it. CVI may use mediation or nonpunitive support, but its immediate objective is violence reduction, and many programs do not conduct a full restorative or transformative process.

Gang Intervention

Gang intervention works with people, groups, and conflicts associated with gang activity. Some CVI programs originated in gang-violence reduction, but community violence may involve neighborhood disputes, interpersonal retaliation, robbery, social networks, or conflicts that do not fit a formal gang classification. Inaccurate gang labels can stigmatize participants and distort intervention decisions.

Key Distinctions

Community violence intervention
Targeted, community-centered action to interrupt serious violence, reduce retaliation, support survivors, and change future risk.
Community violence prevention
The broader field of policies and programs that reduce violence risk across individuals, relationships, institutions, neighborhoods, and society.
Violence interruption
A specific CVI function involving rapid identification and mediation of conflicts likely to become violent.
Street outreach
Continuing relationship-based engagement with people at elevated risk, including but not limited to active conflict mediation.
Credible messenger
A worker or mentor whose relevant experience, relationships, or standing supports trust and influence with a particular participant population.
Hospital-based violence intervention
A model that begins engagement with violently injured patients in a hospital and connects them to safety planning, victim services, recovery support, and continuing case management.
Focused deterrence
A strategy combining direct communication, service offers, community standards, and concentrated legal consequences for continued serious violence.
Primary prevention
Action intended to prevent violence before exposure or elevated individual risk emerges, often through broad improvements in family, school, economic, and community conditions.
Victim services
Assistance addressing the legal, financial, emotional, medical, and safety consequences of victimization. Victim services may operate independently or as part of CVI.

Summary

Community violence intervention is a community-centered public-safety approach that seeks to prevent shootings, homicides, retaliation, and repeat victimization through trusted relationships, targeted engagement, conflict mediation, survivor support, case management, services, and coordinated institutional action. It includes several distinct models, including street outreach, violence interruption, hospital-based intervention, credible-messenger mentoring, and group violence intervention.

CVI works best when a community defines the violence problem precisely, selects an appropriate model, reaches the people and places most closely connected to risk, protects worker and participant safety, delivers real services, establishes clear information boundaries, and measures both implementation and outcomes. Community trust is an operating asset, not a substitute for professional standards, supervision, or accountability.

Research shows that some programs have reduced shootings, homicides, retaliation, or reinjury, while other replications have produced mixed or inconclusive findings. The field's significance lies in establishing that violence reduction can involve community organizations, hospitals, survivors, credible messengers, and public-health institutions as active authorities. Its continuing challenge is to convert locally grounded trust and knowledge into stable, ethical, measurable, and sustainable public-safety systems.
