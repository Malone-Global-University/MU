Confederate Army

The combined Confederate national, state, reserve, militia, and irregular land forces that fought to secure independence for the slaveholding Confederate States of America from 1861 through 1865.

What Was the Confederate Army?

“Confederate Army” is a practical collective term for the land forces that fought under Confederate national, state, and allied authority during the Civil War.

It was not one perfectly unified organization raised exclusively by the central government.

Most long-term field soldiers served in state-identified regiments transferred or mustered into the Provisional Army of the Confederate States.

States also maintained militia, reserves, local-defense units, home guards, and temporary forces whose relationship to Confederate commanders differed across time and place.

Terminology

Confederate Army
The broad historical term for Confederate land forces.
Army of the Confederate States
The small permanent Regular Army authorized by the Confederate government.
Provisional Army of the Confederate States
The temporary national wartime force containing most Confederate volunteer and conscript regiments.
State troops
Forces raised under state authority and sometimes transferred into Confederate service.
Militia
State-organized military bodies used for emergency, local, reserve, or home-defense service.
Reserve forces
Later-war organizations composed especially of younger and older men or soldiers assigned to limited duties.
Home guard
A local organization protecting communities, enforcing conscription, pursuing deserters, guarding prisoners, or resisting raids.
Partisan ranger
A Confederate-authorized irregular unit operating behind or near enemy lines.
Army of Northern Virginia
One major Confederate field army, not a synonym for the entire Confederate Army.

Relationship to the Confederate Government

The Confederate Constitution made the president commander in chief of national military forces.

Congress enacted military laws, appropriations, conscription, exemptions, and regulations.

The War Department administered appointments, recruitment, supply, transportation, prisoners, hospitals, ordnance, and military justice.

Governors and state military authorities retained important power over state organizations, appointments, militia, and local defense.

Institutional Purpose

The Confederate Army existed to defend the Confederate States of America and secure its independence from the United States.

The Confederate government was founded by seceding slave states and constitutionally protected slavery and property in enslaved people.

Individual soldiers served for varied reasons, but their military service supported a government whose central institutional purpose included preservation of slavery and white political supremacy.

Origins in State Forces

Before and during secession, states organized volunteer companies, militias, guards, artillery batteries, and provisional troops.

State forces seized federal forts, arsenals, mints, customhouses, weapons, and military property.

South Carolina troops opened fire on Fort Sumter before a fully developed Confederate field army existed.

Agreements later transferred many state organizations and resources to Confederate national authority.

Army of the Confederate States

Confederate Congress authorized a permanent Regular Army during March 1861.

The proposed establishment was small compared with wartime requirements.

Officers could hold permanent Regular Army rank while serving at higher temporary rank in the Provisional Army.

The Regular Army remained more important as a legal and professional framework than as the principal source of enlisted field strength.

Provisional Army of the Confederate States

The Provisional Army was the principal national wartime force.

It accepted volunteer units raised by states and organized them for Confederate service.

Most infantry, cavalry, and artillery regiments commonly identified as Confederate belonged to this temporary establishment.

Conscripts and reenlisted soldiers were assigned into the same field system.

State Troops

States raised military organizations before and after transferring major forces to the Confederacy.

Some state troops served briefly, defended local territory, guarded infrastructure, or later entered national service.

Governors sometimes attempted to retain units for home defense rather than allow transfer to distant campaigns.

Records may classify a unit differently at different stages of its service.

Militia

State militia laws required or permitted military service outside ordinary Confederate regiments.

Militia guarded towns, bridges, railroads, prisons, depots, coastlines, and state property.

They responded to raids, supported conscription, pursued deserters, and performed emergency defense.

Training, equipment, age, readiness, and legal status varied widely.

State and Confederate Reserves

Expanding conscription eventually brought boys as young as seventeen and men as old as fifty into reserve categories.

Reserve units guarded prisoners, public property, railroads, coastal positions, and communities.

Some reserves entered major combat during emergencies.

The South Carolina State Reserves guarding Florence Stockade illustrate how older and younger soldiers filled essential duties when field armies needed available fighting-age men.

Home Guards and Local Defense

Home guards often operated within a county or small district.

Their duties included defending communities, collecting supplies, apprehending deserters, monitoring enslaved people, and responding to raids.

Local forces could protect civilians but also become instruments of coercion, retaliation, personal feud, and class conflict.

Some home guards possessed uncertain authority or only temporary recognition.

Jefferson Davis as Commander in Chief

President Jefferson Davis exercised close control over military appointments, strategy, departments, and senior command.

His West Point education, regular-army service, Mexican War experience, and tenure as United States secretary of war shaped his leadership.

Davis communicated directly with commanders and sometimes retained officers despite political or battlefield criticism.

Disputes over Joseph E. Johnston, Braxton Bragg, P. G. T. Beauregard, and other generals damaged command relationships.

Confederate War Department

The War Department became the central administrative institution of the Confederate Army.

It managed recruiting, conscription, officer appointments, orders, quartermaster supply, subsistence, ordnance, hospitals, prisoners, engineers, and military records.

Secretaries included Leroy Pope Walker, Judah P. Benjamin, George W. Randolph, James A. Seddon, and John C. Breckinridge.

Frequent leadership change reflected political conflict and the extraordinary burden placed on the department.

Adjutant and Inspector General's Office

General Samuel Cooper served as Adjutant and Inspector General and became the highest-ranking Confederate general by date of rank.

His office issued orders, maintained returns, handled appointments, collected reports, and supported Army administration.

Cooper's role was principally administrative rather than field command.

Many records created or gathered by his office later became part of the National Archives Confederate holdings.

General-in-Chief

The Confederacy lacked a continuously empowered general-in-chief for most of the war.

Davis retained strategic authority, while department and army commanders managed separate theaters.

Congress authorized a stronger general-in-chief position near the end of the war.

Robert E. Lee received the position in February 1865, too late to reverse military collapse.

Rank Structure

Confederate commissioned ranks included lieutenant, captain, major, lieutenant colonel, colonel, brigadier general, major general, lieutenant general, and general.

Enlisted ranks included private, corporal, sergeant, first sergeant, and specialized noncommissioned positions.

Officers might hold different rank in the Regular and Provisional establishments.

Rank, seniority, appointment date, and command assignment produced recurring disputes.

Officer Appointment and Election

Early volunteer companies commonly elected their company officers.

State authorities appointed or confirmed many field officers before units transferred to Confederate service.

The president nominated general officers subject to congressional confirmation.

Election encouraged local trust but did not guarantee military competence.

Reorganization and conscription laws later displaced some elected officers and intensified resentment.

Professional Military Officers

Former United States Army and Navy officers supplied much of the Confederacy's senior professional expertise.

West Point graduates included Robert E. Lee, Joseph E. Johnston, Albert Sidney Johnston, Thomas J. Jackson, Braxton Bragg, and numerous others.

Mexican War service provided command and logistical experience.

Most company and regimental officers nevertheless came from civilian life.

Companies and Regiments

A company was commonly recruited from one town, county, neighborhood, or social network and commanded by a captain.

Ten companies normally formed an infantry regiment under a colonel, lieutenant colonel, and major.

A newly organized regiment could approach 1,000 officers and men but commonly declined through battle, disease, desertion, capture, detached duty, and expired service.

Regimental identity remained one of the strongest sources of soldier loyalty and historical memory.

Battalions, Independent Companies, and Legions

A battalion contained fewer companies than a regiment and could operate independently or later expand.

Independent companies served artillery, cavalry, engineering, guard, and specialized duties.

Some early “legions” combined infantry, cavalry, and artillery in one organization.

Most mixed legions were later divided because separate branch administration proved more practical.

Brigades

A Confederate brigade usually contained several regiments and was commanded by a brigadier general or senior colonel.

Brigades were commonly identified by a current or former commander's name, such as Hood's Brigade or Stonewall Brigade.

Some acquired state, regional, ethnic, or battlefield identities.

Composition changed as regiments transferred, consolidated, or suffered losses.

Divisions, Corps, and Armies

Division
Several brigades, commonly commanded by a major general.
Corps
Several divisions, commonly commanded by a lieutenant general.
Field army
Two or more corps or equivalent formations commanded by a general or senior general officer.
Department
A geographic administrative and operational command responsible for troops and territory.
District
A subordinate geographic command within a department.

Confederate divisions were often larger than Union divisions, and unit names did not guarantee a fixed number of soldiers.

Naming Field Armies

Confederate armies were commonly named for states or regions, while Union armies were often named for rivers.

The Army of Northern Virginia defended Virginia and operated in Maryland and Pennsylvania.

The Army of Tennessee fought principally in the Western Theater rather than within Tennessee alone.

Names, territorial departments, and command boundaries changed during the war.

Army of Northern Virginia

The Army of Northern Virginia became the Confederacy's most famous field army.

Robert E. Lee assumed command in June 1862 after Joseph E. Johnston was wounded.

It fought in the Seven Days, Second Bull Run, Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg, the Overland Campaign, Petersburg, and Appomattox.

Its defense of Richmond gave it exceptional political importance.

Its prominence should not obscure the scale and strategic importance of western and Trans-Mississippi armies.

Army of Tennessee

The Army of Tennessee was the principal Confederate field army in the Western Theater.

Commanders included Braxton Bragg, Joseph E. Johnston, John Bell Hood, and periods of temporary leadership by others.

It fought at Perryville, Stones River, Chickamauga, Chattanooga, Atlanta, Franklin, and Nashville.

Command conflict, territorial losses, casualties, supply problems, and damaging offensive decisions weakened the army.

Trans-Mississippi Forces

Confederate forces west of the Mississippi operated in Missouri, Arkansas, Louisiana, Texas, Indian Territory, and adjacent regions.

The Trans-Mississippi Department became increasingly isolated after Union capture of the Mississippi River.

Edmund Kirby Smith commanded the department during the later war.

Its forces included white Confederate units, state troops, militia, guerrillas, and Native allied regiments.

Infantry

Infantry formed the largest branch of the Confederate Army.

Infantrymen marched, fought in line and skirmish formations, built fortifications, guarded roads, escorted prisoners, and performed camp labor.

Weapons included smoothbore and rifled muskets from Confederate, captured United States, and imported sources.

Infantry bore most battlefield casualties because it seized and held ground.

Cavalry

Cavalry performed reconnaissance, screening, raiding, escort, communications, pursuit, and mounted combat.

Many Confederate cavalrymen initially supplied their own horses and received compensation under military rules.

Loss of a horse could remove a soldier temporarily from mounted service or force transfer.

Prominent cavalry commanders included J. E. B. Stuart, Nathan Bedford Forrest, Joseph Wheeler, Wade Hampton, John Hunt Morgan, and John S. Mosby.

Artillery

Field artillery batteries supported infantry and cavalry with cannon firing solid shot, shell, case, and canister.

Heavy artillery served forts, harbor defenses, siege lines, and fixed positions.

Artillery required trained crews, horses, ammunition chests, caissons, repair facilities, observers, and coordinated movement.

Confederate armies used domestically produced, captured, and imported guns.

Engineers and Pioneers

Engineer officers surveyed terrain and designed forts, bridges, roads, trenches, mines, batteries, and defensive systems.

Enlisted engineers, pioneers, soldiers, impressed civilians, and enslaved laborers carried out construction.

Fortifications became increasingly important at Richmond, Petersburg, Vicksburg, Charleston, Mobile, and other strategic locations.

Signal and Secret Service

Confederate signal personnel used flags, torches, observation stations, codes, and couriers.

Telegraph lines connected headquarters, capitals, rail centers, and departments.

Intelligence activities included scouts, spies, agents, intercepted messages, maps, and civilian informants.

Communication remained vulnerable to damaged wires, captured couriers, distance, and divided commands.

Volunteer Recruitment

Early Confederate armies depended heavily on volunteers.

Political speeches, newspapers, public meetings, militia culture, family networks, community pressure, fear of invasion, and defense of slavery encouraged enlistment.

Companies often adopted names and uniforms before receiving state or national acceptance.

Initial enthusiasm could not replace losses or keep men in service after short enlistment terms expired.

Estimated Enlistment Strength

Confederate military strength cannot be stated as one exact number.

The National Park Service gives an enlistment range from approximately 750,000 to 1,227,890.

The range reflects destroyed records, incomplete rolls, reenlistments, transfers, aliases, state forces, militia, irregulars, and disagreement over who should be counted.

Numbers should therefore be presented as estimates rather than a precise individual headcount.

Occupational Composition

National Park Service data identify farmers as approximately 69 percent of recorded Confederate civilian occupations.

Other soldiers included laborers, mechanics, merchants, clerks, professionals, students, and tradesmen.

The occupational profile reflected the Confederacy's predominantly agricultural economy.

Occupation categories do not reveal wealth, slaveholding, landownership, or precise motivation by themselves.

Social Class

Confederate soldiers came from slaveholding and nonslaveholding households and from multiple economic classes.

Wealthier men possessed greater political influence, horses, equipment, education, and access to exemptions or safer assignments.

Poor and middling farm families carried large military and food-production burdens.

Class diversity among soldiers does not change the slaveholding purpose and policy of the government they defended.

Why Men Served

Confederate soldiers expressed varied and changing motivations:

  • defense of home, family, community, or state;
  • Confederate nationalism and claimed independence;
  • support for slavery, property rights, and white racial order;
  • resistance to United States invasion or occupation;
  • honor, masculinity, duty, and community pressure;
  • comradeship and loyalty to a regiment;
  • adventure, status, pay, or military ambition;
  • conscription or fear of legal punishment;
  • fear of emancipation and social transformation; and
  • reluctance to abandon fellow soldiers after initial enlistment.

Personal motivation and institutional war purpose should be analyzed separately.

Conscription Act of 1862

Confederate Congress enacted national conscription on April 16, 1862.

The law extended many existing enlistments and made white male citizens within specified ages liable for military service.

Initial liability generally covered able-bodied white men from eighteen through thirty-five.

The law was the first national draft statute in American history.

Governors and constitutional critics argued that the central government was taking control of state citizens and militia power.

Expansion of Conscription

Later legislation expanded the age range and tightened national control.

Men up to age forty-five became liable during 1862.

The February 1864 law extended service broadly from age seventeen to fifty, with younger and older men commonly assigned to reserve duties.

Expanding age requirements demonstrated the Confederacy's growing manpower crisis.

Substitution

Early conscription rules allowed a liable man to hire an eligible substitute.

The practice favored people able to pay and encouraged professional substitution, fraud, and desertion.

Public resentment intensified as poorer men remained subject to service.

Congress abolished substitution late in 1863 and required many men who had furnished substitutes to report under new legislation in early 1864.

Exemptions and Details

Confederate law exempted or detailed selected government officials, industrial workers, railroad employees, ministers, teachers, medical personnel, overseers, and other occupations considered essential.

Men could also receive disability exemptions.

Administration varied, and influence, fraud, local politics, or inconsistent medical examination affected outcomes.

Detailed soldiers remained legally in service while performing skilled civilian or industrial work.

Twenty-Slave Exemption

The exemption commonly called the Twenty-Slave Law allowed one white man to remain on a plantation with twenty or more enslaved people, subject to changing conditions.

Supporters claimed it protected agricultural production and white control over enslaved populations.

Critics treated it as evidence that wealthy enslavers escaped burdens imposed on poorer families.

The controversy produced the phrase “a rich man's war and a poor man's fight.”

Conscription Enforcement

Conscript officers, enrolling officers, local officials, military patrols, reserves, and home guards enforced service laws.

Authorities searched for eligible men, deserters, substitutes, and exemption fraud.

Enforcement could be resisted by communities, armed bands, state officials, families, and local courts.

Coercive enforcement damaged Confederate legitimacy in several regions.

Desertion

Desertion increased as casualties, hunger, unpaid wages, defeat, family hardship, and war weariness intensified.

Some deserters hid near home, joined armed bands, crossed Union lines, surrendered, or enlisted in United States service.

Others left temporarily to assist families and later returned.

Confederate authorities used patrols, arrests, execution, amnesty, persuasion, and pressure on families.

Exact desertion numbers are uncertain because absences, straggling, capture, and record failure overlap.

Southern Unionist Resistance

White Southern Unionists resisted Confederate recruiting, taxation, impressment, and military authority.

Resistance was especially strong in portions of Appalachia, eastern Tennessee, western North Carolina, northern Alabama, Arkansas, Texas Hill Country, and other divided regions.

Unionists guided United States forces, sheltered deserters, formed military units, and undermined Confederate control.

The Confederate Army used arrests, executions, occupation, confiscation, and local forces against suspected disloyalty.

Foreign-Born Confederate Soldiers

Irish, German, English, French, Canadian, Jewish, Polish, Italian, and other immigrants served Confederate forces.

New Orleans and other cities produced ethnic companies and regiments.

Immigrant motivations included local loyalty, employment, community pressure, military ambition, political belief, and attachment to adopted states.

Foreign birth did not produce one uniform political position; immigrants also served the United States in far larger numbers.

Native American Confederate Forces

Confederate treaties and alliances brought governments or factions among the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee Creek, Seminole, and other Native peoples into the Confederate military system.

Native nations were internally divided by removal history, slavery, sovereignty, leadership disputes, and wartime strategy.

Native Confederate troops fought principally in Indian Territory, Arkansas, Missouri, and the Trans-Mississippi.

Stand Watie became a Confederate brigadier general and surrendered his command on June 23, 1865.

Enslaved Labor

The Confederate Army relied extensively on enslaved labor.

Enslaved people built fortifications, repaired railroads, drove wagons, handled animals, cooked, washed, nursed, mined, manufactured, carried supplies, and served officers.

Government impressment and hiring arrangements commonly paid enslavers rather than the people performing the labor.

National Archives Confederate Slave Payrolls preserve nearly 6,000 payroll documents naming enslaved laborers, owners, locations, periods of work, and payments.

Personal Servants and Camp Labor

Officers and some enlisted men brought enslaved servants into military camps.

Servants cooked, washed clothing, cared for horses, carried baggage, obtained supplies, and performed domestic work.

Their presence freed white soldiers and officers from labor and supported military effectiveness.

Photographs or memoirs showing Black people in Confederate camps do not by themselves prove soldier status or voluntary allegiance.

Free Black Labor

Free Black people also worked for or were compelled by Confederate military authorities.

They served as craftsmen, musicians, cooks, teamsters, hospital workers, laborers, and servants.

Free legal status did not provide equal citizenship, voting, commissioned rank, or protection from impressment.

Individual voluntary work should be distinguished from lawful combat enlistment and from coerced labor.

Claims of Black Confederate Combat Service

Black people were present with Confederate armies in many support roles, and isolated individuals may have carried weapons during emergencies.

Confederate law and policy generally barred Black men from lawful soldier status before March 1865.

Claims of large organized Black Confederate combat forces are not supported by Confederate military law, muster systems, or surviving unit records.

Researchers should distinguish soldiers, musicians, cooks, servants, laborers, impressed workers, pension claimants, and later commemorative classifications.

Patrick Cleburne's Proposal

Major General Patrick Cleburne proposed arming enslaved men and offering freedom in January 1864.

He argued that emancipation and Black enlistment could provide manpower and undermine Union moral advantage.

Senior leaders suppressed discussion because the proposal threatened slavery and Confederate political unity.

The proposal demonstrates that military necessity and the government's founding social order remained in conflict.

Black Enlistment Law of March 1865

Confederate Congress passed legislation on March 13, 1865, authorizing limited use of enslaved men as soldiers.

The statute did not itself guarantee emancipation and preserved owner and state authority over status.

Davis issued an order calling for manumission of recruits with owner consent.

Small companies began assembling in Richmond, but the capital fell within weeks.

The program did not produce a substantial Black Confederate field army or alter the war's outcome.

Women and the Confederate Army

Women supported military forces through farms, plantations, factories, hospitals, sewing circles, relief associations, clerical work, nursing, food production, and espionage.

Soldiers' wives petitioned for pay, discharge, furlough, food, and protection.

Some women disguised themselves as men and enlisted, though the Army did not authorize women as soldiers.

Black women experienced enslavement, forced labor, family separation, refugee movement, and resistance to Confederate control.

Soldier Experience

Soldiers spent much of their time marching, drilling, guarding, cooking, repairing roads, digging earthworks, gathering fuel, and waiting.

Letters and visits connected armies with families and local political opinion.

Comradeship within companies and regiments sustained morale despite hunger, disease, fear, and defeat.

Soldier experience differed greatly between early-war camps, major field armies, cavalry raids, garrisons, prisons, and reserve units.

Uniforms and Clothing

Confederate regulations prescribed gray uniforms, but actual clothing varied by state, depot, unit, campaign, and supply.

Soldiers wore gray, butternut, brown, captured blue, civilian garments, shell jackets, frock coats, kepis, forage caps, and broad-brimmed hats.

State governments and depots became major clothing suppliers.

Shoes, blankets, coats, and trousers deteriorated rapidly during campaigns.

Weapons and Equipment

Confederate soldiers used domestic, imported, captured, and prewar weapons.

Enfield rifles, Springfield-pattern arms, smoothbore muskets, carbines, revolvers, swords, and varied artillery appeared in service.

Captured United States arsenals and battlefield equipment were important early sources.

Variation in caliber complicated ammunition supply.

Quartermaster Department

The Quartermaster Department supplied clothing, tents, wagons, horses, forage, transportation, fuel, barracks, and equipment.

It operated depots, workshops, contracts, rail movements, wagon trains, and slave-labor hiring systems.

Shortages, inflation, state competition, damaged railroads, and Union occupation weakened supply.

The Confederate Slave Payrolls were created within this administrative system.

Commissary and Food Supply

The Commissary Department purchased, collected, stored, and distributed food.

Soldiers received meat, cornmeal, flour, bread, peas, rice, salt, sugar, coffee substitutes, and other supplies when available.

Tax in kind, impressment, contracts, local purchase, and foraging supported armies.

Transportation failure and civilian scarcity often mattered more than total agricultural production.

Ordnance Department

The Ordnance Department procured and produced firearms, cannon, ammunition, accoutrements, and military equipment.

Josiah Gorgas became one of the Confederacy's most effective administrators.

Arsenals and works operated at Richmond, Augusta, Selma, Macon, Columbus, and other locations.

Imports through the blockade supplemented domestic manufacture.

Nitre, Mining, and War Production

The Nitre and Mining Bureau sought saltpeter, iron, lead, coal, copper, and other strategic materials.

Government agents supervised mines, caves, furnaces, laboratories, and industrial labor.

Enslaved labor, free workers, soldiers, women, and children participated in production.

Confederate military endurance depended heavily on this dispersed industrial system.

Railroads

Railroads transported soldiers, food, weapons, animals, wounded men, and government officials.

Different track gauges, worn rails, limited locomotives, private ownership, damaged bridges, and weak central coordination reduced efficiency.

Confederate government control increased as military necessity overrode private and state objections.

Loss of Chattanooga, Atlanta, Petersburg, and other rail centers had strategic consequences.

Wagons, Horses, and Mules

Armies depended on animal-drawn transportation for food, ammunition, tents, baggage, ambulances, artillery, and forage.

Horses and mules died from disease, exhaustion, wounds, inadequate feed, and overwork.

Cavalrymen often supplied horses, while quartermasters procured replacement animals.

Animal shortages reduced mobility during the later war.

Medical Department

Confederate medical personnel operated regimental aid, field hospitals, general hospitals, laboratories, medical purveying, and evacuation systems.

Surgeons treated wounds, infection, disease, malnutrition, exposure, and camp illness.

The department trained personnel and produced medicines where blockade restricted imports.

Shortages and transportation problems became increasingly severe.

Disease

Disease killed more Confederate soldiers than battle wounds.

Dysentery, diarrhea, typhoid, pneumonia, malaria, measles, smallpox, tuberculosis, and infections spread through camps and hospitals.

Crowding, poor sanitation, contaminated water, limited immunity, and inadequate food increased mortality.

National Park Service estimates list approximately 164,000 Confederate deaths from disease.

Wounds, Surgery, and Disability

Rifle bullets and artillery caused severe bone and tissue damage.

Surgeons used anesthesia for many major operations.

Amputation could prevent death from shattered limbs but carried major infection risk.

Survivors returned with amputations, chronic disease, blindness, hearing loss, neurological injury, and psychological trauma.

Chaplains and Religion

Chaplains conducted worship, funerals, counseling, correspondence, hospital visits, and religious instruction.

Revivals became significant in several armies during 1863 and 1864.

Religious teaching often presented the war as a providential defense of Southern society.

Faith also helped soldiers interpret suffering, death, defeat, and separation from family.

Military Discipline

Officers enforced drill, guard duty, marching order, camp sanitation, property rules, and obedience.

Charges included desertion, absence, disobedience, cowardice, theft, violence, sleeping on post, and mutiny.

Punishments included confinement, labor, loss of pay, humiliation, corporal punishment in some circumstances, and execution.

Enforcement varied by unit, commander, date, and military condition.

Courts-Martial and Military Justice

Courts-martial tried soldiers under Confederate military law and inherited articles of war.

Commanders reviewed proceedings and sentences through military channels.

Military authorities also used provost courts, commissions, and detention against civilians, spies, Unionists, and irregular fighters.

Wartime justice raised continuing conflict over habeas corpus, state courts, and executive power.

Prisoners of War

Confederate forces captured and guarded United States soldiers in jails, camps, stockades, hospitals, and temporary facilities.

Andersonville and Florence became notorious for overcrowding, disease, inadequate shelter, food shortage, and death.

Confederate soldiers captured by the United States also suffered in prison camps.

Resource scarcity, administrative failure, exchange breakdown, retaliation, and government policy all contributed to prisoner suffering.

Prisoner Exchange

The Dix–Hill Cartel of 1862 established exchange and parole procedures.

Disputes arose over paroles, captured civilians, guerrillas, and units violating agreements.

Confederate refusal to exchange Black United States soldiers as equal prisoners became a major cause of breakdown.

Collapse of exchange increased prison populations and deaths on both sides.

Treatment of Black United States Troops

Confederate policy denied equal prisoner-of-war status to captured Black soldiers.

They faced execution, massacre, forced labor, re-enslavement, or transfer to state authorities.

White officers commanding Black troops also faced threatened punishment.

Killings at Fort Pillow and after the Battle of the Crater demonstrated the danger faced by Black Union soldiers.

Eastern Theater

Confederate eastern armies defended Virginia and Richmond while threatening Washington, Maryland, and Pennsylvania.

Major campaigns included First Bull Run, the Peninsula, Seven Days, Second Bull Run, Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg, the Overland Campaign, Petersburg, and Appomattox.

Tactical victories prolonged resistance, but the Confederacy never destroyed the Army of the Potomac or secured permanent Northern political collapse.

Western Theater

Confederate western forces defended the Mississippi River, Tennessee, Kentucky approaches, rail centers, and the Deep South.

Losses at Forts Henry and Donelson opened major waterways.

Shiloh, Corinth, Vicksburg, Chattanooga, Atlanta, Franklin, and Nashville progressively reduced Confederate territory and military capacity.

Western defeats were strategically decisive despite receiving less postwar attention than Lee's campaigns.

Trans-Mississippi Theater

Fighting west of the Mississippi included conventional battles, guerrilla war, Native civil conflict, river campaigns, and border raids.

Confederate forces fought at Wilson's Creek, Pea Ridge, Prairie Grove, Honey Springs, Mansfield, and numerous smaller engagements.

Texas supplied cattle, cotton trade, troops, and access to Mexico after eastern ports closed.

Geographic separation limited coordination with armies east of the river.

Coastal and River Defense

Confederate Army artillery and engineers defended ports, rivers, forts, islands, and harbor approaches.

Soldiers served at Charleston, Savannah, Mobile, Wilmington, New Orleans, Vicksburg, Port Hudson, and numerous smaller positions.

Army defense depended on cooperation with the Confederate Navy and state authorities.

United States naval superiority allowed repeated amphibious and river operations.

Partisan Rangers

Confederate legislation authorized partisan ranger units to operate against United States forces and communications.

John S. Mosby's command became the best-known officially recognized ranger organization.

Partisans gathered intelligence, captured supplies, disrupted railroads, and attacked isolated troops.

Discipline problems, private plunder, and confusion with guerrillas led Congress to restrict most ranger organizations in 1864.

Guerrillas and Irregular Warfare

Guerrilla bands operated with varying degrees of Confederate approval.

Missouri, Arkansas, Kentucky, Tennessee, Virginia, and border regions experienced ambush, assassination, retaliation, and local civil war.

Some irregulars claimed Confederate allegiance while resisting military discipline.

Civilian communities suffered violence from guerrillas, Union counterguerrilla forces, home guards, deserters, and personal enemies.

Strategic Advantages

  • defense of familiar territory;
  • ability to win by avoiding conquest rather than occupying the North;
  • experienced senior officers;
  • strong early volunteer mobilization among white Southerners;
  • large territory difficult to occupy;
  • interior rail and road connections within several theaters;
  • enslaved labor available for military support and agriculture;
  • local civilian intelligence and assistance; and
  • hope that Northern war weariness or foreign intervention would produce independence.

Strategic Weaknesses

  • smaller free population and replacement pool;
  • inferior industrial and financial capacity;
  • limited naval power and blockade vulnerability;
  • fragile railroad system and shortages of rolling stock;
  • competition among national, state, and private authorities;
  • dependence on enslaved labor vulnerable to escape and Union occupation;
  • limited access to weapons, medicine, machinery, and manufactured goods;
  • failure to secure foreign recognition;
  • desertion, Unionism, and class conflict;
  • loss of food-producing and transportation regions;
  • inability to replace experienced officers and soldiers; and
  • declining public confidence after repeated defeat.

Offensive and Defensive Strategy

Confederate strategy generally sought to defend territory while preserving armies capable of continued resistance.

Commanders also invaded Kentucky, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New Mexico, and other Union-held areas.

Offensives aimed to gather supplies, relieve threatened regions, encourage political opposition in the North, recruit supporters, and obtain diplomatic influence.

Aggressive operations sometimes produced tactical success but consumed manpower the Confederacy could not replace.

Robert E. Lee and the Eastern War

Lee believed battlefield victories and movement into Union territory might alter Northern politics.

His army won major engagements through maneuver, concentrated attacks, and effective subordinate command.

The Maryland and Gettysburg invasions failed to obtain decisive strategic results.

High casualties weakened the Army of Northern Virginia even when tactical outcomes favored it.

Western Command Problems

Western commanders confronted larger geography, divided rail systems, river penetration, and multiple United States armies.

Personal conflict among Davis, Joseph E. Johnston, Braxton Bragg, Leonidas Polk, and subordinate generals weakened unity.

The Confederacy repeatedly changed commands without solving supply and strategic problems.

The loss of Vicksburg, Chattanooga, and Atlanta made continued national resistance increasingly difficult.

United States Hard-War Strategy

United States armies increasingly targeted railroads, food, livestock, military industry, plantations, and state infrastructure supporting Confederate forces.

The campaigns of Grant, Sherman, Sheridan, and others reduced the resources available to Confederate armies.

Enslaved people left plantations and labor systems as Union columns advanced.

Destruction of material support made field resistance progressively unsustainable.

Casualties

Exact Confederate casualty totals remain uncertain because records are incomplete.

National Park Service estimates list 483,026 total Confederate casualties:

  • approximately 94,000 killed in battle;
  • approximately 164,000 deaths from disease;
  • approximately 194,026 wounded in action; and
  • approximately 31,000 prisoners of war within the cited summary.

Categories can overlap or differ among sources, and many missing, captured, or later deaths cannot be established precisely.

Manpower Collapse

Union and Confederate forces were closer in total field strength during 1861 than later in the war.

National Park Service estimates indicate that by January 1865 the Union maintained more than 600,000 soldiers while Confederate strength had fallen to roughly 200,000.

Battle losses, disease, capture, desertion, disability, detached service, expired terms, and territorial loss reduced effective Confederate manpower.

Final Campaigns

Petersburg's fall forced evacuation of Richmond and Lee's retreat westward.

Sherman moved through the Carolinas while United States forces controlled most major ports, railroads, and rivers.

The Army of Tennessee had been devastated at Franklin and Nashville.

Confederate departments remained geographically scattered and unable to create one coordinated recovery.

Appomattox Court House

Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant on April 9, 1865.

Officers and men were paroled and allowed to return home subject to compliance with United States law.

Officers retained sidearms and private property, while soldiers owning horses or mules could take them for spring planting under Grant's implementation.

Appomattox did not legally surrender every Confederate force, but it destroyed the possibility of continued resistance by the Confederacy's most important army.

Bennett Place

Joseph E. Johnston surrendered the principal Confederate forces in the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida to William T. Sherman.

Initial terms included broad political provisions that Washington rejected.

Revised military terms were completed on April 26, 1865.

Johnston's surrender involved more soldiers than Lee's Appomattox capitulation.

Other Confederate Surrenders

Richard Taylor surrendered forces in Alabama, Mississippi, and eastern Louisiana in early May.

Edmund Kirby Smith's Trans-Mississippi Department agreed to surrender terms on May 26 and completed arrangements in June.

Stand Watie surrendered his Confederate Cherokee command on June 23.

Smaller units dissolved, surrendered individually, fled abroad, or ceased functioning without one central ceremony.

Parole and Return Home

Paroled Confederate soldiers promised not to resume armed resistance unless properly exchanged.

Parole documents helped men pass through United States lines and return home.

Soldiers faced destroyed farms, missing relatives, emancipation, physical disability, poverty, and political uncertainty.

Parole was a military arrangement and did not automatically restore political rights or grant a presidential pardon.

Demobilization and Dissolution

The Confederate Army did not demobilize through one national administrative process.

Government collapse, surrender, desertion, parole, capture, and dispersal ended units separately.

Pay, records, final rolls, property settlement, and casualty accounting often remained incomplete.

Many soldiers carried weapons, uniforms, documents, or unit memories home.

Confederate Veterans

Veterans returned to agriculture, trade, professions, politics, law enforcement, education, and public office.

Former officers often retained social and political influence during and after Reconstruction.

Disabled veterans and bereaved families depended on relatives, charities, state support, soldiers' homes, and later pensions.

Veteran identity became an important source of white Southern political and cultural authority.

Confederate Pensions

The United States did not operate the ordinary Confederate pension systems preserved in National Archives military files.

Former Confederate states created pensions for qualifying veterans, widows, and in some cases servants or other categories under changing state law.

Eligibility often depended on disability, poverty, residency, service, remarriage status, or proof supplied by comrades.

Confederate pension files are generally held by state archives rather than the National Archives.

Veterans' Organizations

Local Confederate veteran camps developed during the postwar decades.

The United Confederate Veterans formed in 1889 and coordinated reunions, monuments, publications, and public memory.

The Sons of Confederate Veterans organized in 1896 as a descendant organization.

Veterans' groups preserved service information while promoting interpretations often associated with the Lost Cause.

Confederate Army and the Lost Cause

Lost Cause memory emphasized soldier courage, battlefield leadership, local defense, and defeat by overwhelming numbers.

It minimized slavery, Black resistance, Confederate dissent, conscription, desertion, class conflict, and strategic failure.

Robert E. Lee and the Army of Northern Virginia received disproportionate attention compared with western defeats.

Regimental histories, monuments, reunions, textbooks, and films institutionalized this memory.

Soldier Memory and Government Purpose

Commemorating an individual soldier's suffering or family relationship is not identical to endorsing the Confederate government.

Historical analysis must still identify the government, military objective, slavery policy, and racial order that service supported.

Personal motive, unit conduct, governmental purpose, and later symbolic use are separate questions requiring separate evidence.

Uniforms, Battle Flags, and Symbols

Confederate units used national flags, state flags, regimental colors, battle flags, guidons, and locally designed banners.

The best-known Confederate battle flag was associated especially with the Army of Northern Virginia and related designs.

It was not the Confederacy's only military flag or its sole national flag.

Later use in segregationist and white-supremacist movements added meanings beyond original battlefield identification while retaining connection to the slaveholding rebellion.

Confederate Army Records

Important sources include:

  • Compiled Military Service Records;
  • muster rolls, pay rolls, and descriptive rolls;
  • regimental and unit records;
  • general and staff officer records;
  • War Department correspondence and orders;
  • quartermaster, commissary, and ordnance files;
  • hospital and medical records;
  • courts-martial and military-justice files;
  • prisoner and parole records;
  • conscript and exemption records;
  • Confederate Slave Payrolls;
  • state adjutant general and militia records;
  • state pension applications;
  • letters, diaries, photographs, and newspapers;
  • the Official Records of the Union and Confederate Armies; and
  • veterans' questionnaires, memoirs, and reunion publications.

Compiled Military Service Records

The United States War Department created Confederate Compiled Military Service Records from captured, surrendered, and collected wartime documents.

A record may show name, rank, unit, muster, pay, absence, hospitalization, capture, parole, wounds, desertion, and death.

A soldier can possess separate records for multiple organizations or spellings.

National Archives guidance identifies CMSRs and compiled unit records as the two principal federal sources for Confederate Army service.

Limitations of the Records

Confederate records were destroyed during evacuation, battle, fire, abandonment, and government collapse.

Surviving records are less complete than many Union service files.

Missing records do not prove that a person did not serve, while a name on a payroll or camp list does not always establish soldier status.

Researchers should compare CMSRs with state records, census data, pensions, newspapers, family papers, prisoner lists, cemetery records, and unit histories.

Genealogical Research

A researcher should begin with a soldier's name, possible spelling variants, state, county, unit, branch, and approximate age.

The National Park Service Soldiers and Sailors database can identify possible units.

National Archives indexes and CMSRs provide federal military documentation.

State pension files may contain affidavits, family relationships, disability claims, residence, marriage, and witness testimony.

Service claims made decades later should be compared with wartime records.

Institutional Responsibility

Responsibility for Confederate military policy extended beyond individual soldiers.

The president, Congress, War Department, governors, officers, courts, contractors, enslavers, newspapers, churches, and local authorities supplied manpower and legitimacy.

Enslaved labor and racial control were built into military administration.

Decentralization and state authority do not remove the institutional relationship between military service and Confederate national objectives.

Military and Administrative Achievements

Rapid Mobilization

State and national authorities created large armies from volunteer companies within months.

Four Years of Resistance

Confederate forces sustained major war despite inferior overall national resources.

Operational Skill

Several armies and commanders achieved major tactical victories and conducted difficult campaigns.

Industrial Adaptation

Ordnance, mining, rail, depot, and foreign-purchasing systems supplied armies under blockade.

Local and State Integration

The system connected state regiments, central administration, militia, reserves, and civilian production.

Military Record

Surviving records document hundreds of thousands of individual soldiers and military laborers.

Major Failures and Limitations

Defense of Slavery

The Army fought for a government constitutionally committed to human enslavement and racial inequality.

Manpower Policy

Conscription, substitution, exemptions, and class inequality weakened public support.

Command Conflict

Personal and institutional disputes impaired strategy, especially in the Western Theater.

Logistical Breakdown

Rail deterioration, blockade, inflation, shortages, and territorial loss reduced supply.

Failure to Protect Population

The Army could not prevent Union occupation, destruction, self-emancipation, hunger, or displacement.

Prisoner Policy

Refusal to treat Black Union troops equally contributed to massacre and exchange collapse.

Strategic Defeat

Tactical victories did not secure recognition, independence, or destruction of United States military will.

Common Misconceptions

“The Confederate Army Was One Uniform National Force”

It combined a small Regular Army, the much larger Provisional Army, state troops, militia, reserves, home guards, and irregular forces.

“Every Confederate Soldier Was a Wealthy Enslaver”

Soldiers came from multiple classes, but their service defended a government founded to protect slavery.

“No Nonslaveholder Had an Interest in the Confederate Cause”

White racial status, family connections, community pressure, state loyalty, fear of invasion, and political belief shaped support beyond direct slave ownership.

“All Confederate Soldiers Were Volunteers”

Most began as volunteers, but national conscription started in April 1862 and supplied more than ten percent of soldiers according to NPS interpretation.

“The Twenty-Slave Exemption Released Every Enslaver”

It applied under specified plantation conditions and became one of several exemptions rather than a universal slaveholder exemption.

“The Army of Northern Virginia Was the Entire Confederate Army”

Western, Trans-Mississippi, coastal, state, and reserve forces were essential to the war.

“Confederate Divisions and Union Divisions Were Always the Same Size”

Confederate divisions often contained more brigades and could approach the size of smaller Union corps.

“Black People in Confederate Camps Were Necessarily Soldiers”

Most served as enslaved or free laborers, servants, cooks, teamsters, nurses, or craftsmen rather than legally mustered combat soldiers.

“Large Black Confederate Regiments Fought Throughout the War”

Confederate law generally barred Black soldier status until March 1865, and the late program did not create a substantial field force.

“Confederate Strength Is Known Exactly”

Official records are incomplete; NPS guidance gives a wide enlistment range rather than one precise figure.

“The Confederacy Lost Only Because It Had Fewer Soldiers”

Manpower interacted with industry, finance, blockade, logistics, leadership, diplomacy, self-emancipation, desertion, and territorial loss.

“Appomattox Surrendered Every Confederate Soldier”

Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia; other commands surrendered later.

“Confederate Pensions Are Federal Military Pensions at the National Archives”

Most Confederate pension records were created and retained by former Confederate states.

Key Distinctions

Confederate Army
The broad collective term for Confederate land forces.
Army of the Confederate States
The small permanent Regular Army.
Provisional Army of the Confederate States
The principal temporary national wartime force.
State troops
Organizations raised under state authority and sometimes transferred to national service.
Militia
State military organizations used for local, reserve, or emergency duty.
Reserve
A later-war force commonly containing younger, older, or limited-duty men.
Home guard
A local defense and enforcement organization.
Partisan ranger
An irregular unit possessing Confederate military authorization.
Guerrilla
An irregular fighter whose legal relationship to formal command could be uncertain.
Conscript
A white man compelled into military service under Confederate law.
Substitute
A man serving in place of another liable person under early draft rules.
Enslaved military laborer
A person compelled to support military operations without lawful soldier status.
Black Confederate soldier
A narrowly applicable status legally authorized only at the end of the war and requiring evidence of military muster rather than camp presence alone.
Army of Northern Virginia
Lee's principal eastern field army.
Army of Tennessee
The principal Confederate western field army.
Parole
A military promise not to resume hostilities unless lawfully exchanged.

Why the Confederate Army Matters

The Confederate Army was the principal institution that made secession a four-year military rebellion rather than a short constitutional protest.

It mobilized hundreds of thousands of white men, state governments, railroads, industry, farms, military departments, and coerced Black labor.

Its organization shows how a government founded through states' rights arguments centralized manpower and resources under wartime pressure.

The Army's dependence on slavery was direct: it defended the slaveholding state and used enslaved people to construct, transport, feed, and maintain military forces.

Black self-emancipation and United States Colored Troops transformed enslaved people from a Confederate labor resource into participants in Confederate defeat.

Confederate tactical skill prolonged the war, but battlefield success could not overcome strategic inferiority, diplomatic isolation, logistics, internal division, and destruction of the underlying labor system.

Postwar memory of the Confederate soldier became central to Lost Cause narratives, monuments, flags, public names, and debates over slavery and national identity.

Understanding the Army as a complex institution permits recognition of individual experiences without obscuring the governmental purpose and racial order military service defended.

Summary

The Confederate Army was the combined land force defending the Confederate States of America from 1861 through 1865.

It included the small permanent Army of the Confederate States, the much larger Provisional Army, state troops, militia, reserves, home guards, local-defense units, partisan rangers, and Native allied forces.

Most long-term field soldiers served in state-identified regiments within the Provisional Army. Companies and regiments formed brigades, divisions, corps, field armies, departments, and districts.

Jefferson Davis served as commander in chief, while the War Department administered appointments, recruitment, conscription, supply, hospitals, prisoners, ordnance, and military records.

The Confederacy lacked a continuously empowered general-in-chief until Robert E. Lee received the position in February 1865.

The National Park Service estimates Confederate enlistment strength between 750,000 and 1,227,890 because records were destroyed or incomplete.

Most soldiers initially volunteered, but Congress enacted the first national draft law in American history on April 16, 1862. Later laws expanded liability from age seventeen to fifty and created reserve categories.

Soldiers came from multiple social classes and served for varied reasons. Their service nevertheless supported a government constitutionally committed to slavery, slave property, and white political supremacy.

The Army relied extensively on enslaved labor for fortifications, transportation, cooking, nursing, industry, mining, and personal service. Payments commonly went to enslavers rather than laborers.

Black people were generally barred from lawful combat enlistment until March 13, 1865. The late experimental program did not create a substantial Black Confederate field force.

The Army achieved major tactical victories and sustained resistance for four years but could not overcome United States manpower, industry, naval blockade, logistics, territorial conquest, internal conflict, desertion, and self-emancipation.

Disease killed more Confederate soldiers than battle wounds. NPS estimates list approximately 483,026 total casualties, although exact figures remain uncertain.

Lee surrendered at Appomattox on April 9, 1865, but other Confederate armies surrendered separately through June.

Confederate service records are preserved principally through Compiled Military Service Records and unit records at the National Archives, while most Confederate pension files are held by state archives.

Postwar veterans' organizations, monuments, regimental histories, and Lost Cause interpretation transformed Confederate military memory into a major element of Southern and national public culture.
