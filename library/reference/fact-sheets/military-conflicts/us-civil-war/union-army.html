Union Army

The United States land forces that suppressed the Confederate rebellion, preserved the Union, enforced emancipation, and occupied the defeated South during the transition to Reconstruction.

What Was the Union Army?

“Union Army” is the common historical name for the United States land forces that fought the Confederacy during the Civil War.

It was not one uniform organization recruited and administered entirely from Washington.

The force combined the small permanent Regular Army with much larger volunteer regiments raised by loyal states and territories, mustered into federal service, and commanded within national armies and military departments.

Later additions included drafted men, substitutes, Black regiments under the United States Colored Troops system, and specialized organizations serving forts, frontiers, occupied regions, and support functions.

Terminology

Union Army
The common collective term for United States land forces during the Civil War.
United States Army
The national military institution legally continuing before, during, and after the war.
Regular Army
The permanent professional Army established by federal law.
United States Volunteers
Temporary federal wartime forces, including many state-raised regiments mustered into national service.
State volunteers
Units recruited and initially organized through state authority before federal muster.
Militia
State military forces called into federal or state service under constitutional and statutory authority.
Federal troops
A broad term for soldiers serving the United States government.
Army of the Potomac
One named field army within the wider Union Army, not a synonym for the entire force.

The Prewar Regular Army

Before the Civil War, the Regular Army contained approximately 16,000 officers and enlisted men.

Much of it was dispersed among frontier posts, arsenals, forts, and coastal defenses.

Its responsibilities included frontier security, protection of transportation routes, construction, coastal defense, and campaigns against Native nations.

The Army was too small to suppress a rebellion extending across eleven states, forcing rapid dependence on volunteers.

Officer Resignations and Loyalty

Hundreds of United States military officers resigned to join the Confederacy or their seceding states.

Other Southern-born officers remained loyal to the United States, including George H. Thomas, David G. Farragut in the Navy, Winfield Scott, and Montgomery C. Meigs.

The loss of trained officers weakened the Regular Army but also opened command positions for promotions and volunteer officers.

Loyalty decisions reflected state identity, constitutional belief, family, professional duty, slavery, Unionism, and personal calculation.

Lincoln's Initial Mobilization

After the Confederate attack on Fort Sumter, Lincoln called on the states for 75,000 militia on April 15, 1861.

Those troops generally entered short-term service intended to meet an immediate emergency.

On May 3, Lincoln called for additional three-year volunteers and expansion of the Regular Army and Navy.

Congress later authorized and funded much of the mobilization after assembling in special session.

State Recruitment

Loyal state governors, adjutants general, local committees, political leaders, and private citizens raised regiments.

Communities organized companies around towns, counties, neighborhoods, ethnic identities, occupations, and political networks.

States supplied initial uniforms, officers, camps, equipment, and records before federal muster and reimbursement.

This system produced rapid mobilization but also inconsistent training, equipment, administration, and standards.

The Volunteer Army

Volunteer regiments formed the overwhelming majority of Union forces.

Enlistment terms included ninety days, one year, two years, three years, and service for the duration under changing laws.

Volunteer officers could hold ranks separate from their Regular Army rank.

A professional officer might therefore be a captain in the Regular Army and a major general of volunteers at the same time.

Volunteer organizations were temporary and generally disbanded after their terms ended.

Regular Army and Volunteers

The Regular Army supplied professional experience, institutional continuity, staff officers, instructors, and small combat units.

Volunteers supplied the mass manpower required for continental war.

Regular officers frequently commanded volunteer brigades, divisions, corps, and armies.

Volunteer officers also rose through battlefield performance, politics, state influence, or organizational leadership.

Tension existed between professionals who valued training and volunteers who emphasized citizen service and political legitimacy.

Understanding Enlistment Numbers

The often-cited Union enlistment strength of approximately 2.67 million should not be read as an exact count of unique individual soldiers.

A person could reenlist, serve in more than one organization, transfer, or appear under variant names.

Short-term emergency service and later three-year service could create multiple entries.

Historical totals also depend on whether militia, emergency units, Native forces, and other categories are included.

Enlistment strength remains useful for comparing the scale and composition of the force when its limitations are stated.

Recruitment Methods

Recruitment used patriotic rallies, newspapers, posters, speeches, local committees, political organizations, veterans, churches, and personal networks.

Federal, state, county, municipal, and private bounties offered money for enlistment.

Communities sometimes pressured eligible men through public opinion, employers, family expectations, or accusations of cowardice.

As volunteering declined, the government added federal conscription and permitted substitutes.

Bounties and Bounty Jumping

Bounties became increasingly important in filling quotas.

Large combined payments encouraged some men to enlist, desert, change names, and reenlist elsewhere to collect multiple awards.

These “bounty jumpers” generated fraud investigations and administrative controls.

Bounty competition also shifted recruitment toward poorer men and recent immigrants who needed immediate cash.

Federal Conscription

Congress enacted the Enrollment Act on March 3, 1863.

It created national enrollment districts and made specified male citizens and intending immigrants liable for military service.

Drafted men could serve, provide a substitute, or initially pay a commutation fee under statutory rules.

The draft supplied only a minority of soldiers directly but encouraged localities to raise volunteers before drawings occurred.

Provost Marshal General's Bureau

The Enrollment Act created the Provost Marshal General's Bureau under James B. Fry.

The bureau enrolled eligible men, assigned quotas, conducted drafts, investigated evasion, arrested deserters, administered substitutes, and coordinated recruitment.

Local enrollment boards decided exemptions for disability, dependency, alienage, occupation, and other claims.

Administrative errors, unequal quotas, political distrust, and fraud produced controversy.

Draft Resistance

Resistance included evasion, false medical claims, flight, attacks on enrollment officers, destruction of records, local obstruction, and organized violence.

The New York City Draft Riots of July 1863 combined class resentment, opposition to conscription, partisan conflict, and racist attacks on African Americans.

Federal troops and local forces restored order.

Resistance did not represent one unified antiwar movement; motives varied by locality and individual.

Substitutes

Drafted men and some volunteers could hire substitutes under changing federal rules.

Substitute markets attracted immigrants, economically vulnerable men, veterans, minors using false ages, and professional bounty seekers.

The practice intensified criticism that wealthy men could avoid service.

Many Black soldiers were credited toward local quotas, and some entered as substitutes for white draftees.

Companies and Regiments

The company was the basic local unit, commonly led by a captain.

Ten infantry companies normally formed a regiment commanded by a colonel.

A newly organized infantry regiment might contain approximately 1,000 officers and men but commonly declined through battle, disease, discharge, desertion, and detached duty.

Regimental identity remained powerful because soldiers enlisted, trained, fought, and remembered service through the unit.

Brigades, Divisions, Corps, and Armies

Brigade
Usually several regiments commanded by a brigadier general or senior colonel.
Division
Usually several brigades commanded by a major general.
Corps
Usually several divisions with artillery and support, commanded in the Union Army by a major general.
Field army
One or more corps and supporting formations under a senior commander.
Military department
A geographic administrative and operational command.
Military division
A larger geographic command coordinating multiple departments or armies.

Actual strength varied greatly; unit names described command relationships more reliably than fixed numbers.

Army of the Potomac

The Army of the Potomac was the principal Union field army in the Eastern Theater.

Its commanders included George B. McClellan, Ambrose E. Burnside, Joseph Hooker, and George G. Meade.

It fought from the Peninsula and Antietam through Gettysburg, the Overland Campaign, Petersburg, and Appomattox.

Ulysses S. Grant accompanied it after becoming general-in-chief, while Meade retained direct army command.

Western Armies

The Army of the Tennessee became closely associated with Ulysses S. Grant, William T. Sherman, James B. McPherson, and Oliver O. Howard.

The Army of the Cumberland developed from forces operating in Kentucky and Tennessee and fought at Stones River, Chickamauga, Chattanooga, Atlanta, and Nashville.

Western armies captured rivers, forts, rail centers, cities, and agricultural regions essential to Confederate survival.

Infantry

Infantry formed the largest branch and carried rifles, muskets, bayonets, ammunition, packs, and entrenching tools.

Soldiers marched, fought in line and skirmish formations, guarded posts, built works, escorted prisoners, and performed extensive labor.

Rifled weapons increased range, but terrain, smoke, training, command, and visibility limited practical accuracy.

Infantry suffered most battlefield casualties because it held and assaulted ground.

Cavalry

Cavalry performed reconnaissance, screening, raids, communications, escort, pursuit, and mounted combat.

Early Union cavalry was often dispersed among commands, while later consolidation improved strategic effectiveness.

Leaders such as Philip Sheridan, James H. Wilson, John Buford, David Gregg, Benjamin Grierson, and George A. Custer became prominent.

Maintaining horses required enormous quantities of forage, equipment, veterinary care, and replacements.

Artillery

Field artillery batteries supported infantry and cavalry with guns firing solid shot, shell, case, and canister.

Heavy artillery defended forts, cities, coasts, and siege lines.

Artillery units required horses, ammunition trains, mechanics, skilled crews, observers, and coordinated positioning.

Many heavy-artillery regiments later fought as infantry when manpower needs increased.

Engineers and Pioneers

Engineers surveyed terrain, built bridges, roads, forts, trenches, batteries, depots, and field defenses.

Pontoon trains allowed armies to cross major rivers.

Pioneer detachments cleared roads, repaired crossings, and supported movement.

Engineering became increasingly central during sieges at Vicksburg, Petersburg, Charleston, and other fortified locations.

Signal Corps and Communications

Signal officers used flags, torches, observation stations, codes, and telescopes for tactical communication.

The United States Military Telegraph connected the War Department, commanders, rail centers, and occupied cities.

Couriers remained essential when wires failed or tactical movement outran fixed communications.

Balloons provided limited aerial observation during early campaigns.

President Lincoln as Commander in Chief

Lincoln selected senior commanders, defined national strategy, coordinated armies and politics, reviewed military law, and connected operations to emancipation and elections.

He educated himself in military affairs and frequently communicated directly with generals.

Civilian control produced tension when commanders pursued political policies or operational caution inconsistent with administration goals.

Lincoln's authority remained subject to Congress, law, public opinion, courts, and elections.

War Department

The War Department administered recruitment, appointments, orders, supply, transportation, prisoners, military justice, communications, records, and operations.

Edwin M. Stanton imposed energetic central management after becoming secretary in 1862.

The department expanded far beyond its prewar capacity and depended on civilian clerks, contractors, railroads, telegraph operators, state officials, and technical bureaus.

General-in-Chief

Winfield Scott provided early strategic planning but retired in November 1861.

George B. McClellan briefly combined command of the Army of the Potomac with the general-in-chief role.

Henry W. Halleck coordinated armies from Washington beginning in July 1862 but exercised limited field control.

Ulysses S. Grant became lieutenant general and general-in-chief in March 1864 and coordinated simultaneous campaigns against principal Confederate armies and resources.

Political Generals

Some volunteer generals received appointments partly because of political influence, ethnic leadership, state power, or recruitment ability.

Political appointments helped mobilize constituencies and connect the citizen army to democratic government.

Critics argued that military competence was sacrificed to patronage.

Performance varied: some political generals failed, while others became effective commanders or administrators.

Formation of the United States Colored Troops

Black Americans sought military service from the beginning of the war, but federal policy initially restricted enlistment.

Local Black regiments formed under varying authority during 1862.

The Emancipation Proclamation formally authorized acceptance of Black men into United States armed service.

The War Department established the Bureau of Colored Troops on May 22, 1863, to standardize federal organization.

Black Soldiers

Approximately 179,000 Black men served in the United States Army during the Civil War.

They comprised about ten percent of Union Army strength by the end of the conflict.

Soldiers included free Black Northerners, formerly enslaved Southerners, refugees, residents of border states, and recruits from occupied Confederate territory.

Nearly 40,000 Black soldiers died, most from disease and infection.

United States Colored Troops Service

USCT organizations served in infantry, cavalry, heavy artillery, light artillery, garrison, labor, engineering, guard, and combat roles.

They fought at Port Hudson, Milliken's Bend, Fort Wagner, Olustee, Petersburg, New Market Heights, Nashville, Fort Fisher, and Appomattox.

Black troops also occupied Southern communities and enforced emancipation where their presence directly challenged former slaveholders.

Discrimination Against Black Soldiers

Black soldiers initially received lower pay, inferior equipment in some units, limited promotion, and predominantly white commissioned leadership.

They faced racist treatment within the Army and greater danger if captured by Confederate forces.

Black troops protested unequal pay, and Congress eventually authorized retroactive equality under specified conditions.

Military service did not eliminate racism but created a powerful institutional claim to citizenship and political rights.

Native American Service

Native Americans served in Union volunteer units, state forces, scouts, home guards, and regiments in Indian Territory and the states.

National Park Service enlistment figures identify 3,530 Native American Union Army enlistments, although historical categorization and records remain incomplete.

Native nations were internally divided, and some governments or factions allied with the Confederacy.

Union military policy also continued coercive campaigns against Indigenous peoples in the West during the Civil War.

Hispano and Latino Service

Thousands of Hispano New Mexicans served in volunteer and militia units defending the Southwest.

New Mexico Volunteers fought Confederate forces at Valverde, Glorieta Pass, and other locations.

Latino soldiers also served from California, Texas, the Northeast, and other regions.

Language barriers and prejudice affected training and recognition.

Immigrant Soldiers

German, Irish, English, Canadian, Scandinavian, Italian, Polish, Hungarian, Jewish, and other immigrants served throughout Union forces.

The Library of Congress estimates that approximately 150,000 Irish Americans served in the Union Army.

German-speaking regiments and commanders became especially important in Missouri, the Midwest, and major eastern armies.

Ethnic regiments supported recruitment and solidarity but could also experience prejudice, language problems, and political stereotyping.

Southern Unionists

White Southerners from loyal communities and Confederate states enlisted in United States forces.

Unionist service was particularly important in western Virginia, eastern Tennessee, northern Alabama, North Carolina mountain regions, Arkansas, Louisiana, Texas, and border areas.

Southern Union soldiers faced retaliation against families, divided communities, and uncertain postwar status.

Their service complicates a simple North-versus-South geographical description.

Women Who Enlisted as Soldiers

A small number of women disguised themselves as men and entered Union and Confederate regiments.

Exact numbers are unknown because successful disguise left little official documentation.

Women served in camp, marched, fought, became ill, were wounded, and sometimes were discovered only during treatment or death.

The Army did not formally authorize women as enlisted combat soldiers.

Nurses and Hospital Workers

Approximately 20,000 women volunteered in military hospitals according to National Archives interpretation.

Dorothea Dix served as superintendent of female nurses for the Union Army.

Women worked as nurses, matrons, laundresses, cooks, attendants, relief agents, and hospital administrators.

Black women provided essential nursing, cooking, washing, refugee assistance, and community knowledge, often under unequal pay and documentation.

Civilian Employees and Camp Labor

Armies depended on teamsters, railroad workers, telegraph operators, clerks, guides, scouts, cooks, laundresses, nurses, contractors, laborers, and craftsmen.

Freedpeople performed paid and unpaid work around camps, forts, hospitals, depots, and plantations under military control.

Civilian workers could face military discipline without receiving full soldier status, pay, pensions, or recognition.

Quartermaster Department

The Quartermaster Department supplied clothing, tents, horses, mules, wagons, forage, transportation, barracks, fuel, and camp equipment.

Quartermaster General Montgomery C. Meigs became one of the most important administrators of the Union war effort.

The department worked through depots, railroads, riverboats, contractors, warehouses, repair shops, and field trains.

Superior logistical capacity allowed Union armies to operate deep in Confederate territory.

Subsistence and Food

The Subsistence Department purchased and distributed rations.

Standard supplies could include meat, hard bread, flour, beans, rice, coffee, sugar, salt, and vegetables when available.

Soldiers supplemented rations through local purchase, packages, gardening, hunting, and foraging.

Transportation delays, spoilage, campaign conditions, and corruption produced irregular supply.

Ordnance Department

The Ordnance Department procured, manufactured, inspected, stored, repaired, and distributed weapons and ammunition.

Federal arsenals and private manufacturers produced rifles, muskets, cannon, cartridges, swords, and equipment.

Imported European arms supplemented domestic production.

Standardization improved over time but units initially carried a wide variety of weapons and ammunition calibers.

Railroads and Military Transportation

Union armies used railroads to move troops, artillery, animals, food, ammunition, and wounded soldiers.

The United States Military Railroads operated captured and federalized lines in major theaters.

Engineer Herman Haupt became prominent in railroad bridge construction, scheduling, repair, and movement.

Rail capacity supported larger armies but created dependence on vulnerable tracks, bridges, depots, and junctions.

Rivers and Steamboats

Rivers served as strategic highways in the Western and Trans-Mississippi theaters.

Army transports moved forces and supplies in coordination with Navy gunboats.

Control of the Tennessee, Cumberland, Mississippi, Arkansas, and Red Rivers supported campaigns and occupation.

River operations demonstrated the importance of joint Army–Navy planning.

Medical Department

The Army Medical Department expanded hospitals, surgeons, medical supply, ambulance systems, records, and evacuation.

Surgeon General William A. Hammond promoted reform before being removed after conflict within the department.

Jonathan Letterman's ambulance and field-hospital organization in the Army of the Potomac became an important model.

The Sanitary Commission and Christian Commission supplemented official care with supplies, inspections, nursing, and relief.

Disease

Disease killed more Union soldiers than combat.

Dysentery, diarrhea, typhoid, pneumonia, malaria, measles, smallpox, tuberculosis, and infections spread through camps and hospitals.

Incomplete understanding of germs, contaminated water, poor sanitation, crowding, and inexperienced recruits increased mortality.

Sanitary reforms improved outcomes without eliminating epidemic danger.

Wounds, Surgery, and Disability

Rifles, artillery, and close-range combat caused severe bone and tissue damage.

Surgeons used anesthesia widely for major operations.

Amputation was often the safest available response to shattered limbs but carried risks of infection and death.

Surviving veterans returned with amputations, chronic illness, neurological injury, blindness, hearing loss, and psychological trauma.

Camp Life

Soldiers spent much of their service drilling, marching, cooking, cleaning, guarding, building roads, writing letters, and waiting.

Camps developed routines of roll call, inspection, fatigue duty, picket duty, worship, recreation, and punishment.

Gambling, alcohol, prostitution, theft, desertion, disease, and conflict challenged discipline.

Comradeship within companies and regiments became a major source of loyalty.

Uniforms and Equipment

Dark blue coats or jackets and light or dark blue trousers became common Union identifiers.

Soldiers wore forage caps, kepis, campaign hats, boots or shoes, belts, cartridge boxes, canteens, haversacks, blankets, and packs.

State-issued uniforms varied widely during early mobilization.

Clothing quality, fit, supply, climate, and campaign wear produced considerable variation in the field.

Military Discipline

Officers enforced orders through drill, inspections, guardhouses, courts-martial, fines, confinement, labor, dismissal, and execution for selected offenses.

Desertion, absence, sleeping on post, disobedience, theft, cowardice, mutiny, and violence could produce charges.

Discipline varied among units and commanders.

Citizen soldiers often expected treatment consistent with democratic dignity and resisted officers they considered abusive or incompetent.

Military Justice

Courts-martial tried military offenses under the Articles of War and related orders.

Military commissions also tried civilians and irregular combatants in certain areas.

President Lincoln reviewed many death sentences and became known for pardons or commutations in selected cases.

After the war, the Supreme Court limited military trials of civilians where civil courts remained open in Ex parte Milligan.

Provost and Occupation Duties

Provost marshals maintained order, controlled movement, issued passes, investigated crime, guarded prisoners, enforced military regulations, and administered occupied towns.

Soldiers protected railroads, elections, courthouses, refugees, government property, and supply depots.

Occupation placed Army officers in roles resembling police, judges, mayors, and emergency administrators.

Army Policy Toward Slavery

Early commanders differed sharply over whether to return escaped enslaved people, employ them, protect them, or declare them free.

Benjamin Butler's “contraband” policy at Fort Monroe provided an early basis for refusing returns to owners supporting rebellion.

Confiscation Acts, military orders, and the Emancipation Proclamation progressively changed policy.

Enforcement remained uneven until Union occupation and Black military service made restoration of slave control increasingly impossible.

Freedpeople and Refugee Camps

Hundreds of thousands of freedom-seekers entered Union lines.

Army officers created camps, farms, hospitals, schools, labor systems, and relief arrangements under varying local policies.

Refugees experienced freedom and protection but also disease, overcrowding, coercive labor, inadequate rations, and military paternalism.

Many wartime responsibilities transferred to the Freedmen's Bureau in 1865.

Enforcing Emancipation

The Emancipation Proclamation had practical effect where Union forces entered rebellious territory.

Soldiers announced freedom, prevented re-enslavement, recruited Black men, supervised labor, and displaced Confederate civil authority.

Some white Union soldiers resisted emancipation or abused Black civilians.

Army policy changed institutional power even when individual attitudes remained racist.

Guerrilla Warfare

Union troops fought guerrillas, bushwhackers, partisan rangers, saboteurs, and local Confederate networks.

Counterguerrilla measures included patrols, escorts, arrests, loyalty oaths, confiscation, relocation, retaliation, and destruction of support resources.

Missouri, Arkansas, Tennessee, Kentucky, Virginia, and border regions experienced especially intense irregular warfare.

Civilians frequently suffered from both guerrilla violence and military countermeasures.

Western Frontier Service

State volunteers replaced many Regular Army units transferred from western posts to the Civil War's main theaters.

Union troops guarded trails, forts, telegraph lines, settlements, and mail routes.

Federal and territorial forces also conducted campaigns against Native peoples, including operations associated with forced removal and the Sand Creek Massacre.

Service for the Union did not make all Army operations emancipatory or protective.

Army–Navy Cooperation

Union success frequently depended on cooperation between land and naval forces.

Joint operations captured Forts Henry and Donelson, New Orleans approaches, Mississippi River positions, coastal islands, Port Royal, Mobile Bay defenses, and Fort Fisher.

Gunboats protected transports and supplied firepower, while soldiers captured forts and occupied territory.

Rival commands, communication problems, weather, and differing priorities sometimes weakened cooperation.

Hard War and Destruction of Confederate Resources

Union strategy expanded from defeating armies toward destroying transportation, food, industry, government property, and labor systems supporting Confederate resistance.

Campaigns under Grant, Sherman, Sheridan, and others targeted railroads, crops, livestock, factories, and military infrastructure.

Commanders distinguished officially between military resources and indiscriminate violence, but civilians experienced loss, hunger, displacement, and destruction.

“Hard war” sought to make continued rebellion materially and politically impossible.

Desertion and Absence

Union soldiers deserted because of fear, illness, family crisis, poor leadership, unpaid wages, bounty fraud, political opposition, or exhaustion.

Others overstayed leave, straggled, or disappeared during movement.

Authorities used patrols, detectives, provost marshals, rewards, arrests, amnesties, and punishment.

Desertion statistics are difficult to interpret because records include repeat offenses, mistaken absences, and men who later returned.

Politics Within the Army

Soldiers debated Union, slavery, emancipation, party politics, commanders, conscription, and the 1864 election.

Regimental newspapers, letters, speeches, voting, and camp organizations connected armies to civilian politics.

Many soldiers initially prioritized Union and later accepted emancipation as necessary to victory.

Others remained hostile to racial equality even while fighting a government founded to protect slavery.

Soldier Voting

States developed absentee ballots, field voting, furloughs, and other methods allowing soldiers to participate in elections.

Military votes became important during state contests and the presidential election of 1864.

Lincoln received strong support among soldiers, though voting rules and political pressure varied.

Black soldiers serving before the Fifteenth Amendment generally lacked voting rights in many home jurisdictions.

Deaths and Casualties

Approximately 360,000 Union soldiers died as a result of the war.

Disease accounted for more deaths than battle wounds.

Hundreds of thousands more were wounded, captured, missing, or permanently disabled.

Exact totals vary because military records are incomplete and categories differ.

Prisoners of War

Union soldiers were held in Confederate prisons, camps, jails, and temporary enclosures.

Andersonville became the largest and most deadly Confederate prison, with nearly 13,000 Union deaths.

The prisoner-exchange system collapsed partly because the Confederacy refused to treat captured Black Union soldiers equally.

The Union Army also guarded Confederate prisoners in camps where disease, cold, crowding, and inadequate supplies caused suffering.

Confederate Surrenders

The Union Army accepted separate surrenders from major Confederate commands.

Grant accepted Robert E. Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865.

Sherman accepted Joseph E. Johnston's military surrender at Bennett Place on April 26 after revising broader political terms rejected in Washington.

Other commands surrendered through May and June.

Grand Review of the Armies

The Grand Review occurred in Washington on May 23 and 24, 1865.

The Army of the Potomac marched on the first day, followed by Sherman's western armies on the second.

The event celebrated victory, national reunion, and the citizen-soldier army.

Most Black regiments did not participate, reflecting continuing racial exclusion within national commemoration.

Demobilization

The War Department rapidly discharged most volunteer soldiers after major Confederate surrenders.

Units travelled to state rendezvous, received final pay and documents, and formally mustered out.

Administrative backlogs, missing records, illness, transportation, claims, and unresolved discipline delayed some discharges.

The Regular Army continued as the permanent national force.

Postwar USCT Service

Many USCT regiments remained in service after most white volunteer units mustered out.

They garrisoned forts, occupied Southern communities, guarded borders, and served in Texas and along the Rio Grande.

Three-year enlistments and political concern about French intervention in Mexico contributed to continued service.

Black soldiers enforced freedom in communities where former enslavers resisted the new order.

Occupation and Reconstruction

Army officers supervised surrender, parole, property, public order, oaths, labor disputes, refugees, elections, and restoration of civil government.

The Freedmen's Bureau relied on military personnel and protection.

Under Military Reconstruction after 1867, Army district commanders supervised registration, constitutional conventions, and public order in ten former Confederate states.

Army protection became central to whether Black citizenship could operate against white-supremacist violence.

Postwar Regular Army

Congress reorganized the peacetime Army after the war.

In 1866, legislation established Black Regular Army regiments later consolidated as the 9th and 10th Cavalry and 24th and 25th Infantry.

Veterans also served in occupation, frontier warfare, Reconstruction enforcement, and national expansion.

Wartime experience influenced staff organization, medicine, logistics, command, records, and professional education.

Veterans

Veterans returned to farms, factories, professions, politics, education, and western settlement.

Service created networks that influenced elections, public employment, business, and community leadership.

Disabled veterans and bereaved families sought pensions, medical care, soldiers' homes, prosthetics, and public assistance.

Black veterans became leaders in churches, schools, Republican politics, civil-rights campaigns, and migration.

Federal Pensions

Union veterans and eligible widows, children, parents, and dependents could apply for federal pensions under changing laws.

Early pensions focused on service-related disability or death.

Later laws broadened eligibility and made the pension system one of the federal government's largest programs.

National Archives pension files may contain medical evidence, affidavits, marriage records, family information, service history, and correspondence.

Grand Army of the Republic

Union veterans founded the Grand Army of the Republic in 1866.

The organization promoted comradeship, memorialization, pensions, Union nationalism, and Republican political influence.

Black veterans participated, although local racial practices differed.

GAR posts helped establish Memorial Day traditions and preserve Union military memory.

National Cemeteries

The United States created a national cemetery system to bury and identify Union dead.

Cemeteries were established near battlefields, hospitals, prison sites, and major military centers.

Thousands of bodies were reinterred after the war.

Identification was incomplete because soldiers lacked standardized identification tags and battlefield records were imperfect.

Medal of Honor

Congress created the Army Medal of Honor during the Civil War.

Hundreds of Union soldiers received the award for actions during the conflict.

Standards and review procedures changed over time, and some awards were later revoked or restored.

Black recipients included soldiers recognized for courage at New Market Heights and other engagements.

  • orders, correspondence, and reports;
  • state adjutant general records;
  • United States Colored Troops records;
  • provost marshal and enrollment records;
  • photographs, maps, diaries, and letters; and
  • the published Official Records of the Union and Confederate Armies.

Compiled Military Service Records

The War Department created Compiled Military Service Records by abstracting information from original wartime documents.

A volunteer soldier generally has a separate CMSR for each regiment or organization in which he served.

Files may show rank, muster dates, absence, hospitalization, capture, pay status, wounds, discharge, and death.

Most Union volunteer records remain distributed among federal microfilm, digital collections, and state repositories.

Official Records

The War Department published The War of the Rebellion: A Compilation of the Official Records of the Union and Confederate Armies.

The multivolume work contains reports, orders, correspondence, prisoner material, and an atlas.

It remains indispensable but reflects documents selected, preserved, and edited by government compilers.

Official reports should be compared with unit records, diaries, newspapers, maps, and opposing accounts.

Historical Memory

Union memory emphasized preservation of the nation, soldier sacrifice, constitutional government, and increasingly emancipation.

Black veterans and communities emphasized liberation, military citizenship, equal rights, and federal protection.

White reconciliation sometimes celebrated courage on both sides while minimizing slavery and Black service.

Monuments, regimental histories, reunions, battlefield parks, cemeteries, films, and textbooks shaped later understanding.

Limitations and Failures

Racial Discrimination

The Army delayed Black enlistment, segregated most Black units, restricted Black officers, and initially paid Black soldiers less.

Civilian Harm

Occupation, foraging, confiscation, destruction, and counterguerrilla policies imposed severe burdens on civilians.

Native Dispossession

Union forces continued violent western expansion and campaigns against Native peoples.

Administrative Inconsistency

Rapid mobilization produced corruption, defective supplies, uneven officers, fraud, and record errors.

Disease and Sanitation

Camp conditions and limited medical knowledge caused preventable death on a massive scale.

Unequal Discipline

Punishment, promotion, pay, and recognition varied by race, class, unit, patronage, and command.

Major Achievements

Preservation of the United States

The Army defeated organized secession and restored federal authority.

Destruction of Slavery

Military occupation, emancipation, Black service, and Confederate defeat made abolition possible.

Mass Mobilization

The government integrated states, volunteers, conscription, industry, transportation, and finance into a national force.

Logistical Development

Railroads, depots, telegraphs, medical systems, river transport, and supply departments supported continental campaigns.

Black Military Citizenship

Approximately 179,000 Black soldiers established an institutional claim to freedom and national membership.

Professional Legacy

Wartime experience shaped later Army organization, staff practice, medicine, records, and command.

Common Misconceptions

“The Union Army Was the Same as the Regular Army”

Most Union soldiers served in temporary volunteer regiments rather than the small permanent Regular Army.

“Every Union Soldier Came From a Northern Free State”

Soldiers also came from border states, western territories, Southern Unionist communities, immigrant populations, Native nations, and formerly enslaved communities.

“The 2.67 Million Figure Counts Unique Individuals Exactly”

It is an enlistment-strength figure that can include reenlistments and multiple service entries.

“The Union Fought From the Beginning Solely to Abolish Slavery”

Its initial official objective was preservation of the Union; emancipation became a formal military objective as the war developed.

“Black Soldiers Served Only as Laborers”

USCT regiments served in combat, infantry, artillery, cavalry, garrison, engineering, occupation, and support roles.

“All Union Soldiers Supported Racial Equality”

Attitudes varied widely, and institutional emancipation policy coexisted with racism inside the Army.

“The Army of the Potomac Was the Entire Union Army”

It was one major field army; western, Gulf, coastal, Trans-Mississippi, and frontier forces were also essential.

“Most Soldiers Were Drafted”

The great majority entered as volunteers, although the draft and threat of draft strongly influenced later recruitment.

“Disease Was a Minor Cause of Death”

Disease killed more Union soldiers than battlefield wounds.

“Appomattox Immediately Disbanded the Union Army”

Other Confederate commands remained, occupation continued, and demobilization occurred over months.

“Women Had No Army Service”

Women served extensively as nurses and civilian workers, and some enlisted secretly as soldiers.

“Union Victory Automatically Secured Equal Rights”

Victory abolished slavery, but Reconstruction and later civil-rights struggles determined whether freedom and citizenship would be protected.

Key Distinctions

Union Army
The collective wartime United States land forces.
Regular Army
The permanent professional federal Army.
United States Volunteers
Temporary wartime troops serving under federal authority.
State militia
State military forces that could be called into state or federal service.
Volunteer regiment
A state-raised unit mustered into United States service.
United States Colored Troops
Federally organized Black volunteer regiments administered through the Bureau of Colored Troops.
Army of the Potomac
The principal eastern field army, not the entire Union Army.
Corps
A formation generally composed of several divisions.
Military department
A geographic command responsible for operations and administration.
Drafted soldier
A man selected through federal conscription procedures.
Substitute
A man who served in place of another person under legal wartime rules.
Contraband
A wartime designation initially used for enslaved people escaping owners supporting rebellion.
Occupation duty
Military administration and security in captured or surrendered territory.
Veteran pension
A federal benefit based on qualifying Union service, disability, death, or later statutory eligibility.

Why the Union Army Matters

The Union Army was the principal institution through which the United States defeated secession and preserved national government.

It transformed from a small professional force into a mass citizen army drawing on states, immigrants, Black communities, border regions, territories, industry, railroads, and federal administration.

The Army's movement into Confederate territory weakened slavery before national law abolished it.

Black enlistment converted formerly enslaved people from claimed Confederate property into armed agents of United States victory and freedom.

The force demonstrated the power of logistics, medicine, rail transportation, telegraphy, joint operations, taxation, contracting, and centralized mobilization.

Its record also includes racial discrimination, civilian destruction, coercive occupation, unequal discipline, and campaigns against Native peoples.

Union military victory ended slavery but did not by itself secure equal citizenship. Army protection during Reconstruction became central to whether the constitutional results of victory would survive.

Summary

The Union Army was the common name for the United States land forces that fought the Confederate rebellion from 1861 through 1865 and continued occupation duties afterward.

It combined the small permanent Regular Army with a much larger force of state volunteer regiments mustered into federal service, militia, conscripts, substitutes, United States Colored Troops, and specialized support organizations.

The National Park Service lists 2,672,341 Union Army enlistments, but that total measures enlistment strength rather than a perfectly deduplicated count of individuals.

Volunteer regiments supplied most soldiers, while Regular Army officers and departments supplied professional experience, staff administration, training, engineering, logistics, medicine, and continuity.

The force was organized through companies, regiments, brigades, divisions, corps, field armies, military departments, and military divisions.

Its largest branches were infantry, cavalry, and artillery, supported by engineers, signal troops, quartermasters, subsistence, ordnance, medical services, provost officers, railroads, telegraphs, and civilian workers.

Approximately 179,000 Black men served as soldiers, mainly through the United States Colored Troops. They faced lower initial pay, restricted commissions, segregation, racism, and severe danger if captured.

Immigrants, Native Americans, Hispano volunteers, Southern Unionists, border-state residents, women hospital workers, and disguised women soldiers also formed part of the wider military system.

The Army initially fought officially to preserve the Union. Confiscation, self-emancipation, the Emancipation Proclamation, and Black enlistment made destruction of slavery inseparable from victory.

Superior manpower, industry, railroads, river transport, logistics, naval cooperation, medicine, federal finance, and coordinated strategy allowed Union forces to penetrate and defeat the Confederacy.

Approximately 360,000 Union soldiers died, with disease killing more than battle wounds. Hundreds of thousands more were wounded, captured, missing, or disabled.

After Confederate surrender, most volunteer regiments demobilized, while Regular Army and USCT units continued occupation, border, and Reconstruction service.

The Union Army preserved the United States and helped abolish slavery, but its victory did not automatically secure equal citizenship. The protection and enforcement of freedom remained the central military and political problem of Reconstruction.
