Say Her Name

The Black feminist racial-justice movement created to expose the public erasure of Black women, girls, and femmes subjected to police and state violence, preserve their names and stories, support their families, and build gender-inclusive approaches to public safety and accountability.

What Is Say Her Name?

Say Her Name is a Black feminist racial-justice movement created to make Black women, girls, and femmes visible within public discussions of police violence and state violence.

The movement arose from a recurring pattern: police killings of Black men often became nationally recognized symbols, while comparable cases involving Black women received less sustained media coverage, protest activity, institutional investigation, philanthropic support, and public remembrance.

The words “say her name” function as more than a memorial phrase. They direct participants to identify the person, tell her story, understand the conditions that exposed her to state violence, listen to her family, and connect remembrance to demands for institutional change.

The movement is not limited to fatal police shootings. It examines traffic stops, raids, custody, mental-health calls, domestic-violence responses, police sexual violence, pregnancy, disability, poverty, gender identity, public protest, and other settings in which Black women experience police power differently or remain outside familiar narratives of victimization.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains Say Her Name as a named campaign, intellectual framework, family network, memorial practice, and policy project.

It covers the campaign's founding, the 2015 report and vigil, the Mothers Network, principal categories of state violence, representative cases, the Breonna Taylor turning point, public policy demands, movement methods, legal context, criticisms, and status through August 2026.

It does not attempt to produce a complete list of every Black woman, girl, transgender woman, femme, or gender-nonconforming person killed or harmed by law enforcement.

Many individual cases require separate fact sheets because their legal records, official investigations, family claims, and disputed circumstances cannot be adequately explained in a short case summary.

The fact sheet also distinguishes the organized #SayHerName campaign from ordinary uses of the words “say her name,” documentary titles, unrelated memorial hashtags, and later political appropriations.

Name, Hashtag, and Styling

The movement is commonly written as #SayHerName when referring to its social-media identity and as Say Her Name when referring to the broader campaign.

The African American Policy Forum uses both forms. The hashtag compresses the movement's instruction into a searchable digital label, while the spaced phrase emphasizes the vocal and ritual act of naming.

Capitalization varies in journalism and scholarship, but the canonical movement title should retain the words Say Her Name.

Institutional Founders

African American Policy Forum. AAPF is a research, legal, educational, and advocacy organization founded in 1996. It connects academic analysis, public policy, media intervention, and movement organizing around structural inequality.

Center for Intersectionality and Social Policy Studies. The Columbia Law School center was a founding institutional partner and supplied an academic platform for research concerning problems obscured when race, gender, class, sexuality, disability, and other categories are treated separately.

AAPF remains the institutional home of the campaign. Its related work includes the Why We Can't Wait campaign, Black Girls Matter, Her Dream Deferred, and public advocacy concerning intersectionality and critical race theory.

Kimberlé W. Crenshaw

Kimberlé W. Crenshaw is a legal scholar whose work addresses civil rights, race, gender, constitutional law, critical race theory, and Black feminist legal thought.

She introduced the term intersectionality in legal scholarship to explain how Black women could be excluded when discrimination law and political movements treated race and sex as separate categories.

Crenshaw co-founded AAPF, directed the Center for Intersectionality and Social Policy Studies, helped create Say Her Name, co-authored the 2015 report, convened families, developed public rituals, and later expanded the campaign through a book, performance, advocate network, and memorial archive.

Her central intervention was not that Black women had never opposed police violence. It was that dominant frameworks repeatedly failed to recognize their experiences as central evidence of racialized state violence.

Intersectionality

Intersectionality is the movement's principal analytical method. It examines how forms of power and social classification operate together rather than treating each identity or institution as an isolated variable.

A Black woman may encounter policing through race and gender at the same time. Her vulnerability may also be shaped by poverty, disability, mental illness, pregnancy, age, housing status, sexuality, transgender identity, family role, or immigration status.

Say Her Name challenges two incomplete frames. A racial-justice frame may recognize anti-Black policing while imagining its typical victim as a cisgender man. A gender-violence frame may recognize violence against women while imagining its typical victim as white, privately victimized, and separate from police or criminal-justice systems.

Black women's experiences may disappear between those frames unless a movement deliberately centers the intersection.

The Problem of Public Silence

Say Her Name identifies public silence as a second injury following state violence.

A death may receive limited local reporting, disappear from national news, and fail to become a recognized reference point within racial-justice activism. Families may then experience grief without broad public acknowledgment, sustained advocacy, or institutional pressure.

AAPF has described this as a compounded loss: families lose a loved one and then confront the social loss of her story, reputation, and place in collective memory.

The movement responds by making names repeatable, stories teachable, and family testimony part of public political knowledge.

The Dominant Victim Frame

Public narratives of anti-Black police violence frequently center a Black man stopped, physically controlled, shot, beaten, or killed in public, followed by video evidence and protest.

That sequence describes many important cases. It becomes limiting when treated as the only recognizable form of racialized police violence.

Black women may be killed inside homes, during mental-health calls, while attempting to obtain protection from domestic violence, in vehicles, in custody, during low-level enforcement, or when officers treat them as physically impervious to pain.

Gendered expectations can make their cases appear less representative even when the underlying use of state power is equally important.

Respectability and the “Perfect Victim”

Public sympathy is often distributed according to expectations concerning innocence, appearance, work, motherhood, sexuality, disability, criminal history, behavior, and emotional control.

Black women may be denied victim status when they are angry, poor, mentally ill, armed, transgender, involved in sex work, accused of an offense, protecting themselves, or otherwise outside a narrow image of respectable femininity.

Say Her Name does not require every person memorialized to be factually identical or legally blameless in every respect. It requires scrutiny of whether state force was lawful, proportionate, avoidable, discriminatory, and accountable without making public remembrance dependent upon idealized victimhood.

Black Feminist Foundations

Say Her Name developed from a long tradition of Black women's organizing against slavery, lynching, sexual violence, police abuse, labor exploitation, segregation, criminalization, and state neglect.

Black feminist organizers have repeatedly argued that racial violence and gender violence cannot be separated cleanly. Police may fail to protect Black women from private violence while also subjecting them to public coercion, arrest, harassment, or assault.

Organizations and writings associated with INCITE! Women of Color Against Violence, the Audre Lorde Project, Critical Resistance, Andrea Ritchie, Beth Richie, and other Black feminist and queer movements helped establish the intellectual and organizing context from which Say Her Name emerged.

Relationship to Black Lives Matter

Say Her Name and Black Lives Matter emerged within the same broader struggle against anti-Black state violence.

Black Lives Matter was created by Black women and included queer and feminist political commitments. Public mobilization nevertheless continued to center the names of men in many protests, news stories, signs, and movement narratives.

Say Her Name functioned as an internal and external corrective. It did not argue that attention to Black men was unjustified. It argued that a movement organized around the proposition that Black lives matter must also possess the language, data, rituals, and policy demands necessary to recognize Black women.

The 2014 Protest Context

The police killings of Eric Garner in New York and Michael Brown in Ferguson produced mass demonstrations during the second half of 2014.

Protesters repeated Garner's final words, “I can't breathe,” and used the names of Brown, Garner, Tamir Rice, and other Black male victims as national symbols.

During the same period, Michelle Cusseaux and Tanisha Anderson died after police responded to mental-health crises. Their cases did not produce comparable national recognition.

AAPF participants carried a banner naming Black women during a December 2014 march and began chanting “Say! Her! Name!” AAPF identifies December 14, 2014, as the formal launch date.

Campaign Launch and Institutional Development

The founding intervention was simple and strategic. AAPF placed women's names together on one banner, made the absence of those names visible, and converted that absence into a chant.

The campaign then developed research, family testimony, vigils, organizing tools, and policy recommendations around the chant.

This sequence distinguished Say Her Name from an unstructured hashtag. The digital phrase was backed by an organization, a theory of erasure, a body of case research, family relationships, and a developing agenda for institutional change.

Her Dream Deferred

AAPF's Her Dream Deferred series created a public forum for discussing the status of Black women and girls.

On March 30, 2015, the series included a Say Her Name program focused on gendered analysis of racialized state violence.

The program helped establish the intellectual and public framework expanded in the May 2015 report and connected police violence to education, economic security, health, sexuality, and other institutions affecting Black women.

The 2015 Say Her Name Report

AAPF and the Center for Intersectionality and Social Policy Studies released Say Her Name: Resisting Police Brutality Against Black Women on May 20, 2015.

Kimberlé W. Crenshaw, Andrea J. Ritchie, Rachel Anspach, Rachel Gilmer, and Luke Harris authored the report.

The report documented cases of Black women and girls killed by police or subjected to other forms of law-enforcement violence, supplied an intersectional framework explaining why those cases did not fit dominant public narratives, and proposed movement and policy responses.

It did not claim to provide a complete statistical census. Its purpose was to expose a neglected pattern through documented cases and create categories capable of guiding further research and organizing.

Principal Categories Identified by the Report

The report organized cases around patterns rather than presenting an undifferentiated list.

Black women were killed or harmed while driving, walking, seeking help, protecting children, experiencing mental-health crises, surviving domestic violence, resisting sexual or physical abuse, protesting, or being detained.

Police encountered them in homes, streets, vehicles, hospitals, jails, schools, and public institutions.

The categories demonstrated that gender affects the setting and meaning of state violence and that reforms designed around one familiar type of encounter may leave other forms untouched.

First Say Her Name Vigil

AAPF and local partners held the first major Say Her Name vigil in Union Square in New York City on May 20, 2015.

Family members of Alberta Spruill, Rekia Boyd, Shantel Davis, Shelly Frey, Kayla Moore, Kyam Livingston, Miriam Carey, Michelle Cusseaux, and Tanisha Anderson participated.

The gathering used speeches, testimony, poetry, music, photographs, signs, and collective recitation of names.

It brought families from different cities into one public space and demonstrated that their cases were not isolated local tragedies. The vigil also transformed report publication into a movement ritual centered upon family voices.

National Day of Action

The report and vigil contributed to a national day of action involving demonstrations in several cities.

Participants displayed names and photographs of Black women and girls, held vigils, conducted direct action, and challenged local movements to broaden their public narratives.

In San Francisco, Black women used a body-centered protest to confront the contradiction between public consumption of Black women's bodies and public indifference to their deaths.

Such actions demonstrated the campaign's use of art, vulnerability, and disruption alongside conventional protest.

The Say Her Name Mothers Network

The #SayHerName Mothers Network is the campaign's principal family institution.

AAPF officially convened the network in November 2016, approximately a year and a half after families participated in the first vigil.

The name reflects the prominent role of mothers, but the network also includes sisters, aunts, and other relatives.

Members provide mutual support, preserve the stories of their loved ones, participate in public events, advise campaign strategy, lobby officials, and welcome newly bereaved families.

The network shifts families from the margins of advocacy into a continuing decision-making and community role.

The “Loss of the Loss”

Families may experience the death itself and a second social injury when the public does not recognize that the death matters.

News coverage may emphasize alleged misconduct, mental illness, poverty, or criminal accusation while giving little attention to the person's life.

Officials may delay information, refuse responsibility, or describe the incident solely from the officer's perspective. Movements may omit the person's name from speeches and memorials.

AAPF's concept of the “loss of the loss” describes this denial of communal recognition and support. The Mothers Network responds through collective mourning, testimony, advocacy, and long-term relationship.

Family Testimony

Say Her Name treats family members as sources of knowledge rather than symbolic figures invited only after institutional conclusions are reached.

Families can explain medical history, disability, personality, work, parenting, prior police contact, and the practical consequences of a death.

Their testimony can challenge official descriptions that reduce a person to a threat, suspect, or mental-health diagnosis.

Family testimony is not automatically conclusive concerning every disputed event. It is nevertheless essential evidence concerning the person's humanity, institutional communication, and long-term effects.

Naming as a Movement Method

Names individualize a problem that statistics can make abstract. A name allows researchers and organizers to locate records, photographs, family members, legal cases, news reports, public officials, and policy failures.

Repetition prevents the person from disappearing after a brief news cycle and establishes a relationship among cases separated by time and geography.

The method carries risk when a name becomes a symbol detached from the person's actual life. Say Her Name therefore increasingly pairs naming with the instruction to tell her story.

Storytelling

The movement asks what the person loved, how she spoke, whom she cared for, what work she performed, and what future was interrupted.

This approach resists a public record composed almost entirely of police reports, autopsies, criminal allegations, and litigation.

Storytelling also supplies context. A “subject with a hammer” may have been a woman changing her locks during a mental-health crisis. A “driver who failed to stop” may have been a mother with a child in the vehicle.

Human context does not decide every legal issue, but it corrects records that otherwise begin and end with state characterization.

Ritual, Art, and Collective Mourning

Vigils, name recitations, photographs, candles, songs, poetry, silence, and family testimony create a shared ritual of remembrance.

Ritual serves an emotional function for families and a political function by declaring that a death belongs within public history.

AAPF uses the term artivism for work combining art and activism. Say Her Name has used poetry, music, visual art, theater, film discussion, banners, photography, spoken word, performance, and digital media.

The movement's artistic work emphasizes not only how people died but the lives and futures that should have continued.

Hashtag Activism and Digital Memory

The hashtag made the phrase searchable, repeatable, and available for decentralized use. Users could attach a name, image, case article, event announcement, or policy demand to a common movement label.

Digital circulation helped overcome traditional media gatekeeping but also produced problems of compression, misinformation, trend cycles, appropriation, and symbolic participation without sustained organizing.

AAPF repeatedly emphasizes that Say Her Name is more than a hashtag. Its institutional substance lies in research, family support, political education, advocacy, and community formation.

AAPF's online In Memoriam archive extends the movement beyond individual protest moments by preserving names, dates, places, photographs, case descriptions, and links.

Fatal Force and Gender-Specific Settings

Fatal shootings remain the most visible form of police violence addressed by the movement, but the settings differ from the dominant public image.

Women have been shot in homes, cars, parking lots, streets, public buildings, and areas controlled by federal security agencies.

Officers have described firearms, knives, vehicles, tools, rapid movement, noncompliance, or perceived threats. Families and advocates have challenged whether officers created the danger, misread behavior, failed to de-escalate, or used deadly force when alternatives were available.

Each shooting requires case-specific analysis. The broader movement claim is that shootings of Black women are less likely to become durable national symbols even when they raise major public questions.

The Home as a Site of Police Violence

Several widely recognized Say Her Name cases occurred inside or immediately around a home.

Police entered for warrants, wellness checks, domestic-disturbance calls, mental-health transport, eviction, or family requests for assistance.

The home is conventionally treated as a place of privacy and safety. For Black women, it may become the site where public authority and private vulnerability converge.

The movement therefore demands reforms addressing warrant accuracy, knock-and-announce practice, tactical entry, crisis response, domestic violence, officer identification, and the presence of children.

Mental-Health Crisis Response

Families often call emergency services because they lack another rapid crisis-response system.

Armed officers may arrive with limited information, time pressure, tactical priorities, and authority to use force. Symptoms such as fear, confusion, withdrawal, agitation, or inability to follow commands may be interpreted as defiance or aggression.

Michelle Cusseaux, Tanisha Anderson, Kayla Moore, Charleena Lyles, and other women became central examples.

Say Her Name advocates non-police crisis response, trained behavioral-health teams, medical intervention, family consultation, de-escalation, and limitations on prone restraint and weapon use.

Domestic Violence and Calls for Help

Black women may encounter police while seeking protection from intimate-partner or family violence.

Officers may misunderstand who requires protection, arrest the survivor, treat emotional behavior as a threat, or escalate the presence of a weapon.

Charleena Lyles, Atatiana Jefferson, Niani Finlayson, and other cases raise different questions concerning domestic conflict, welfare checks, emergency response, and home entry.

These cases are not factually identical. Their relationship within Say Her Name concerns the recurring transformation of a request for safety or assistance into surveillance, confrontation, or death.

Vehicles, Low-Level Enforcement, and Collateral Risk

Police shootings involving vehicles present recurring disputes over whether the vehicle was being used as a weapon, whether an officer placed himself in its path, and whether shooting into a moving vehicle increased danger.

Shelly Frey, Shantel Davis, India Kager, Jessica Williams, Ta'Kiya Young, and other Black women were killed in vehicle-related encounters.

Some were suspected of theft or other offenses. Others were passengers or collateral participants in an operation directed at someone else.

The movement uses these cases to examine low-level enforcement, tactical positioning, shooting-at-vehicles policies, children in cars, and unequal assumptions of dangerousness.

Custody, Jail, and Medical Neglect

State violence can continue after arrest. Black women may experience denial of medication, delayed medical care, inadequate suicide prevention, unsafe restraint, isolation, humiliation, or disbelief concerning symptoms.

Kyam Livingston died in New York police custody after witnesses said she repeatedly requested medical attention. Sandra Bland was found dead in a Texas county jail three days after a traffic stop and arrest.

Say Her Name includes custody because the state assumes direct responsibility for safety, monitoring, and medical care after it deprives a person of liberty.

Police Sexual Violence

The movement's original analysis extended beyond killing to sexual harassment, coercive searches, assault, rape, and exploitation by officers.

Police sexual violence may occur during traffic stops, street encounters, vice enforcement, custody, searches, or threatened arrest.

Victims may fear disbelief, retaliation, criminalization, immigration consequences, or exposure of sex work.

Say Her Name treats sexual violence by an officer as an abuse of state power rather than solely private misconduct by an individual employee.

Black Transgender Women, Femmes, and Gender-Nonconforming People

Say Her Name explicitly includes Black transgender women, femmes, and gender-nonconforming people.

This inclusion responds to their exposure to police harassment, vice enforcement, homelessness, violence, incarceration, misgendering, invasive searches, unsafe placement, and inadequate emergency care.

Kayla Moore and Mya Hall became prominent early examples.

The campaign's terminology broadened over time from “women and girls” to “women, girls, and femmes” to represent these realities more accurately.

Disability and the “Superhuman” Stereotype

Mental illness, physical disability, cognitive disability, deafness, and age can affect communication and compliance during police encounters.

Officers may issue rapid commands that a person cannot understand or follow, ignore family information, or use restraint that creates heightened medical risk.

Black women have also been stereotyped as unusually strong, aggressive, pain tolerant, or difficult to control. That stereotype can obscure vulnerability and justify force.

Say Her Name connects racial justice to disability justice by asking whether emergency systems provide care or transform disability into perceived criminal threat.

Pregnancy, Motherhood, and Children

Black women have been killed or subjected to force while pregnant, caring for children, or in the presence of children.

Korryn Gaines was killed in her home while her young son was nearby and was also shot. Atatiana Jefferson was killed while caring for her nephew. Ta'Kiya Young was pregnant when an officer shot her through a vehicle windshield.

Children may witness death, suffer physical injury, lose a caregiver, or become participants in later litigation and public advocacy.

The movement calls for force policies that recognize pregnancy and the presence of children as critical safety factors.

Michelle Cusseaux

Michelle Cusseaux was a fifty-year-old Black woman in Phoenix, Arizona, who experienced serious mental illness.

On August 13, 2014, police arrived at her home to serve a court-authorized mental-health pickup order and transport her for treatment. An officer shot Cusseaux after the door was opened and she appeared holding a hammer.

Her mother, Frances Garrett, publicly displayed Cusseaux's casket and became a leading advocate for changes in crisis response. The case illustrated how an intervention intended to obtain care can become an armed police encounter ending in death.

Tanisha Anderson

Tanisha Anderson was a thirty-seven-year-old Black woman in Cleveland whose family contacted authorities in November 2014 because she was experiencing a mental-health crisis.

Police restrained Anderson face down, handcuffed her, and placed pressure upon her body. She became unresponsive and died.

Her death occurred approximately one month before AAPF launched Say Her Name and became foundational to the movement's argument that crisis systems should not treat armed police as the automatic first or only form of assistance.

Rekia Boyd

Rekia Boyd was a twenty-two-year-old Black woman fatally shot in Chicago in March 2012.

Off-duty police detective Dante Servin fired from his vehicle toward a group near his home. Servin said he perceived a weapon, while evidence indicated that a person in the group held a cellphone.

A judge acquitted Servin of involuntary manslaughter in 2015 after concluding that the charged theory did not fit an act the court characterized as intentional. Boyd's family became central to the first Say Her Name vigil and Mothers Network.

Shantel Davis

Shantel Davis was a twenty-three-year-old Black woman killed by a New York Police Department officer in Brooklyn in June 2012.

Police pursued a vehicle they believed had been stolen. After the vehicle crashed, an officer entered or reached into it and shot Davis during a struggle.

Police described an accidental discharge; family and community advocates disputed the account and demanded accountability. The case became an early example of vehicle pursuit, close-range force, and limited national recognition.

Shelly Frey

Shelly Frey was a twenty-seven-year-old Black mother killed in Houston in December 2012.

An off-duty sheriff's deputy working store security pursued women suspected of shoplifting and fired into their vehicle, stating that it moved toward him.

The case raised questions concerning private retail security, off-duty police authority, shooting at vehicles, children in the car, and escalation of a low-level property allegation into lethal force.

Kayla Moore

Kayla Moore was a forty-one-year-old Black transgender woman who died during a Berkeley, California, police encounter in February 2013.

Police responded to a mental-health call and attempted to arrest Moore on a warrant associated with another name. Several officers restrained her face down; she stopped breathing and died.

Advocates emphasized the interaction of race, transgender identity, body size, mental illness, restraint, misgendering, and emergency medical response.

Kyam Livingston

Kyam Livingston was a thirty-seven-year-old Black woman who died in New York police custody in July 2013.

She had been arrested after a family dispute and held in a crowded Brooklyn precinct cell. Other detainees said Livingston repeatedly complained of pain and requested medical assistance before becoming unresponsive.

The case demonstrated that state violence may operate through neglect and indifference as well as direct force.

Miriam Carey

Miriam Carey was a thirty-four-year-old Black dental hygienist killed by federal officers in Washington, D.C., in October 2013.

Carey drove through or struck security barriers near the White House and Capitol during an extended vehicle pursuit while her infant daughter was inside the vehicle.

Federal prosecutors declined criminal charges, concluding that evidence was insufficient to prove unlawful force beyond a reasonable doubt. Her family disputed official characterizations and argued that mental-health history simplified a complex encounter.

Alberta Spruill

Alberta Spruill was a fifty-seven-year-old city employee in Harlem who died after New York police mistakenly raided her apartment in May 2003.

Officers relied upon incorrect information and used a stun grenade while executing a warrant intended for another person. Spruill was handcuffed and experienced severe distress before dying at a hospital.

Her family's participation in the 2015 vigil demonstrated the movement's recovery of earlier cases from public silence.

Sandra Bland

Sandra Bland was a twenty-eight-year-old Black woman arrested in Waller County, Texas, after a traffic stop on July 10, 2015.

Video showed the officer escalating the encounter after Bland questioned an order to extinguish a cigarette and resisted being removed from the vehicle. She was charged with assaulting a public servant and held in jail.

Bland was found hanging in her cell on July 13. Authorities ruled the death a suicide, while her family and supporters challenged the stop, detention, jail screening, supervision, and public explanations.

Mya Hall

Mya Hall was a twenty-seven-year-old Black transgender woman killed by security officers at the National Security Agency's Fort Meade entrance in March 2015.

Hall and another transgender woman were in a vehicle that entered a restricted exit lane and failed to stop at the checkpoint. Officers fired upon the vehicle.

Her death received limited national attention and became an example of the intersection of race, transgender identity, poverty, sex-work criminalization, federal security, and media neglect.

India Kager

India Kager was a twenty-seven-year-old Navy veteran and postal worker killed by Virginia Beach police in September 2015.

She sat in a vehicle with her infant child and her partner, whom police sought in connection with a violent investigation. Officers fired numerous rounds after police said her partner shot at them.

Kager was not the principal target and the child survived. Her case illustrates how tactical operations directed at another person can expose women and children to lethal risk.

Korryn Gaines

Korryn Gaines was a twenty-three-year-old Black mother killed by Baltimore County police in August 2016.

Officers entered her apartment to serve warrants connected to traffic offenses and failure to appear. Gaines possessed a shotgun and recorded portions of the prolonged standoff.

Police shot her after stating that she threatened an officer and moved the weapon; her young son was also wounded. The case produced litigation and debate concerning warrants, home entry, mental health, firearms, motherhood, and escalation.

Charleena Lyles

Charleena Lyles was a thirty-year-old pregnant Black mother killed by Seattle police in June 2017 after she called to report a burglary.

Officers said Lyles displayed knives and moved toward them. Police shot her in the apartment while several children were nearby.

The case raised questions concerning prior knowledge of mental-health concerns, officer planning, de-escalation, less-lethal options, pregnancy, and the transformation of a request for help into a fatal encounter.

Atatiana Jefferson

Atatiana Jefferson was a twenty-eight-year-old Black woman killed by a Fort Worth police officer inside her home in October 2019.

A neighbor requested a nonemergency welfare check after noticing an open door. Officers moved around the home without announcing themselves as police.

Jefferson, who was caring for her young nephew, retrieved a firearm after hearing activity outside. An officer fired through a window and was later convicted of manslaughter.

Breonna Taylor

Breonna Taylor was a twenty-six-year-old Black emergency medical worker killed by Louisville police in her apartment during the early hours of March 13, 2020.

Officers executed a search warrant associated with a narcotics investigation. Taylor's boyfriend, Kenneth Walker, fired one shot after the door was breached, stating that he believed intruders were entering. Officers returned fire, striking Taylor; no drugs were found in the apartment.

Later federal charges alleged false or misleading warrant information and reckless gunfire. Taylor's killing became the most internationally recognized Say Her Name case.

Ma'Khia Bryant

Ma'Khia Bryant was a sixteen-year-old Black girl killed by a Columbus, Ohio, police officer in April 2021.

Body-camera and other video showed Bryant holding a knife and moving toward another young woman when the officer fired. Prosecutors did not charge the officer.

The case demonstrates an important distinction: movement remembrance does not require denying visible danger or predetermining criminal liability. It asks whether a Black girl's death receives contextual and humane public analysis.

Ta'Kiya Young

Ta'Kiya Young was a twenty-one-year-old pregnant Black woman shot by police in an Ohio grocery-store parking lot in August 2023.

Store employees accused her of shoplifting. Officers confronted Young in a vehicle, and one officer fired through the windshield as the car moved forward.

Young and her unborn child died. The case renewed questions concerning low-level property allegations, tactical positioning, vehicle-threat doctrine, pregnancy, and deadly force.

Niani Finlayson

Niani Finlayson was a twenty-seven-year-old Black woman killed by a Los Angeles County sheriff's deputy in December 2023.

Finlayson called for help during a domestic-violence incident and reported that a former partner would not leave. Deputies entered and encountered her holding a knife.

A deputy fired within seconds, killing her in the presence of her young daughter. AAPF identifies the case as a contemporary example of a Black woman seeking protection and becoming the person killed by responding law enforcement.

Why the Cases Differ

The people memorialized by Say Her Name did not encounter police under one uniform set of facts.

Some were unarmed and uninvolved in criminal activity. Some possessed weapons, fled, resisted arrest, experienced mental-health crises, or were accused of offenses.

Some died from gunfire, others from restraint, medical neglect, or custody conditions. Legal outcomes include convictions, acquittals, declinations, settlements, administrative findings, and unresolved disputes.

The movement's unifying claim concerns visibility, analysis, and accountability. It does not erase the factual and legal distinctions necessary to judge each case accurately.

Breonna Taylor and the Expansion of Say Her Name

Say Her Name existed for more than five years before Taylor's death.

The phrase became globally recognizable during the 2020 protest wave because activists used it to challenge the delay in public attention and accountability surrounding her case.

Taylor's profession, age, home setting, relationship, and the warrant process made the campaign's argument accessible to a broad public. Her name appeared on signs, murals, clothing, sports broadcasts, speeches, and demonstrations.

This visibility was historically significant. It also created a risk that one highly visible case would substitute for learning the wider history of Black women killed by police.

Breonna Taylor Legal and Institutional Aftermath

Louisville settled a civil lawsuit with Taylor's family and adopted policy changes. Kentucky and local governments restricted no-knock warrants in various forms.

The United States Department of Justice charged current and former Louisville officers with federal offenses connected to the warrant and shooting.

One former officer pleaded guilty to conspiracy, and another was convicted in 2024 of federal civil-rights offenses arising from shots fired into the apartment.

A separate Justice Department investigation found a broader pattern of unconstitutional conduct by Louisville police, including invalid warrants and unlawful no-knock practices. A federal reform agreement was secured in December 2024.

The Data Problem

The United States historically lacked a complete, mandatory, and timely national database of police killings and serious uses of force.

Gender identity, pregnancy, disability, custody status, sexual violence, and officer role were not consistently recorded. News databases often depended upon local reporting and public records.

Transgender victims could be misgendered, while deaths associated with restraint, jail neglect, federal security, or off-duty officers could be classified differently from on-duty shootings.

These weaknesses make Black women's exposure harder to measure and allow public invisibility to become a data problem as well as a narrative problem.

Counting and Classification

A memorial campaign and a statistical database serve different functions.

A database must define inclusion rules concerning causation, law-enforcement status, custody, suicide, off-duty conduct, federal security, and indirect death.

A memorial campaign may include cases that illuminate state violence even when a death is not classified as an officer-involved shooting. Sandra Bland's jail death and Kyam Livingston's alleged denial of care are examples.

Researchers should identify the definition behind every number rather than treating all lists as interchangeable.

Media Coverage and Official Narratives

Media attention depends upon available video, family access, local reporting capacity, timing, social-media circulation, official transparency, and whether a case fits familiar narratives.

A public street shooting recorded by witnesses may generate immediate visibility. A restraint death inside a home or jail may initially be described only through an official statement.

Police departments often release the first account and may emphasize the suspected offense, weapon, officer fear, or refusal to comply. Video, autopsy evidence, dispatch recordings, warrants, and later investigation may confirm, modify, or contradict that account.

Say Her Name encourages journalists to investigate cases before a hashtag becomes nationally popular and to include family and community sources.

Survivors of Police Violence

The movement was created around deaths and public erasure but also addresses Black women who survive police violence.

Survivors may experience sexual assault, unlawful search, beating, Taser use, injury during arrest, pregnancy-related force, medical neglect, or retaliation.

Focusing only on death can reproduce the narrow frame the campaign seeks to disrupt. AAPF's policy recommendations call for recognition of the multiplicity of ways state violence affects Black women, including nonfatal abuse.

Movement Demands

AAPF's demands combine remembrance, accountability, institutional reform, and community investment.

The campaign calls upon movements and public institutions to honor the memories and tell the stories of Black women and girls killed by police.

It demands accountability from officers and government entities, including acknowledgment of responsibility and apology to families where liability is established.

It also advocates forms of safety that do not rely automatically upon armed police and asks which institutions should respond to medical and social crises.

Community Safety Investment

Say Her Name calls for public resources to be directed toward mental-health services, domestic-violence programs, housing, education, employment, shelter, and other institutions that address conditions commonly routed into police contact.

The proposal reflects a preventive model of safety. It assumes that emergency police response cannot substitute for accessible care, stable housing, income, treatment, and community support.

Critics may disagree concerning the appropriate scale of budget reallocation. The movement's operational claim is that communities need capable non-police systems before families can realistically avoid police during crises.

No-Knock Warrants

The campaign demands an end to no-knock warrants.

Rapid, unannounced entry creates a risk that occupants will mistake police for intruders, especially during nighttime operations. Officers may then interpret defensive movement or gunfire as proof that deadly force is necessary.

Breonna Taylor's death made this risk nationally visible, although disputes concerning announcement and execution must be distinguished from the warrant's original authorization.

Reform proposals include prohibition, strict judicial findings, supervisory approval, body-camera requirements, accurate intelligence, tactical planning, and post-operation review.

Alternative Crisis Response

AAPF calls for an end to routine police-first response to mental-health crises and many domestic-disturbance calls.

Alternatives include behavioral-health clinicians, emergency medical personnel, community responders, domestic-violence specialists, peer counselors, and teams with access to police backup when an identified safety condition requires it.

Alternative response requires dispatch screening, staffing, authority, transportation, treatment capacity, data systems, and clear escalation rules. Removing police without building a capable replacement would not satisfy the movement's safety objective.

Pregnancy, Children, Searches, and Gender Identity

Say Her Name demands policies restricting Tasers and excessive force against pregnant people and children.

Pregnancy changes the medical risks associated with electrical weapons, restraint, impact, stress, and delayed care. Children require age-appropriate de-escalation and cannot be treated as physically or developmentally equivalent to adults.

The campaign also calls for police to respect gender identity and expression during stops, searches, transport, booking, and custody and opposes searches conducted to determine gender through anatomy.

Policy should address pronouns, names, officer assignment, strip searches, housing placement, medication, medical care, safety from assault, documentation, and enforceable consequences.

Forms of Accountability

Accountability may include criminal prosecution, civil liability, discipline, decertification, public findings, policy change, apology, compensation, records release, and independent monitoring.

These mechanisms answer different questions. A prosecutor asks whether evidence proves a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. A civil case asks whether rights were violated under an applicable standard. An administrative review asks whether policy and training were followed.

Say Her Name rejects the assumption that the absence of criminal charges ends all inquiry into avoidability, institutional failure, or family harm.

AAPF also calls for acknowledgment and apology where responsibility is established, because settlements without admission can compensate families while leaving the public record unchanged.

Support for Families

Families may need legal counsel, funeral assistance, mental-health services, travel, childcare, media support, records access, security, and long-term community.

Public attention can be intrusive as well as supportive. Relatives may face pressure to become full-time advocates while grieving.

Say Her Name calls upon organizations to support all families affected by state violence rather than concentrating resources only on the most visible cases.

The Mothers Network institutionalizes this obligation beyond the first news cycle.

Accountability within Movements

AAPF asks racial-justice organizations to examine patriarchy, homophobia, transphobia, and other forms of exclusion within their own communities.

A movement can oppose racist policing while reproducing gender hierarchy in leadership, media attention, funding, and public memory.

It can invoke Black women as organizers while neglecting Black women as victims.

Say Her Name treats inclusion as an operational standard. Names, stories, speakers, policy platforms, budgets, and family services must demonstrate the commitment.

Gender-Inclusive Policy Design

A policy designed only around public street stops may not address fatal home entries. A body-camera policy may not correct dispatch failure, bad warrant information, custody neglect, or sexual misconduct.

A ban on chokeholds may not address prone restraint, vehicle shootings, or crisis-response decisions.

Say Her Name calls for an intersectional review of policy platforms so reform addresses the full range of encounters affecting Black women.

This requires case analysis, disaggregated data, family consultation, and evaluation of outcomes by race and gender together.

Weeks of Action, Lobbying, and Public Mobilization

AAPF, the Mothers Network, Black Youth Project 100, and allied organizations have organized national weeks of action.

Activities include vigils, panels, teach-ins, social-media campaigns, public art, film screenings, policy discussions, and calls to local organizations.

Mothers Network members participated in the Women's March on Washington and lobbied for police reform on Capitol Hill.

Their presence challenged women's movements to include state violence against Black women and lawmakers to understand police reform as a women's, family, disability, and gender-justice issue.

The 2023 Say Her Name Book

Haymarket Books published #SayHerName: Black Women's Stories of Police Violence and Public Silence by Kimberlé Crenshaw and the African American Policy Forum in July 2023.

The book expanded the original report through movement history, analytical writing, family narratives, and Black feminist reflection.

It emphasized that Black women, girls, and femmes represented in the movement ranged from children to elders and explained naming and storytelling as practices of mobilization rather than passive commemoration.

The publication preserved the campaign's history beyond the fast-changing environment of social media.

The Lives That Should Have Been

AAPF developed a theatrical project titled #SayHerName: The Lives That Should Have Been from interviews with Mothers Network participants.

Rather than ending each woman's story at the moment of death, the work imagines an alternative realm in which she survived and continued living.

The project debuted at the Hammer Museum in Los Angeles in 2019. AAPF continued developing and presenting it, including a New York staged reading in December 2025.

The play demonstrates the campaign's movement from naming the dead toward restoring interrupted possibility.

Say Her Name Advocates

AAPF developed an advocate program encouraging participants to bear witness, educate themselves, amplify family demands, and activate their communities.

The program treats advocacy as an ongoing practice rather than a single social-media post.

Participants are expected to learn the intersectional conditions surrounding cases, preserve the campaign's meaning, and build local capacity for remembrance and policy work.

The advocate model also helps AAPF respond when the phrase is widely used without connection to Black women or the campaign's original purpose.

Public Culture, Symbolism, and Commodification

Breonna Taylor's name appeared in murals, public art, professional sports, fashion, television, music, corporate statements, political speeches, and global protest.

This visibility represented a major break from the pattern Say Her Name identified.

Public repetition also generated criticism when institutions used the phrase without supporting the family, explaining the case, or changing relevant policy.

Symbolic recognition can therefore be both a movement achievement and a form of commodification. The campaign's deeper standard is whether naming produces knowledge, accountability, care, and institutional change.

Say Her Name: The Life and Death of Sandra Bland

Say Her Name: The Life and Death of Sandra Bland is a documentary film about Bland's arrest, jail death, family, and investigation.

The film title reflects the movement phrase but the documentary is not the same entity as the AAPF campaign.

AAPF publicly connected the film to the Mothers Network and the wider effort to preserve Bland's story.

The distinction is important for classification: the movement belongs under social movements, while the documentary is a separate cultural work.

“Say Their Names” and Broader Memorial Language

“Say their names” is a broader memorial phrase used for multiple victims of racial violence and state violence.

It can support inclusive remembrance but can obscure the specific intervention made by Say Her Name if the gendered critique disappears.

Say Her Name was created because nominally universal racial-justice narratives repeatedly failed to include Black women.

A broader phrase should expand remembrance without erasing the history and institutional ownership of the original campaign.

Appropriation, Dilution, and Movement Ownership

The words “say her name” can be used in ordinary speech and have been applied to women outside the movement's original population and analysis.

AAPF has objected when political figures use the phrase to promote unrelated causes, attack racial-justice movements, or reverse the campaign's meaning.

Such use may be legally permissible while remaining historically misleading. The organized campaign was created specifically to address the erasure of Black women, girls, and femmes subjected to racist police and state violence.

AAPF created and maintains the campaign, but the hashtag is also used by families, local organizers, journalists, educators, artists, and unaffiliated people. Not every use represents an official AAPF position.

Legal Framework

Say Her Name is a social movement and analytical framework, not a single legal cause of action.

Individual cases may involve the Fourth Amendment, Fourteenth Amendment, civil-rights statutes, state tort law, criminal law, disability law, wrongful-death claims, jail standards, warrant law, public-records law, and local policy.

Legal standards differ according to whether the person was seized, detained, incarcerated, seeking medical care, or subjected to discrimination.

A movement conclusion that a case demonstrates state violence may therefore be broader than the question a particular court is authorized to decide.

Fourth Amendment and Warrant Law

The Fourth Amendment governs arrests, stops, searches, warrants, and many uses of force. Force is evaluated according to objective reasonableness under the circumstances known to the officer.

Courts consider immediate threat, severity of the suspected offense, resistance, flight, warning, time for decision, and other facts. The standard is case specific and does not automatically resolve broader questions concerning tactics, training, racial disparity, or avoidability.

Warrants require probable cause and must describe the place to be searched and items or people sought. False statements, stale information, wrong addresses, inadequate verification, and poor supervision can expose uninvolved residents to armed entry.

Alberta Spruill and Breonna Taylor became major examples of the consequences of defective or misleading warrant information.

Duty of Care in Custody

Once government detains a person, it assumes legal responsibilities concerning safety, medical care, monitoring, and protection from self-harm or abuse.

The applicable constitutional standard may depend upon whether the person is a pretrial detainee or convicted prisoner.

Jail policy, suicide screening, medication, observation, emergency response, and staff communication become central evidence.

Sandra Bland and Kyam Livingston demonstrate why public analysis cannot end with the legality of the initial arrest.

Criminal Prosecution and Civil Litigation

Criminal cases against officers require proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Federal civil-rights prosecutions generally require proof that an officer willfully deprived a person of a constitutional right.

State offenses may require proof of intent, recklessness, causation, or unjustification. Charging decisions can fail when prosecutors select an offense that does not match the evidence, as critics argued in the Rekia Boyd case.

Families may also pursue wrongful-death, negligence, constitutional, disability, or other civil claims. Qualified immunity, municipal-liability rules, limitation periods, causation, and evidentiary barriers may restrict recovery.

Settlements can provide compensation and policy commitments without a trial verdict. Say Her Name treats litigation as one accountability pathway but not a complete substitute for public acknowledgment and systemic reform.

Pattern-or-Practice Investigations

Federal civil-rights investigations may examine whether a police department regularly uses excessive force, conducts unlawful stops or searches, discriminates, or violates the rights of people with disabilities.

Such investigations move beyond one officer and examine policy, supervision, training, data, complaint systems, and institutional culture.

The Justice Department's Louisville investigation after Breonna Taylor's death found broader unconstitutional practices, including invalid warrants and unlawful no-knock entries.

Pattern-or-practice findings are legally specific and should not be used as a substitute for proving the facts of every individual case.

Data Reform and Evidence Standards

Effective data systems should record race, gender, gender identity where lawfully and safely collected, age, disability indicators, pregnancy where relevant, encounter type, location, force, injury, custody status, officer assignment, and outcome.

Agencies should separate lethal force, restraint deaths, custody deaths, sexual misconduct, searches, and crisis response rather than combining them into one category.

Public reporting must protect privacy while allowing analysis and should be audited for missing information, misgendering, inconsistent definitions, and agency underreporting.

National estimates depend upon source definitions and record completeness. Researchers should distinguish fatal shootings, all police-caused deaths, deaths in custody, and broader state violence.

Critiques and Debates

Some critics argue that the movement applies a unifying political frame to cases with materially different facts.

Others question whether cases involving weapons, active threats, suicide rulings, or indirect state responsibility should be grouped together.

Supporters respond that the movement does not require identical facts and that the differences reveal how gendered state violence operates across multiple settings.

Debate also concerns whether the campaign focuses too heavily on death, whether media attention to Breonna Taylor changed the original problem, and whether broad use of the phrase weakens AAPF's institutional work.

A rigorous fact sheet should present the campaign's framework while preserving case-specific legal distinctions and uncertainty.

The Limits of Centering Death

Fatal cases create moral urgency and recognizable names but can overshadow surviving Black women who experience sexual assault, injury, incarceration, family separation, or repeated harassment.

A movement focused only on the dead may also neglect prevention and the material conditions that produce police contact.

AAPF's later work expands the campaign toward community safety, non-police response, advocate training, and the lives that should have continued.

Gender Terminology and Inclusion

Early campaign materials often used the phrase “Black women and girls.” Later materials more consistently include Black women, girls, and femmes.

This development reflects increased attention to transgender women and gender-nonconforming people.

Terminology remains contested because identity, community language, family recognition, and historical records do not always align.

Accurate writing should use a person's known name and pronouns and avoid retroactively imposing terminology unsupported by reliable evidence.

Public Attention after 2020

Breonna Taylor's name demonstrated that a Black woman killed by police could become a central national symbol.

The development partially changed the landscape identified in 2014 but did not eliminate unequal attention.

Later cases involving Black women often received brief coverage without the sustained organizing, philanthropic support, or political response associated with Taylor.

AAPF's continuing memorial archive and advocate program indicate that the movement still regards public erasure as an active problem.

Ten Years of Say Her Name

AAPF marked the campaign's tenth anniversary on December 14, 2024.

The anniversary presented the movement as a decade-long institution rather than a temporary protest slogan.

AAPF emphasized community-informed advocacy, policy change, artivism, remembrance, and the continuing need to tell the stories of Black women, girls, and femmes.

The anniversary also occurred during political attacks upon intersectionality, critical race theory, civil-rights education, and the campaign's historical meaning.

Status through August 2026

Say Her Name remained an active campaign of the African American Policy Forum.

Its infrastructure included the Mothers Network, In Memoriam archive, policy demands, advocate program, educational materials, book, public events, and theatrical work.

The campaign continued to add contemporary cases and connect new families to a longer history of Black women's encounters with state violence.

No single law or institutional reform had resolved the full set of problems identified by the campaign. It remained both a memorial project and an argument for redesigning public safety through an intersectional lens.

Historical and Continuing Significance

Say Her Name changed public language by making omission itself visible.

Before the campaign, many Black women killed by police remained isolated local cases. The movement provided a shared name, analytical framework, ritual, archive, family network, and policy agenda.

It expanded the public meaning of police violence beyond male-centered street encounters and demonstrated how Black feminist theory can become movement infrastructure linking scholarship, family testimony, protest, art, digital media, and reform.

Its continuing relevance depends upon preserving historical specificity while adapting to new cases, technologies, institutions, and forms of state power.

Misinformation and Common Errors

Say Her Name was not created after Breonna Taylor's death. AAPF launched the campaign in December 2014.

The campaign is not merely a hashtag. It includes research, family organizing, policy advocacy, public ritual, archives, art, and education.

The movement does not concern only police shootings. It also addresses restraint, custody, medical neglect, sexual violence, crisis response, and other forms of state violence.

The campaign does not argue that every case is factually or legally identical.

Inclusion in a memorial archive does not itself establish criminal liability by an officer.

The absence of criminal charges does not establish that policy, tactics, training, or institutional systems were adequate.

Say Her Name does not oppose attention to Black men killed by police. It challenges the exclusion of Black women from a movement that claims to defend all Black life.

The movement includes transgender women and femmes and should not be described as limited to cisgender women.

The Mothers Network includes family members beyond biological mothers.

The first major Say Her Name vigil occurred in Union Square on May 20, 2015.

Sandra Bland died in jail after an arrest; she was not shot by police.

Breonna Taylor was killed during execution of a search warrant in her home; the case was not an ordinary street stop.

Ma'Khia Bryant's case involved an immediate knife threat shown on video; remembrance does not require denying that fact.

“Say their names” and “Say Her Name” are related but not interchangeable when explaining the campaign's gender-specific intervention.

The HBO documentary about Sandra Bland is a separate cultural work and not the organizational campaign itself.

Public use of the words “say her name” does not mean that AAPF endorsed the speaker, cause, or characterization.

Summary

Say Her Name is a Black feminist racial-justice movement created by the African American Policy Forum and the Center for Intersectionality and Social Policy Studies in December 2014. It emerged because Black women killed by police were repeatedly excluded from national narratives that centered male victims of anti-Black state violence.

The campaign combines intersectional analysis, case research, family testimony, public naming, vigils, art, digital organizing, policy advocacy, and the Say Her Name Mothers Network. Its framework addresses fatal shootings, lethal restraint, deaths in custody, mental-health and domestic-violence response, warrants, vehicle encounters, police sexual violence, disability, pregnancy, transgender identity, and other gender-specific forms of state power.

Breonna Taylor's killing in 2020 transformed Say Her Name into a globally recognized phrase, but the movement predates her case and includes a much larger history. Its continuing significance lies in requiring movements, governments, police departments, researchers, and the public to recognize Black women as central subjects of civil-rights analysis, communal grief, and public-safety reform.
