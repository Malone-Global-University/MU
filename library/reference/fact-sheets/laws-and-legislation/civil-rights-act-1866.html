Civil Rights Act of 1866

The first federal civil-rights statute to declare national citizenship and protect basic civil rights without regard to race or previous enslavement.

What Was the Civil Rights Act of 1866?

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 was a federal law enacted during Reconstruction to establish national citizenship and protect a defined set of civil rights against racial discrimination. It was adopted after slavery had been constitutionally abolished but Southern states had begun using Black Codes and other legal restrictions to limit the freedom of formerly enslaved people.

The act declared most people born in the United States and not subject to a foreign power to be United States citizens. It then required that citizens of every race and color receive the same legal capacity as white citizens in specified areas involving contracts, courts, evidence, property, personal security, punishment, taxation, licensing, and related legal burdens.

The statute was not a general equality law covering every aspect of public and private life. It did not establish voting rights, prohibit all forms of segregation, guarantee public accommodations, redistribute land, or create a complete national system of social and political equality.

Historical Context

The Civil War ended the Confederacy, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout United States jurisdiction, except as punishment following a criminal conviction. Abolition ended lawful ownership of human beings, but it did not answer how citizenship, civil rights, labor relations, property ownership, courts, and political participation would be governed after emancipation.

Under President Andrew Johnson's approach to Presidential Reconstruction, former Confederate states reorganized their governments rapidly and enacted laws regulating the lives of freedpeople. These laws often restricted movement, employment, contracts, property, court participation, occupational access, family autonomy, and personal security.

Members of Congress concluded that emancipation would remain insecure if states could deny formerly enslaved people the ordinary legal capacities needed to live as free persons. The Civil Rights Act therefore attempted to give practical legal content to freedom by creating federal citizenship and protecting a core group of civil rights.

Relationship to the Black Codes

The Black Codes were state and local laws enacted principally in the former Confederate states during 1865 and 1866. Although the provisions varied, they commonly attempted to control Black labor, restrict movement, limit occupational and property opportunities, impose unequal criminal penalties, and preserve a dependent racial order after slavery.

The Civil Rights Act directly challenged these systems by declaring that state laws, ordinances, regulations, and customs could not lawfully deny the protected rights enumerated in the federal statute on the basis of race, color, or previous enslavement.

The act did not automatically remove every discriminatory practice. Effective protection depended on federal officers, courts, prosecutors, marshals, local conditions, political commitment, access to legal remedies, and the willingness of witnesses and officials to participate in enforcement.

Drafting and Legislative Purpose

Senator Lyman Trumbull introduced the civil-rights bill in the Senate during the first session of the Thirty-Ninth Congress. Trumbull and other congressional supporters argued that the enforcement power of the Thirteenth Amendment allowed Congress to prevent states and private actors from recreating the practical conditions or legal incidents of slavery.

The bill sought to establish a national minimum of civil freedom. Its structure linked citizenship to legal capacity: a person recognized as a citizen had to be able to contract, use courts, acquire and transfer property, receive legal protection, and face the same punishments and legal burdens imposed on white citizens.

The statute represented a major shift in federalism. Before the Civil War, civil status and many private-law rights were governed primarily by states. The act asserted that the federal government could intervene when state laws or customs denied citizens basic civil rights because of race or previous enslavement.

Citizenship Declaration

Section 1 declared that people born in the United States and not subject to a foreign power were citizens, while excluding the category then described in federal law as “Indians not taxed.” The principal immediate purpose was to reject the position that African Americans could be denied United States citizenship because of race or previous enslavement.

This provision directly repudiated the citizenship assumptions associated with Dred Scott v. Sandford, the 1857 Supreme Court decision that had denied national citizenship to people of African descent under the Constitution as the Court then interpreted it.

The act's citizenship rule was statutory rather than constitutional. Concern that a later Congress might repeal it, combined with doubts about the extent of congressional authority, helped drive adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause.

The Native American Exclusion

The act excluded the legal category then described as “Indians not taxed.” In the nineteenth-century federal framework, that phrase generally referred to Native people whose political relationship was understood principally through tribal nations rather than ordinary state and federal citizenship.

The exclusion did not mean that no Native person could hold United States citizenship. Citizenship could arise through treaties, statutes, military service, allotment policies, or other legal mechanisms. Nevertheless, many Native people remained outside the citizenship declaration established by the 1866 act.

Congress later enacted the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924, which declared all noncitizen Native Americans born within the territorial limits of the United States to be United States citizens while preserving tribal and other property rights. Citizenship did not itself eliminate state-level barriers to Native voting or resolve the separate sovereignty of tribal nations.

Civil Rights Protected by the Act

The act did not define civil rights in the abstract. It listed legal capacities that Congress considered necessary for practical freedom and equal civil status.

Contracts

Citizens were to have equal legal capacity to enter into and enforce contracts. This protection was central in a post-emancipation economy because Black Codes and discriminatory customs often attempted to control employment agreements, wages, occupational choice, and the ability to leave abusive labor relationships.

Access to Courts

Citizens were to have equal ability to sue, become parties to legal proceedings, and present evidence. These protections addressed state rules and courtroom practices that restricted Black testimony or made it difficult for African Americans to enforce contracts, protect property, or seek redress for violence and fraud.

Property Rights

Citizens were to have equal rights to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property. This provision challenged legal systems that attempted to confine African Americans to dependent labor while preventing land ownership and economic independence.

Security of Person and Property

Citizens were entitled to the equal benefit of laws and legal proceedings protecting personal safety and property. Formal equality required more than permission to own property; courts and law-enforcement institutions also had to provide legal protection when rights were violated.

Equal Punishments and Legal Burdens

The act prohibited states and territories from imposing different punishments, penalties, taxes, licenses, or similar exactions because of race or previous enslavement. This provision responded to laws that criminalized ordinary behavior by Black residents or imposed unequal sanctions for comparable conduct.

Rights the Act Did Not Guarantee

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 protected an important but limited category of civil rights. It did not establish every right later associated with equal citizenship.

Voting Rights

The act did not grant voting rights. Racial discrimination in voting was addressed more directly through Reconstruction state constitutions, federal Reconstruction legislation, and the Fifteenth Amendment.

Public Office and Jury Service

The statute did not expressly guarantee a general right to hold public office or serve on juries. Later constitutional and statutory developments addressed these areas more directly.

Public Accommodations and Segregation

The act did not create the comprehensive public-accommodations protections later attempted by the Civil Rights Act of 1875 or established through twentieth-century civil-rights legislation. It also did not abolish all forms of racial segregation.

Land Redistribution

Equal legal capacity to purchase and hold property did not provide land, credit, capital, or compensation. The act therefore removed certain legal barriers without resolving the severe economic inequality produced by slavery.

Protection From All Discrimination

The statute focused on race and previous condition of servitude in relation to specified civil rights. It was not a general prohibition covering every form of discrimination or every public and private activity.

Federal Enforcement Mechanisms

The act created a federal enforcement structure rather than relying exclusively on state courts and local officials. This was essential because many of the institutions expected to protect freedpeople were themselves enforcing discriminatory laws or customs.

Federal Criminal Liability

A person acting under color of a law, ordinance, regulation, or custom could face federal criminal penalties for depriving an inhabitant of rights protected by the act or imposing racially unequal punishment.

Federal Court Jurisdiction

Federal district and circuit courts received jurisdiction over offenses and certain civil or criminal matters involving people who were denied or could not enforce the protected rights in state or local courts. Some state proceedings could be removed to federal court.

Federal Officers

United States attorneys, marshals, deputy marshals, federal commissioners, Freedmen's Bureau personnel, and other authorized officers could institute proceedings, make arrests, execute warrants, and bring accused violators before federal tribunals.

Use of Federal Forces

The statute authorized the president to employ land or naval forces, or the militia, when necessary to prevent violations and enforce the act. This reflected congressional recognition that civil-rights enforcement might encounter organized resistance or local official obstruction.

Appeal to the Supreme Court

Questions of law arising under the act could ultimately reach the Supreme Court, giving the federal judiciary a role in defining the statute's scope and validity.

President Andrew Johnson's Veto

President Andrew Johnson vetoed the bill on March 27, 1866. He objected to the federal declaration of citizenship, questioned Congress's constitutional authority, criticized the displacement of state law and state courts, and argued that the measure concentrated excessive power in the national government.

Johnson also objected to legislation that he characterized as granting special treatment to newly emancipated African Americans. His veto message reflected a broader conflict with Congress over the terms of Reconstruction and the extent of federal responsibility for protecting freedpeople.

Supporters of the bill rejected Johnson's position. They argued that formal abolition was insufficient while states could use law and custom to deny the basic civil capacities associated with freedom.

Congressional Override

The Senate voted on April 6, 1866, to override the veto by 33 votes to 15. The House of Representatives followed on April 9 by a vote of 122 to 41, satisfying the constitutional requirement that two-thirds of each chamber approve a vetoed bill.

The Civil Rights Act became law on April 9, 1866. The override was a major institutional defeat for Johnson and an important turning point in the struggle between the president and Congress over Reconstruction.

The House historian describes the measure as the first occasion on which Congress legislated directly on civil rights. Its enactment demonstrated that congressional Reconstruction would not be limited to ending slavery or restoring former Confederate state governments.

Constitutional Basis and Debate

Supporters relied substantially on Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment, which authorizes Congress to enforce abolition through appropriate legislation. They reasoned that Congress could prohibit legal disabilities and racial systems that functioned as badges or incidents of slavery.

Opponents argued that the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery but did not give Congress general authority over state civil law, property, contracts, courts, or criminal justice. This disagreement raised fundamental questions about the relationship among emancipation, federal power, state sovereignty, and individual rights.

The constitutional uncertainty surrounding the act was one reason congressional Republicans pursued the Fourteenth Amendment. Constitutionalizing citizenship, due process, equal protection, and federal enforcement authority would place core Reconstruction principles beyond ordinary statutory repeal and provide an additional foundation for federal civil-rights legislation.

Relationship to the Fourteenth Amendment

The Civil Rights Act preceded the Fourteenth Amendment. The act used congressional legislation to declare citizenship and protect specified civil rights; the amendment placed national citizenship and broader restrictions on state action into the Constitution.

The Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause used different wording from the 1866 act and established citizenship for people born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. Its Privileges or Immunities, Due Process, and Equal Protection Clauses addressed state action through constitutional commands rather than ordinary statutory rules.

The act and amendment should not be treated as identical. The act enumerated particular civil rights and supplied detailed enforcement procedures. The amendment created constitutional principles whose meaning developed through legislation and judicial interpretation.

Implementation and Resistance

Federal enforcement operated under difficult conditions. Many Southern courts, police forces, employers, landowners, and local governments resisted equal civil status. African Americans seeking to use the act faced violence, intimidation, poverty, limited access to counsel, hostile juries, and the risk of retaliation.

The Freedmen's Bureau and federal military authorities sometimes provided forums and protection when state systems failed. Their authority, geographic reach, staffing, and political support were nevertheless limited and changed over time.

White supremacist violence and organized resistance intensified during Reconstruction. Congress responded with additional measures, including the Reconstruction Acts and Enforcement Acts, and with constitutional amendments addressing citizenship, equal protection, and voting rights.

Continuing Legal Legacy

Portions of the Civil Rights Act of 1866 were later reenacted, revised, and codified. Its principal surviving protections are associated with 42 U.S.C. §§ 1981 and 1982.

42 U.S.C. § 1981

Section 1981 protects equal rights to make and enforce contracts, participate in legal proceedings, give evidence, receive the equal benefit of laws protecting persons and property, and avoid racially unequal legal burdens. Later amendments expressly defined contract enforcement to include performance, modification, termination, and enjoyment of contractual benefits and conditions.

42 U.S.C. § 1982

Section 1982 protects the equal property rights of United States citizens to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property.

Private Discrimination

In Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. in 1968, the Supreme Court held that the property-rights protection derived from the 1866 act reaches private as well as governmental racial discrimination in property transactions. In Runyon v. McCrary in 1976, the Court applied the contract-rights protection to private racial discrimination in contracting.

These decisions gave modern force to Reconstruction-era statutory language and confirmed that the act's legacy extends beyond invalidating discriminatory state laws.

Representative Example: Property Ownership

A state law prohibiting Black citizens from purchasing or leasing land would conflict directly with the civil-rights protections established by the act. The statute treated the legal ability to acquire and transfer property as a basic incident of freedom and citizenship.

The same principle also addressed discriminatory private transactions as later interpreted by the Supreme Court. A property owner or developer could not lawfully refuse to sell or rent property solely because of the prospective purchaser's race under the surviving federal protection derived from the 1866 act.

Legal capacity did not ensure practical access. Freedpeople still faced lack of capital, discriminatory lending, violence, fraud, land dispossession, and unequal local administration. The distinction between a formal right and the resources needed to exercise that right remained significant.

Key Distinctions

Abolition
The constitutional termination of slavery through the Thirteenth Amendment.
Citizenship
Formal membership in the national political community, declared by statute in 1866 and later constitutionalized through the Fourteenth Amendment.
Civil rights
Legal capacities involving contracts, courts, evidence, property, personal security, and equal legal burdens.
Political rights
Rights involving voting, representation, public office, and participation in government; these were not generally granted by the 1866 act.
Social equality
A broader and historically contested category involving social relations, institutions, services, and public accommodations; the act did not create a comprehensive social-equality code.
Formal equality
A rule requiring the same legal rights and burdens without racial distinction.
Substantive freedom
The practical ability to exercise rights, secure protection, acquire resources, and live without coercion or violence.

The act was a major advance in formal legal equality, but formal recognition did not automatically produce safe, universal, or economically meaningful access to the rights it protected.

Why the Civil Rights Act of 1866 Matters

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 established that emancipation required more than the formal abolition of slavery. Freedom depended on enforceable legal status, access to courts, property ownership, contract rights, equal protection of person and property, and freedom from racially unequal punishments.

The act also transformed the relationship between the federal government and individual rights. Congress asserted that national institutions could intervene when states used law or custom to deny basic civil rights because of race.

Its enactment exposed the limits of Presidential Reconstruction, accelerated the conflict between Congress and Andrew Johnson, and helped produce the Fourteenth Amendment and later Reconstruction legislation.

The survival of its core contract and property protections demonstrates that Reconstruction was not only a temporary postwar program. It created federal civil-rights rules that remain part of United States law.

Summary

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 was enacted during Reconstruction to declare national citizenship and protect specified civil rights without racial discrimination. It responded directly to the Black Codes by requiring equal legal capacity in contracts, courts, evidence, property, personal security, punishment, taxation, licensing, and related civil matters.

President Andrew Johnson vetoed the bill, but Congress overrode him and enacted it on April 9, 1866. The law created federal criminal penalties, federal court jurisdiction, and enforcement authority for federal officers because state and local institutions could not be relied upon to protect freedpeople.

The act did not establish voting rights, comprehensive desegregation, land redistribution, or complete social equality. Nevertheless, it became a foundational Reconstruction statute, influenced the Fourteenth Amendment, and survives in modern federal protections governing contracts and property.
