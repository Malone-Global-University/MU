What Was Executive Order 14074?

Executive Order 14074 was a presidential directive coordinating federal policy across policing, accountability, data, incarceration, supervision, reentry, grants, and criminal-justice administration.

It used the President's executive authority to direct federal departments and agencies and to shape federal assistance to nonfederal governments.

It was broader than a police use-of-force order because it extended from officer recruitment through prison conditions and reentry.

Formal Title and Citation

The formal title was “Advancing Effective, Accountable Policing and Criminal Justice Practices To Enhance Public Trust and Public Safety.”

The order was signed May 25, 2022 and published May 31, 2022 at 87 Federal Register 32945.

The official Federal Register and GovInfo versions are the controlling primary texts.

What Is an Executive Order?

An executive order is a written presidential directive governing executive-branch operations or implementing authority assigned by the Constitution or federal statute.

It can bind agencies and executive officers while in force.

It cannot lawfully exceed presidential authority, appropriate money that Congress has not provided, or amend an act of Congress.

Executive Order Versus Statute

A statute is enacted through Congress and presidential approval or veto override.

An executive order is issued by the President and can ordinarily be revoked or superseded by a later President.

This difference explains why Executive Order 14074 could direct federal policy but could not enact the full George Floyd Justice in Policing Act.

Legal Authority

The order invoked the President's authority under the Constitution and laws of the United States.

Specific directives relied upon existing agency statutes, grant programs, employment authority, procurement authority, data responsibilities, correctional authority, and presidential supervision of the executive branch.

Each agency action still had to remain within the law governing that agency.

Appropriations Limitation

Section 23 required implementation to remain subject to the availability of appropriations.

The order could direct agencies to identify resources, prioritise grants, or request funding, but it could not create unlimited spending authority.

Congress retained the power of the purse.

No Privately Enforceable Right

Section 23 stated that the order did not create a substantive or procedural right enforceable in law or equity.

A person ordinarily could not sue merely by alleging violation of the executive order.

Independent constitutional, statutory, regulatory, contractual, or administrative claims could still exist.

Federalism

Most police officers in the United States work for state, local, tribal, territorial, campus, transit, or special-jurisdiction agencies.

The President does not command those agencies in the same manner as executive-branch agencies.

The order therefore used grants, accreditation, equipment rules, federal task forces, guidance, technical assistance, and voluntary adoption to influence nonfederal policing.

Federal Mandates

DOJ repeatedly explained that the order's mandatory policing provisions applied to federal law-enforcement agencies.

Federal agencies were required to adopt policies equivalent to or stronger than designated DOJ models.

The order did not directly impose every federal policy upon every municipal officer.

State, Tribal, Local, and Territorial Incentives

Nonfederal agencies were encouraged through federal grants, accreditation standards, equipment-transfer rules, training, technical assistance, and model guidance.

Agencies accepting federal money could face requirements attached under existing grant authority.

The order's influence therefore varied according to program participation and later agency implementation.

George Floyd and the Date of the Order

President Biden signed the order on the second anniversary of George Floyd's murder.

The timing made the order an explicit response to the national movement against police violence and racial injustice.

The text also addressed officer safety, violent crime, staffing shortages, public trust, and broader criminal-justice reform.

Relationship to Failed Congressional Legislation

The House passed versions of the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act in 2020 and 2021, but the Senate did not pass either version.

The executive order adopted some policies that the executive branch could implement without new legislation.

It acknowledged that system-wide reform, new legal remedies, and major funding required Congress.

Public Trust and Public Safety

Section 1 argued that public safety and public trust are mutually reinforcing.

The order described fair treatment, transparency, accountability, officer support, and effective enforcement as connected rather than competing goals.

Critics disputed parts of this framework, but it supplied the organising principle for the entire directive.

Race and Systemic Racism

The order acknowledged that fatal police encounters had disproportionately affected Black and Brown people and other people of color.

It described racial and ethnic disparities as continuing features of the criminal-justice system.

This framing influenced anti-bias training, data collection, profiling guidance, accreditation, and community-engagement directives.

Recognition of Law-Enforcement Service

The order stated that most officers perform difficult and life-threatening work with honor and integrity.

It addressed officer wellness, suicide prevention, recruitment, retention, training, equipment, and staffing challenges.

The directive therefore combined accountability reform with institutional support for law-enforcement personnel.

Scope Beyond Police Departments

Executive Order 14074 addressed federal detention, prisons, supervised release, reentry, the First Step Act, crime reporting, behavioral-health services, and alternatives to incarceration.

Its title referred to policing and criminal-justice practices rather than policing alone.

A fact sheet that covers only chokeholds and body cameras omits much of the order.

Section 2—Sharing Federal Best Practices

Section 2 directed DOJ to produce guidance and strengthen federal practices involving deaths in custody and civil-rights enforcement.

It addressed both nonfederal best-practice guidance and internal federal accountability.

The section connected investigation quality, family treatment, discipline, and federal criminal civil-rights capacity.

Independent Investigations of Deaths in Custody

The Attorney General was directed to issue guidance for independent criminal investigations of custody deaths potentially involving law-enforcement or prison personnel.

Independence could involve a separate agency, prosecutor, state official, or other structure capable of reducing conflicts.

Guidance did not itself rewrite every state's homicide-investigation law.

Training for Deprivation-of-Rights Cases

DOJ was directed to assess and improve training for federal agents, supervisors, and prosecutors handling 18 U.S.C. § 242 cases.

Section 242 criminalises willful deprivation of federal rights under color of law.

The order did not change the statutory willfulness standard.

Federal Deadly-Force and Custody-Death Investigations

Federal agencies were directed to examine investigation and discipline practices for incidents involving deadly force or death in custody.

The review concerned timeliness, consistency, independence, and accountability.

Administrative and criminal investigations remained legally distinct.

Notification of Families

The order required guidance on timely and appropriate notification and support for families or emergency contacts after a death in custody.

Notification practices affect dignity, trust, evidence, public communication, and trauma.

The directive recognised that accountability includes treatment of survivors, not only investigation of the officer.

Timely and Consistent Discipline

Federal law-enforcement agencies were directed to assess whether their procedures caused unwarranted delay or inconsistent discipline.

Agencies were instructed to make appropriate changes without abrogating collective-bargaining obligations.

The order did not dictate one penalty for every incident.

Section 3—Recruitment, Hiring, Promotion, and Retention

Section 3 created an interagency workforce initiative led by the Office of Personnel Management.

It addressed diversity, expertise, screening, promotion, retention, and agency-specific challenges.

The objective was a qualified, service-oriented, professional federal law-enforcement workforce.

Federal Recruitment Working Group

OPM was directed to convene agency leaders and develop common policies and best practices.

The group was to publish an action plan and identify barriers to recruitment and retention.

Federal agencies vary substantially in mission, authority, location, risk, and workforce requirements.

Applicant Screening

The order called for screening mechanisms capable of identifying unqualified applicants.

Relevant subjects could include prior misconduct, employment history, criminal history, integrity, psychological suitability, certification, and background information.

Screening must comply with due process, privacy, employment-discrimination law, and accurate recordkeeping.

Diversity and Inclusion

The workforce section encouraged recruitment, hiring, promotion, and retention of a diverse and expert workforce.

The theory was that broader representation can improve legitimacy, language capacity, institutional knowledge, and community relationships.

Diversity did not replace qualification, performance, or accountability requirements.

Promotion and Leadership

Promotion systems can shape organisational culture by determining who supervises, trains, evaluates, and disciplines officers.

The order encouraged fair and professional promotion practices.

Specific federal agencies retained authority to develop lawful merit systems consistent with governing personnel rules.

Retention

Federal agencies faced staffing shortages, competition, retirement, burnout, low morale, and specialised skill demands.

Retention policies can include pay, assignment, leadership, wellness, career development, family support, and safe working conditions.

Retention of qualified officers should not become protection of serious misconduct.

Guidance for Nonfederal Agencies

The Attorney General was directed to publish recruitment, hiring, promotion, and retention guidance for state, tribal, local, and territorial agencies.

The resulting guidance identified best practices rather than one mandatory national civil-service code.

Local agencies remained subject to state law, collective bargaining, certification, and municipal authority.

Section 4—Officer Wellness

Section 4 treated officer wellness as a public-safety and institutional-performance issue.

It required best-practice reporting on trauma, mental health, substance-use disorders, resources, and suicide prevention.

The directive linked wellness to retention, judgment, family stability, and safe service.

Occupational Trauma

Officers may encounter death, violence, abuse, disaster, injury, hostility, and repeated human suffering.

Untreated trauma can affect health, relationships, decision-making, substance use, absenteeism, and suicide risk.

Wellness programs require confidentiality while preserving fitness-for-duty and public-safety responsibilities.

Substance-Use Support

The order included support for officers experiencing substance-use disorders.

Effective systems distinguish voluntary treatment, impairment on duty, misconduct, safety risk, and disability rights.

Support cannot excuse criminal conduct or unsafe service.

Officer Suicide Prevention

DOJ and HHS were directed to assess evidence and recommend suicide-prevention strategies.

The work included encouraging participation in the FBI Law Enforcement Suicide Data Collection while respecting privacy.

Accurate data are necessary because stigma and inconsistent reporting can conceal the scale of the problem.

Section 5—National Law Enforcement Accountability Database

Section 5 directed the Attorney General to establish NLEAD as a central repository concerning federal officer misconduct, commendations, and awards.

The database was intended to inform hiring, promotion, assignment, task-force participation, and other personnel decisions.

It was not designed as a complete public registry of every allegation against every American officer.

NLEAD Due Process and Privacy

The order required compliance with the Privacy Act and appropriate officer due-process protections.

Victim confidentiality, witness safety, sensitive operations, and accurate status information also mattered.

A database entry could represent different types of official action and required source-record verification.

NLEAD Record Categories

Qualifying records included convictions, enforcement-authority suspensions, misconduct-related termination, resignation or retirement during serious-misconduct investigation, sustained serious misconduct, and specified civil judgments.

The system also included commendations and awards associated with officers having qualifying records.

The categories did not include every complaint regardless of disposition.

Pointer-System Design

The federal privacy assessment described a system that could point an authorized user to the agency holding the underlying record.

The design reduced central duplication of complete personnel files.

Its usefulness depended on source agencies maintaining accurate records and responding to authorized requests.

NLEAD Launch

DOJ launched the National Law Enforcement Accountability Database on December 18, 2023.

It became one of the order's most concrete institutional products.

Its launch did not transform it into the public National Police Misconduct Registry proposed in congressional legislation.

First NLEAD Statistics

The first BJS report counted 4,790 qualifying incidents involving 4,011 federal law-enforcement officers for events from 2018 through 2023.

Approximately 63 percent were sustained serious-misconduct complaints or associated disciplinary records.

The report described qualifying records rather than the rate of all misconduct.

NLEAD Public Access

The order required DOJ to assess what information could appropriately be public.

Record-level access was principally restricted to authorised government users, while public reporting was aggregate and anonymised.

Transparency and officer privacy remained in tension.

Section 6—Use-of-Force Data

Federal agencies were directed to submit monthly data to the FBI National Use-of-Force Data Collection.

The directive covered deaths, serious bodily injuries, firearm discharges at persons, and zero-incident reports.

It sought to make federal participation mandatory within the executive branch.

Use-of-Force Data Elements

Required information included time, location, reason for contact, suspected offense, charges, subject and officer characteristics, type of force, injuries, and circumstances.

Detailed incident data allow analysis of patterns not visible in national totals.

Definitions, completeness, and participation determine reliability.

State and Local Use-of-Force Reporting

The Attorney General was directed to improve nonfederal participation through grants, training, technical assistance, and other lawful mechanisms.

The FBI collection remained dependent upon agency reporting.

The order could not compel every local agency through direct presidential command.

Publication and Coverage Limits

FBI publication practices depend on population coverage and data quality.

National totals can be withheld when participation is insufficient.

An accountability initiative should distinguish records submitted from representative national statistics.

Section 7—Chokeholds and Carotid Restraints

Federal agencies had ninety days to issue policies equivalent to or stronger than DOJ's September 2021 policy.

The policy generally prohibited chokeholds and carotid restraints except where deadly force was authorised by law.

Annual or continuing training was required.

Chokehold and Carotid Restraint

A chokehold generally applies pressure to the airway or throat.

A carotid restraint applies pressure intended to restrict blood flow to the brain.

Terminology varies, making agency definitions and medical effects important.

Deadly-Force Exception

The federal policy did not describe an absolute ban under every conceivable circumstance.

It permitted covered restraints when deadly force was legally authorised.

The exception reflected the classification of such restraints as potentially lethal tactics.

No Direct Nationwide Local Chokehold Ban

Section 7 directed federal agencies.

State and local adoption was encouraged through accreditation and grants.

The order did not itself enact a criminal or civil statutory ban governing every local department.

Section 8—Federal Use-of-Force Standards

Federal agencies were required to adopt policies equivalent to or stronger than DOJ's May 2022 use-of-force policy.

The policy emphasised valuing and preserving human life.

It addressed deadly force, de-escalation, intervention, medical aid, training, reporting, and accountability.

Deadly Force

The DOJ model allowed deadly force when an officer reasonably believed the subject posed an imminent danger of death or serious physical injury to the officer or another person.

The order did not enact the more restrictive statutory last-resort standard proposed in the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act.

Constitutional standards and agency policy remained separate sources.

De-escalation

Agencies were directed to train officers in de-escalation and employ it when feasible.

Techniques can include time, distance, cover, communication, tactical repositioning, warnings, and specialised resources.

Feasibility depends on the threat and circumstances.

Duty to Intervene

The DOJ policy imposed an affirmative duty to prevent or stop another officer's excessive or otherwise prohibited force when intervention was possible.

The duty addressed police culture as well as the primary officer's conduct.

Reporting and protection from retaliation are necessary for effective intervention systems.

Duty to Request or Render Medical Aid

The policy expressly required officers to request or render appropriate medical aid.

Medical response matters after firearms, restraints, conducted-energy weapons, chemical agents, positional restraint, or visible distress.

The duty is separate from whether the preceding force was lawful.

Annual Force Training

Federal agencies were directed to provide annual evidence-informed training consistent with the model policy.

Training should address law, tactics, medical risk, de-escalation, intervention, documentation, and supervision.

Completion records do not by themselves establish competent field performance.

Early-Warning and Risk-Management Systems

Federal agencies were directed to implement tools identifying problematic conduct and opportunities for intervention.

Indicators can include force, complaints, pursuits, injuries, lawsuits, reporting problems, or other events.

An alert is a management signal rather than proof of misconduct.

Accountability for Policy Violations

Agencies were required to use effective mechanisms for holding officers accountable for force-policy violations.

Administrative discipline remained subject to evidence, due process, collective bargaining, and agency rules.

A policy violation did not automatically establish criminal guilt.

Section 9—Anti-Bias Training and Guidance

OPM and DOJ were directed to develop evidence-informed training concerning implicit bias and improper profiling.

Protected characteristics included race, ethnicity, national origin, limited English proficiency, religion, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, and disability.

Federal officers were generally expected to complete training annually.

Bias and Profiling Complaints

Federal agencies were directed to maintain effective procedures for receiving, investigating, and responding to bias complaints.

The order therefore connected training to complaint accountability.

A credible system requires accessible intake, evidence, timely findings, and protection from retaliation.

Joint Task Forces and International Training

Federal agencies controlling joint task forces or international technical-assistance programs were directed to incorporate bias and profiling training.

This extended federal expectations to nonfederal officers participating in federally controlled activities.

The provision did not govern every action those officers took outside the federal program.

Updated Protected-Characteristics Guidance

DOJ and DHS updated federal guidance on use of race, ethnicity, gender, national origin, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, and disability.

The guidance expanded covered personnel and added disability.

It allowed consideration of protected characteristics only in limited, defined circumstances.

Section 10—No-Knock Entries

Federal agencies had sixty days to issue policies equivalent to or stronger than DOJ's September 2021 no-knock policy.

The policy sharply limited unannounced entry and provided guidance for safer announced entry.

Agencies were required to maintain records and issue annual public reports.

Judicially Authorized No-Knock Entries

Annual reports were to distinguish entries conducted pursuant to judicial authorization.

A judge's authorization did not eliminate agency-policy restrictions.

Officers could be prohibited by executive policy from using a tactic that constitutional law might permit.

Exigent-Circumstance Entries

Reports were also to identify unannounced entries based upon exigent circumstances.

Exigency can arise from immediate danger, escape, or destruction of evidence under governing law.

The policy required documentation rather than treating every tactical concern as automatic exigency.

No-Knock Injury Reporting

Public reports were to include injuries to officers and other people during no-knock entries.

Injury data support evaluation of tactical risk.

Counts require context concerning operation type, resistance, weapons, warrant validity, time, and reporting completeness.

Restriction Rather Than Universal Ban

Executive Order 14074 did not ban every federal or local no-knock entry.

It required federal policies equivalent to DOJ's restrictive model and public reporting.

Comprehensive national statutory prohibition would have required Congress or state legislation.

Section 11—Community Effects of Police Force

Section 11 required study of the physical, mental, and public-health effects of law-enforcement force.

It expressly included disparate effects upon communities of color.

The section treated force as a community-health issue as well as a legal incident.

Physical and Mental Health Effects

Police violence can affect injured people, witnesses, families, officers, neighbours, and communities.

Effects can include trauma, grief, fear, distrust, stress, avoidance of services, and public-health consequences.

The order directed HHS to study lawful and unlawful uses of force rather than assuming only unlawful force has community effects.

Mental-Health and Social Supports

DOJ, HHS, and OMB were directed to identify available and needed resources for people and communities affected by force.

Potential supports include trauma services, counseling, family assistance, community healing, and social services.

The order did not create an individual entitlement to a specific service.

Law-Enforcement–Community Dialogues

DOJ was directed to issue best-practice guidance for dialogues between law enforcement and communities, especially after deadly force.

Dialogue can improve communication and identify reforms.

It cannot substitute for investigation, disclosure, discipline, compensation, or legal accountability.

Official Death Notification

The order required best practices for timely, appropriate notification and support after deaths in custody.

Families should not learn of a death first through news, social media, or unofficial sources where avoidable.

Notification protocols must account for identification, next of kin, investigation, language, culture, and trauma.

Section 12—Military Equipment

Section 12 directed review and restriction of federal transfers and federally funded purchases of specified military equipment.

It reversed the policy direction of Executive Order 13809.

The section distinguished prohibited property from controlled property subject to safeguards.

Prohibited Equipment Categories

The order identified categories such as certain firearms and ammunition, grenade launchers, weaponised vehicles, bayonets, explosives, and specified unmanned aerial systems for restriction where law allowed.

The exact list and exceptions controlled implementation.

Ordinary protective gear and every vehicle were not categorically prohibited.

Controlled Equipment Safeguards

Other equipment could require justification, training, inventory, civilian-governing-body notice, community notice, and return conditions.

These requirements sought local democratic oversight of acquisition.

Equipment policy concerns both legitimate emergency capability and risk of militarized deployment.

Return After Civil-Rights Findings

Recipients could be required to return controlled equipment after a final judicial finding or DOJ determination of a civil-rights pattern or practice.

The rule connected federal material support to constitutional compliance.

Implementation depended upon program authority and final findings.

Section 13—Body Cameras and Advanced Technology

Section 13 required federal agencies to adopt body-camera policies equivalent to or stronger than DOJ's June 2021 model.

It also addressed facial recognition, biometric information, predictive algorithms, artificial intelligence, privacy, and civil liberties.

The technology section extended beyond recording devices.

Federal Body-Camera Scope

The underlying DOJ model focused initially on planned arrest and search-warrant operations where force could reasonably be anticipated.

Other agencies developed mission-specific policies.

The order did not require continuous recording of every federal employee throughout every workday.

Publication of Camera Policies

Federal agencies were directed to make body-camera policies public.

Publication allowed public comparison of activation, retention, access, privacy, and release rules.

A published policy still required implementation, funding, equipment, supervision, and auditing.

Camera Implementation Resources

Agencies were directed to identify resources needed for full implementation.

Costs include devices, replacement, storage, cybersecurity, redaction, discovery, staff, public requests, training, and maintenance.

A mandate without appropriations can remain partially implemented.

Serious-Injury and Death Footage

The DOJ model included a presumption favouring release of recordings depicting conduct resulting in serious bodily injury or death as soon as practical.

Release remained subject to law, privacy, investigation, security, victim interests, and redaction.

The order did not make every recording immediately public.

Officer Review of Body-Camera Footage

The order led to a DOJ study of whether officers should review recordings before reports or statements after force.

The January 2025 report identified two viable approaches: an initial perceptual account followed by review and supplementation, or review before the initial report.

It did not select one mandatory national rule.

Facial Recognition and Biometric Technology

The order required interagency assessment of facial recognition and other biometric technologies.

The review considered accuracy, demographic performance, privacy, civil liberties, governance, and law-enforcement utility.

DOJ published a biometric-technology report in December 2024.

Predictive Algorithms

Section 13 addressed predictive algorithms and advanced surveillance technologies.

These tools can influence deployment, investigation, identification, risk assessment, and resource allocation.

Bias, opacity, data quality, feedback loops, and constitutional constraints require independent governance.

Advanced Surveillance and Forensic Technology

The criminal-justice statistics working group was directed to assess acquisition, use, and oversight of advanced surveillance and forensic technologies.

The scope included commercial cyber-intrusion tools.

The objective was equitable, transparent, accountable, constitutional, and effective use.

Section 14—Behavioral-Health and Disability Response

DOJ and HHS were directed to issue guidance on emergency response involving behavioral-health crises and disability.

The section recognised that police are often asked to address problems outside traditional law-enforcement expertise.

It considered police, clinical, social-service, and community-based models.

Co-Responder Models

Co-responder programs pair officers with clinicians, social workers, emergency medical personnel, or other specialists.

They can improve assessment and access to services while retaining police capacity for safety threats.

Programs require clear dispatch, confidentiality, role, and risk protocols.

Alternative Responder Models

Alternative responders send civilian crisis teams to appropriate calls without police as the primary response.

The model seeks to reduce arrest and force when no serious safety threat exists.

Dispatch accuracy, backup, worker safety, service availability, and legal authority are essential.

Community Crisis Services

The order encouraged consideration of crisis centres, supported housing, assertive community treatment, peer support, and post-crisis services.

Response quality depends on a functioning health and social-service system.

Police reform alone cannot create adequate housing or treatment capacity.

Sedatives and Ketamine

The section specifically required assessment of risks associated with sedatives such as ketamine outside hospital settings.

Medical drugs can become part of coercive police encounters even when administered by health personnel.

Clinical authority, consent, monitoring, dosage, transport, and accountability require clear rules.

Section 15—Alternatives and Reentry

Section 15 established the Federal Interagency Alternatives and Reentry Committee.

The committee included departments responsible for justice, health, housing, labor, education, veterans, transportation, economic policy, and other services.

Its mission extended from avoiding unnecessary criminal-justice contact to successful return after incarceration.

Alternatives to Arrest and Incarceration

The committee was directed to identify strategies reducing unnecessary interactions, arrests, prosecution, detention, and incarceration.

Models can include diversion, treatment, civil responses, restorative justice, crisis services, and problem-solving programs.

Alternatives must be evaluated for safety, equity, access, voluntariness, and outcomes.

Reentry

Reentry policy concerns housing, employment, education, health, identification, benefits, family connection, transportation, and supervision after incarceration.

Barriers can increase instability and recidivism.

The order treated reentry as a whole-of-government responsibility.

Probation and Supervised Release

The order required review of resources and demographic outcomes for people on federal probation or supervised release.

It addressed revocations, modifications, reinstatement, sentence length, housing, employment, and education.

Technical violations and service access are central to supervision reform.

Interagency Strategic Plan

DOJ published a strategic plan titled Rehabilitation, Reentry, and Reaffirming Trust.

The plan coordinated alternatives, incarceration, and reentry goals.

A strategic plan guides executive action but does not itself create statutory rights or appropriations.

Section 16—Prisons and Jails

Section 16 addressed health, restrictive housing, federal detention conditions, and correctional practices.

Some COVID-19 provisions were tied to the duration of the federal public-health emergency.

Other directives concerned continuing conditions of confinement and legal obligations.

COVID-19 Measures

The order directed federal correctional authorities to continue appropriate masking, distancing, testing, vaccination, and alternatives to facility-wide lockdowns during the public-health emergency.

The federal COVID-19 public-health emergency later ended.

Time-limited health directives should not be described as permanent current requirements.

Restrictive Housing

DOJ was directed to examine and reduce restrictive housing, ensure lawful use, and implement standards and recommendations.

Restrictive housing includes forms of solitary or highly isolated confinement.

Legal, medical, disability, safety, disciplinary, and human-rights concerns overlap.

Bureau of Prisons and U.S. Marshals Service

The order addressed people held in BOP facilities and people detained for the Marshals Service.

Federal detainees can be housed in federal, state, local, or private facilities under different arrangements.

Oversight requires consistent standards across custody providers.

Conditions-of-Confinement Reports

DOJ issued reports concerning restrictive housing and federal detention conditions.

Completed reports remain part of the historical administrative record after revocation.

Their recommendations are not automatically current binding policy unless adopted through another authority.

Section 17—First Step Act

Section 17 directed continued implementation of the First Step Act.

The First Step Act is a federal statute and remained law independently of the executive order.

The order focused on staffing, programming, earned-time credits, risk assessment, disparities, and reporting.

Rehabilitative Programming and Staffing

The Attorney General was directed to report on programming staff, resources, prison staffing, and funding needs.

Earned-time systems depend upon actual access to approved programs.

A statutory opportunity can be ineffective if staffing or program capacity is inadequate.

PATTERN Risk Assessment

The order required analysis of the Prisoner Assessment Tool Targeting Estimated Risk and Needs.

It addressed risk categories, earned-time credits, recidivism outcomes, static and dynamic factors, and possible disparities.

Risk tools can support allocation while reproducing past inequalities if poorly designed.

The Order Did Not Create the First Step Act

Congress enacted the First Step Act in 2018.

Executive Order 14074 directed executive implementation and review.

Revocation of the order did not repeal the statute.

Section 18—Criminal-Justice Statistics

Section 18 addressed NIBRS participation and broader federal criminal-justice statistics.

It created a working group involving DOJ, OMB, the White House, data officials, and outside stakeholders.

The objective was more timely, complete, disaggregated, and publicly useful data.

National Incident-Based Reporting System

NIBRS collects detailed incident-level crime information and replaced the older Summary Reporting System as the national standard.

The order required a report on transition and recommendations for greater participation.

Agency participation and data quality remain essential to national estimates.

Victimization and Police-Contact Surveys

The order called for review of the National Crime Victimization Survey and Police-Public Contact Survey Supplement.

It sought more frequent information on force, injury, death, trust, and perceived bias.

Survey data can capture events never reported to police or entered in agency databases.

Working Group on Criminal Justice Statistics

The Equitable Data Working Group and National Science and Technology Council were directed to create a specialised criminal-justice statistics group.

The group could consult civil-rights, civil-liberties, privacy, defense, law-enforcement, and data experts.

Its work connected data governance with technology oversight.

Section 19—Accreditation

The Attorney General was directed to promote nonfederal law-enforcement accreditation.

DOJ would identify authorised independent credentialing bodies and connect accreditation to discretionary grants.

The order relied on institutional incentives rather than direct presidential licensing of local departments.

Independent Credentialing Bodies

DOJ standards required independent assessment rather than agency self-certification alone.

Accrediting bodies were expected to examine policies connected to recruitment, wellness, force, restraints, warrants, data, and accountability.

Independence, methodology, competence, and transparency determine accreditation value.

Accreditation Stakeholders

DOJ was directed to consult accreditation organisations, law enforcement, civil-rights groups, community organisations, civilian oversight, and accountability professionals.

Consultation was intended to prevent standards from reflecting only police or advocacy perspectives.

Stakeholder participation did not transfer final executive authority.

Limits of Accreditation

Accreditation indicates compliance with selected standards at a particular time.

It is not a judicial ruling that every officer acted lawfully.

An accredited agency can still experience misconduct, weak supervision, discriminatory outcomes, or policy violations.

Section 20—Grantmaking

Section 20 directed DOJ, HHS, DHS, and other agencies to review grants and promote adoption of order policies.

Federal grants became the principal lever for state and local influence.

Agencies had to act consistently with existing grant statutes and appropriations.

Discretionary Grant Preferences

Agencies could prioritise applicants adopting specified policies where grant law permitted.

Preferences could concern accreditation, force, cameras, data, crisis response, training, or accountability.

A preference differs from a statutory condition imposed by Congress.

Training and Technical Assistance

The order encouraged nonfinancial support including training, implementation guidance, data assistance, and model policies.

Technical assistance can be especially important for small, rural, tribal, and under-resourced agencies.

Assistance remains voluntary unless tied to an enforceable program condition.

Title VI Review

Federal agencies were directed to examine enforcement of Title VI nondiscrimination requirements in grant programs.

Title VI applies to recipients of federal financial assistance under statutory and regulatory rules.

The order did not create Title VI but sought stronger administrative oversight.

Safe Streets Act Nondiscrimination

DOJ also reviewed nondiscrimination requirements associated with the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act.

Law-enforcement grants can carry civil-rights obligations independent of the executive order.

Revocation did not repeal those statutes.

Section 21—Definitions

The order defined covered federal law-enforcement agencies and key accountability terms.

Definitions limited and clarified the database and implementation provisions.

They should not automatically be imported into unrelated statutes or agency policies.

Sustained Complaint

The order defined a sustained complaint or disciplinary record as an allegation upheld through a completed official proceeding.

A sustained record is stronger than an unadjudicated complaint.

It is still distinct from a criminal conviction or civil judgment.

Serious Misconduct

The order identified excessive force, bias, discrimination, obstruction of justice, false reports, false statements under oath, theft, and sexual misconduct.

The list guided NLEAD and related accountability actions.

Agency definitions and source proceedings remained important.

Section 22—Prior Executive Orders

Executive Order 14074 revoked Executive Orders 13809 and 13929.

It also superseded prior executive orders to the extent of conflict or overlap.

The provision changed executive policy concerning equipment transfers and safe-policing accreditation.

Executive Order 13809

Executive Order 13809 had restored state and local access to federal surplus equipment.

Executive Order 14074 reversed that direction and required renewed restrictions and safeguards.

Its later revocation did not expressly state that Executive Order 13809 was revived.

Executive Order 13929

Executive Order 13929, Safe Policing for Safe Communities, established earlier accreditation and grant policies after George Floyd's murder.

Executive Order 14074 revoked it and substituted a broader framework.

Executive Order 14148 later revoked 14074 but did not expressly reinstate 13929.

Section 23—General Provisions

Section 23 protected lawful agency authority and OMB functions.

It required consistency with law and available appropriations.

It disclaimed creation of privately enforceable rights.

Implementation Products

DOJ published reports, guidance, standards, strategic plans, policies, and fact sheets under the order.

The archived DOJ index lists products concerning cameras, biometrics, dialogues, accreditation, force, no-knock entries, crisis response, officer wellness, reentry, confinement, NIBRS, and custody deaths.

Each product has its own date, scope, legal status, and present relevance.

More Than Ninety Deliverables

DOJ described the order as containing more than ninety steps or deliverables.

The count reflects reports, guidance, policies, databases, working groups, assessments, and implementation actions.

Completion of a deliverable did not necessarily establish full operational compliance across every agency.

One-Year Implementation Actions

On May 25, 2023, DOJ announced ten additional reports and guidance documents.

These included profiling guidance, accreditation standards, officer wellness, crisis response, custody-death investigation, family notification, confinement, reentry, and data work.

The announcement demonstrated substantial administrative implementation before revocation.

May 2024 Implementation Update

DOJ issued another fact sheet and additional products in May 2024.

The update included credentialing standards, community-dialogue guidance, no-knock reporting, use-of-force community supports, recruitment guidance, and technology work.

The materials later moved to the DOJ archive.

Archived DOJ Materials

DOJ's Executive Order 14074 pages were placed in the department's archive after the change in administration.

Archived status means the pages document prior policy and implementation rather than necessarily state current department policy.

Researchers should cite the historical date and verify any claimed continuing rule.

Policies That Predated the Order

DOJ issued its chokehold and no-knock policy in September 2021, body-camera policy in June 2021, and updated force policy in May 2022.

Executive Order 14074 required other federal agencies to use those policies as models.

Because the source policies predated the order and may rest on separate authority, revocation of the order did not automatically answer their current status.

Agency Policies Adopted Under the Order

Federal agencies issued or revised force, camera, restraint, warrant, data, and profiling policies in response to the order.

A later President could direct rescission or replacement.

Absent specific action, lawyers and researchers must examine each current agency manual rather than assume uniform survival or automatic disappearance.

DOJ Inspector General Findings

The DOJ Inspector General reported that the department incorporated the updated use-of-force policy into formal guidance but had not fully formalised the chokehold and no-knock restrictions in the same manner.

The finding illustrated the difference between a memorandum, manual provision, executive order, and durable agency directive.

Administrative form affects permanence, training, auditing, and accountability.

Revocation by Executive Order 14148

President Donald J. Trump revoked Executive Order 14074 on January 20, 2025.

The revocation was part of a broad list of prior presidential actions rescinded on the first day of the new administration.

From that date, Executive Order 14074 no longer served as a binding presidential directive.

Review of Derivative Actions

Executive Order 14148 directed the Domestic Policy Council and National Economic Council to review federal actions taken under revoked orders.

They were directed to take necessary steps to rescind, replace, or amend derivative actions as appropriate.

This structure confirms that revocation did not automatically and identically dispose of every policy, database, grant, report, or regulation.

Revocation Did Not Repeal Statutes

The President could not repeal the First Step Act, Privacy Act, Title VI, Safe Streets Act, Death in Custody Reporting Act, or other acts of Congress by revoking an order.

Statutory duties continued according to their own terms.

The order's implementation emphasis could change without eliminating the law.

Revocation Did Not Vacate Court Orders

A presidential revocation does not automatically terminate federal consent decrees or judgments.

DOJ can seek modification or termination through the courts.

The court retains authority over its orders until lawful judicial action occurs.

Revocation Did Not Automatically Delete Records

Completed reports, databases, personnel records, camera footage, grant files, and Privacy Act systems remained subject to records law.

Deletion or retention depends on statutes, schedules, litigation holds, system notices, and agency action.

Historical records remain relevant to accountability and research.

NLEAD After Revocation

DOJ's current systems-of-record page continued to list the NLEAD system notice in 2026.

The continued listing indicates the federal records system had not simply vanished from the Privacy Act inventory.

Public official materials did not clearly establish that every former mandatory query, submission, and reporting rule continued unchanged.

Body-Camera Policies After Revocation

Some federal body-camera pages and policies remained publicly accessible after revocation.

Public accessibility does not alone prove that every requirement remained current and unchanged.

Current agency policy and later directives control.

Executive Order 14288

On April 28, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order 14288, Strengthening and Unleashing America's Law Enforcement to Pursue Criminals and Protect Innocent Citizens.

It established a markedly different policy emphasis centred on aggressive crime enforcement, officer legal protection, resources, equipment, compensation, and review of federal police-reform agreements.

It did not reinstate Executive Order 14074.

Officer Legal Resources Under Executive Order 14288

Executive Order 14288 directed creation of mechanisms for legal resources and indemnification for officers who unjustly incurred expenses or liabilities from official duties.

The directive reflected concern that officers could be unfairly targeted by local or state authorities.

Implementation remained subject to law and appropriations.

Review of Police Consent Decrees

Executive Order 14288 directed review of federal consent decrees, settlements, and post-judgment orders involving state or local law enforcement.

The administration sought modification or rescission of arrangements viewed as impeding law-enforcement functions.

Courts, not the President alone, control modification of judicial decrees.

Change in Federal Policy

Executive Orders 14074 and 14288 express different theories of the principal problem in policing.

The former emphasised trust, accountability, racial disparity, data, alternatives, and national standards.

The latter emphasised crime control, officer protection, legal support, operational freedom, equipment, and resources.

Arguments Supporting Executive Order 14074

Supporters argued that the President had a duty to lead federal law enforcement by example after congressional reform failed.

They viewed force standards, cameras, data, misconduct screening, crisis response, officer wellness, and reentry as mutually reinforcing public-safety reforms.

They also argued that federal grants should not subsidise unconstitutional or unaccountable policing.

Arguments Opposing Executive Order 14074

Opponents argued that the order federalised policing indirectly, promoted ideological concepts, burdened officers, restricted useful tactics and equipment, and exceeded appropriate executive influence.

Some objected to profiling, equity, and systemic-racism language.

Others argued that the order's legal disclaimers and reliance on guidance made it more symbolic than enforceable.

Civil-Rights Criticism

Some civil-rights advocates considered the order inadequate because it could not reform qualified immunity, Section 242's mental state, private damages remedies, or every local department.

Others criticised exceptions, slow implementation, restricted database access, incomplete data, and continued police funding.

The executive order represented reform within existing institutions rather than abolition or comprehensive congressional legislation.

Law-Enforcement Perspectives

Some police leaders supported clearer standards, wellness, recruitment, training, cameras, data, and crisis alternatives.

Others opposed restrictions, federal grant leverage, equipment limits, profiling guidance, or perceived second-guessing.

Law-enforcement organisations did not hold one uniform position.

African American Historical Significance

Executive Order 14074 was explicitly shaped by the national response to the murder of a Black man by police.

Its language recognised unequal effects of policing on Black and Brown communities and treated federal leadership as part of a longer civil-rights responsibility.

Its revocation demonstrated the fragility of reform resting primarily on presidential direction rather than statute.

Administrative Governance

The order illustrates how modern presidents govern through interagency committees, model policies, reports, grant criteria, data systems, procurement, personnel rules, and public guidance.

These tools can produce substantial change without a new statute.

They can also be altered rapidly when administrations change.

Durability of Executive Action

Executive action can be implemented quickly within existing authority.

It is generally less durable than legislation because a later President can revoke the directive.

Durability increases when policies become statutes, regulations, contracts, appropriations, court orders, personnel systems, or established agency practices.

Separation of Powers

Congress writes statutes and controls appropriations; the President supervises the executive branch; courts decide legal disputes.

Executive Order 14074 operated principally through presidential supervision and existing delegated authority.

Its disclaimer and repeated references to applicable law reflected those constitutional boundaries.

Measures of Implementation

Implementation could be measured through policy issuance, training, equipment deployment, data submission, report publication, accreditation, grant decisions, database use, crisis programs, prison reforms, and reentry resources.

A completed memorandum or report is an output rather than proof of improved public safety or civil rights.

Outcome evaluation requires long-term data.

Possible Outcome Measures

Possible measures include force and injury, public trust, complaints, officer safety, wellness, suicide, recruitment, retention, data completeness, misconduct screening, no-knock injuries, crisis outcomes, confinement conditions, and reentry success.

The order contained too many components for one aggregate success measure.

Each intervention requires its own evaluation design.

Conditions for Failure

The program could fail through inadequate appropriations, agency resistance, poor data, symbolic policies, weak training, incomplete grant leverage, privacy problems, political reversal, or lack of state and local adoption.

The 2025 revocation ended the coordinating presidential mandate before long-term evaluation was possible.

Some projects reached completion while others remained uncertain.

Research and Interpretation

Researchers should distinguish the order's text, agency implementation, archived guidance, completed reports, independent statutes, and later policy changes.

A requirement directed to federal agencies should not be described as a nationwide local mandate.

A completed deliverable should not be described as current policy without verification.

Current Status Through August 1, 2026

Executive Order 14074 remained revoked.

Executive Order 14288 supplied the current administration's principal general presidential directive concerning law-enforcement support and policy.

Archived implementation documents remained authoritative evidence of what the prior administration did, while current legal effect depended upon separate statutes, agency policies, regulations, records notices, grants, contracts, and court orders.

A federal agency had to issue a policy equivalent to or stronger than DOJ's model, train officers annually, use early-warning tools, and maintain accountability mechanisms.

Federal Body Camera

An agency had to adopt and publish a policy modelled on DOJ's camera requirements and identify implementation resources.

An authorised official could query NLEAD and seek source records concerning qualifying prior misconduct before hiring, promotion, assignment, or task-force participation.

Federal Prison Program

DOJ reviewed restrictive housing, health conditions, First Step Act implementation, staffing, programming, earned-time credits, and risk-assessment disparities.

Misinformation and Common Errors

“Executive Order 14074 Is Still in Force”

It was revoked on January 20, 2025.

“The Order Was an Act of Congress”

It was a presidential directive, not enacted legislation.

“It Enacted the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act”

The executive order could not enact the congressional bill or amend statutes requiring legislation.

“It Abolished Qualified Immunity”

It did not amend Section 1983 or abolish the judicial doctrine.

“It Changed the Criminal Standard in 18 U.S.C. § 242”

It directed improved training but did not replace the statutory willfulness standard.

“It Directly Controlled Every Local Police Department”

Mandatory policing provisions applied to federal agencies; nonfederal influence relied largely on grants, guidance, accreditation, equipment, and task forces.

“It Absolutely Banned Every Chokehold”

Federal policy generally prohibited chokeholds and carotid restraints except when deadly force was legally authorised.

“It Banned Every No-Knock Entry Nationwide”

It restricted federal entries and promoted nonfederal adoption; it did not rewrite every state warrant law.

“It Required Every Federal Officer to Record Every Moment”

Federal camera policies were mission- and event-specific and contained privacy, operational, and legal limitations.

“NLEAD Was a Public Search Engine for Every Police Officer”

It principally served authorised federal employment and assignment decisions and covered qualifying federal records.

“Every Complaint Was Entered as Proven Misconduct”

The database focused on defined official records, including sustained serious misconduct and other qualifying events.

“The Order Created the First Step Act”

Congress enacted the First Step Act in 2018; the order directed executive implementation and review.

“The Order Created Title VI and the Safe Streets Act”

Those statutes predated the order; it directed stronger grant oversight.

“Revocation Repealed Every Policy Mentioned in the Order”

Some policies predated the order or rested on separate authority and required individual review.

“Revocation Deleted NLEAD and All Records”

DOJ continued to list the NLEAD system notice in 2026; records remain governed by separate law and agency action.

“Revocation Automatically Reinstated Executive Orders 13809 and 13929”

Executive Order 14148 revoked 14074 but did not expressly state that the earlier orders were restored.

“Archived DOJ Reports Are Current Binding Rules”

They document implementation and may remain informative, but present legal force requires separate verification.

“Executive Order 14288 Continued the Same Policy”

It established a materially different law-enforcement policy direction.

“The Order Created a Private Right to Sue”

Section 23 expressly disclaimed privately enforceable rights.

“A Completed Deliverable Proved the Reform Worked”

Publication or policy adoption is an output; effectiveness requires outcome evidence.

Key Distinctions

Executive order
A presidential directive governing executive action within existing authority.
Statute
A law enacted through Congress and the constitutional legislative process.
Agency policy
An internal rule or directive adopted by a department or component.
Regulation
A legally promulgated agency rule issued under statutory authority.
Guidance
Agency advice or interpretation that may not carry the force of a legislative rule.
Grant condition
A requirement attached to receipt or eligibility for federal funds.
Accreditation
Independent organisational assessment against recognised standards.
Executive Order 14074
The 2022 Biden policing and criminal-justice directive.
Executive Order 14148
The January 2025 order revoking Executive Order 14074 and numerous other actions.

The April 2025 Trump directive emphasising law-enforcement support, resources, legal protection, and operational authority.
NLEAD
A federal accountability records system principally supporting authorised personnel decisions.
National Police Misconduct Registry
A broader public registry proposed in congressional legislation but not created by Executive Order 14074.
Use-of-force policy
An agency rule governing officer conduct.
Constitutional standard
The minimum legal rule applied by courts under the Constitution.
Chokehold
A restraint applying pressure to the airway or throat.
Carotid restraint
A restraint applying pressure intended to restrict blood flow to the brain.
No-knock entry
Entry without ordinary announcement of identity, authority, and purpose.
Co-responder
A response pairing law enforcement with a health or social-service professional.
Alternative responder
A civilian crisis team serving as the primary response in appropriate incidents.
Revocation
Termination of the executive order's force by a later presidential action.
Repeal
Legislative elimination of a statute; revocation of an executive order is not statutory repeal.

Historical and Continuing Significance

Executive Order 14074 was the most comprehensive presidential response to the police-reform movement following George Floyd's murder.

It created one framework connecting officer support, force, restraints, warrants, cameras, misconduct screening, data, technology, behavioral health, incarceration, reentry, accreditation, grants, and racial justice.

The order demonstrated the power of presidential administration to produce policies, databases, standards, and reports without waiting for Congress.

It also demonstrated the limits of that strategy: it could not amend major civil-rights statutes, could not directly command every local department, remained dependent upon appropriations and existing law, and could be revoked by a successor.

For African American history, the order represents federal recognition that police violence and criminal-justice disparities affecting Black communities required national executive action.

Its revocation and replacement by a substantially different law-enforcement agenda illustrate how quickly national policy can shift when reform is based upon executive direction rather than durable legislation.

Summary

Executive Order 14074 was President Biden's comprehensive May 25, 2022 directive on policing and criminal-justice administration.

It required federal agencies to adopt or strengthen policies concerning force, chokeholds, carotid restraints, no-knock entries, body cameras, anti-bias training, officer wellness, recruitment, misconduct screening, data, and advanced technology.

It established the National Law Enforcement Accountability Database, required federal use-of-force reporting, promoted independent custody-death investigations, and used grants and accreditation to encourage state, tribal, local, and territorial reform.

The order also addressed behavioral-health crisis response, alternatives to arrest, incarceration, reentry, federal prison conditions, the First Step Act, NIBRS, surveys, criminal-justice statistics, and nondiscrimination in federal grants.

Its mandatory policing rules applied to federal law-enforcement agencies. It did not directly command every local police department, amend Section 1983, abolish qualified immunity, or change Section 242's criminal standard.

DOJ reported more than ninety implementation steps and produced a substantial archive of reports, policies, standards, databases, and guidance between 2022 and January 2025.

President Trump revoked Executive Order 14074 through Executive Order 14148 on January 20, 2025.

Revocation ended the presidential directive but did not automatically repeal statutes, vacate court orders, delete records, cancel contracts, or rescind every independently authorised agency policy.

Executive Order 14288 established a substantially different law-enforcement policy direction in April 2025.

As of August 1, 2026, Executive Order 14074 remained revoked. Its historical influence survived through completed reports, institutional systems, agency practices, and the continuing national debate over whether police reform should rest on executive action or durable legislation.
