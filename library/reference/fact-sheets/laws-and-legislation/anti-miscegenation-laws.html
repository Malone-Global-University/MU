Anti-Miscegenation Laws

Colonial, state, and local laws that prohibited, voided, or punished marriages and intimate relationships across officially defined racial lines.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Marriage law, criminal law, racial-classification law, segregation law, and constitutional civil-rights issue.
Basic definition
Anti-miscegenation laws prohibited or invalidated marriages and, in some jurisdictions, cohabitation or sexual relationships between people assigned to different racial categories.
Preferred descriptive term
Interracial-marriage bans or racial marriage restrictions. “Miscegenation” is a historically loaded term created during the Civil War era.
Principal period
Seventeenth-century colonial America through 1967, with unenforceable provisions remaining in some state texts afterward.
Principal purpose
Maintain white supremacy, regulate racial identity, preserve hereditary racial boundaries, control family formation, and prevent legal recognition of interracial households.
Principal targets
Black-white marriages in Southern and many other states, with various jurisdictions also restricting marriages involving Native American, Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, “Mongolian,” “Malay,” or other classified groups.
Common legal mechanisms
Marriage-license denial, automatic invalidity, criminal penalties, banishment, refusal to recognize out-of-state marriages, cohabitation offenses, racial registration, and penalties for clerks or ministers.
Constitutional conflict
Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment.
Early Supreme Court precedent
Pace v. Alabama (1883) upheld a harsher interracial adultery and fornication law under an “equal application” theory.
First state high-court invalidation
Perez v. Sharp (California, 1948).
Interracial cohabitation ruling
McLaughlin v. Florida (1964) invalidated a law criminalizing cohabitation specifically for interracial unmarried couples.
Decisive national ruling
Loving v. Virginia (1967) unanimously invalidated racial marriage bans.
Number of states at the time of Loving
Virginia and fifteen other states retained laws prohibiting interracial marriage when the Supreme Court decided the case.
Historical significance
Anti-miscegenation laws made marriage a governmental instrument for defining race, maintaining slavery and segregation, and denying families equal legal recognition.

What Were Anti-Miscegenation Laws?

Anti-miscegenation laws were laws that separated people into racial categories and prohibited certain categories from marrying one another.

Some provisions also criminalized interracial cohabitation, adultery, fornication, or attempts to evade the marriage law by leaving the state.

These laws did more than regulate ceremonies. They determined whether a household existed legally and affected inheritance, legitimacy, custody, property, pensions, and public records.

Terminology

The term “miscegenation” referred to racial mixing through marriage, sexual relations, or reproduction.

The word was coined during the 1864 presidential campaign and circulated through political propaganda accusing Republicans and abolitionists of promoting interracial relationships.

The laws themselves were centuries older than the term.

“Interracial-marriage bans” is usually clearer and avoids repeating the racial ideology embedded in the historical label.

Race as a Legal Classification

Legislatures did not merely observe fixed racial categories; they created and revised them.

Statutes defined who counted as white, Black, mulatto, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, Mongolian, Malay, Filipino, or another category.

Classification could depend on ancestry, appearance, community reputation, records, surnames, or fractions of supposed racial “blood.”

Marriage law then gave those classifications practical consequences.

Colonial Origins

Restrictions on interracial marriage and sexual relationships appeared in British North American colonies during the seventeenth century.

Colonial governments were constructing hereditary racial slavery and distinguishing Europeans from Africans and Native Americans.

Marriage restrictions helped turn racial identity into a durable legal status inherited across generations.

Virginia's 1691 Law

Virginia enacted a law in 1691 providing for banishment of a white person who married a person classified as Black, mulatto, or Indian.

The law developed within a larger system regulating slavery, freedom, reproduction, and racial status.

Later Virginia statutes changed wording and penalties while preserving the basic prohibition.

Relationship to Slavery

Racial marriage restrictions arose alongside hereditary slavery.

They protected the legal separation between people classified as white and the enslaved or free Black population.

They also obscured the reality that interracial sexual relations occurred widely, including relationships produced through coercion and sexual violence by white enslavers.

The law punished or denied recognition to interracial families while the slave system permitted white men to exercise enormous power over enslaved women.

Status of Children

Colonial slavery increasingly assigned a child's free or enslaved status through the mother.

Children born to enslaved women could therefore be enslaved regardless of the father's identity.

Marriage bans prevented many interracial parents from forming legally recognized households and helped preserve racial and property boundaries.

Property and Inheritance

Legal marriage created rights involving inheritance, dower, support, legitimacy, property, and estate administration.

Declaring an interracial marriage void could prevent a surviving spouse or child from receiving those protections.

Racial marriage restrictions therefore protected white property distribution as well as social separation.

Family Legitimacy

A void marriage could cause children to be treated as born outside lawful wedlock under older legal rules.

Families could lose support, inheritance, parental presumptions, and public recognition.

The state used marriage validity to convert racial ideology into everyday family law.

After United States Independence

States inherited or enacted racial marriage restrictions after the Revolution.

The laws varied greatly by region, racial categories, penalties, and treatment of marriages performed elsewhere.

Some states repealed restrictions during the nineteenth century, while others strengthened them during Reconstruction, Jim Crow, or the early twentieth-century eugenics movement.

Regional Patterns

Southern states concentrated heavily on Black-white marriage as part of slavery and Jim Crow.

Western states often added restrictions directed at Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, “Mongolian,” or “Malay” people.

Some states also restricted marriage between white and Native American people.

The racial combinations prohibited in one state could remain lawful in another.

Asian American Targets

Western marriage laws developed alongside Chinese exclusion, alien land restrictions, immigration controls, and hostility toward Asian labor.

Legislatures used broad labels such as “Mongolian” or “Malay” that did not correspond consistently to personal identity or modern geography.

California expanded its restrictions over time to include additional Asian and Pacific groups.

Native American Targets

Colonial and state laws sometimes prohibited marriage between white and Native people.

Virginia's later racial-registration system threatened the legal identity of Native communities by classifying many people as “colored.”

Marriage law, birth registration, tribal identity, and the state's binary racial system became closely connected.

Marriage-License Denial

Marriage clerks could be prohibited from issuing licenses to couples placed in forbidden racial combinations.

Applicants might have to state their race, present records, or satisfy an official investigating ancestry.

Clerks or ministers could face penalties for authorizing or performing a prohibited marriage.

Automatic Invalidity

Some statutes declared prohibited marriages absolutely void without requiring a divorce or annulment proceeding.

The state could therefore deny that a marriage ever had legal existence.

This affected spouses, children, estates, insurance, pensions, and public records.

Out-of-State Marriages

Couples sometimes traveled to a jurisdiction where their marriage was lawful.

States differed over whether they would recognize such marriages after the couple returned.

Some laws specifically criminalized leaving the state to evade the prohibition and returning to live as husband and wife.

That provision became central in the prosecution of Richard and Mildred Loving.

Cohabitation Laws

Some states created separate crimes for interracial couples who lived together, occupied the same room, or engaged in sexual relationships.

These laws could punish conduct differently when the partners belonged to different races.

McLaughlin v. Florida invalidated one such racial cohabitation law in 1964.

Adultery and Fornication

States commonly regulated sexual relationships outside marriage.

Some imposed substantially harsher punishment when one partner was white and the other Black.

Alabama's law examined in Pace v. Alabama prescribed two to seven years of imprisonment or hard labor for interracial adultery or fornication.

Criminal Penalties

Penalties could include fines, jail, penitentiary sentences, hard labor, or banishment.

Officials could prosecute spouses, unmarried partners, ministers, or license officials.

The threat of prosecution also forced families to relocate, conceal relationships, or live without legal protection.

The “Equal Application” Argument

Defenders argued that interracial-marriage laws did not discriminate because both partners received the same punishment.

This reasoning ignored that the offense existed only because the partners had different racial classifications.

It also ignored that the statutes were designed to maintain white supremacy rather than regulate every marriage equally.

The Supreme Court accepted the argument in Pace but rejected it in McLaughlin and Loving.

Racial Recordkeeping

Marriage restrictions depended on government knowing or assigning each person's race.

Birth certificates, marriage applications, death certificates, census records, school records, and family histories became evidence.

Registrars could alter records or challenge a person's reported identity.

The result was a bureaucracy of racial surveillance.

Blood-Fraction Rules

Laws sometimes defined race by a fractional amount of ancestry, such as one-half, one-fourth, one-eighth, or one-sixteenth.

Other laws treated any identifiable Black ancestry as controlling.

These fractions had no stable scientific basis and changed with political objectives.

Family members with the same ancestry could be classified differently by different officials or statutes.

The “One-Drop” Principle

Some twentieth-century laws and officials attempted to classify anyone with any ascertainable Black ancestry as Black or “colored.”

This approach maximized the population excluded from whiteness while maintaining whiteness as a protected legal status.

Marriage law helped enforce that boundary across generations.

Eugenics and Racial Purity

Early twentieth-century eugenicists claimed that government should control reproduction to improve the population.

They combined racial hierarchy, pseudoscience, immigration restriction, sterilization policy, and marriage regulation.

Anti-miscegenation laws were presented as protections for “racial integrity,” heredity, public health, and social order.

These claims gave scientific language to longstanding white-supremacist policy.

Virginia Racial Integrity Act of 1924

Virginia's Racial Integrity Act prohibited a white person from marrying anyone not legally classified as white.

It required strict racial definitions and supported government recording of racial identity.

The law formed the central statutory scheme challenged in Loving v. Virginia.

Walter Ashby Plecker

Walter Plecker headed Virginia's Bureau of Vital Statistics from 1912 to 1946.

He promoted the Racial Integrity Act and enforced strict racial classification through birth and marriage records.

Plecker challenged Native identities and pressed local officials to classify many families as “colored.”

His administration demonstrates how a marriage ban depended on recordkeeping officials as well as police and courts.

The “Pocahontas Exception”

Virginia's definition of white permitted a person with one-sixteenth or less American Indian ancestry and no other nonwhite ancestry to remain legally white.

The exception accommodated prominent white Virginians who claimed descent from Pocahontas and John Rolfe.

It exposed the political selectivity of supposedly rigid racial science.

Damage to Indigenous Records

Virginia's binary racial administration erased or altered the recorded identity of many Native people.

Birth, marriage, and death records could classify tribal families as “colored.”

Descendant communities later faced difficulty documenting continuity and genealogy because official records denied their identities.

Social Enforcement

Marriage restrictions operated with segregation, racial etiquette, housing exclusion, employment discrimination, and violence.

Families could face threats, expulsion, boycotts, job loss, or mob attack even when no prosecution occurred.

The law signaled official approval for private prejudice and community surveillance.

Gender and Unequal Power

Statutes often described both partners as equally punishable, but racial and gender power were not equal.

Black women had historically been denied legal protection against sexual exploitation by white men under slavery and segregation.

White-supremacist politics simultaneously portrayed consensual interracial relationships as threats to white womanhood and racial purity.

Enforcement reflected these unequal social assumptions.

Religious Marriage and Civil Law

A church or minister might consider a couple morally and religiously eligible to marry while civil law denied a license.

Clergy could face legal penalties for performing prohibited ceremonies.

In Perez v. Sharp, the applicants also raised religious-liberty arguments because their church permitted their marriage.

Reconstruction and the Fourteenth Amendment

The Fourteenth Amendment established citizenship, due process, and equal protection after the Civil War.

Anti-miscegenation laws remained in force despite the amendment.

Courts initially treated marriage as a field of broad state authority and accepted the claim that equal penalties avoided unconstitutional discrimination.

Civil Rights Act of 1866

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared United States citizenship and protected specified civil rights without regard to race.

It did not immediately eliminate state racial marriage restrictions.

Disputes over whether marriage belonged among protected civil rights continued into the twentieth century.

Pace v. Alabama

Tony Pace, a Black man, and Mary Cox, a white woman, were convicted under Alabama law for living together in adultery or fornication.

The interracial offense carried a much harsher penalty than comparable same-race conduct.

In 1883, the Supreme Court upheld the convictions because the statute punished both interracial partners equally.

The decision became a major precedent supporting racial relationship laws.

Limitations of the Pace Reasoning

Pace focused on whether the two defendants received the same penalty.

It did not adequately examine why the conduct became a separate crime only when the participants belonged to different races.

Later equal-protection decisions rejected this narrow understanding.

California's Marriage Restrictions

California prohibited marriages between white persons and Black or mulatto persons from its first legislative session in 1850.

Later amendments extended the prohibition to people classified as Mongolian or Malay.

The law therefore combined Black-white segregation with anti-Asian policy.

Perez v. Sharp

Andrea Perez and Sylvester Davis sought a California marriage license.

California law classified Perez as white and Davis as Black and prohibited the marriage.

In 1948, the California Supreme Court invalidated the restrictions.

It became the first state high court to hold that racial marriage bans violated constitutional equality.

Importance of Perez

The court treated marriage as a fundamental right and required more than prejudice or tradition to justify racial interference.

It rejected arguments that social hostility toward children could justify laws perpetuating that hostility.

The case supplied an important precedent for later challenges, including the Lovings' appeal.

Naim v. Naim

Naim v. Naim involved a Virginia marriage between a white woman and an Asian man.

In 1955, Virginia's highest court upheld the state's racial marriage policy and described racial integrity as a legitimate state purpose.

The United States Supreme Court did not use the case to decide the national constitutionality of anti-miscegenation laws.

Virginia courts later relied on Naim in the Loving litigation.

McLaughlin v. Florida

Florida criminalized an unmarried Black-white couple's habitual nighttime cohabitation in the same room.

No identical offense applied to an unmarried same-race couple merely for the same conduct.

In 1964, the Supreme Court unanimously held the racial cohabitation statute unconstitutional under equal protection.

The Court expressly rejected the limited equal-application reasoning associated with Pace.

What McLaughlin Left Open

McLaughlin invalidated a racial cohabitation offense.

The Court did not yet decide the constitutionality of state marriage bans in that case.

That final question reached the Court through Richard and Mildred Loving.

Richard and Mildred Loving

Richard Perry Loving and Mildred Delores Jeter Loving grew up in Central Point, Caroline County, Virginia.

Richard was legally classified as white, while Mildred was classified under Virginia law as a “colored person” and had African American and Native American ancestry.

They married in Washington, D.C., on June 2, 1958, where their marriage was lawful.

Arrest and Conviction

After returning to Virginia, the Lovings were arrested and prosecuted under provisions barring interracial marriage and out-of-state evasion.

They pleaded guilty in January 1959.

The court imposed one-year prison sentences but suspended them on the condition that the couple leave Virginia and not return together for twenty-five years.

Banishment From Virginia

The suspended sentence forced the Lovings to live in Washington, D.C.

They could not safely return together to their home community and relatives in Virginia.

The punishment demonstrates how marriage restrictions regulated residence, family support, and attachment to place as well as legal status.

The Lovings' Legal Challenge

Mildred Loving wrote to Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy and was referred to the American Civil Liberties Union.

Attorneys Bernard Cohen and Philip Hirschkop challenged the convictions under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The case proceeded through Virginia courts and a related federal action before reaching the United States Supreme Court.

Virginia's Arguments

Virginia argued that both spouses were punished equally and therefore equal protection was satisfied.

The state also claimed broad authority to regulate marriage and defended the policy as preservation of racial integrity.

The Supreme Court recognized these justifications as expressions of white supremacy rather than legitimate neutral objectives.

Loving v. Virginia

The Supreme Court issued its unanimous decision on June 12, 1967.

Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote the opinion.

The Court reversed the Lovings' convictions and held Virginia's racial marriage statutes unconstitutional.

The ruling invalidated comparable laws throughout the United States.

Equal Protection Holding

The Court rejected the claim that equal punishment of both spouses cured the racial discrimination.

The statutes made race the basis of the offense and prohibited only marriages crossing designated racial lines.

Racial classifications required the most demanding constitutional scrutiny.

Maintaining white supremacy was not a permissible state objective.

Due Process Holding

The Court also held that the laws deprived the Lovings of liberty without due process.

Freedom to marry was described as a vital personal right and a basic civil right.

The choice of whom to marry belonged to the individual and could not be restricted solely because of race.

Racial Classifications and Strict Scrutiny

Loving treated explicit racial classifications as constitutionally suspect and subject to the most rigid review.

A state could not rely on tradition, social prejudice, or disputed pseudoscience.

The law had to serve a permissible overriding objective independent of racial discrimination, which Virginia's scheme did not.

States With Bans in 1967

The Supreme Court identified Virginia and fifteen other states with interracial-marriage prohibitions:

  • Alabama;
  • Arkansas;
  • Delaware;
  • Florida;
  • Georgia;
  • Kentucky;
  • Louisiana;
  • Mississippi;
  • Missouri;
  • North Carolina;
  • Oklahoma;
  • South Carolina;
  • Tennessee;
  • Texas;
  • Virginia; and
  • West Virginia.

Repeals Before Loving

Fourteen states had repealed their interracial-marriage laws during the fifteen years before the decision.

Maryland repealed its prohibition after the Loving litigation began and before the Supreme Court's ruling.

California's law had been judicially invalidated in 1948.

These changes showed a declining but still substantial legal regime when the Court acted nationally.

Immediate Legal Effect

States could no longer deny marriage licenses, void marriages, or prosecute couples because of the spouses' racial classifications.

Convictions based on the unconstitutional laws lost legal foundation.

State officials were required to recognize interracial marriages on the same legal basis as other marriages.

Unenforceable Text Remaining on the Books

Some state constitutions or codes retained obsolete racial marriage language after 1967.

Such language could no longer be enforced because the Supreme Court's constitutional ruling controlled.

Later repeal campaigns removed some of these symbolic remnants through legislation or referendum.

Family-Law Consequences

The end of racial marriage bans affected inheritance, legitimacy, divorce, spousal support, pensions, taxation, immigration, insurance, and custody.

Legal marriage opened access to benefits and responsibilities previously denied to interracial families.

Earlier families could still face record gaps, property loss, or disputes created under the former laws.

Race and Child Custody

After Loving, courts could not treat interracial marriage itself as unlawful.

The Supreme Court later held that a court could not remove a child from a parent merely because the parent entered an interracial marriage and others might react with prejudice.

Private bias could not justify governmental racial discrimination.

Military and International Marriages

American service members who married abroad could face uncertainty about whether their home states would recognize spouses classified as Asian or nonwhite.

Military and immigration administration therefore interacted with state marriage restrictions.

Regional differences made family status depend partly on domicile and state law.

Immigration and Naturalization

Racial marriage bans intersected with federal laws restricting immigration and naturalization by race.

Asian immigrants and their American-born descendants could confront both federal exclusion and state marriage barriers.

Marriage did not operate as an equal route to citizenship or family unity when racial eligibility rules controlled immigration.

Political and Cultural Propaganda

Opponents of abolition and civil rights repeatedly warned that racial equality would produce interracial marriage.

Political cartoons and pamphlets used imagined interracial couples to inflame white fear.

This propaganda connected equal citizenship with the supposed loss of white racial and sexual control.

Private Prejudice After Loving

The decision ended legal prohibitions but did not eliminate family opposition, housing discrimination, harassment, violence, or social stigma.

Interracial couples continued to navigate prejudice after gaining equal marriage rights.

Formal equality and social acceptance developed at different rates.

Constitutional Legacy of Loving

Loving became a major precedent concerning racial classifications and the fundamental right to marry.

Later courts cited its equal-protection and liberty reasoning in cases involving marriage, family autonomy, and intimate choice.

Its central holding remains that government may not restrict marriage solely because of race.

Loving Day

June 12, the date of the Supreme Court decision, is widely commemorated as Loving Day.

Observances celebrate interracial families, marriage equality, and the Lovings' legal victory.

The commemoration also provides an opportunity to study the wider racial-classification system the case dismantled.

Research Cautions

Laws differed by state, date, racial category, degree of ancestry, and whether the marriage occurred inside or outside the jurisdiction.

“Interracial marriage” did not identify the same prohibited combinations everywhere.

A marriage listed as void may nevertheless have been treated differently for inheritance, children, or another limited purpose.

Official racial labels should be reported as historical legal language rather than accepted as accurate descriptions of identity.

The absence of prosecution does not prove that a law lacked coercive effect.

Common Misconceptions

“Anti-Miscegenation Laws Began During Jim Crow”

Their origins reached back to seventeenth-century colonial slavery, although many were strengthened during Jim Crow and the eugenics era.

“The Word ‘Miscegenation’ Is an Ancient Legal Term”

The word was coined during the Civil War era; the laws predated it by centuries.

“The Laws Applied Only to Black and White People”

Many jurisdictions also restricted marriages involving Native American, Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, or other classified groups.

“Race Was Obvious and Consistently Defined”

States used conflicting ancestry fractions, appearance, records, family reputation, and administrative judgment.

“The Laws Only Prevented Wedding Ceremonies”

They also voided marriages, criminalized cohabitation, punished evasion, and affected inheritance, custody, pensions, and children.

“A Marriage Legal in One State Was Valid Everywhere”

Some states refused recognition when residents traveled elsewhere to evade local law.

“Both Partners Were Treated Equally, So the Laws Were Not Discriminatory”

The offense existed only because of racial classification and was designed to preserve white supremacy.

“Pace v. Alabama Struck Down Interracial Relationship Laws”

It upheld Alabama's harsher interracial adultery and fornication statute in 1883.

“Loving Was the First Court Ever to Reject an Interracial-Marriage Ban”

California's highest court invalidated its law in Perez v. Sharp in 1948.

“McLaughlin v. Florida Decided the Marriage Question”

It invalidated a racial cohabitation law; Loving resolved the marriage bans nationally.

“The Lovings Were Married Illegally Everywhere”

Their marriage was lawful in Washington, D.C.; Virginia criminalized their return and cohabitation as spouses.

“The Lovings Received an Immediate Unconditional Release”

Their prison terms were suspended on condition that they leave Virginia and not return together for twenty-five years.

“Loving Relied Only on Equal Protection”

The Court relied on both Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment.

“The 1967 Decision Removed Every Ban From Printed State Law Immediately”

The provisions became unenforceable, but obsolete text remained in some codes or constitutions until later repeal.

“Ending the Laws Ended All Opposition to Interracial Families”

Private discrimination, stigma, family hostility, and violence continued after legal invalidation.

Key Distinctions

Anti-miscegenation law
A historical law prohibiting or punishing marriage or intimate relationships across defined racial lines.
Interracial-marriage ban
A clearer modern description of a law preventing people classified as different races from marrying.
Marriage-license restriction
A rule forbidding an official from issuing a license to a prohibited couple.
Void marriage
A marriage treated by law as having no legal validity.
Voidable marriage
A marriage remaining legally effective until a court annuls it.
Cohabitation law
A law punishing people for living together in an intimate relationship.
Equal application
The rejected argument that a racial law is nondiscriminatory when it punishes both interracial partners equally.
Racial integrity
A white-supremacist and eugenic concept claiming that government should preserve a supposedly pure racial population.
Blood fraction
A legal formula classifying race according to a claimed percentage of ancestry.
Pocahontas exception
Virginia's allowance treating a limited amount of American Indian ancestry as compatible with legal whiteness.
Equal Protection Clause
The Fourteenth Amendment guarantee prohibiting invidious state racial discrimination.
Due Process Clause
The Fourteenth Amendment protection of liberty, including the fundamental freedom to marry.
Strict scrutiny
The most demanding judicial review applied to explicit racial classifications.
Comity
A jurisdiction's recognition of legal acts, such as marriages, validly performed elsewhere.
Loving Day
The June 12 commemoration of Loving v. Virginia and interracial marriage equality.

Why Anti-Miscegenation Laws Matter

These laws demonstrate that race was created and enforced through family law as well as slavery, voting restrictions, schools, and public segregation.

They used the licensing power of the state to decide which families could legally exist.

By voiding marriages, they affected inheritance, property, legitimacy, custody, support, pensions, and citizenship.

Their colonial origins reveal the connection between racial slavery, reproduction, and hereditary status.

Their application to Black, Native American, Asian, and other communities shows that racial classification varied by regional political needs.

Virginia's Racial Integrity Act illustrates how eugenics and vital-statistics bureaucracy turned racial ideology into official records.

Pace demonstrates how equal-treatment language can conceal discrimination when the law creates an offense through race.

Perez shows the important role of state constitutional courts in developing civil-rights protections.

McLaughlin dismantled the equal-application theory before the marriage issue reached the Court.

Loving joined racial equality with the fundamental freedom to marry.

The history shows that removal of legal barriers is necessary but does not immediately eliminate private hostility or accumulated family harm.

The subject remains central to constitutional equality, family autonomy, racial classification, Indigenous records, Asian American history, civil rights, and the relationship between private life and state power.

Summary

Anti-miscegenation laws prohibited or invalidated marriages and intimate relationships across government-defined racial lines.

Their origins reached back to seventeenth-century colonial America and the construction of hereditary racial slavery.

The term “miscegenation” itself was coined much later during the 1864 presidential campaign as racial political propaganda.

The laws protected white supremacy by regulating family formation, inheritance, legitimacy, residence, and reproduction.

Southern laws concentrated on Black-white relationships, while Western states frequently added restrictions aimed at Asian and Pacific peoples.

Native American communities were also affected through marriage restrictions and official racial reclassification.

States denied licenses, declared marriages void, criminalized cohabitation, punished ministers, and prosecuted couples who married elsewhere and returned home.

Racial classification depended on ancestry fractions, public reputation, appearance, vital records, and discretionary officials.

Virginia's 1924 Racial Integrity Act combined a marriage ban with strict government registration of racial identity.

Walter Plecker's enforcement damaged Indigenous records and helped create a binary system of “white” and “colored” identity.

In Pace v. Alabama in 1883, the Supreme Court upheld harsher punishment for interracial adultery or fornication under an equal-application theory.

The California Supreme Court rejected its state's racial marriage law in Perez v. Sharp in 1948.

Virginia's highest court upheld racial marriage policy in Naim v. Naim in 1955.

In McLaughlin v. Florida in 1964, the Supreme Court invalidated a racial cohabitation offense and rejected Pace's limited reasoning.

Richard and Mildred Loving married lawfully in Washington, D.C., in 1958 and returned to Virginia.

Virginia convicted them and suspended one-year prison sentences on the condition that they leave the state for twenty-five years.

Their attorneys challenged the convictions under the Fourteenth Amendment.

On June 12, 1967, the Supreme Court unanimously invalidated Virginia's laws in Loving v. Virginia.

The Court held that equal punishment did not cure a law built on racial classification and white supremacy.

It also held that freedom to marry is a fundamental liberty protected by due process.

Virginia and fifteen other states retained racial marriage bans when the decision was issued.

Loving made those provisions unenforceable nationwide, although obsolete text remained in some state documents until later repeal.

The decision ended legal prohibition but did not immediately end private prejudice or repair the family, property, and recordkeeping harms created over centuries.

Anti-miscegenation laws remain essential to understanding slavery, Jim Crow, eugenics, racial identity, family law, equal protection, due process, and the relationship between private life and government power.
