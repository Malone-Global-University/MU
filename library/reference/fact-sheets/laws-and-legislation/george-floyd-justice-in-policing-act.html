George Floyd Justice in Policing Act

A series of comprehensive federal police-reform bills introduced after George Floyd's murder to expand legal accountability, restrict specified police practices, improve data and transparency, establish national standards, and condition federal grants upon state and local reform—none of which had become law through August 1, 2026.

What Is the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act?

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act is the common title used for several related but legally separate congressional bills.

Each Congress begins a new legislative cycle. A bill that does not become law expires at the end of that Congress and must be reintroduced under a new number.

The 2020, 2021, 2024, and 2025 measures therefore form a legislative family rather than one continuously pending legal instrument.

A Bill, Not an Existing Comprehensive Act

The word “Act” appears in the proposed short title, but the measure does not become an act of Congress unless both chambers pass identical text and the President signs it or Congress overrides a veto.

House passage in 2020 and 2021 did not make those bills federal law.

Descriptions should use “the bill would” rather than “the law requires” when discussing provisions that were never enacted.

George Floyd

George Floyd was a 46-year-old Black man killed during an arrest in Minneapolis on May 25, 2020.

Officer Derek Chauvin pressed his knee on Floyd's neck and upper back while Floyd was handcuffed, prone, and repeatedly stated that he could not breathe.

Bystander video documented the killing and contradicted the initial police department description.

Murder and Criminal Cases

A Minnesota jury convicted Chauvin of second-degree unintentional murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

Chauvin and the other involved former officers also faced federal civil-rights proceedings.

Those prosecutions concerned individual criminal responsibility and are legally distinct from the proposed national legislation.

National Protest Movement

Floyd's murder produced demonstrations in all fifty states and internationally.

Protesters demanded prosecution, limits on police force, changes in police budgets and responsibilities, stronger civil remedies, racial justice, and institutional accountability.

The bill emerged within that broader movement but did not represent every reform proposal advanced by protesters.

Other Deaths Reflected in the Bill

The legislation responded to a longer history of police killings and abuse involving Black Americans and other communities.

Its no-knock warrant provisions were associated especially with Breonna Taylor's death, while its chokehold provisions incorporated an Eric Garner–named subsection.

The bill's content should not be reduced solely to the facts of Floyd's murder.

Federal and Local Policing Structure

The United States contains thousands of federal, state, local, tribal, territorial, campus, transit, school, and special-jurisdiction law-enforcement agencies.

Congress has direct authority over federal agencies but relies heavily on spending, civil-rights, commerce, and constitutional enforcement powers when influencing state and local policing.

This structure explains why many provisions combine direct federal rules with conditions on grants.

The Legislative Family

The major House versions are H.R. 7120 in 2020, H.R. 1280 in 2021, H.R. 8525 in 2024, and H.R. 5361 in 2025.

The 2024 Senate version was S. 4991.

The versions share a common architecture but must be cited separately because sponsors, dates, text, appropriations, and legal effects can differ.

H.R. 7120—2020 Version

Representative Karen Bass introduced H.R. 7120 on June 8, 2020, two weeks after Floyd's murder.

The House Judiciary Committee reported an amended bill.

The proposal combined civil-rights enforcement, qualified-immunity reform, national data systems, profiling restrictions, force standards, body cameras, and grant conditions.

House Passage in 2020

The House passed H.R. 7120 on June 25, 2020.

The recorded vote was 236 in favor and 181 against.

House passage established an official chamber position but did not send an enacted statute into force.

Senate Outcome in 2020

The Senate received H.R. 7120 and placed it on the legislative calendar.

The Senate did not pass the House bill before the 116th Congress ended.

A separate Republican policing proposal also failed to advance through the Senate's procedural threshold, leaving no comprehensive agreement.

H.R. 1280—2021 Version

Representative Bass introduced H.R. 1280 on February 24, 2021.

The measure substantially renewed the structure of the 2020 House bill.

Congress.gov's CRS summary described reforms involving criminal enforcement, qualified immunity, pattern-or-practice investigations, profiling, force, warrants, restraints, misconduct records, and training.

House Passage in 2021

The House passed H.R. 1280 on March 3, 2021.

The vote was 220–212.

The bill was received in the Senate on March 9 but did not receive Senate passage.

Partisan Voting Pattern

Both House passages occurred largely along party lines.

The 2021 roll call recorded 219 Democratic votes and one Republican vote in favor, with two Democrats and 210 Republicans opposed.

The voting pattern reflected deep disagreement over liability, force, federal authority, and the proper scope of national police reform.

Bipartisan Senate Negotiations

Senators Cory Booker and Tim Scott and Representative Karen Bass participated in negotiations seeking a bipartisan policing agreement.

Negotiators discussed qualified immunity, officer accountability, misconduct records, accreditation, force, grants, and other issues.

The negotiations ended without a final bill in September 2021, and each side publicly blamed the other for the failure.

Expiration of H.R. 1280

H.R. 1280 did not carry automatically into the next Congress.

When the 117th Congress ended, the bill expired.

Later sponsors had to introduce new measures even when using substantially similar text.

H.R. 8525—2024 House Version

Representative Sheila Jackson Lee introduced H.R. 8525 on May 23, 2024.

It was referred to the House Judiciary, Armed Services, and Energy and Commerce Committees and later to an Energy and Commerce subcommittee.

Congress.gov listed the bill at the introduced stage rather than as passed.

S. 4991—2024 Senate Version

Senator Cory Booker introduced S. 4991 on August 1, 2024.

The Senate read the bill twice and referred it to the Judiciary Committee.

It remained introduced when the 118th Congress ended.

2024 Reintroduction Context

Sponsors connected the 2024 reintroduction to continuing police killings and the death of Sonya Massey in Illinois.

The reintroduction demonstrated that the proposal remained part of national debate after the immediate 2020 protest period.

It did not receive committee or floor passage.

H.R. 5361—2025 House Version

Representative Glenn Ivey introduced H.R. 5361 on September 15, 2025.

The bill was referred to the House Judiciary, Armed Services, and Energy and Commerce Committees.

The introduced text retained the broad structure of earlier versions while updating dates, appropriations, and component provisions.

Current Legislative Status

Congress.gov listed H.R. 5361 as introduced.

Its latest listed action remained the September 15, 2025 committee referral.

As of August 1, 2026, it had not passed the House, passed the Senate, reached the President, or become law.

No Completed CRS Summary for H.R. 5361

Congress.gov stated that a formal summary of H.R. 5361 was still in progress on the retrieved bill page.

The detailed description in this fact sheet therefore relies principally on the introduced bill text itself.

Where the 2025 text repeats earlier House-passed provisions, earlier CRS summaries provide useful historical context but do not replace the current text.

Five-Title Structure

Title I addresses police accountability in courts and institutional oversight.

Title II addresses misconduct and force data.

Title III addresses profiling, training, force, tactical practices, military equipment, innovation, and cameras.

Title IV addresses sexual acts under color of law, and Title V contains severability and savings provisions.

Defined Terms

The bill defines federal, local, and tribal law-enforcement officers, states, local governments, deadly force, use of force, less-lethal force, and facial recognition.

Its definition of deadly force includes firearms, restraints restricting blood or oxygen flow to the brain, and multiple electronic-control-weapon discharges.

Definitions determine the reach of later sections and should be read before applying a provision to a particular incident.

Existing 18 U.S.C. § 242

Section 242 criminalizes willful deprivation of federal rights under color of law.

It applies to police officers and other public officials rather than only to law enforcement.

The willfulness standard requires proof of a specific culpable state of mind and has long been viewed by reformers as difficult to establish.

Proposed Mens Rea Change

Section 101 would replace “willfully” with “knowingly or recklessly.”

A knowing or reckless standard would allow federal prosecution without proving the current willfulness formulation.

The proposal would materially alter criminal exposure and therefore became one of the bill's most contested provisions.

Meaning of Recklessness

Recklessness generally concerns conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, but precise interpretation depends on statutory text and criminal-law doctrine.

The bill would not eliminate the need to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

It would not make every mistaken, negligent, or policy-violating act a federal felony.

Death-Causation Language

The bill would state that an act results in death when it is a substantial factor contributing to the person's death.

This language addresses cases involving multiple contributing causes.

Prosecutors would still have to prove the statutory deprivation, required mental state, causation, and other elements.

Removal of the Section 242 Death Penalty

The 2025 text would strike Section 242's language authorizing a death sentence where death results.

Other imprisonment penalties would remain according to the amended statute.

The change should be distinguished from state homicide sentencing and unrelated federal capital statutes.

Existing Qualified Immunity

Qualified immunity is a judicial doctrine affecting damages claims against government officials.

In Section 1983 litigation, an officer may receive immunity unless existing law placed the constitutional violation beyond reasonable debate.

The doctrine does not prevent criminal prosecution, employer discipline, injunctive relief in every circumstance, or municipal liability under separate rules.

Proposed Qualified-Immunity Reform

Section 102 would amend Section 1983 and address actions against federal investigative or law-enforcement officers.

It would prevent specified officers from using good faith, a reasonable belief in legality, lack of clearly established law, or uncertainty in the law as an immunity or defense.

Plaintiffs would still have to establish an underlying legal violation, causation, standing, damages, and procedural requirements.

Scope of the Qualified-Immunity Provision

The text is directed to local law-enforcement officers and federal investigative or law-enforcement officers as defined by statute.

It is not written as a complete abolition of qualified immunity for every government employee.

The provision also does not automatically make a municipality liable for every officer violation.

Arguments Supporting Qualified-Immunity Reform

Supporters argue that people whose rights are violated should not lose solely because no prior court decided a materially similar case.

They contend that damages liability deters misconduct and reinforces constitutional accountability.

They also argue that indemnification commonly means governments, rather than individual officers, pay many judgments.

Arguments Opposing Qualified-Immunity Reform

Opponents argue that officers must make rapid decisions under uncertain law and should not face ruinous litigation for reasonable mistakes.

They warn that increased exposure could harm recruitment, retention, initiative, and public safety.

Some critics prefer employer liability, insurance, indemnification reform, or narrower changes rather than officer-focused liability.

Existing Pattern-or-Practice Authority

34 U.S.C. § 12601 authorizes the Attorney General to seek equitable and declaratory relief against governmental law-enforcement patterns or practices violating federal rights.

The existing statute is institutional and civil rather than a personal damages remedy.

It remained law independently of the proposed bill.

DOJ Subpoena Authority

H.R. 5361 would give the Attorney General administrative subpoena authority in Section 12601 investigations.

The authority would reach documents, electronically stored information, tangible evidence, and witness testimony.

A federal district court could enforce a subpoena after refusal or noncompliance.

Prosecutorial Patterns and Practices

The bill would add prosecutors to the language concerning covered conduct.

This would extend the statutory framework beyond police agencies to qualifying prosecutorial patterns or practices.

Application would require careful separation of advocacy, charging discretion, immunity doctrines, and systemic federal-rights violations.

State Attorney General Actions

The bill would authorize state attorneys general or designated state officials to bring federal civil actions to eliminate covered patterns or practices within their states.

State officials would receive comparable subpoena authority.

Federal DOJ authority would remain available even when a state action exists.

State Pattern-or-Practice Grants

The bill would authorize grants to help states conduct pattern-or-practice investigations.

The 2025 text authorizes $100 million annually for fiscal years 2026 through 2028 for that program.

Authorization does not itself provide money; Congress would still need to make appropriations available.

Collective-Bargaining and Consent-Decree Provision

The bill would restrict Byrne or COPS grant funds to local agencies entering or renewing contracts that prevent federal pattern-or-practice relief or conflict with consent-decree terms.

The provision directly connects federal grants, police-union contracts, and constitutional reform.

It would not abolish police collective bargaining generally.

Independent Investigations of Deadly Force

Section 104 defines independent investigation broadly.

Models include state-attorney-general investigation, independent prosecutors, civilian review bodies, automatic referral, and agreements assigning investigation to another agency.

The purpose is to reduce conflicts when a local agency or regular prosecutor investigates officers with whom it routinely works.

Independence Does Not Predetermine Outcome

An independent investigation may result in charges, declination, administrative findings, policy recommendations, or insufficient evidence.

Independence concerns institutional relationship and public confidence rather than a requirement to prosecute.

Criminal proof remains beyond a reasonable doubt.

Grants for Independent Investigation Systems

The bill would support jurisdictions establishing independent structures for police deadly-force cases.

Grant design can influence prosecutorial organisation without directly federalizing every state homicide investigation.

States would retain substantial authority over criminal codes and charging procedure.

Accreditation Analysis

The Attorney General would review existing accreditation standards and methods used nationwide.

Accreditation is a professional compliance system rather than a criminal or civil judgment.

The bill treats accreditation as a mechanism for establishing minimum organisational practices.

Uniform Accreditation Standards

DOJ would develop standards concerning use of force, misconduct investigation, civilian review, bias, hiring, training, supervision, data, and other agency practices.

Stakeholder consultation would include law enforcement, unions, civil-rights organisations, civilian-oversight professionals, and community representatives.

Accreditation would not by itself prove that every officer action is constitutional.

Accreditation and Federal Grants

Federal grants would encourage or require movement toward approved standards.

Smaller agencies could need financial and technical assistance to comply.

Critics warn that national accreditation can become expensive or bureaucratic; supporters argue that fragmented local standards leave major accountability gaps.

Law Enforcement Trust and Integrity Grants

The bill would authorize grants supporting accreditation, community trust, misconduct review, recruitment, training, and data systems.

Grant programs would operate through DOJ and existing federal public-safety funding structures.

Eligibility and enforcement would depend on regulations and appropriations after enactment.

National Task Force on Law-Enforcement Oversight

The bill would establish a national task force to examine law-enforcement oversight and accountability.

The body would include varied institutional and community perspectives.

A task force can recommend standards but cannot replace legislation, agency rulemaking, courts, or local implementation.

Federal Data on Law-Enforcement Practices

The Attorney General would collect information concerning federal law-enforcement practices.

Potential subjects include stops, searches, arrests, force, complaints, and demographic information.

Federal reporting is important because state and local grant conditions do not govern every federal agency.

National Police Misconduct Registry

The bill would require DOJ to establish the registry within 180 days after enactment.

The registry would cover federal and local law-enforcement officers as defined in the bill.

It would be materially broader and more public than a decertification-only index.

Complaint Categories in the Registry

The registry would distinguish credible or disciplined complaints, pending complaints, and complaints ending in exoneration, unfounded findings, or not-sustained findings.

Use-of-force and racial-profiling complaints would be separately identifiable.

This structure attempts to preserve procedural status rather than presenting all allegations as proven.

Discipline and Termination Records

The registry would contain disciplinary and termination records and reasons for termination.

A final employment action is different from criminal conviction or decertification.

Appeals, arbitration, reinstatement, and correction procedures would need accurate integration.

Certification Records

The registry would include records connected to officer certification.

Certification action can include revocation, suspension, surrender, or other state licensing consequences.

A state certification system remains legally distinct from a local employer's personnel decision.

Lawsuits and Settlements

The bill would include lawsuits against officers and settlements of those lawsuits.

A filed lawsuit contains allegations, while a settlement may occur without an admission or judicial finding.

A responsible database must label procedural status to avoid equating payment with adjudicated misconduct.

Resignation or Retirement Under Investigation

The registry would include instances in which an officer resigns or retires while under an active use-of-force investigation.

The record would not itself prove the allegation.

It would alert future employers that a serious matter remained unresolved when the officer departed.

Public Searchability

The proposal describes a national public registry rather than only a restricted hiring index.

Public access would support journalism, research, civil litigation, community oversight, and hiring scrutiny.

Privacy, victim protection, due process, identity matching, correction, and cybersecurity would require detailed regulations.

Preemployment Database Checks

Agencies receiving specified grants would have to certify that they reviewed relevant misconduct and certification records before hiring.

A database match would prompt further investigation rather than automatically establish disqualification.

No match would not prove a completely clean history because reporting systems can remain incomplete.

Relationship to Existing Databases

The proposed registry should be distinguished from the National Decertification Index and the federal National Law Enforcement Accountability Database.

Those systems have different coverage, access, definitions, and purposes.

The bill sought a broader national accountability structure rather than merely renaming an existing database.

PRIDE Act Subtitle

The Police Reporting Information, Data, and Evidence Act provisions would create extensive use-of-force reporting requirements.

The subtitle addresses collection, public reporting, compliance, audits, and federal agency participation.

Data quality would determine whether the system produces meaningful national statistics.

Reportable Use-of-Force Events

The text includes force resulting in death or serious injury, firearm discharges, force against vulnerable groups, deaths in custody, and force during arrest or booking.

States and tribes would establish systems requiring officers and agencies to report qualifying incidents.

Definitions must be applied consistently across jurisdictions.

Incident-Level Information

Required information would include date, time, location, officer and civilian characteristics, the type of force, the police objective, resistance, and surrounding circumstances.

Reports would address de-escalation efforts and reasons alternatives were not used where applicable.

Incident-level data allow more analysis than aggregate annual totals.

Demographic Data

The bill requires or contemplates disaggregation by race, ethnicity, sex, disability, religion, age, and other characteristics in relevant data systems.

Demographic categories must protect privacy and account for unknown or perceived identity.

Disparity alone does not decide constitutionality but can reveal patterns requiring investigation.

Use-of-Force Record Retention

Reportable records would be retained for at least four years under the 2025 text.

Retention supports auditing, research, complaint investigation, and pattern detection.

Longer retention may be required by litigation, criminal cases, state law, grant conditions, or agency policy.

Annual Reporting Audits

States and tribes would audit their use-of-force reporting systems annually.

An audit tests whether agencies are submitting complete and accurate information.

Without compliance review, national databases can appear comprehensive while omitting nonparticipating or poorly reporting agencies.

Grant Enforcement

The bill uses Byrne and COPS funding to encourage reporting and policy compliance.

Noncompliant allocations can be reduced or reallocated, subject to statutory procedures.

Grant conditioning influences recipients but does not directly govern agencies that receive no affected funds unless another provision applies.

Small-Agency Assistance

The bill includes grants for smaller law-enforcement agencies meeting specified conditions.

Small agencies often lack data personnel, legal counsel, public dashboards, and evidence systems.

Technical support is essential if national reporting requirements are to avoid excluding rural and tribal jurisdictions.

Deaths in Custody

The 2025 proposal includes reporting concerning deaths in custody.

The subject overlaps with the Death in Custody Reporting Act and existing DOJ collection responsibilities.

GAO has repeatedly identified gaps in national custody-death data and stated that H.R. 5361 would address a continuing congressional consideration if enacted.

Racial and Religious Profiling

The bill incorporates the End Racial and Religious Profiling Act framework.

Profiling involves reliance on protected characteristics in law-enforcement decisions except where a sufficiently specific suspect description or other lawful factor applies.

The exact statutory definition controls and should not be reduced to any use of race in a police record.

Federal Profiling Prohibition

Federal law-enforcement agents would be prohibited from racial, religious, and other discriminatory profiling as defined by the bill.

Federal agencies would adopt compliant policies, training, complaint, discipline, and data practices.

The proposal would create legal consequences beyond voluntary guidance.

Profiling Enforcement and Remedies

The bill would authorize enforcement mechanisms for prohibited profiling.

Available relief would focus on stopping discriminatory policy and practice and providing legal accountability according to the statutory scheme.

Claims would still require proof of the elements established by the enacted text.

State and Local Profiling Conditions

State and local agencies would be influenced through federal grant conditions.

Recipients would adopt policies, training, complaint procedures, and data systems addressing profiling.

Congress frequently uses spending conditions where direct federal command over local police is constitutionally or politically contested.

Profiling Data Collection

The bill would require detailed data concerning stops, searches, citations, arrests, and related enforcement activity.

Useful analysis requires denominators, location, deployment, calls for service, hit rates, legal justification, and data quality.

Raw racial disparity should neither be ignored nor treated as self-explanatory.

Publication and Privacy Limits

The profiling subtitle contains rules concerning publication and limitations on identifying information.

Public transparency must be balanced against victim, witness, officer, juvenile, medical, and investigative privacy.

Aggregate data should remain sufficiently detailed to reveal meaningful patterns.

Racial-Bias Training

The bill would require federal training and use grants to promote state and local training concerning racial bias and profiling.

Training can address implicit and explicit bias, constitutional law, procedural justice, history, and decision-making.

Training alone cannot replace supervision, accountability, data, and policy.

Duty-to-Intervene Training

Section 361 includes training on use of force and the duty to intervene.

The duty concerns stopping another officer's excessive force when a realistic opportunity exists.

Effective implementation also requires reporting, anti-retaliation protection, supervision, and discipline.

Federal No-Knock Drug Warrants

Section 362 would amend the Controlled Substances Act so that a warrant authorized under that section requires officers to provide notice of authority and purpose before entry.

The provision is limited to the federal drug-warrant context specified by the statute.

It should not be described as a direct nationwide ban on every no-knock entry for every offense.

State and Local No-Knock Grant Condition

After the implementation period, a state or local government could not receive affected COPS funding unless it had a law prohibiting no-knock warrants in drug cases.

The condition applies through federal funding rather than direct amendment of every state search-warrant code.

Jurisdictions would need to define emergency, announcement, entry, and remedy rules in their own laws.

Breonna Taylor and No-Knock Reform

Louisville officers killed Breonna Taylor during execution of a narcotics search warrant in March 2020.

Her death became the central national example associated with no-knock and nighttime warrant reform.

The legal and factual circumstances involved warrant preparation, announcement disputes, gunfire, target information, and accountability beyond the warrant label alone.

Chokehold and Carotid-Hold Definition

The bill defines covered restraints to include pressure to the throat or windpipe and maneuvers restricting blood or oxygen flow to the brain.

It also includes carotid artery restraints that prevent or hinder breathing or reduce air intake.

Medical and tactical terminology varies among agencies, making statutory definitions important.

Chokehold Grant Conditions

States and local governments would lose eligibility for affected Byrne and COPS grants unless their laws prohibited law-enforcement use of chokeholds or carotid holds.

The provision would require legislation, not merely a changeable department policy.

The text should be read for any exceptions and implementation rules before applying it to a specific jurisdiction.

Eric Garner Excessive Use of Force Prevention Act

The chokehold subsection includes the short title Eric Garner Excessive Use of Force Prevention Act.

Garner died in New York City in 2014 after an officer used a prohibited neck restraint during an arrest.

His repeated statement that he could not breathe became a central phrase in the national movement against police violence.

Chokeholds Under Section 242

The bill would add language treating specified throat, windpipe, blood-flow, or oxygen-flow restraints as punishment, pain, or penalty for Section 242 purposes.

That language would interact with the amended criminal mental-state requirement.

Criminal conviction would still require proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

PEACE Act

Section 364 is titled the Police Exercising Absolute Care With Everyone Act of 2025.

It would establish statutory federal standards for less-lethal and deadly force.

The provision goes beyond training guidance by defining when federal officers may use force.

Less-Lethal Force Standard

Federal less-lethal force would have to be necessary and proportional to effect an arrest of a person whom the officer has probable cause to believe committed a criminal offense.

Reasonable alternatives would have to be exhausted.

This standard is more restrictive than a rule asking only whether force was objectively reasonable.

Deadly Force Standard

Deadly force would be allowed only when necessary as a last resort to prevent imminent and serious bodily injury or death to the officer or another person.

The force could create no substantial risk of injury to a third person.

Reasonable alternatives would have to be exhausted.

Warnings Before Force

When feasible, a federal officer would identify as law enforcement, request surrender, and warn that force will be used if the person resists or flees.

Feasibility accounts for rapidly developing threats.

A warning requirement does not require an officer to delay when doing so would create immediate danger.

De-escalation

The bill defines de-escalation as proactive conduct creating time, distance, options, and resources to gain voluntary compliance and reduce force.

Examples include verbal persuasion, warnings, tactical repositioning, slowing the incident, waiting, and calling specialised resources.

De-escalation is not always possible, but the bill would require consideration of reasonable alternatives.

Guidance for Vulnerable or High-Risk Encounters

The Attorney General would issue guidance concerning force involving pregnant people, children and youth, older people, people with disabilities, people affected by substances, people with serious medical conditions, and people with limited English proficiency.

The categories address heightened medical, communication, and developmental risks.

They do not create identical tactics for every person within a category.

Officer Conduct Leading to Force

The bill's totality-of-circumstances language includes officer actions leading up to the use of force.

Its justification provision addresses gross negligence contributing to the claimed necessity for force.

The approach responds to criticism of legal analysis focused only upon the final seconds of an encounter.

State and Local Force Standards

Federal officers would be governed directly by the PEACE Act provisions.

State and local agencies would be encouraged through grants and policy conditions to adopt comparable standards.

Congress would not itself rewrite every state criminal justification statute through one direct command.

Statutory Rule and Constitutional Floor

The Fourth Amendment supplies a constitutional reasonableness floor under Supreme Court doctrine.

Congress can impose stricter statutory rules on federal officers and attach policy conditions to grants.

A violation of agency policy or statute does not automatically establish every element of a constitutional damages claim.

Stop Militarizing Law Enforcement Act

Section 365 would restrict transfer of specified military equipment to state and local law-enforcement agencies.

The proposal concerns the federal 1033 program and related transfer mechanisms.

Restricted categories and exceptions must be read from the text rather than inferred from the phrase military equipment.

Arguments Supporting Transfer Restrictions

Supporters argue that armored weapons and military hardware can escalate encounters, distort police culture, and suppress protest.

They seek public notice, justification, training, inventory, and return of prohibited property.

They distinguish ordinary protective equipment from weapons designed for warfare.

Arguments Opposing Transfer Restrictions

Opponents argue that surplus equipment can protect officers and civilians during active shooters, terrorism, disasters, rescues, and barricades.

Small agencies may be unable to purchase equivalent equipment commercially.

The policy dispute concerns categories, controls, mission, oversight, and use rather than whether every transferred item is harmful.

Public-Safety Innovation Grants

The bill would fund alternatives and innovations intended to reduce unnecessary police contact and improve public safety.

Potential models include civilian crisis response, violence intervention, mental-health services, and community-based programs.

Grant evaluation should measure safety, service quality, equity, cost, and unintended consequences.

Federal Body-Camera Requirement

The 2025 text states that federal law-enforcement officers shall wear body cameras.

The camera would be positioned to maximise its ability to record the officer's activities.

Detailed provisions govern activation, prohibited recording, retention, access, disclosure, redaction, discipline, and evidentiary use.

Federal Camera Activation

Video and audio would generally activate when an officer responds to a call for service or initiates an enforcement or investigative stop with a member of the public.

The bill addresses circumstances in which immediate danger prevents activation at the first moment.

Activation rules are central because a camera that begins after force or entry may miss the disputed event.

Body-Camera Privacy

The bill addresses homes, victims, minors, medical settings, personal communications, and people incidentally recorded.

It distinguishes footage subject to long-term retention from routine footage.

Federal disclosure would require redaction and exceptions protecting non-law-enforcement subjects.

Body-Camera Retention

Specified serious incidents would receive a minimum three-year retention period.

People recorded could request preservation under defined circumstances.

Litigation holds, criminal cases, complaints, public requests, and other laws could require longer retention.

Body-Camera Disclosure

The bill provides a public-request framework and prioritises footage in which a recorded person was killed, shot, or grievously injured.

For approved qualifying requests, the text establishes an expedited period that can be as short as five days.

Privacy exceptions and written permission requirements apply to specified categories.

Officer Review and Evidentiary Use

The bill regulates access to and use of federal camera footage.

Review rules affect report writing, investigation, discipline, criminal cases, and public disclosure.

Video is powerful evidence but does not reproduce an officer's exact vision, attention, touch, depth perception, or events outside the frame.

Facial-Recognition Prohibition

Cameras and recording devices required under the federal subtitle could not use or be equipped with facial-recognition technology.

Footage from those systems could not be subjected to facial recognition.

The provision responds to concern that an accountability camera could become a continuous biometric-surveillance tool.

Federal Patrol-Vehicle Cameras

The bill includes in-car recording requirements for marked federal patrol vehicles.

Vehicle-camera provisions address traffic and patrol encounters not fully captured by body cameras.

Exceptions and operational rules would be implemented through regulations.

State and Local Body-Camera Requirements

The Police CAMERA Act portion would use federal grant assurances and funding rules to promote state and local body-camera programs.

Applicants would devote a portion of specified funds to cameras and associated systems.

A body-camera mandate requires ongoing funding for storage, cybersecurity, redaction, discovery, public records, replacement, and staff.

Cameras Are Not Complete Accountability

A camera cannot guarantee activation, honest classification, preservation, independent investigation, or public release.

Agencies require audit logs, chain of custody, retention, discipline, privacy, and external oversight.

Technology should support rather than replace legal and institutional reform.

Closing the Law-Enforcement Consent Loophole

Title IV addresses sexual acts involving officers and people under arrest, detention, or custody.

The power imbalance makes meaningful legal consent impossible or highly suspect within the covered custodial relationship.

The bill would establish a federal prohibition and incentivise comparable state laws.

Federal Officer Prohibition

Federal law-enforcement officers would be prohibited from engaging in covered sexual acts while acting under color of law with a person in custody or detention.

The provision treats the officer's official power as central to the offense.

Other sexual assault, coercion, and civil-rights laws can also apply.

State Law Incentives

States would be encouraged through funding conditions to enact laws penalising covered sexual conduct.

State criminal codes differ in definitions, grading, custody, coercion, and consent.

The federal proposal seeks a minimum national policy while leaving state implementation details.

Severability

Title V includes a severability clause.

If a court invalidated one provision, the remaining provisions could continue where legally operable.

Severability does not guarantee that every remaining provision would function exactly as Congress intended.

Savings Clause

The savings clause protects other existing legal rights, remedies, and authorities unless the bill expressly changes them.

The proposal would supplement rather than replace many state laws, civil-rights remedies, agency policies, and criminal statutes.

A court would interpret the clause in relation to the specific conflict.

Direct Federal Rules

Congress possesses its clearest authority when regulating federal law-enforcement officers and federal agencies.

The bill directly addresses federal force, profiling, cameras, vehicles, facial recognition, misconduct reporting, and custodial sexual conduct.

Agency regulations would be necessary to implement many operational details.

Federal Grant Conditions

The bill uses Byrne and COPS grants to influence state and local law.

Conditions address warrants, chokeholds, force, profiling, reporting, hiring, accreditation, body cameras, and other subjects.

A recipient can face reduced or unavailable funding rather than direct federal command.

Constitutional Questions About Spending Conditions

Congress may attach conditions to federal funds when constitutional requirements are satisfied.

Conditions must be stated clearly and remain related to the federal program under applicable doctrine.

A coercion challenge would depend on the amount, structure, notice, and practical effect of the condition.

State and Local Variation

Many jurisdictions already adopted parts of the proposed framework after 2020.

State laws vary on force, neck restraints, warrants, certification, records, arbitration, civilian oversight, body cameras, and attorney-general authority.

Existing state reform does not convert the federal bill into law or make every jurisdiction compliant with its exact text.

Tribal Law Enforcement

The bill includes tribal officers and Indian tribes in several definitions, grants, and reporting systems.

Tribal policing operates within tribal sovereignty, federal trust responsibilities, cross-deputisation, and complex criminal jurisdiction.

Implementation must avoid treating tribal governments as ordinary municipal subdivisions.

Rulemaking

If enacted, DOJ and other agencies would issue regulations, guidance, grant rules, forms, data standards, and implementation schedules.

The introduced bill text cannot answer every operational question.

Rulemaking would create additional opportunities for public comment, litigation, technical revision, and administrative delay.

Authorisations and Appropriations

The bill authorises spending for grants, data, investigations, training, and administration.

An authorisation permits Congress to fund a program but does not itself guarantee annual appropriations.

Implementation could be partial if later appropriations were insufficient.

Congressional Budget Analysis

CBO published a cost estimate for the 2021 House bill.

CBO also informed Congress that state and local data and compliance requirements could cost intergovernmental entities several hundred million dollars annually, while portions enforcing constitutional or anti-discrimination rights received special treatment under the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act.

The 2025 bill had no CBO estimate listed on Congress.gov as of the page used for this fact sheet.

State and Local Costs

Costs could include databases, cameras, storage, investigators, prosecutors, accreditation, training, reporting, privacy review, redaction, audits, and litigation.

Federal grants would offset but might not cover all continuing expenses.

Smaller agencies and rural jurisdictions could face disproportionate administrative burdens.

Claimed Public Benefits

Supporters anticipate fewer unconstitutional encounters, stronger accountability, improved evidence, better hiring, national transparency, and increased trust.

They argue that avoided deaths, injuries, settlements, prosecutions, and community harm are also economic benefits.

Actual outcomes would depend on implementation rather than enactment alone.

Law-Enforcement Perspectives Supporting Reform

Some officers and police leaders support national data, duty to intervene, body cameras, accreditation, independent investigations, and clearer force rules.

Uniform standards can protect officers from inconsistent local expectations and improve legitimacy.

Support for selected provisions does not imply support for the entire bill.

Law-Enforcement Opposition

Police organisations have objected especially to qualified-immunity reform, criminal mens rea changes, restrictive force standards, grant mandates, and litigation exposure.

They argue that the bill insufficiently accounts for rapid threats and could deter lawful proactive policing.

Opposition also reflects concern about morale, recruitment, retention, insurance, and political rhetoric.

Civil-Rights Support

Civil-rights organisations have supported the legislation as a comprehensive federal response to police violence and racial bias.

They emphasize that existing remedies leave many people without relief and allow misconduct records to remain fragmented.

Some advocates nevertheless consider the bill insufficient compared with abolition, budget reallocation, community control, or broader criminal-justice transformation.

Reform and Abolition Perspectives

The bill is a reform measure: it seeks to regulate, professionalise, document, and hold police accountable.

Police-abolition frameworks question whether better rules can correct the institution itself.

Support for racial justice does not imply agreement about whether federal police reform, defunding, abolition, or community investment should be the primary strategy.

Alternative Congressional Approaches

Republican legislators proposed narrower policing packages emphasizing grants, data, training, body cameras, and local incentives while generally preserving qualified immunity and a less restrictive force framework.

Supporters described those proposals as more practical and protective of officers.

Opponents described them as inadequate to create enforceable accountability.

Major Negotiating Barriers

Qualified immunity, officer and municipal liability, criminal prosecution standards, misconduct databases, grant penalties, and federal force rules were central areas of disagreement.

Legislators also disagreed about whether to impose national mandates or promote voluntary state reform.

The inability to resolve those questions prevented a bipartisan comprehensive bill.

Relationship to Executive Order 14074

President Joseph Biden issued Executive Order 14074 in May 2022 after Congress did not enact the 2021 bill.

The order directed federal agencies to revise force, restraint, warrant, camera, accreditation, data, and accountability practices within executive authority.

It could not amend Section 1983, change Section 242's statutory mens rea, or enact the full congressional proposal.

Revocation of Executive Order 14074

President Donald Trump revoked Executive Order 14074 on January 20, 2025.

Revocation ended the order but did not repeal statutes, state laws, court judgments, or independently valid agency rules automatically.

The 2025 House bill represented a renewed effort to place reforms in legislation rather than rely upon executive direction.

Separate Reforms That Became Law

Congress and states have enacted narrower laws affecting lynching, federal officer cameras, military equipment, reporting, grants, or other justice subjects at different times.

Those enactments should not be mislabelled as passage of the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act.

A comprehensive fact sheet must distinguish individual reforms from the status of the full package.

Meaning of Naming the Bill for George Floyd

Naming connects the legislative project to Floyd's life and the movement produced by his murder.

It also places his name on provisions extending far beyond the Minneapolis incident.

The title carries memorial, political, moral, and legislative significance but does not itself define legal effect.

George Floyd's Family and Advocacy

Members of Floyd's family testified, met with officials, and advocated for federal reform.

Their advocacy gave the legislation personal and moral visibility.

The family did not possess formal authority to enact or interpret the bill.

African American Historical Significance

The bill belongs to a long history of federal proposals responding to local and state failures to protect Black civil rights.

Its central subjects—force, profiling, warrants, accountability, federal enforcement, and public records—have repeatedly shaped African American encounters with government.

Its repeated failure to become law also demonstrates the difficulty of translating mass protest into durable national legislation.

Federal Civil-Rights Enforcement Tradition

Reconstruction civil-rights statutes, Section 1983, Section 242, modern anti-discrimination laws, and pattern-or-practice authority provide historical foundations.

The proposed act would strengthen or expand several of those mechanisms.

It does not create federal involvement in policing from nothing; it changes the extent and tools of that involvement.

Individual and Institutional Accountability

Section 242 and civil damages concern individual conduct and responsibility.

Pattern-or-practice investigations, accreditation, grants, data, supervision, and training concern institutions.

The bill combines both because individual misconduct can arise from systemic policy and systemic reform still requires individual accountability.

Data Are Not Accountability by Themselves

A database can reveal patterns only when agencies report accurately and users interpret status correctly.

Public information does not guarantee investigation, discipline, prosecution, compensation, or reform.

The bill pairs data with hiring, grant, civil, criminal, and institutional mechanisms.

Training Is Not Accountability by Itself

Training can improve knowledge and skills but cannot correct deliberate abuse without supervision and consequences.

An agency can document training while tolerating contrary field practice.

The bill combines training with reporting, discipline, accreditation, force standards, and legal remedies.

Federal Standard Versus Local Policy

Many departments already prohibit chokeholds, require cameras, or use de-escalation policies.

A local policy can be changed by agency leadership and may lack a private remedy.

A federal statute or grant condition creates a different level of durability, enforcement, and national consistency.

Possible Unintended Effects

Agencies might withdraw from affected grants, reduce proactive activity, struggle to recruit, overclassify force, or divert resources into compliance.

Public databases could spread inaccurate allegations or discourage complaint filing if privacy is weak.

Good implementation requires correction systems, technical support, evaluation, and adaptation.

Measures of Implementation

Congress and DOJ would need to measure reporting completeness, accreditation, use-of-force outcomes, complaints, lawsuits, hiring checks, training, body-camera compliance, and grant enforcement.

Implementation should examine race, disability, age, geography, agency size, and incident type.

A count of policies adopted would not establish that field conduct changed.

Measures of Success

Possible measures include fewer unlawful killings and injuries, reduced racial disparity, better complaint resolution, complete national data, fewer wandering officers, stronger officer intervention, and increased public trust.

Officer safety, crime, response time, staffing, and lawful enforcement should also be evaluated.

Success requires balancing rights and public safety rather than assuming that one metric controls.

Conditions for Failure

The law could fail through inadequate appropriations, weak regulations, agency nonreporting, poor data quality, litigation delay, grant avoidance, political reversal, or symbolic compliance.

A registry could fail if source records are incomplete or correction processes are absent.

Force rules could fail if training, supervision, discipline, and emergency resources do not support them.

Research Needs

Researchers would need baseline and post-implementation data across different jurisdictions.

Randomized evaluation is often impossible for national legal change, requiring careful quasi-experimental and longitudinal methods.

Research should distinguish provisions rather than attribute every outcome to the entire act.

Comparing Versions

The 2020 and 2021 House-passed versions provide the clearest legislative history because they received floor votes and CRS summaries.

The 2024 and 2025 introduced versions show continued sponsorship and updated text.

A citation must identify the Congress, chamber, bill number, version, and date.

How to Read H.R. 5361

Begin with the definitions and table of contents.

Then distinguish provisions that directly amend the United States Code from grant conditions, authorisations, studies, guidance, and reporting duties.

Finally, check effective dates, rulemaking deadlines, appropriations, exceptions, and savings clauses.

Research and Interpretation

The controlling source for proposed legal language is the official bill text from GovInfo or Congress.gov.

Congress.gov status controls whether a measure is introduced, passed, or enacted.

Sponsor summaries describe advocacy positions and are useful for purpose but do not replace statutory text.

Historical versions should not be merged silently into the current bill.

Representative Effects If H.R. 5361 Were Enacted

Federal Civil-Rights Prosecution

A federal prosecutor could charge a qualifying rights deprivation using the proposed knowing-or-reckless standard rather than proving present-law willfulness.

Civil Damages Lawsuit

A local officer sued under Section 1983 could not rely upon the bill's prohibited good-faith or clearly-established-law defenses, although the plaintiff would still need to prove an underlying violation and damages.

DOJ Systemic Investigation

DOJ could subpoena agency records and testimony during a pattern-or-practice investigation instead of depending entirely upon voluntary production or litigation discovery.

State Attorney General Action

A state attorney general could bring a federal action to eliminate a police pattern or practice within the state.

Police Hiring

A grant-receiving agency would certify that it checked specified misconduct and certification systems before hiring an applicant from another department.

No-Knock Drug Warrant

A federal Controlled Substances Act warrant would require announcement, while a state lacking a drug-case no-knock prohibition could lose eligibility for affected COPS funding.

Chokehold Policy

A state or locality without a legal prohibition on chokeholds and carotid holds could lose eligibility for affected Byrne and COPS grants.

Federal Deadly Force

A federal officer would have to satisfy the statutory necessity, last-resort, imminent-threat, third-party-risk, and alternatives requirements.

Body-Camera Footage

Federal footage of a person being killed, shot, or grievously injured could receive extended retention and expedited public-request treatment, subject to approval, privacy, and redaction rules.

Custodial Sexual Conduct

A federal officer could face a specific statutory prohibition for sexual conduct with a person under arrest, detention, or custody.

Major Support and Opposition Arguments

Accountability

Supporters argue that existing criminal and civil standards allow serious violations to go unpunished. Opponents argue that the bill would punish reasonable mistakes and encourage defensive policing.

National Standards

Supporters argue that constitutional rights should not depend on geography. Opponents argue that states and localities need flexibility to govern policing according to local law and conditions.

Qualified Immunity

Supporters seek meaningful civil remedies. Opponents fear personal liability, recruitment problems, and litigation based upon uncertain law.

Use of Force

Supporters describe necessity, proportionality, and de-escalation as life-saving standards. Opponents argue that “last resort” rules can be judged with hindsight and may endanger officers or bystanders.

Data

Supporters seek a national factual foundation. Opponents and administrators warn about cost, inconsistent definitions, privacy, incomplete reporting, and misuse of allegations.

Grant Conditions

Supporters describe grant leverage as a constitutional method of achieving reform. Opponents describe the conditions as federal coercion and an indirect takeover of local policy.

Body Cameras

Supporters emphasise evidence and transparency. Critics warn that cameras can expand surveillance and impose major storage, redaction, and disclosure costs.

Military Equipment

Supporters seek to demilitarize police culture. Opponents argue that properly controlled equipment is necessary for active threats, disasters, and rescue.

Misinformation and Common Errors

“The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act Is Current Federal Law”

No comprehensive version had become law by August 1, 2026.

“The 2020 House Vote Enacted the Bill”

House passage alone is insufficient; the Senate did not pass the bill.

“The 2021 Bill Passed Congress”

It passed the House but not the Senate.

“H.R. 5361 Is the Same Legal Instrument as H.R. 1280”

They are separate bills introduced in different Congresses, even where language is similar.

“Every Provision Directly Commands Local Police”

Many state and local provisions operate through federal grant conditions rather than direct regulation.

“The Bill Would Abolish Police Departments”

It is a regulation, accountability, data, training, and funding proposal—not a police-abolition bill.

“The Bill Defunds All Police”

It authorises grants and conditions specified federal funds; it does not eliminate all police funding.

“It Bans Every No-Knock Warrant Nationwide”

The 2025 text directly changes specified federal drug warrants and conditions COPS grants on state or local drug-case prohibitions.

“It Directly Bans Every State Chokehold Without Exception”

The principal state and local mechanism is eligibility for affected Byrne and COPS funds.

“Qualified-Immunity Reform Guarantees Every Plaintiff Wins”

Plaintiffs would still have to establish a legal violation, causation, standing, and damages.

“Qualified Immunity Is the Same as Criminal Immunity”

Qualified immunity concerns civil damages and does not prevent a valid criminal prosecution.

“Changing Section 242 Would Remove the Burden of Proof”

Federal prosecutors would still have to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

“A National Registry Would List Only Proven Misconduct”

The proposal includes separately labelled pending, sustained, exonerated, unfounded, and not-sustained complaint categories.

“A Lawsuit or Settlement Is a Misconduct Conviction”

Lawsuits contain allegations, and settlements frequently occur without admissions or judicial findings.

“The PEACE Act Merely Repeats Graham v. Connor”

It proposes more specific necessity, proportionality, alternatives, warning, and last-resort requirements for federal officers.

“Body Cameras Record Exactly What Officers See”

A camera does not reproduce attention, peripheral vision, depth perception, touch, or events outside its frame.

“The Bill Authorises Facial Recognition on Body-Camera Footage”

The federal-camera subtitle prohibits required camera systems and their footage from using facial recognition.

“Executive Order 14074 Enacted the Bill”

An executive order cannot amend statutes or enact the full congressional package.

“Revoking Executive Order 14074 Repealed H.R. 5361”

H.R. 5361 is a separate legislative proposal; it was introduced after the order's revocation.

“Every Police-Reform Measure Since 2020 Is Part of This Act”

State statutes, local ordinances, executive orders, agency policies, and separate federal bills have independent legal identities.

Historical and Continuing Significance

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act is the principal comprehensive federal legislative response to the movement produced by Floyd's murder.

It attempts to connect individual liability, institutional reform, national data, tactical restrictions, training, grants, body cameras, civil-rights enforcement, and public transparency within one framework.

Its repeated House passage and repeated failure to become law reveal both the breadth of support for major reform and the depth of disagreement over liability, force, federalism, and police authority.

For African American history, the proposal belongs to the continuing struggle to make federal power protect Black citizens when state and local systems fail to prevent or remedy official violence.

The bill is also historically important because many of its individual ideas spread into state statutes, local ordinances, consent decrees, executive policies, and agency reforms even without comprehensive federal enactment.

Its future importance depends upon whether Congress continues to pursue one broad package or divides the proposal into narrower measures capable of bipartisan passage.

Summary

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act is a family of comprehensive federal police-reform bills introduced after George Floyd's murder.

H.R. 7120 passed the House in 2020 by 236–181, and H.R. 1280 passed the House in 2021 by 220–212. Neither passed the Senate.

H.R. 8525 and S. 4991 were introduced in 2024 but did not advance beyond the introduced stage.

H.R. 5361 was introduced on September 15, 2025, by Representative Glenn Ivey and referred to three House committees. It remained introduced as of August 1, 2026.

The 2025 bill would change the federal criminal civil-rights mental-state standard, restrict qualified immunity, expand pattern-or-practice authority, support independent deadly-force investigations, establish accreditation standards, and create a public National Police Misconduct Registry.

It would also require extensive force and policing data, prohibit profiling, support duty-to-intervene training, restrict no-knock drug warrants and chokeholds through federal and grant mechanisms, establish a necessity-based federal force standard, limit military-equipment transfers, and fund public-safety innovation.

Federal officers would face detailed body-camera, vehicle-camera, disclosure, retention, and facial-recognition rules, while state and local agencies would be influenced through grant conditions.

The legislation would prohibit covered sexual conduct by federal officers with people in custody and encourage comparable state laws.

Supporters view the proposal as necessary national civil-rights infrastructure. Opponents view it as excessive liability, costly federal intrusion, and a threat to effective policing.

No description should present the proposal as enacted law. Its historical importance lies in the breadth of reform it assembled, the House votes it received, the national movement it represented, and the continuing debate created by Congress's failure to enact a comprehensive agreement.
