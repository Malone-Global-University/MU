Eric Garner

An African American husband, father, grandfather, worker, and Staten Island community member whose death during a New York City police arrest on July 17, 2014, was recorded by a bystander and made “I Can't Breathe” a defining demand for police accountability.

Who Was Eric Garner?

Eric Garner was a Black New Yorker whose life centered upon family, work, neighborhood relationships, economic struggle, and recurring health problems.

He was a husband, father, grandfather, son, friend, former city parks worker, and familiar presence in Staten Island's Tompkinsville community.

Garner did not hold public office or lead a national movement during his life. He became internationally known because a bystander recorded police placing him in a prohibited neck restraint and holding him face down while he repeatedly stated that he could not breathe.

Biography and Death as Distinct Subjects

Eric Garner's biography and the death of Eric Garner are related but distinct reference subjects.

A biography explains his family, employment, health, personality, community relationships, and continuing legacy. A historical-event page would focus more narrowly upon the July 17 arrest, use of force, medical response, investigations, legal proceedings, and public reaction.

Preserving this distinction prevents Garner's complete life from being reduced to the minutes captured on Ramsey Orta's telephone.

Family

Garner was the son of Gwen Carr and was married to Esaw Garner. He was the father of six children and a grandfather.

Family members described him as affectionate, humorous, protective, and physically imposing without being naturally violent.

After his death, his mother, wife, daughters, and other relatives became public advocates for police accountability and insisted that he be remembered as a person with relationships and responsibilities rather than only as a body in a police video.

Employment and Economic Life

Garner had worked for the New York City Department of Parks and Recreation in horticultural, maintenance, and related outdoor work.

Health limitations made stable employment more difficult. Like many people living at the edge of the formal labor market, he relied at times upon irregular income and informal economic activity.

Police had repeatedly arrested or cited him for allegedly selling individual untaxed cigarettes. His death therefore raised questions about how heavily government should use arrest and physical force to regulate minor tax and licensing offenses.

Health Conditions

Garner had asthma, obesity, and significant cardiovascular disease.

These conditions increased his vulnerability to restricted breathing, chest compression, stress, and prolonged prone restraint.

A person's medical vulnerability does not remove constitutional protection or excuse force that causes death. Police and emergency personnel must adjust their response when a restrained person displays obvious respiratory distress.

Community Relationships

Friends and relatives described Garner as a neighborhood peacemaker. His size made him noticeable, but people who knew him frequently described him as generous and socially engaged.

He spent time near Tompkinsville Park and local businesses and had repeated contact with officers assigned to the area. Those recurring contacts contributed to the frustration visible in the video when Garner demanded that the repeated treatment stop.

Tompkinsville Enforcement Context

Tompkinsville Park and nearby Bay Street businesses had generated resident and merchant complaints involving drugs, alcohol, disorder, theft, and the sale of untaxed cigarettes.

The NYPD conducted enforcement operations in the area. Police Commissioner James O'Neill later stated that officers approached Garner after D'Amico said he observed a cigarette sale.

Garner denied selling cigarettes during the recorded encounter. The dispute over whether a sale occurred did not resolve the separate question of whether the force used to complete the arrest was lawful and proportionate.

Untaxed Cigarettes and “Loosies”

New York imposes substantial taxes upon cigarettes. Individual cigarettes removed from untaxed or improperly taxed packages are commonly called loosies.

Selling them can violate state and municipal law. Enforcement formed part of a broader strategy addressing unlicensed street commerce and quality-of-life offenses.

Critics argued that aggressive enforcement placed poor Black communities under repeated surveillance and arrest for minor economic activity. Garner's death became the clearest example of how a low-level revenue offense could escalate into lethal government force.

Prior Police Contacts

Garner had been arrested repeatedly before July 2014, including for allegations involving untaxed cigarettes. He had previously complained about abusive or humiliating police treatment.

His prior record was irrelevant to whether officers used reasonable force during the fatal arrest except where an officer lawfully knew and relied upon specific information at the time.

After Garner's death, information concerning his alleged arrest and medical histories appeared in news reports, producing accusations that officials had improperly disclosed confidential information to discredit him.

July 17, 2014

Plainclothes NYPD officers approached Garner on Bay Street during the afternoon and intended to arrest him for allegedly selling individual untaxed cigarettes.

Garner denied the accusation and complained that police repeatedly harassed him. Ramsey Orta began recording the encounter with a cellphone.

The recording captured the officers' attempt to handcuff Garner, Pantaleo's neck restraint, the fall to the pavement, Garner's repeated respiratory warnings, and portions of the medical response.

Resistance and Police Authority

Garner verbally objected and moved his arms away when officers attempted to handcuff him.

Officers possessed authority to use reasonable force to complete a lawful arrest. That authority did not permit a prohibited chokehold, unnecessary compression, or failure to respond to a medical emergency.

A central principle of use-of-force law is that officers must reassess force continuously as resistance, threat, position, and medical condition change.

Daniel Pantaleo's Takedown

Pantaleo approached Garner from behind as officers attempted to control his arms.

The federal investigation described Pantaleo as initially appearing to attempt an approved rear-takedown or seat-belt maneuver. As the men struggled and collided with a storefront window, Pantaleo wrapped his arm around Garner's neck.

Federal prosecutors estimated that the effective chokehold lasted approximately seven seconds. The later NYPD disciplinary process concluded that Pantaleo recklessly used and maintained a prohibited chokehold.

NYPD Chokehold Ban

The NYPD had prohibited chokeholds since 1993. Department policy broadly prohibited pressure to the throat or windpipe that could prevent or hinder breathing.

A policy violation is not automatically a criminal offense. Policy nevertheless defines the conduct expected of officers and may support discipline when a technique creates unnecessary risk.

Garner's death exposed the gap between a written prohibition and the ability of training, supervision, reporting, and discipline to enforce it.

Prone Restraint

Garner and Pantaleo struck a storefront window and fell to the sidewalk. Several officers moved to control Garner's arms and body and placed him face down.

Prone restraint can restrict chest expansion, especially when several officers apply body weight or pressure. Risks increase for people who are obese, asthmatic, physically exhausted, experiencing cardiac disease, intoxicated, or under extreme stress.

Garner's medical findings identified prone positioning and chest compression as parts of the cause of death rather than treating the neck hold as the only mechanism.

“I Can't Breathe”

Garner stated that he could not breathe eleven times while officers restrained him on the ground.

He was able to speak briefly, but speech does not prove that a person is receiving sufficient air or oxygen.

Officers did not promptly reposition him into a safer recovery position or begin effective medical care. Garner became quiet, motionless, and unresponsive.

The phrase became a national rallying cry because it recorded a direct medical warning that government personnel failed to treat with adequate urgency.

Ramsey Orta's Video

Ramsey Orta recorded the encounter from close range.

His video preserved Garner's words, the officers' movements, the neck restraint, the prone position, the lack of immediate medical intervention, and the conduct of responding personnel.

Without the recording, the public record would have depended much more heavily upon police reports and official statements. The video became a major example of citizen documentation functioning as evidence and public accountability.

Orta later reported police surveillance and retaliation, while authorities stated that his later arrests concerned independent criminal conduct. Those disputes do not alter the evidentiary importance of the recording.

The Right to Record Police

Recording police performing public duties is generally protected by the First Amendment, subject to reasonable safety and interference restrictions.

A person may document an arrest without physically obstructing officers. Citizen video does not eliminate the need for medical, forensic, and legal analysis, but it preserves events that might otherwise be disputed or omitted.

Medical Response

Officers called for an ambulance and checked Garner's pulse. They did not provide substantial medical aid while he remained unresponsive on the sidewalk.

Emergency medical personnel arrived but were criticized for the limited care provided before moving Garner to an ambulance.

Garner was transported to Richmond University Medical Center, where he was pronounced dead.

Medical Examiner Findings

The New York City medical examiner classified Garner's death as homicide.

The official cause included compression of the neck, compression of the chest, and prone positioning during police restraint. Asthma, obesity, and hypertensive cardiovascular disease were identified as contributing conditions.

A medical classification of homicide means that another person's actions caused or contributed to death. It does not independently determine whether a criminal offense occurred or whether an individual possessed the intent required by a criminal statute.

Medical Disagreement

The medical examiner connected the chokehold and restraint to Garner's death. Defense and federal-review experts raised alternative or additional mechanisms involving cardiac arrest, stress, obesity, asthma, and prone restraint.

The federal decision emphasized uncertainty over whether prosecutors could prove that the neck hold itself caused death and that Pantaleo acted willfully.

The administrative disciplinary process used a different burden and concluded that Pantaleo recklessly violated NYPD policy.

Richmond County Grand Jury

Richmond County District Attorney Daniel Donovan presented evidence concerning Garner's death to a grand jury.

On December 3, 2014, the grand jury declined to indict Pantaleo. The decision meant that the required number of jurors did not find probable cause for a criminal charge submitted through the secret proceeding.

It was not an acquittal following a public trial.

Grand-Jury Secrecy

New York law generally protects grand-jury testimony and evidence from public disclosure.

Civil-rights organizations, journalists, and public officials sought disclosure because the encounter was recorded and the public could not understand how the legal decision had been reached.

Courts largely preserved secrecy, emphasizing the traditional protection given to witnesses, jurors, and future investigations. The dispute became part of a wider debate over whether police-death grand juries require greater transparency.

Federal Civil-Rights Investigation

The Justice Department opened a federal criminal civil-rights investigation after the state grand jury declined to indict.

Federal agents and prosecutors reviewed the videos, autopsy, medical evidence, witness testimony, police training, departmental proceedings, and the circumstances of the arrest.

To prosecute, the government had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that an officer willfully used objectively unreasonable force. Willfulness required proof that the officer acted with the specific intent to do what federal law forbids, not merely that the officer made a mistake or violated policy.

Justice Department Declines Charges

On July 16, 2019, the Justice Department announced that it would not bring federal criminal charges against Pantaleo or another officer.

Prosecutors cited the rapidly developing struggle, the apparent initial attempt to use an approved takedown, the short duration of the neck hold, Garner's physical size, his resistance to handcuffing, and disagreement among medical experts.

The department described Garner's death as a tragedy while emphasizing that tragedy and a prosecutable federal crime are legally distinct.

What the Federal Decision Did Not Mean

The federal decision did not declare Pantaleo's conduct consistent with NYPD policy, reverse the homicide classification, determine that the arrest was wise or proportionate, or prevent departmental discipline.

It established only that federal prosecutors believed the evidence could not satisfy every element of a criminal civil-rights offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

$5.9 Million Civil Settlement

Garner's estate filed a claim against New York City.

On July 13, 2015, the city comptroller announced a $5.9 million pre-litigation settlement. The city did not admit liability.

A civil settlement compensates an estate and resolves legal claims without requiring a criminal conviction or public trial.

NYPD Disciplinary Trial

The Civilian Complaint Review Board prosecuted departmental charges against Pantaleo in 2019.

The proceeding examined whether he recklessly used a prohibited chokehold and whether his conduct violated departmental rules.

Video evidence, medical testimony, officer training, the duration of the hold, and the continued restraint after Garner reached the ground were central.

Administrative Finding and Firing

Deputy Commissioner of Trials Rosemarie Maldonado found that Pantaleo recklessly used a prohibited chokehold and recommended termination.

Police Commissioner James O'Neill fired Pantaleo on August 19, 2019. O'Neill distinguished the initial decision to attempt an arrest from Pantaleo's decision to maintain the prohibited neck restraint as the men reached the ground.

The firing occurred more than five years after Garner's death. Garner's family welcomed the termination but criticized the delay and absence of criminal charges.

Appeal of the Firing

Pantaleo challenged his termination and sought reinstatement.

In 2021, a New York appellate court upheld the disciplinary determination, finding substantial evidence that Pantaleo recklessly maintained a prohibited chokehold after emergency circumstances no longer justified it.

The ruling preserved the distinction between the absence of criminal charges and valid administrative discipline.

Other Officers and Official Paperwork

Several officers assisted in restraining Garner. Justin D'Amico prepared arrest-related paperwork after Garner died.

Later court proceedings identified questions concerning whether official forms inaccurately stated that no force had been used and exaggerated the seriousness of the cigarette offense.

Pantaleo's disciplinary case did not resolve every issue concerning the actions, reports, or medical duties of the other officers.

Public Judicial Inquiry

Gwen Carr and other petitioners used New York City Charter Section 1109 to seek a public judicial inquiry.

New York courts authorized inquiry into the legal basis for the stop and arrest, force used by officers other than Pantaleo, official documents, alleged leaks of confidential information, and the failure of police to render medical aid.

Public testimony occurred in 2021. The inquiry created a public record but was not a criminal trial and was not required to produce a final declaration of individual guilt.

Protests Following Garner's Death

Demonstrations began during the summer of 2014 and intensified after the grand-jury decision.

Tens of thousands of people marched in New York City, and solidarity events occurred throughout the country.

Protesters used die-ins, bridge and roadway occupations, vigils, marches, civil disobedience, and public memorials. Most demonstrations were peaceful, although arrests and confrontations occurred.

Relationship to Black Lives Matter

Black Lives Matter began in 2013 after George Zimmerman's acquittal in the killing of Trayvon Martin.

Garner's death occurred shortly before Michael Brown was killed in Ferguson. The two 2014 cases helped transform Black Lives Matter into a sustained national movement against police violence.

Garner's case also demonstrated that lethal force could arise from low-level enforcement and physical restraint rather than only from police gunfire.

“I Can't Breathe” as a Movement Phrase

Protesters printed the phrase on shirts, signs, banners, artwork, and social-media posts. Athletes and public figures wore it during demonstrations and sporting events.

The phrase expressed Garner's literal respiratory emergency and a broader claim that oppressive institutions denied Black communities space, safety, health, and political voice.

George Floyd repeated the same warning during his murder in 2020, giving Garner's final words renewed global significance.

Erica Garner

Garner's daughter Erica became a prominent activist after his death. She organized demonstrations, demanded accountability, supported other affected families, and criticized both police institutions and political leaders.

Erica Garner died in December 2017 at age twenty-seven after severe cardiac complications. Her death deepened the family's loss and became part of public discussion concerning grief, stress, health inequality, and the burdens placed upon relatives who become movement leaders.

Gwen Carr

Garner's mother Gwen Carr became a national advocate for police accountability.

She supported the campaign to fire Pantaleo, the Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act, transparency proceedings, federal policing legislation, and families affected by police violence.

Carr helped obtain the public judicial inquiry after ordinary criminal and administrative processes left major questions unresolved.

Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act

New York enacted the Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act in June 2020.

The law created the felony offense of aggravated strangulation for police and peace officers when an officer commits criminal obstruction of breathing or uses a chokehold or similar restraint and thereby causes serious physical injury or death.

The offense is a class C felony. The statute converted specified chokehold conduct from a departmental policy violation into a potential state crime when severe harm results.

Policy Ban and Criminal Prohibition

The NYPD policy ban existed for more than twenty years before Garner died. A policy violation could produce discipline but did not automatically constitute a specific felony.

The 2020 state law created a criminal offense with statutory elements and a criminal burden of proof.

Neither form of regulation is effective without reporting, investigation, evidence preservation, prosecution, and institutional willingness to impose consequences.

Police Disciplinary-Record Transparency

New York Civil Rights Law Section 50-a had restricted public access to police disciplinary records.

Garner's family and advocates encountered the law while seeking information concerning Pantaleo and the officers involved.

New York repealed Section 50-a in 2020 as part of a broader police-reform package. Garner's case was one of the major examples used to demonstrate how secrecy could obstruct accountability.

Low-Level Enforcement and Escalation

Garner's death began with enforcement of an alleged minor tax and licensing offense.

Governments may choose warnings, summonses, civil penalties, administrative enforcement, or non-police responses for low-level conduct.

Police officials stated that Garner's refusal to provide identification required transport to a precinct. Critics argued that a government system should not create foreseeable risk of death over the suspected sale of individual cigarettes.

Relationship to Broken-Windows Policing

Broken-windows policing emphasizes enforcement of visible disorder and low-level offenses as part of maintaining public order and preventing more serious crime.

Opponents argued that intensive low-level enforcement produced repeated contact, racial disparity, humiliation, arrest records, and unnecessary escalation.

Supporters argued that residents and businesses have a legitimate interest in safe, orderly public spaces and enforcement of tax and licensing law.

Garner's case demonstrated that the method, proportionality, and cumulative burden of enforcement matter as much as the formal validity of the underlying offense.

Duty to Recognize Medical Distress

A restrained person who reports breathing difficulty requires immediate reassessment.

Officers must reduce unnecessary pressure, reposition the person when safe, monitor consciousness and breathing, summon medical assistance, and provide appropriate aid.

Calling an ambulance does not necessarily satisfy the duty when a person is becoming unresponsive in front of officers.

Garner's case became a central example of the danger of treating verbal distress as resistance or exaggeration rather than as medical evidence.

Individual and Institutional Accountability

Individual accountability asks whether Pantaleo or another person committed a crime, civil wrong, or policy violation.

Institutional accountability asks why prohibited conduct occurred, why discipline took five years, why medical aid was inadequate, whether official reports were accurate, and whether confidential information was leaked.

The grand jury and federal investigation did not produce criminal charges. The civil settlement, disciplinary firing, appellate decision, legislation, and judicial inquiry addressed different forms of responsibility.

Eric Garner as a Public Symbol

Garner became a symbol of police brutality, respiratory restraint, unequal enforcement, and government indifference to direct warnings of medical distress.

His case differed from many police shootings because the lethal process was gradual, physical, audible, and recorded at close range.

Garner was more than his final words, body size, health conditions, arrest history, or alleged street commerce. His historical significance includes both the individual life lost and the institutional lessons made visible through his death.

Misinformation and Common Errors

Garner was unarmed and was approached over an alleged low-level cigarette offense.

Garner denied selling cigarettes during the recorded encounter, while police later stated that an officer had observed a sale.

The direct neck hold did not remain in exactly the same position throughout the full ground restraint.

Garner began stating that he could not breathe after the direct neck hold had been released, while chest compression and prone restraint continued. This timing does not eliminate the chokehold from the medical sequence or excuse failure to respond to respiratory distress.

The medical examiner classified the death as homicide, but medical homicide is not identical to a criminal murder finding.

Pantaleo was not acquitted at trial. A grand jury declined to indict, federal prosecutors declined charges, and the NYPD later fired him after an administrative trial.

The $5.9 million settlement did not include an admission of municipal liability.

“I Can't Breathe” originated as Garner's direct medical warning before becoming a movement slogan later associated with George Floyd.

Major Outcomes

Garner's death was classified as homicide, but no officer received state or federal criminal charges.

New York City paid a $5.9 million settlement to his estate.

The NYPD fired Pantaleo after a departmental trial, and a court upheld the termination.

Garner's family obtained a rare public judicial inquiry into unresolved government conduct.

New York enacted a felony anti-chokehold law and repealed a major police-record secrecy statute.

The case expanded Black Lives Matter, strengthened the right-to-record movement, and permanently established “I Can't Breathe” as a demand for police accountability.

Limits and Unresolved Issues

No criminal court evaluated Pantaleo's conduct at a public trial.

Grand-jury secrecy prevented the public from reviewing the full state charging record.

Pantaleo remained employed for five years after Garner's death.

The disciplinary case focused principally upon Pantaleo and did not resolve every question concerning other officers, reports, leaks, or medical aid.

Chokeholds and similar restraints continued to produce deaths nationally despite policy bans and later legislation.

George Floyd's murder in 2020 demonstrated that officers could still ignore the same warning Garner had repeated six years earlier.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Garner was unarmed and was approached over an alleged low-level cigarette offense.

Garner denied selling cigarettes during the recorded encounter, while police later stated that an officer had observed a sale.

Garner resisted handcuffing by pulling his arms away. This permitted reasonable arrest force but did not authorize prohibited or medically dangerous restraint.

The accepted federal description placed the direct neck hold at approximately seven seconds, while chest compression and prone restraint continued longer.

Garner began repeating that he could not breathe after the direct neck hold had been released. This timing does not eliminate the chokehold from the medical sequence or excuse failure to respond to respiratory distress.

The medical examiner's homicide classification did not itself prove murder, manslaughter, or a federal civil-rights offense.

Pantaleo was never acquitted by a trial jury. A grand jury declined indictment, and federal prosecutors declined charges.

The NYPD administrative tribunal later found a policy violation and recommended termination under a different legal standard.

The civil settlement did not include a city admission of liability.

Ramsey Orta's later arrests do not alter the authenticity or historical importance of his video.

Eric Garner and George Floyd are distinct people and cases, although Floyd repeated the same warning during his murder in 2020.

Why Eric Garner Matters

Eric Garner's death made visible the lethal potential of police restraint during enforcement of a minor offense.

The case demonstrated that government force can kill through compression, position, stress, and delayed medical response without a firearm.

Garner's repeated warnings established that a restrained person's ability to speak does not prove adequate breathing and that officers must treat respiratory complaints as urgent medical evidence.

Ramsey Orta's video showed how citizen recording can preserve facts that official paperwork may omit or describe differently.

The grand-jury and federal decisions illustrated the difficulty of converting visible misconduct and medical homicide into criminal charges under demanding legal standards.

Pantaleo's eventual firing demonstrated that administrative responsibility can exist even when criminal liability is not established.

The five-year delay showed how overlapping investigations and deference among agencies can postpone employment accountability.

The judicial inquiry demonstrated that public attention is not equivalent to institutional transparency and that unresolved official conduct may require additional legal mechanisms.

The anti-chokehold law showed how a death can convert a departmental rule into a statutory criminal prohibition.

Garner's final words connected the emerging Black Lives Matter movement of 2014 to the global George Floyd protests of 2020.

Summary

Eric Bernard Garner was a New York City husband, father, grandfather, former parks worker, and Staten Island community member. He experienced serious health problems, economic instability, and repeated police contact involving allegations that he sold individual untaxed cigarettes.

On July 17, 2014, NYPD officers attempted to arrest Garner near Tompkinsville Park. Daniel Pantaleo placed an arm around Garner's neck during a takedown, and several officers restrained him face down. Garner repeated “I can't breathe” eleven times before becoming unresponsive. Ramsey Orta recorded the encounter. The medical examiner classified Garner's death as homicide caused by neck compression, chest compression, and prone positioning, with serious health conditions contributing.

A state grand jury and federal prosecutors declined criminal charges. New York City paid a $5.9 million civil settlement, and the NYPD fired Pantaleo after a disciplinary trial found reckless use of a prohibited chokehold. Garner's death expanded Black Lives Matter, inspired the Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act, strengthened demands for police-record transparency and the right to record, and made “I Can't Breathe” one of the defining statements of the modern struggle against police violence.
