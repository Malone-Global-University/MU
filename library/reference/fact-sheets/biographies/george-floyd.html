George Floyd

An African American father, worker, athlete, musician, and community member whose murder by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin on May 25, 2020, triggered a historic national and international movement against police violence and racial injustice.

Who Was George Floyd?

George Floyd was a private citizen whose life became internationally known only after his murder by a police officer.

He was a son, brother, father, grandfather, friend, athlete, musician, truck driver, security worker, and member of Black communities in Houston and Minneapolis.

Floyd's public significance does not rest upon a political office, formal movement leadership position, or professional celebrity.

His death became historically important because a bystander recorded Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin maintaining a lethal restraint while Floyd was handcuffed, prone, and repeatedly stating that he could not breathe.

The video, prosecutions, and protests transformed Floyd from a previously little-known individual into a global symbol of police violence and racial injustice.

Birth and Family

George Perry Floyd Jr. was born on October 14, 1973, in Fayetteville, North Carolina.

His parents were George Perry Floyd Sr. and Larcenia “Cissy” Jones Floyd.

After his parents separated, his mother moved the children to Houston, Texas.

Floyd grew up with siblings in a close family whose members later described him as protective, affectionate, humorous, and deeply attached to his mother.

Relatives commonly called him Perry, while friends and community members called him Big Floyd because of his height and physical presence.

Houston's Third Ward

Floyd was raised principally in Houston's Third Ward, a historically African American community.

His family lived in Cuney Homes, a public-housing development commonly known within the community as the Bricks.

The Third Ward possessed important Black churches, schools, businesses, cultural institutions, musicians, athletes, and civic organizations.

It also faced poverty, housing inequality, disinvestment, crime, drug markets, and limited economic opportunity.

Floyd's life developed within both the institutional strengths and economic pressures of this community.

Education and School Years

Floyd attended schools in Houston and graduated from Jack Yates Senior High School in 1993.

Yates is a historically Black high school with a major role in Third Ward educational, athletic, and cultural life.

Floyd participated in football and basketball.

His size and athletic ability made sports an important source of identity and opportunity.

Family members and teammates later remembered his ambitions in athletics and his ability to encourage other students.

Football and Basketball

Floyd played tight end for the Yates football team and power forward for the basketball team.

The Yates football team reached the Texas state championship game during his high-school years.

Floyd's height, strength, and personality made him a recognizable figure among classmates.

Athletics offered a possible path toward college and economic mobility, as it did for many young people in communities with limited access to other opportunities.

College Attendance

Floyd attended South Florida Community College, now known as South Florida State College, on an athletic scholarship.

He played football and basketball during his college years.

He later attended Texas A&M University–Kingsville for a period but did not complete a degree.

His college attendance reflected athletic opportunity but did not produce the stable professional path he had hoped to obtain.

Music and Houston Hip-Hop

Floyd participated in Houston's hip-hop culture under the name Big Floyd.

He was associated with the group Presidential Playas and appeared in music connected to DJ Screw and the city's chopped-and-screwed sound.

Houston's underground rap community served as a cultural network through which neighborhood identity, personal experience, ambition, and local reputation were expressed.

Floyd did not become a nationally known recording artist during his lifetime, but his music remains part of the historical record of his life before 2020.

Adult Life in Houston

As an adult, Floyd worked in a series of jobs, including truck driving and security work.

He was a father and later a grandfather.

Friends and relatives described him as someone who struggled economically while attempting to support family members and maintain community relationships.

His life included periods of employment, music, religious involvement, addiction, criminal prosecution, incarceration, and efforts to begin again.

A complete biography should neither erase these difficulties nor use them to imply that Floyd was less entitled to constitutional protection.

Criminal History and Incarceration

Floyd accumulated several criminal convictions in Texas during adulthood.

His most serious case involved a 2007 aggravated-robbery charge that he resolved through a guilty plea.

He served several years in prison before returning to Houston.

This history affected his employment, financial stability, and effort to rebuild his life.

Floyd's earlier convictions were unrelated to the alleged counterfeit bill on May 25, 2020, and did not authorize police to punish or kill him.

Constitutional protections governing arrest, force, medical care, and due process apply to people regardless of past convictions.

Religious and Community Life

After leaving prison, Floyd became involved with Christian ministry and community outreach in Houston.

He worked with local religious figures attempting to connect young people, churches, neighborhood programs, and employment opportunities.

Friends later described him as encouraging younger men to avoid violence and seek constructive paths.

Floyd was not a nationally recognized minister or political organizer.

His community role was informal and developed through personal relationships rather than institutional office.

Move to Minneapolis

Floyd moved to the Minneapolis area during the 2010s seeking employment, recovery, and a new beginning.

The move formed part of a broader pattern in which people with criminal records or histories of addiction relocate in search of work, housing, and supportive relationships.

Floyd maintained ties to Houston and his family while developing a new network in Minnesota.

Friends in Minneapolis described him as socially outgoing and recognizable because of his height and personality.

Employment in Minneapolis

Floyd worked in truck driving, security, and related employment.

He served as a security worker at Conga Latin Bistro and worked at other locations in the Minneapolis area.

Security work depended upon public events, nightlife, restaurants, and close personal contact.

The COVID-19 pandemic sharply reduced this type of employment when businesses closed or limited operations.

Floyd was therefore experiencing the same sudden economic instability that affected millions of service workers in 2020.

Health and Addiction

Floyd experienced substance-use problems during portions of his adult life.

He also had cardiovascular disease and had tested positive for COVID-19 before his death.

Toxicology identified fentanyl and methamphetamine in his system.

These medical facts became major issues during Chauvin's trial, where the defense argued that Floyd's health and drug use created reasonable doubt concerning causation.

The jury rejected that defense and convicted Chauvin of murder and manslaughter.

The medical examiner classified the death as a homicide caused by cardiopulmonary arrest complicating law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression, while listing heart disease and drug exposure as other significant conditions.

The COVID-19 Pandemic

Floyd's death occurred during the first major phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Black Americans faced disproportionate illness, death, frontline occupational exposure, unemployment, and housing insecurity.

Pandemic closures disrupted Floyd's security employment and daily life.

The video of him stating that he could not breathe circulated during a public-health emergency already revealing racial inequality.

This context contributed to the intensity with which many people connected Floyd's death to broader institutional conditions.

Cup Foods and the Counterfeit-Bill Report

On May 25, 2020, Floyd entered Cup Foods, a convenience store at the intersection of East 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

An employee believed that Floyd had used a counterfeit twenty-dollar bill to purchase cigarettes.

Store employees approached Floyd outside and later called police after he did not return the cigarettes.

The allegation concerned a low-level, nonviolent property offense.

Whether Floyd knowingly possessed counterfeit currency was never adjudicated because he was killed during the police response.

Police Arrival and Initial Arrest

Minneapolis police officers Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng arrived first.

They approached Floyd while he was seated in a vehicle.

Lane pointed a firearm during the initial contact, ordered Floyd to show his hands, and removed him from the vehicle.

The officers handcuffed Floyd and moved him toward a police vehicle.

Floyd expressed distress and resistance to entering the vehicle, including statements concerning claustrophobia and difficulty breathing.

Derek Chauvin and Tou Thao then arrived.

The Prone Restraint

The officers brought Floyd to the ground and restrained him in a prone position.

Chauvin placed his knee across Floyd's neck and upper back area.

Kueng restrained Floyd's back and lower body, Lane controlled his legs, and Thao kept bystanders away.

Floyd was handcuffed and increasingly unable to move.

He repeatedly stated that he could not breathe, called for his mother, expressed fear that he would die, and eventually stopped speaking and moving.

Chauvin continued the restraint for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds.

Failure to Intervene and Provide Medical Aid

Bystanders repeatedly told the officers that Floyd could not breathe and urged them to check his pulse.

Lane asked whether the officers should roll Floyd onto his side.

Kueng later checked for a pulse and stated that he could not find one.

The officers did not end the restraint promptly or provide effective medical assistance.

Federal prosecutors later established that Kueng and Thao willfully failed to intervene against Chauvin's unreasonable force and that Kueng, Lane, and Thao willfully failed to provide aid for Floyd's serious medical need.

Their convictions made the duty to intervene and duty to render medical assistance central legal lessons of the case.

Darnella Frazier's Video

Seventeen-year-old Darnella Frazier encountered the arrest while walking with a younger relative.

She used her telephone to record the officers' restraint of Floyd.

The video documented Floyd's words, the officers' positions, the duration of the restraint, bystander warnings, and Floyd's loss of consciousness.

Frazier uploaded the recording to social media, where it directly challenged the initial police account.

In 2021, the Pulitzer Prize Board awarded Frazier a special citation for courageously recording Floyd's murder and demonstrating the importance of citizen documentation.

The Initial Police Account

The Minneapolis Police Department's initial public statement described the event as a “man dies after medical incident during police interaction.”

That framing did not disclose the prolonged prone restraint, Chauvin's knee position, Floyd's repeated distress, or the bystanders' warnings.

The bystander video made the omission immediately visible.

The contrast became an important example of why independent evidence, public records, body cameras, witnesses, and external investigation are necessary when government personnel use force.

Medical Response and Death

Emergency medical personnel arrived and transported Floyd to Hennepin County Medical Center.

Resuscitation efforts were unsuccessful.

Floyd was pronounced dead on May 25, 2020.

His death occurred in police custody following a prolonged restraint that continued after he became unresponsive.

Medical Examiner Findings

The Hennepin County medical examiner classified Floyd's manner of death as homicide.

The official cause was cardiopulmonary arrest complicating law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression.

Other significant conditions included arteriosclerotic and hypertensive heart disease, fentanyl intoxication, and recent methamphetamine use.

A homicide classification is a medical determination that another person's actions contributed to death; it does not by itself establish a criminal offense.

Criminal responsibility was determined separately through investigation, charging, trial, guilty pleas, and judgment.

Independent Family Autopsy

Floyd's family retained independent physicians to examine the death.

The family experts described the cause as asphyxia resulting from sustained pressure and restraint.

The county and independent reports used different medical language but both classified the death as homicide and connected it to the restraint.

During Chauvin's trial, multiple medical experts explained how restricted breathing, prone positioning, pressure, low oxygen, and Floyd's underlying health interacted.

Dismissal of the Officers

The Minneapolis Police Department fired Chauvin, Kueng, Lane, and Thao on May 26, one day after Floyd's death.

Firing ended their employment but did not determine criminal responsibility.

State and federal investigators gathered video, body-camera recordings, medical evidence, training records, witness testimony, and officer statements.

The case eventually produced separate state and federal prosecutions.

Protests Following Floyd's Murder

Demonstrations began in Minneapolis on May 26 and spread rapidly.

Protesters demanded arrest and prosecution of the officers and broader changes to policing and racial institutions.

Events occurred in every state, the District of Columbia, and countries throughout the world.

Most demonstrations were peaceful, while some cities experienced looting, arson, assaults, shootings, police violence, curfews, mass arrests, and military deployment.

Floyd's name became a central symbol connecting one murder to a longer history of Black deaths involving police and unequal justice.

Minneapolis Civil Unrest

Minneapolis experienced serious civil disorder during the first days after Floyd's death.

Businesses were looted or burned, police and protesters clashed, and emergency services were overwhelmed.

On May 28, officers abandoned the Minneapolis Police Department's Third Precinct building, which was then entered and set on fire.

Minnesota deployed the National Guard, and officials imposed curfews.

The unrest was historically connected to Floyd's murder but should not be treated as conduct directed or controlled by Floyd or his family.

Memorial Services and Funeral

Public and private memorial services were held in Minneapolis, Raeford in North Carolina, and Houston.

Family members, clergy, civil-rights leaders, public officials, athletes, entertainers, and community residents attended.

Floyd's funeral took place in Houston on June 9, 2020.

He was buried at Houston Memorial Gardens in Pearland, Texas, near his mother.

The services joined family mourning with public demands for legal accountability and institutional reform.

Philonise Floyd's Congressional Testimony

On June 10, 2020, Floyd's brother Philonise testified before the United States House Judiciary Committee.

He described George as a beloved family member rather than merely the person visible in the arrest video.

He asked Congress to ensure that Floyd's death produced meaningful change in police accountability.

The testimony placed the family directly within the national legislative process and challenged officials to translate public mourning into law.

State Charges

Hennepin County prosecutors initially charged Chauvin with third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

Minnesota Governor Tim Walz later assigned Attorney General Keith Ellison to lead the prosecution in cooperation with the Hennepin County Attorney's Office.

On June 3, 2020, prosecutors added a second-degree unintentional murder charge against Chauvin and charged Kueng, Lane, and Thao with aiding and abetting murder and manslaughter.

The charges identified Floyd's death as a criminal homicide rather than merely an employment-policy violation.

State v. Derek Chauvin

Chauvin's state criminal trial began in March 2021 in Hennepin County District Court.

Prosecutors presented bystander video, police body-camera recordings, medical testimony, use-of-force experts, officer testimony, emergency personnel, and evidence concerning department training.

Senior Minneapolis police officials testified that Chauvin's restraint was inconsistent with department policy and lawful police practice.

The defense argued that Floyd resisted arrest and that drug use, heart disease, and other medical conditions created reasonable doubt concerning causation.

On April 20, 2021, the jury found Chauvin guilty on all three counts submitted at trial.

State Conviction and Sentence

Chauvin was convicted of second-degree unintentional murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

The court sentenced him to twenty-two and one-half years in prison on June 25, 2021.

The Minnesota Court of Appeals later upheld the principal convictions.

The third-degree-murder conviction was later vacated because of a subsequent Minnesota Supreme Court interpretation of that offense, but the second-degree-murder and manslaughter convictions and sentence remained in force.

Chauvin has continued to pursue postconviction challenges, which do not alter the judgment unless a court grants relief.

Federal Civil-Rights Case Against Chauvin

Federal prosecutors charged Chauvin with willfully depriving Floyd of his constitutional right to be free from unreasonable force.

Chauvin pleaded guilty in December 2021.

His plea admitted that he maintained unreasonable force even after Floyd became unresponsive and that his conduct resulted in bodily injury and death.

Chauvin also pleaded guilty to violating the civil rights of a fourteen-year-old boy in a separate 2017 police encounter.

A federal judge sentenced him to 252 months, with credit for time served, to run concurrently with his state sentence.

Federal Convictions of Kueng, Lane, and Thao

A federal jury convicted Kueng, Lane, and Thao in February 2022.

Kueng and Thao were convicted of willfully failing to intervene to stop Chauvin's unreasonable force.

Kueng, Lane, and Thao were convicted of willfully failing to provide medical aid while Floyd was restrained and clearly in serious medical need.

The federal verdict established that officers can be criminally responsible not only for directly applying unlawful force but also for deliberately failing to stop it or provide necessary care.

State Cases Against Kueng, Lane, and Thao

Thomas Lane pleaded guilty to aiding and abetting second-degree manslaughter.

J. Alexander Kueng also pleaded guilty to aiding and abetting second-degree manslaughter.

Tou Thao chose a stipulated-evidence bench trial and was found guilty of aiding and abetting second-degree manslaughter.

By 2023, all four former officers involved in Floyd's death had been convicted in federal court, state court, or both.

Civil Settlement with Minneapolis

Floyd's family filed a federal civil-rights lawsuit against the City of Minneapolis and the four former officers.

In March 2021, the Minneapolis City Council approved a $27 million settlement.

The agreement designated $500,000 for the benefit of the community surrounding 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

A civil settlement provides compensation and resolves legal claims without functioning as a criminal conviction.

The settlement was announced during jury selection in Chauvin's criminal trial, producing defense objections concerning potential prejudice.

Institutional Investigation of Minneapolis Policing

The United States Department of Justice opened a pattern-or-practice investigation of Minneapolis and its police department in April 2021.

In June 2023, the department reported reasonable cause to believe that Minneapolis police engaged in patterns of excessive force, racial discrimination, violations of protected speech, and discrimination against people with behavioral-health disabilities.

The report also identified deficiencies in accountability, training, supervision, and officer wellness.

Federal investigators concluded that institutional problems made conduct like that inflicted upon Floyd possible.

Police-Reform Agreements

Minneapolis entered a court-enforceable settlement agreement with the Minnesota Department of Human Rights in 2023.

The agreement addresses use of force, stops, searches, supervision, training, data, accountability, and community engagement.

The United States Department of Justice filed a proposed federal consent decree in January 2025.

In May 2025, the Justice Department announced that it was dismissing the federal lawsuit and proposed decree.

Minneapolis officials subsequently stated that the city would continue implementing reforms compatible with the active state agreement.

This sequence demonstrates that institutional reform can change with federal administrations even when local and state obligations continue.

George Floyd Justice in Policing Act

Members of Congress introduced federal policing legislation bearing Floyd's name.

The proposal addressed federal use-of-force standards, chokeholds, no-knock warrants, qualified immunity, misconduct records, racial profiling, body cameras, data collection, and federal grants.

The House of Representatives passed versions in 2020 and 2021.

The Senate did not enact the measure, and bipartisan negotiations did not produce a final statute.

The legislation's failure demonstrated the difference between broad public recognition of Floyd's name and congressional agreement concerning national police regulation.

Federal Executive Action

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. issued Executive Order 14074 on May 25, 2022, the second anniversary of Floyd's murder.

The order addressed federal use-of-force policy, chokeholds, no-knock entries, body cameras, misconduct information, accreditation, data, and grant conditions.

Executive action could govern federal agencies and influence state and local departments through grants and standards.

It could not enact every provision of the proposed congressional legislation or independently control all state and local police agencies.

George Floyd Square

The intersection of 38th Street and Chicago Avenue became an informal memorial and protest space known as George Floyd Square.

Residents and visitors placed flowers, signs, art, candles, names of people killed by police, and other memorial objects at the site.

Community members organized food distribution, mutual aid, public events, political education, and security.

The area also became the subject of disputes involving traffic access, public safety, city authority, memorial preservation, local business, and redevelopment.

Minneapolis has undertaken a long-term process to redesign the intersection while preserving its historical meaning.

Global Memorialization

Murals, monuments, street names, artworks, exhibitions, songs, books, films, scholarships, foundations, and public ceremonies commemorated Floyd.

Protesters repeated his name in demonstrations across the world.

Museums and archives collected signs, photographs, digital records, personal testimony, and objects from memorial sites.

Memorialization preserved evidence of public response but also produced debate concerning commercialization, political symbolism, historical accuracy, and the relationship between remembrance and reform.

Family Advocacy

Members of Floyd's family became public advocates following his murder.

They testified before lawmakers, attended demonstrations, supported criminal accountability, promoted police reform, and participated in memorial and charitable activity.

Family advocacy helped prevent Floyd from being reduced to police footage or a political symbol.

It also placed the continuing emotional burden of public representation upon relatives who had not chosen the circumstances making them nationally visible.

George Floyd as a Public Symbol

Floyd became a symbol of police brutality and racial injustice because his murder was clearly documented and connected to many earlier cases.

Symbols make complex issues publicly understandable, but they can also flatten the life of the person being represented.

Floyd was neither a flawless saint nor the collection of negative facts emphasized by efforts to discredit him.

His constitutional rights did not depend upon moral perfection, sobriety, wealth, employment status, education, or criminal record.

The historical question is not whether Floyd lived without difficulty. It is whether police officers lawfully exercised government authority during his arrest.

Misinformation and Competing Claims

Floyd's death generated extensive misinformation and politically motivated reinterpretation.

Claims have attempted to attribute the death entirely to drugs, heart disease, COVID-19, or an unrelated medical condition.

Those claims conflict with the official homicide finding, trial evidence, state murder conviction, and Chauvin's federal guilty plea admitting that unreasonable force resulted in Floyd's death.

Medical conditions and toxicology were relevant evidence but did not legally excuse the restraint.

Chauvin and supporters have continued to pursue legal challenges. A collateral challenge is not equivalent to a reversal or finding of innocence.

Accurate treatment must distinguish an allegation, expert theory, pending challenge, court ruling, conviction, and final judgment.

Medical and Legal Causation

Medical causation asks what physiological processes and contributing conditions produced death.

Criminal causation asks whether a defendant's unlawful conduct was legally sufficient to cause the death under the governing statute.

A victim's vulnerability does not ordinarily relieve a defendant whose conduct causes death.

The prosecution did not have to prove that Floyd possessed perfect health or that restraint was the only condition present.

The jury found beyond a reasonable doubt that Chauvin's criminal conduct caused Floyd's death.

Historical Context

Floyd's murder occurred within a long history of racialized policing and public controversy concerning force against Black people.

Earlier cases involving Rodney King, Amadou Diallo, Sean Bell, Oscar Grant, Eric Garner, Michael Brown, Freddie Gray, Philando Castile, Breonna Taylor, and many others had produced protest and demands for accountability.

Black Lives Matter had developed since 2013 as a movement connecting these cases.

Floyd's death became a turning point because the recording was unusually clear, prolonged, public, and witnessed by people actively warning the officers.

The 2020 pandemic and accumulated movement infrastructure allowed the response to spread with exceptional speed and scale.

Institutional Outcomes

Floyd's murder produced criminal convictions of all four former officers involved.

It generated one of the largest civil settlements in a police-death case at the time.

Governments revised chokehold, restraint, intervention, body-camera, medical-care, reporting, and accountability policies.

Federal and state investigations documented broader deficiencies within Minneapolis policing.

The case strengthened public understanding of the duty to intervene, the importance of bystander recording, and the difference between individual prosecution and institutional reform.

The principal national legislation bearing Floyd's name did not become law, and policy outcomes varied considerably among jurisdictions.

Limits of the Legal and Political Response

Convicting the officers responsible did not eliminate police violence or racial inequality.

State and local reforms were uneven, and some were weakened, reversed, delayed, or contested.

Congress did not establish a comprehensive national policing statute in Floyd's name.

The federal government later abandoned its proposed Minneapolis consent decree, demonstrating the vulnerability of reform policy to political change.

Public attention also declined while disputes concerning crime, police staffing, protest tactics, and movement organizations increased.

Floyd's case achieved rare accountability without establishing that comparable accountability would occur consistently in other cases.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

George Floyd and the murder of George Floyd are related but distinct fact-sheet subjects. A biography should explain his life rather than reduce him entirely to the circumstances of his death.

Floyd did not found or lead Black Lives Matter. The movement began in 2013 and expanded dramatically after his murder.

Floyd's past convictions, addiction, health conditions, and suspected use of counterfeit currency did not eliminate his constitutional rights or authorize unlawful force.

The official medical examiner listed heart disease and drug exposure as significant conditions while classifying the death as homicide caused by law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression.

Darnella Frazier's recording was not police body-camera footage. It was independent bystander documentation.

The initial allegation that Floyd used counterfeit currency was never tried in court and should not be presented as a proven knowing offense.

Chauvin's state and federal cases were separate prosecutions under different laws.

The vacation of Chauvin's third-degree-murder count did not erase his second-degree-murder and manslaughter convictions.

The convictions of Kueng, Lane, and Thao rested principally upon failure to intervene, failure to render medical aid, and aiding and abetting responsibility rather than a finding that each applied the same force as Chauvin.

Most protests following Floyd's murder were peaceful, although serious civil disorder and property destruction occurred in some places.

Demonstrators, movement organizations, rioters, looters, police officers, counterprotesters, and unaffiliated criminal actors should not be treated as interchangeable categories.

Legal appeals and postconviction petitions are mechanisms for challenging judgments. Filing a challenge does not itself invalidate a conviction.

Why George Floyd Matters

George Floyd's murder became one of the most consequential police killings in modern history.

The event demonstrated the evidentiary importance of an ordinary witness recording government conduct in public.

The contrast between the initial police statement and Frazier's video exposed the danger of relying solely upon an agency's first account of its own use of force.

The prosecution established that an officer can be convicted of murder for unlawful force used during an arrest and that accompanying officers can face criminal liability for failing to intervene or provide medical aid.

Floyd's death connected personal vulnerability to wider institutional questions concerning force policy, supervision, training, accountability, medical response, race, and public trust.

The protests demonstrated that a local event can become global when clear evidence intersects with established movement networks and shared historical grievance.

Floyd's biography also illustrates why victims should not be divided into categories of morally perfect and undeserving. Constitutional protection applies to people whose lives include poverty, addiction, criminal records, illness, and struggle.

The civil settlement, criminal convictions, policy changes, and memorials produced significant accountability but did not resolve the national problem of police violence.

Floyd remains historically important both as an individual whose life was wrongfully taken and as the name attached to a continuing international demand for equal justice.

Summary

George Perry Floyd Jr. was born in North Carolina, raised in Houston's Third Ward, and known to family and friends as Perry or Big Floyd. His life included family, athletics, music, security work, economic hardship, addiction, incarceration, religious involvement, and efforts to establish a new life in Minneapolis.

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police arrested Floyd after a store reported a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill. Derek Chauvin maintained an unlawful prone restraint for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds while Floyd was handcuffed, repeatedly stated that he could not breathe, became unresponsive, and died. Darnella Frazier's video documented the murder and contradicted the initial police account.

Chauvin was convicted of state murder and manslaughter offenses and pleaded guilty to federal civil-rights charges. Kueng, Lane, and Thao were also convicted for their roles. Floyd's murder triggered worldwide protests, major institutional investigations, police reforms, public memorials, and continuing political debate. His historical importance rests both in the life wrongfully taken and in the global demand for accountability that followed.
