Breonna Taylor

A twenty-six-year-old African American emergency medical worker whose killing by Louisville police during a late-night search-warrant raid on March 13, 2020, became a national symbol of police accountability, the sanctity of the home, Black women's visibility, and the dangers of false warrants and poorly executed raids.

Who Was Breonna Taylor?

Breonna Taylor was an African American emergency medical worker living in Louisville, Kentucky.

She worked in healthcare, maintained close relationships with her mother, sister, extended family, friends, and partner, and hoped to become a nurse.

Taylor was not the principal target of the narcotics investigation that brought police to her apartment.

Her name became internationally known after officers forced entry into her home after midnight and killed her during an exchange of gunfire initiated when her boyfriend believed intruders were entering.

Taylor's public significance rests both in the life that was lost and in the institutional failures surrounding the warrant, raid, shooting, investigation, and legal response.

Biography and Killing as Distinct Subjects

Breonna Taylor's biography and the killing of Breonna Taylor are related but distinct reference subjects.

A biography explains her childhood, education, work, aspirations, family, and personal relationships.

A historical-event page would focus more narrowly upon the warrant, forced entry, gunfire, investigations, prosecutions, protests, and policy response.

Preserving this distinction prevents Taylor from being remembered only as a victim within a police case.

Early Life and Family

Taylor was born in Grand Rapids, Michigan, on June 5, 1993.

She moved to Louisville during her youth and developed strong family and community ties there.

Her mother, Tamika Palmer, became one of the principal public advocates seeking justice after Taylor's death.

Family members described Taylor as energetic, caring, hardworking, humorous, and committed to helping other people.

Her nickname among relatives and friends included “Breeway,” a name later used for memorial and protest spaces.

Education and Healthcare Work

Taylor attended Western High School in Louisville.

She later pursued healthcare training and became certified as an emergency medical technician.

Taylor worked as an emergency medical technician and emergency-room technician.

Her employment placed her within the workforce responsible for responding to illness, injury, and medical emergencies.

She intended to continue her education and become a nurse.

Personal Life and Aspirations

Taylor maintained a close relationship with her mother and younger sister.

She had formed a relationship with Kenneth Walker and was building an adult life in Louisville.

Family members described her hopes for homeownership, professional advancement, marriage, children, and continued medical work.

These ordinary aspirations became part of the demand that the public remember Taylor as a complete person rather than only as the subject of police records.

The Narcotics Investigation

Louisville police were investigating suspected drug activity associated principally with Jamarcus Glover.

Glover was Taylor's former boyfriend and did not live at her apartment.

Police sought warrants for several locations connected to the investigation, including Taylor's home.

The asserted link to Taylor's address included a claim that Glover received packages there.

Federal prosecutors later alleged that officers lacked probable cause and used false, misleading, and stale information to obtain the warrant.

The Search-Warrant Affidavit

Detective Joshua Jaynes prepared the affidavit supporting the warrant for Taylor's apartment.

The affidavit stated that a postal inspector had verified that Glover received packages at Taylor's address.

Kelly Goodlett later admitted in federal court that this statement was false and misleading.

She admitted that officers did not possess timely and truthful information sufficient to establish probable cause and that she conspired to create a false explanation after Taylor was killed.

Sergeant Kyle Meany approved portions of the warrant process in a supervisory role.

The false-affidavit issue demonstrated that a search can become dangerous before officers reach the door if the information presented to a judge is unreliable.

Fourth Amendment Principles

The Fourth Amendment protects people against unreasonable searches and seizures.

Search warrants must be supported by probable cause and must describe the place to be searched and items to be seized with sufficient particularity.

Officers seeking a warrant must not knowingly or recklessly include material false statements or omit information necessary to a truthful evaluation.

A judge's signature does not cure deliberate deception by officers supplying the facts.

The Taylor case therefore concerned both the manner of entry and the integrity of the process used to obtain judicial authority.

No-Knock Authorization

The warrant authorized officers to enter without first knocking and announcing their identity and purpose.

Police commonly seek no-knock authorization when they claim that advance notice would create danger, allow destruction of evidence, or permit escape.

Nighttime no-knock raids carry heightened risks because occupants may awaken suddenly, mistake police for criminals, possess lawful firearms, or respond before understanding what is happening.

Although the warrant contained no-knock authorization, officers involved in Taylor's raid testified that they decided to knock before forcing entry.

The existence of no-knock authorization and the factual question of whether occupants received effective notice are related but separate issues.

Did Police Announce Themselves?

Officers stated that they knocked repeatedly and announced that they were police.

Walker stated that he and Taylor heard pounding but did not hear a clear police announcement and believed someone was breaking into the apartment.

Most nearby witnesses initially reported that they did not hear officers identify themselves.

One witness later stated that he heard a single police announcement.

Federal trial stipulations in Hankison's case stated that officers knocked and announced, while also noting that there was no indication the occupants had heard the announcement.

The careful conclusion is that officers knocked, but whether Taylor and Walker received effective notice that the people outside were police remained disputed.

The Night of March 13, 2020

Taylor and Walker were inside the apartment shortly after midnight.

After knocking and receiving no response, officers used a battering ram to force the door.

Taylor and Walker moved into the hallway to determine who was entering.

Walker possessed a lawfully owned handgun.

Believing the forced entry was a home invasion, he fired one shot toward the doorway.

Kenneth Walker's Gunshot

Walker's single shot struck Sergeant Jonathan Mattingly in the leg.

Walker later stated that he did not know the people entering were police and fired in defense of himself and Taylor.

His conduct became central to the state determination that officers at the doorway were legally justified in returning fire.

It also became central to later federal rulings concerning whether false warrant information could be treated as the legal cause of Taylor's death.

The case illustrates the lethal danger created when armed police force entry into a home whose lawful occupants believe they are facing unknown intruders.

Police Return Fire

Mattingly fired six rounds down the apartment hallway after being shot.

Detective Myles Cosgrove moved into the doorway and fired sixteen rounds.

Taylor was struck multiple times.

Federal trial stipulations stated that at least two of Mattingly's bullets and at least three of Cosgrove's bullets struck Taylor.

One of Cosgrove's bullets caused the fatal injury.

Walker was not struck.

Brett Hankison's Blind Gunfire

Hankison moved away from the doorway to the side of the apartment.

He fired five shots through a covered sliding-glass door and five through a covered bedroom window.

He could not see a target through the blinds and curtains.

His bullets entered Taylor's apartment, passed through interior walls, and entered a neighboring apartment occupied by adults and a child.

None of Hankison's rounds struck Taylor, Walker, the neighboring family, or another officer.

His conduct nevertheless created the basis for state wanton-endangerment charges and a later federal civil-rights conviction.

Medical Response

Taylor collapsed in the hallway after being shot.

Police initially focused upon the wounded officer, securing the scene, and arresting Walker.

Taylor did not receive immediate effective medical treatment capable of saving her life.

She died on the floor of her home.

The delayed and disorganized response became part of public criticism of the raid and the institutional handling of its consequences.

No Drugs or Evidence Found

Police found no drugs in Taylor's apartment.

The home did not produce evidence establishing that Taylor was participating in the drug activity under investigation.

In later federal proceedings, prosecutors described the warrant as unsupported by probable cause and founded upon false information.

The absence of evidence reinforced the conclusion that Taylor's home should not have been subjected to the dangerous raid.

Arrest and Prosecution of Kenneth Walker

Police arrested Walker after the shooting.

Prosecutors charged him with attempted murder and assault of a police officer.

Walker maintained that he acted in lawful self-defense because he believed unknown intruders were breaking into the apartment.

The Commonwealth initially dismissed the prosecution without prejudice while investigations continued.

In March 2021, a Kentucky judge dismissed the charges with prejudice, preventing the state from refiling them based upon the same incident.

Public Attention and Say Her Name

Taylor's killing did not receive immediate national attention comparable to the response following George Floyd's murder two months later.

Her family, attorneys, local journalists, activists, and community organizations worked to make the warrant problems and her death publicly visible.

COVID-19 restrictions limited traditional public gatherings and funeral practices during the first weeks after her death.

“Say Her Name” became a central demand associated with Taylor and with the broader effort to make Black women and girls harmed by police more visible.

Louisville and National Protests

Protesters gathered repeatedly in downtown Louisville and at Jefferson Square Park.

Demonstrations demanded release of records, termination and prosecution of officers, dismissal of charges against Walker, abolition of no-knock raids, and broader police reform.

Protesters renamed Jefferson Square Park “Injustice Square” or “Breeway.”

After Minneapolis police murdered George Floyd on May 25, Taylor's name became one of the principal names repeated during national and international demonstrations.

Most protests were peaceful, although Louisville and other cities experienced clashes, property damage, arrests, curfews, and police use of force.

Louisville's Breonna's Law

Louisville Metro Council unanimously passed Breonna's Law on June 11, 2020.

The ordinance banned no-knock search warrants within the Louisville Metro Police Department.

It required officers executing search warrants to knock and announce themselves and regulated body-camera activation before, during, and after execution.

The ordinance directly linked Taylor's death to search-warrant policy and attempted to reduce the risk of another surprise nighttime entry.

Kentucky Senate Bill 4

Kentucky enacted Senate Bill 4 in 2021 following statewide review of search-warrant practices.

The law restricted no-knock warrants to specified serious circumstances, required additional authorization, limited execution times, and imposed documentation and planning rules.

The statewide measure did not create the complete prohibition adopted by Louisville.

The distinction between a ban and a restriction is important when comparing local and state reforms.

Civil Lawsuit and $12 Million Settlement

Taylor's mother filed a wrongful-death and civil-rights lawsuit against Louisville and police personnel.

On September 15, 2020, Louisville Metro Government agreed to a $12 million settlement.

The agreement included policy commitments involving search warrants, body cameras, officer accountability, police records, training, housing incentives, social workers, and review of drug-testing procedures.

A civil settlement provides compensation and policy commitments but does not establish criminal guilt.

The payment was one of the largest police-misconduct settlements in Louisville history.

Kentucky Grand Jury

Kentucky Attorney General Daniel Cameron presented evidence to a Jefferson County grand jury in September 2020.

The grand jury indicted Hankison on three counts of first-degree wanton endangerment for bullets that entered a neighboring apartment.

It did not indict Mattingly or Cosgrove for Taylor's death.

Cameron stated that Mattingly and Cosgrove were legally justified in returning fire after Walker shot Mattingly.

Grand jurors later stated publicly that prosecutors had not offered them homicide charges involving Taylor and challenged public descriptions implying that they had independently rejected such charges.

The dispute damaged public confidence in the transparency of the state process.

State v. Brett Hankison

Kentucky prosecuted Hankison for first-degree wanton endangerment of Taylor's neighbors.

The charges did not allege that his bullets killed Taylor.

A jury acquitted Hankison on all state counts in March 2022.

The acquittal prevented state punishment under the charged offenses but did not prohibit a federal prosecution for separate constitutional crimes.

Federal Charges Announced in 2022

On August 4, 2022, the Justice Department announced charges against four current or former Louisville officers.

Joshua Jaynes and Kyle Meany were charged with civil-rights and obstruction offenses connected to the warrant affidavit.

Kelly Goodlett was charged by information with conspiracy involving the false warrant and later cover-up.

Brett Hankison was charged with federal civil-rights offenses for firing blindly into Taylor's apartment and a neighboring home.

None of the officers who entered the apartment were charged federally with directly firing the fatal bullet.

Kelly Goodlett's Guilty Plea

Goodlett pleaded guilty on August 23, 2022.

She admitted conspiring with another detective to falsify the affidavit used to obtain the warrant.

She admitted knowing that the package claim was false, misleading, and stale.

She also admitted participating in an effort to create a false cover story after Taylor's death.

Her plea provided a judicial admission that the warrant process contained deliberate misconduct.

As of August 1, 2026, her sentencing was scheduled for September 23, 2026.

Federal Cases Against Joshua Jaynes and Kyle Meany

Federal prosecutors alleged that Jaynes and Meany participated in preparing or approving a false warrant affidavit and in later obstruction or false statements.

In August 2024, a federal judge dismissed the felony portions alleging that the false warrant legally caused Taylor's death.

The court reasoned that Walker's gunshot and the resulting exchange of fire interrupted the causal chain alleged by prosecutors.

Additional charges remained for a time, and prosecutors filed a superseding indictment.

In March 2026, the Justice Department asked the court to dismiss the remaining cases in the interest of justice.

On March 27, the judge dismissed the charges against Jaynes and Meany with prejudice.

The dismissal did not erase Goodlett's guilty plea or establish that the warrant affidavit was truthful.

Federal Case Against Brett Hankison

Federal prosecutors charged Hankison with willfully using unconstitutional deadly force when he fired into covered windows and doors without seeing a target.

His first federal trial ended in a mistrial in November 2023 because the jury could not reach a verdict.

Prosecutors tried him again in 2024.

On November 1, 2024, a jury convicted Hankison of violating Taylor's Fourth Amendment rights by firing five shots through her covered bedroom window.

The jury found that the offense involved a dangerous weapon and an attempt to kill.

It acquitted him on the count involving the neighboring apartment.

Hankison's Federal Sentence

A federal judge sentenced Hankison to thirty-three months in prison in July 2025.

The court rejected a Justice Department recommendation that he receive only one day of imprisonment and supervised release.

The sentence also included a period of supervised release.

Hankison appealed his conviction and sentence.

His bullets did not strike Taylor, but the conviction established that unconstitutional deadly force can violate a person's rights even when another officer fires the fatal bullet.

No Officer Convicted of Taylor's Homicide

No officer was convicted of murder or manslaughter for firing the bullet that killed Taylor.

State authorities treated Mattingly's and Cosgrove's return fire as legally justified after Walker shot Mattingly.

Hankison's federal conviction concerned his separate volley of blind gunfire and the danger it created for Taylor.

Goodlett's conviction concerned the warrant and cover-up rather than the shooting itself.

This distinction explains why the case produced federal convictions without a homicide conviction for Taylor's death.

Federal Investigation of Louisville Policing

The Justice Department opened a pattern-or-practice investigation of Louisville Metro and the Louisville Metro Police Department in 2021.

In March 2023, the department found reasonable cause to believe that Louisville and LMPD engaged in patterns or practices violating the Constitution and federal law.

Findings included excessive force, unlawful search-warrant practices, discriminatory enforcement against Black people, unlawful stops and searches, violations involving protest, and discrimination against people with behavioral-health disabilities.

The report identified failures of management, training, supervision, and accountability.

It placed the Taylor raid within a broader institutional system rather than treating it as a completely isolated event.

Proposed Louisville Consent Decree

Louisville and the Justice Department negotiated a detailed reform agreement filed in federal court in December 2024.

The proposed decree addressed force, search warrants, stops, arrests, protest response, racial discrimination, behavioral-health encounters, training, supervision, data, and accountability.

In May 2025, the Justice Department announced that it would abandon the Louisville and Minneapolis consent-decree cases.

The Louisville decree never became effective, and a federal judge dismissed the case at the end of 2025.

Louisville officials created a voluntary local “Community Commitment” intended to continue many negotiated reforms with independent monitoring but without federal court enforcement.

Black Women and Police Violence

Taylor's case challenged the assumption that public concern about police violence should focus principally upon men killed during street encounters.

Black women may experience police violence during home raids, traffic stops, mental-health responses, domestic incidents, custody, protest, and efforts to defend themselves or family members.

Their cases have often received less media attention and fewer movement resources.

Taylor's prominence helped expand public recognition of women including Sandra Bland, Rekia Boyd, Atatiana Jefferson, and others.

The Sanctity of the Home

Constitutional law traditionally treats the home as one of the places receiving the strongest protection against government intrusion.

Forced entry after midnight presents exceptional risks to privacy, sleep, family safety, lawful gun ownership, and accurate identification.

Taylor's death demonstrated how false warrant information and tactical decisions can convert a home into a lethal scene.

The case also showed the practical conflict between aggressive drug enforcement and the right of occupants to defend themselves against people they reasonably believe are intruders.

Relationship to the War on Drugs

The warrant arose from narcotics enforcement.

Drug investigations have frequently relied upon informants, surveillance, package allegations, rapid warrants, militarized tactics, and simultaneous searches of multiple locations.

These methods can create pressure to interpret weak associations as probable cause.

Critics argue that the Taylor raid reflected a wider system in which aggressive enforcement imposed severe risks upon Black neighborhoods and people not charged with drug offenses.

The case demonstrates why claimed danger must be supported by truthful, current, address-specific evidence.

Firearms and Self-Defense

Walker lawfully possessed the handgun he fired.

Kentucky law recognizes a right of self-defense within the home under qualifying circumstances.

Police officers also possess a right to defend themselves against gunfire when they reasonably perceive a lethal threat.

The tragedy arose because occupants and police operated from incompatible understandings of the same forced entry.

The warrant process and announcement practices created the circumstances in which those claims collided.

Memorialization and Public Memory

Taylor's image appeared in murals, protest signs, paintings, exhibitions, clothing, public art, and memorial installations.

Artist Amy Sherald painted a major portrait of Taylor that appeared on the September 2020 cover of Vanity Fair.

The portrait later entered the collections of the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture and the Speed Art Museum.

The museum presentation emphasized Taylor's life, Black womanhood, mourning, and the political demand to remember her as more than a case file.

Breonna Taylor as a Public Symbol

Taylor became a symbol of the right to be safe within one's own home.

She also became a symbol of Black women whose deaths receive less recognition than comparable cases involving men.

Public symbols simplify complex events, but Taylor should not be reduced to the phrase “sleeping in her bed.”

She had been in bed earlier, but evidence indicates that she and Walker were standing in the hallway when police forced entry and opened fire.

Correcting that detail does not reduce the injustice of her death. It improves historical accuracy.

Individual and Institutional Accountability

Individual accountability asks whether a particular officer committed a crime, civil wrong, policy violation, or professional offense.

Institutional accountability asks whether warrant procedures, supervision, narcotics strategy, tactical planning, announcement practices, medical response, reporting, and disciplinary systems made the event possible.

Taylor's case produced individual federal convictions, employment terminations, a civil settlement, local legislation, state legislation, and a federal pattern-or-practice report.

It did not produce a homicide conviction for the officer whose bullet killed her.

The mixed outcomes illustrate how legal systems divide responsibility among criminal law, civil law, employment discipline, and institutional reform.

Major Outcomes

Louisville banned no-knock warrants through Breonna's Law.

Kentucky restricted no-knock warrants statewide.

Taylor's family obtained a $12 million civil settlement and policy commitments.

Goodlett admitted conspiring to falsify the warrant affidavit.

A federal jury convicted Hankison of violating Taylor's constitutional rights through unlawful deadly force.

The Justice Department documented systemic civil-rights violations within Louisville policing.

Taylor's case expanded national attention to Black women, nighttime raids, warrant integrity, and the dangers created when lawful gun ownership and unclear police entry collide.

Limits and Unresolved Issues

No officer was convicted of homicide for Taylor's death.

The state grand-jury process produced lasting disputes concerning transparency and the charges presented.

Federal charges against Jaynes and Meany were ultimately dismissed despite the false-warrant conduct admitted by Goodlett.

Goodlett had not been sentenced as of this page's update date.

The proposed federal police consent decree was abandoned before taking effect.

No-knock and quick-knock raids continued to exist under varying rules outside Louisville.

Policy reform did not eliminate deaths arising from flawed warrants, mistaken addresses, poor announcements, or armed home entries.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Taylor was an emergency medical worker, but accounts vary concerning the exact position and employer she held at different times. “Emergency medical technician” and “emergency-room technician” are the most consistently supported descriptions.

She was not the principal target of the narcotics investigation.

Police found no drugs in her apartment.

Taylor was not asleep in bed when she was struck. She and Walker had risen and moved into the hallway after hearing pounding at the door.

Correcting the location of her body does not justify the raid or reduce the significance of her death.

The warrant authorized no-knock entry, but officers testified that they knocked. Whether the occupants heard an effective police announcement remains disputed.

Walker fired the first shot after the door was forced. He stated that he believed intruders were entering, and his criminal charges were dismissed with prejudice.

Cosgrove fired the fatal bullet. Hankison's bullets did not strike Taylor.

Hankison's state acquittal and federal conviction involved different laws and protected interests.

No officer was convicted of Taylor's homicide.

Goodlett's guilty plea established conspiracy involving the false affidavit and cover-up. Jaynes and Meany were not convicted, and their charges were dismissed with prejudice.

A false affidavit can establish misconduct even when later courts dispute whether it was the legal cause of a resulting death.

The 2024 Louisville consent decree was proposed but never became effective.

An allegation, indictment, guilty plea, acquittal, dismissal, administrative firing, and civil settlement are distinct legal outcomes.

Why Breonna Taylor Matters

Taylor's killing made the constitutional protection of the home a central issue in the modern movement against police violence.

It demonstrated how deliberate or reckless falsehoods in a warrant affidavit can produce lethal consequences even when the officers executing the warrant do not know the affidavit is false.

The raid exposed the danger of forced nighttime entry into a home containing armed occupants who may not understand that police are entering.

The case showed that a technically authorized no-knock warrant can remain dangerous even when officers decide to knock if their announcement is not heard or understood.

Taylor's name broadened public recognition of Black women harmed by police and strengthened the Say Her Name movement.

The state grand-jury controversy demonstrated how limited disclosure and incomplete explanations can damage trust in prosecutorial institutions.

Goodlett's plea provided formal confirmation that the warrant affidavit contained deliberate falsehoods, while the dismissal of the other warrant cases illustrated the difficulty of translating institutional misconduct into individual federal convictions.

Hankison's conviction established that blind gunfire can violate the constitutional rights of a person inside a home even when the officer's bullets do not cause the death.

Louisville's local law, Kentucky's statewide restrictions, the civil settlement, and the federal findings demonstrated the policy reach of Taylor's case.

The abandonment of the proposed consent decree showed how reform efforts can lose federal enforcement when political leadership changes.

Breonna Taylor remains historically important as a healthcare worker whose life was taken in her home and as a symbol of the demand that warrants, police entry, use of force, and criminal accountability receive equal scrutiny when the person killed is a Black woman.

Summary

Breonna Taylor was a twenty-six-year-old emergency medical worker who hoped to become a nurse. She was born in Michigan, raised primarily in Louisville, and remembered by her family as caring, hardworking, and deeply connected to the people around her.

Shortly after midnight on March 13, 2020, Louisville police forced entry into Taylor's apartment while executing a warrant connected to a narcotics investigation focused upon a former boyfriend who lived elsewhere. Kenneth Walker fired one shot after believing intruders were entering. Police returned fire, and Myles Cosgrove fired the bullet identified as fatal. Brett Hankison separately fired ten blind shots through covered doors and windows.

Police found no drugs. Walker's charges were dismissed, Louisville paid Taylor's estate $12 million, local and state governments changed warrant laws, Kelly Goodlett admitted conspiring to falsify the warrant affidavit, and a federal jury convicted Hankison of violating Taylor's civil rights. No officer was convicted of Taylor's homicide. Taylor's case became a defining example of the dangers of false warrants, nighttime raids, unclear announcements, and the underrecognition of Black women killed by police.
