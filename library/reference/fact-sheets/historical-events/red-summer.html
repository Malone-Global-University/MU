Red Summer

The nationwide wave of anti-Black mob attacks, massacres, lynchings, labor conflict, and racial fighting in 1919—met in many communities by organized Black resistance and a renewed demand for full citizenship after World War I.

What Was Red Summer?

Red Summer was the name given to the wave of racial violence that spread across the United States in 1919.

White mobs attacked Black people, neighborhoods, homes, businesses, workers, veterans, farmers, and political organizers.

In several cities, Black residents defended themselves collectively, producing sustained armed conflict rather than one-sided mob destruction alone.

The events occurred in different local settings but shared a national structure of white resistance to Black mobility, labor competition, military service, political organization, and demands for equal citizenship.

Origin of the Name

James Weldon Johnson of the NAACP used the term “Red Summer” to describe the widespread bloodshed of 1919.

Johnson investigated racial violence, traveled to affected communities, communicated with federal officials, and publicized the national pattern.

“Red” referred principally to blood, although the violence coincided with the First Red Scare and official accusations that Black activism reflected radical foreign influence.

How Broad Was the Violence?

Authoritative historical summaries commonly identify at least twenty-five major racial outbreaks during the year.

The number varies because some researchers count only sustained urban riots, while others include rural massacres, attempted lynchings, smaller attacks, or related events beyond the summer months.

No single death total captures the entire period because records were incomplete, officials minimized Black deaths, and rural victims sometimes disappeared from formal counts.

Problems With the Term “Race Riot”

“Race riot” may imply that two racial groups entered a conflict as equally situated aggressors.

In many Red Summer events, white mobs initiated attacks, law enforcement failed to protect Black residents, and government institutions treated Black defense as criminal rebellion.

Terms such as anti-Black riot, racial massacre, white mob attack, or lynching may describe particular events more accurately.

“Riot” remains important when it is part of the historical name of an event, commission, court record, or archive.

A National Pattern, Not a Southern Exception

Red Summer violence occurred in the South, North, Midwest, West, and the nation's capital.

Northern cities did not generally duplicate every Southern Jim Crow statute, but housing segregation, employment discrimination, white gangs, police bias, and political exclusion produced racial boundaries.

The nationwide geography challenged the claim that racial oppression belonged only to the former Confederacy.

World War I

The United States entered World War I in 1917.

More than 350,000 African Americans served in the armed forces, most within segregated units.

Black soldiers experienced discrimination in training, assignments, promotion, transportation, and military justice.

Military service nevertheless strengthened demands that a nation fighting for democracy abroad recognize Black citizenship at home.

Black Soldiers and Veterans

Returning veterans wore uniforms that represented national service and claims to equal status.

White civilians and officials sometimes treated the uniform itself as an unacceptable challenge to the racial order.

Veterans were assaulted, forced to remove uniforms, denied public accommodations, and lynched.

Military training also meant that some veterans possessed organizational experience, weapons knowledge, and a determination to defend their communities.

“We Return Fighting”

W. E. B. Du Bois used the language of returning fighting to demand democracy and equal citizenship after the war.

The phrase summarized a wider mood among Black veterans, writers, workers, and organizers.

It did not necessarily mean initiating violence.

It expressed refusal to accept disfranchisement, segregation, lynching, and humiliation after military service.

The Great Migration

World War I accelerated African American migration from the rural South to Northern and Midwestern cities.

Migrants sought industrial employment, education, political participation, family security, and escape from lynching and Jim Crow.

Black newspapers promoted migration and published information about jobs and travel.

White residents and officials responded with employment discrimination, restrictive housing practices, neighborhood violence, and political hostility.

Housing Segregation and Overcrowding

Black migrants were restricted to limited residential districts through custom, violence, real-estate practices, and later restrictive covenants.

Growing populations encountered overcrowding, inflated rents, deteriorating buildings, and limited access to new housing.

White residents attacked Black families crossing neighborhood boundaries.

Housing conflict became a central cause and consequence of urban Red Summer violence.

Labor Conflict

Wartime industries recruited Black workers during labor shortages.

After the armistice, returning white servicemen sought jobs while employers reduced production.

Many labor unions excluded Black workers or restricted them to segregated locals and inferior positions.

Employers sometimes hired Black workers during strikes, creating accusations of strikebreaking even where exclusion had denied Black workers full union membership.

White workers could interpret Black employment as a threat rather than challenge discriminatory employers and unions.

Postwar Economic Instability

Demobilization produced layoffs, labor unrest, inflation, housing shortages, and uncertainty.

Economic stress did not cause racial hatred automatically.

White supremacist institutions directed insecurity toward Black workers and residents and defined equal competition as racial invasion.

Business interests, political machines, newspapers, gangs, and employers sometimes benefited from dividing workers by race.

The First Red Scare

The Russian Revolution, wartime repression, labor strikes, bombings, and political radicalism contributed to the First Red Scare.

Government officials and newspapers associated unions, immigrants, socialists, anarchists, and Black protest with subversion.

Federal investigators searched for Bolshevik influence in Black organizations rather than treating racial injustice as a legitimate cause of protest.

Accusations of radicalism supplied a political excuse for surveillance and repression.

The Wilson Administration

President Woodrow Wilson's administration expanded segregation within federal offices.

Federal authorities monitored Black political organizations and publications.

Wilson condemned some mob violence but did not secure comprehensive federal anti-lynching legislation or national protection against Red Summer attacks.

The federal government's weakness reinforced demands for enforceable civil-rights law.

Lynching in 1919

Red Summer occurred within a broader national regime of lynching.

Black people were killed through hanging, shooting, burning, beating, and public torture, often after unverified accusations or challenges to racial authority.

As many as seventy-two or more lynchings were recorded in 1919, although historical compilations use different definitions and incomplete evidence.

The absence of punishment taught mobs that collective racial violence could proceed without serious consequence.

Sexual Accusations and Racial Myth

White mobs frequently justified violence through accusations that a Black man had assaulted or insulted a white woman.

Newspapers repeated unverified allegations and portrayed Black men as a threat to white womanhood.

These narratives concealed sexual violence against Black women and used gender ideology to defend white political control.

Several major Red Summer outbreaks began after accusations that were false, disputed, or never tested in a fair court.

Black Women During Red Summer

Black women defended homes, organized relief, protected children, raised funds, reported violence, treated injuries, and maintained community networks.

Women were also assaulted, displaced, and exposed to gendered racial threats.

In Washington, seventeen-year-old Carrie Johnson fired at men entering her home during the violence and faced prosecution after a police officer was killed.

Women's participation demonstrates that Black self-defense and political organization were not exclusively male activities.

The Black Press

Black newspapers connected events across regions and challenged white-controlled accounts.

The Chicago Defender promoted migration, reported racial attacks, and defended Black citizenship.

The Crisis, published by the NAACP, documented lynching, veterans' experiences, and political demands.

The Messenger connected racial equality with labor, socialism, and opposition to wartime repression.

Sensationalist White Newspapers

Some white newspapers published inflammatory accusations, exaggerated crime stories, and racial stereotypes.

Headlines could mobilize white crowds before allegations were investigated.

Reports often described Black self-defense as aggression while minimizing the original mob attack.

Competing newspapers sometimes used racial fear to increase circulation.

The NAACP

The NAACP investigated Red Summer violence, publicized evidence, petitioned officials, expanded branches, and supported legal defense.

James Weldon Johnson investigated Washington and interpreted the events as a national crisis.

Walter White used his ability to pass as white to investigate racial violence and obtain information from perpetrators and officials.

The crisis increased interest in organized national civil-rights action.

The New Negro

“New Negro” described a developing spirit of racial pride, political assertiveness, cultural production, migration, and refusal to accept humiliation.

The term had earlier uses but became strongly associated with the postwar generation.

Red Summer showed this transformation through community defense, journalism, veterans' activism, union interest, and national organization.

The New Negro was not one ideology; it included integrationist, nationalist, socialist, labor, religious, and cultural currents.

Claude McKay and “If We Must Die”

Jamaican-born poet Claude McKay wrote “If We Must Die” during the Red Summer period.

The sonnet expressed dignified resistance against mob attack and became one of the era's most recognized literary works.

Its importance reflects how poetry and cultural expression responded to political violence.

Marcus Garvey and Black Nationalism

Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association expanded rapidly during the postwar period.

Garvey promoted Black pride, institution building, international identity, economic independence, and opposition to white domination.

Red Summer strengthened the appeal of movements arguing that Black security could not depend entirely on white institutions.

Charleston, South Carolina

One of the early major 1919 outbreaks occurred in Charleston in May.

White sailors and civilians attacked Black residents, businesses, and pedestrians.

Black residents resisted, and United States Marines and naval personnel were used to restore order.

The involvement of uniformed white servicemen foreshadowed later attacks in Washington.

Jenkins County, Georgia

Violence in Jenkins County developed from conflict at a Black church gathering and disputes involving local law enforcement.

White posses attacked Black residents, and several people were killed.

The event illustrates how rural outbreaks could emerge from police authority, accusations, and efforts to punish an entire Black community.

Longview, Texas

The Longview violence occurred in July 1919 after publication of an article in The Chicago Defender concerning the killing of a Black man and the treatment of a Black woman.

A white group attacked Black teacher and newspaper correspondent Samuel L. Jones.

Armed Black residents defended Jones and other homes.

White mobs burned Black residences and businesses, and authorities imposed martial law.

Organized Defense in Longview

Black residents gathered weapons and established defensive positions after threats and attacks.

Their resistance prevented the violence from remaining completely one-sided.

Authorities responded by disarming Black defenders and arresting Black residents, illustrating how government often treated community defense as the principal public-order problem.

Washington, D.C.

The Washington outbreak began on July 19 after newspapers sensationalized allegations that Black men had attacked white women.

White sailors, soldiers, Marines, veterans, and civilians attacked Black pedestrians, pulled people from streetcars, and moved toward Black neighborhoods.

Police protection was slow and inadequate.

Black residents organized armed defense, and the violence continued for several days before heavy rain and federal military intervention helped end it.

Black Self-Defense in the Nation's Capital

Black veterans and civilians formed patrols, guarded streets, and defended homes.

Women and young people also participated in household protection.

The organized response made Washington a prominent example of Black communities refusing passive submission to mob attack.

White newspapers frequently described defense as evidence of disorder while minimizing the earlier assaults.

Why Washington Was Especially Significant

The violence occurred in the national capital within sight of federal power.

The Wilson administration had segregated federal workplaces and failed to protect Black residents promptly.

The event challenged the nation's claim that racial violence was a local Southern problem.

It also demonstrated the political confidence of a large Black professional, working-class, and veteran community.

Chicago, Illinois

Chicago experienced the most extensively documented urban conflict of Red Summer.

Wartime migration had increased the Black population while restrictive housing and neighborhood violence confined families to the South Side Black Belt.

Labor conflict, white athletic clubs and gangs, political machines, police discrimination, and competition over public space increased tension.

The Killing of Eugene Williams

On July 27, seventeen-year-old Eugene Williams was swimming with other Black youths in Lake Michigan.

Their raft crossed an unofficial racial boundary near a beach used by white residents.

A white man threw stones toward the boys, and Williams drowned.

A white police officer refused to arrest the accused attacker, intensifying anger at the beach.

The Chicago Violence

White gangs attacked Black people traveling to and from work, invaded neighborhood edges, and set fires.

Black residents defended homes, businesses, hospitals, and streets.

Police conduct was inconsistent, and many Black residents believed officers protected white attackers or arrested Black defenders disproportionately.

Illinois National Guard troops eventually entered the affected districts.

Chicago Casualties and Destruction

Thirty-eight people were killed: twenty-three Black and fifteen white.

More than five hundred people were injured, with Black residents forming the majority of the injured.

Fires and attacks left approximately one thousand Black families homeless.

Damage fell heavily on Black households despite fighting involving people from both racial groups.

Chicago Labor Relations

Chicago's stockyards and industrial workplaces employed Black migrants and European immigrant workers.

Union exclusion and employer manipulation made interracial labor solidarity difficult.

Employers had recruited Black workers during strikes, while unions often failed to include them as equal members.

Racial violence weakened workers while preserving the authority of employers and political machines.

Chicago Commission on Race Relations

Illinois Governor Frank Lowden appointed an interracial commission to investigate the riot and its causes.

The commission's 1922 report, The Negro in Chicago, examined housing, employment, policing, migration, public opinion, recreation, and racial contact.

It became an important early institutional study of urban racial inequality.

Investigation did not itself dismantle segregation or punish most perpetrators.

Knoxville, Tennessee

The Knoxville violence occurred August 30–31 after police accused Maurice Mays, a Black political figure, of murdering a white woman.

A white mob stormed the county jail seeking to lynch him, but officials had moved him elsewhere.

The mob looted weapons and entered the Black business district.

Armed Black residents defended the neighborhood, and National Guard forces intervened.

Maurice Mays

Maurice Mays was convicted and executed after trials shaped by racial pressure and disputed evidence.

Many modern historians consider the conviction unreliable or wrongful.

The accusation against him supplied the immediate pretext for a mob assault on a wider Black community.

Omaha, Nebraska

Omaha's violence developed amid labor conflict, migration, sensationalist crime reporting, and racial political tension.

A white woman accused Black packinghouse worker Will Brown of assault.

On September 28, a white mob surrounded and burned the Douglas County Courthouse, overpowered authorities, seized Brown, and lynched him.

The Lynching of Will Brown

The mob beat, shot, hanged, and burned Will Brown's body.

Participants displayed and photographed the remains.

Mayor Edward P. Smith attempted to prevent the lynching, and the mob hanged him before rescuers cut him down.

Federal troops entered Omaha to restore order.

Political Meaning of the Omaha Attack

The Omaha mob attacked the courthouse as well as its prisoner.

The event demonstrated the collapse of legal authority when officials could not or would not protect a Black defendant.

It also showed that public lynching and mass racial terror extended far beyond the South.

Elaine, Arkansas

The Elaine Massacre began in Phillips County on the night of September 30.

Black sharecroppers and tenant farmers affiliated with the Progressive Farmers and Household Union met at a church near Hoop Spur.

They sought legal assistance and fair accounting from white landowners who controlled cotton settlements and credit.

A shooting involving white men near the meeting became the pretext for a large-scale attack on Black residents.

Sharecropping and Cotton Accounts

Black farmers raised cotton on land owned by white planters and depended on landowners and merchants for credit, supplies, marketing, and account settlement.

Farmers frequently suspected that owners withheld proceeds or falsified accounts.

Union organization threatened the economic control of planters.

White officials portrayed the demand for legal accounting as a conspiracy for racial insurrection.

The Elaine Massacre

Armed white posses entered the county and killed Black residents over several days.

Black men, women, and children were attacked across a wide area, including people with no connection to the union meeting.

Arkansas National Guard and federal troops were deployed.

Testimony concerning the role of troops and the scale of killing conflicts, but the official response treated Black residents as insurgents rather than victims of a massacre.

The Disputed Elaine Death Toll

Five white people died.

The number of Black deaths was never established reliably.

Estimates range from dozens to several hundred.

Officials failed to conduct a complete accounting, bodies were removed or buried without adequate documentation, and white authorities controlled the early narrative.

A responsible fact sheet should state the uncertainty rather than present one estimate as settled.

Mass Arrests and Torture

Authorities arrested nearly three hundred Black people.

Prisoners were held in dangerous conditions, and investigators used beatings, threats, and torture to obtain statements.

White participants in the massacre generally avoided comparable prosecution.

The criminal process transformed victims and defenders into defendants.

The Elaine Twelve

Twelve Black men were convicted of murder and sentenced to death after rapid trials.

White mobs surrounded the courthouse, defense representation was grossly inadequate, and all-white juries deliberated briefly.

The NAACP supported appeals and investigations challenging the convictions.

Moore v. Dempsey

In 1923, the Supreme Court held that federal courts could examine whether state convictions had been produced through mob domination and denial of due process.

The decision did not declare every defendant innocent directly.

It opened federal habeas corpus review where state proceedings were an empty form controlled by mob pressure.

Moore v. Dempsey became a major constitutional-development case involving federal protection against racially corrupted state criminal justice.

Other Red Summer Communities

Significant racial attacks or disturbances also occurred in locations including Bisbee, Arizona; Syracuse, New York; New London, Connecticut; Norfolk, Virginia; Newberry, South Carolina; Putnam County, Georgia; and other communities.

Local details differed, but recurring patterns included white servicemen, police bias, lynching accusations, labor disputes, neighborhood boundaries, and attacks on Black mobility.

A national history should not reduce Red Summer to Washington, Chicago, Omaha, and Elaine alone.

Black Armed Self-Defense

Black residents purchased firearms, established neighborhood patrols, guarded homes, and warned one another of approaching mobs.

Veterans often contributed military experience.

Self-defense did not prevent all deaths or destruction, but it altered mob expectations and public memory.

Many Black observers interpreted 1919 as a moment when communities demonstrated they would no longer accept attack without resistance.

Self-Defense and the Law

The legal right of individual self-defense did not guarantee equal treatment by police, prosecutors, or juries.

Black defenders could be arrested while white attackers remained free.

Officials sometimes confiscated weapons from Black neighborhoods before controlling white mobs.

The unequal administration of public order was itself part of the racial system.

Police Conduct

Police responses varied among cities and individual officers.

Common problems included delayed intervention, refusal to arrest white attackers, disproportionate arrest of Black residents, cooperation with mobs, and failure to protect prisoners.

Some officers attempted to stop attacks or protect institutions.

Institutional patterns matter more than isolated examples when evaluating government responsibility.

National Guard and Federal Troops

Governors and federal authorities deployed troops in several major conflicts.

Military forces sometimes stopped mob attacks and restored transportation or emergency services.

In other places, troops disarmed Black defenders, reinforced white official narratives, or contributed to violence.

Military intervention therefore requires local analysis rather than automatic treatment as neutral protection.

Government Failure

Local governments often failed at their most basic obligation: protecting residents from mob violence.

State governments rarely prosecuted perpetrators proportionately to the scale of attacks.

The federal government lacked and did not enact a comprehensive anti-lynching law.

Government failure encouraged further massacres during the early 1920s.

The Campaign for Federal Anti-Lynching Law

The NAACP intensified campaigns for federal legislation holding officials and counties accountable for lynching.

Representative Leonidas Dyer introduced a major federal anti-lynching bill.

The House passed the Dyer bill in 1922, but Southern senators prevented enactment.

The failure illustrated how disfranchisement gave segregationist states congressional power without representing much of their Black population.

Congressional Response

Members of Congress received petitions and reports concerning racial violence.

Congressional action remained constrained by committee power, Senate obstruction, federalism arguments, and the political influence of the segregated South.

No national law enacted immediately after Red Summer supplied the protection civil-rights organizations demanded.

Investigations and Reports

Civil-rights organizations, journalists, grand juries, coroners, state commissions, and federal agencies investigated portions of the violence.

Reports often disagreed about who initiated conflict, the number of deaths, and official responsibility.

White-controlled investigations could minimize mob organization or blame Black residents.

Black newspapers and NAACP reports supplied essential counter-records.

Suppression and Loss of Memory

Many communities minimized Red Summer in official histories, school curricula, monuments, and newspapers.

Families sometimes avoided public discussion because perpetrators or their descendants retained local power.

Massacre sites lacked markers, and victims' names disappeared from public records.

Archival research and community commemoration have recovered much of the history, but important gaps remain.

Commemoration

Centennial projects in 2019 expanded public knowledge of Red Summer.

Museums, archives, universities, churches, descendants, artists, and local historical organizations developed exhibits, markers, conferences, and digital projects.

Chicago community initiatives have worked to identify and commemorate each person killed in the 1919 riot.

Elaine memorial efforts continue to confront disputed numbers and local resistance to the term massacre.

Red Summer and the Tulsa Race Massacre

The Tulsa Race Massacre occurred May 31–June 1, 1921.

It was not part of the 1919 Red Summer.

Tulsa belonged to the broader postwar wave of white attacks on Black communities, including Ocoee in 1920, Rosewood in 1923, and other events.

Connecting these histories is useful, but dates and event names should remain distinct.

Red Summer and East St. Louis

White mobs attacked Black workers and residents in East St. Louis, Illinois, in 1917.

The massacre preceded Red Summer but revealed many of the same conditions: industrial labor conflict, migration, white union hostility, police failure, and mob destruction.

East St. Louis influenced Black protest, NAACP organizing, and the 1917 Silent Parade in New York.

Red Summer and the Houston Mutiny

The Houston conflict of 1917 involved Black soldiers of the Twenty-Fourth Infantry, white police violence, military discipline, and an armed march.

It was not part of Red Summer.

Its mass courts-martial and executions formed part of the wartime context shaping Black distrust of law enforcement and military justice.

Connection to the Harlem Renaissance

Red Summer occurred during the development of the New Negro movement and the cultural flowering later called the Harlem Renaissance.

Migration, veterans' experiences, racial violence, urban life, African diasporic identity, and political militancy shaped literature, music, art, and journalism.

Cultural production did not stand apart from political conflict; it interpreted and challenged the racial order exposed in 1919.

Civil-Rights Legacy

Red Summer expanded NAACP membership and strengthened demands for federal action.

It encouraged documentation, litigation, local branch organization, anti-lynching campaigns, and Black political participation.

The willingness of communities to defend themselves became an important tradition alongside nonviolent protest and courtroom strategy.

Later civil-rights movements inherited both the institutional gains and unresolved federal failures of 1919.

Labor Legacy

Red Summer demonstrated the danger of racially divided labor movements.

Excluding Black workers allowed employers to use race to weaken strikes and made Black employment appear dependent on anti-union action.

Later interracial union campaigns attempted to organize workers across racial lines, although discrimination remained within organized labor.

Housing Legacy

Urban violence hardened neighborhood boundaries and accelerated restrictive real-estate practices.

White homeowners, associations, brokers, lenders, and governments developed additional mechanisms to contain Black residential expansion.

The resulting segregation shaped schools, wealth, public services, transportation, and later conflicts over integration.

Political Legacy

Black migrants became increasingly important voters in Northern cities where they could register more readily than in the disfranchised South.

Political parties competed for Black votes while often failing to confront housing, employment, and police discrimination.

Red Summer strengthened the understanding that migration and voting could alter national political coalitions.

  • Black newspapers including The Chicago Defender and movement journals including The Messenger;
  • local white newspapers, wire reports, editorials, advertisements, and crime reporting;
  • police, sheriff, coroner, jail, court, grand-jury, military, and National Guard records;
  • federal intelligence, Justice Department, War Department, and presidential records;
  • World War I service records, veterans' papers, unit histories, and pension files;
  • census, migration, employment, union, stockyard, housing, and property records;
  • Chicago Commission on Race Relations testimony and The Negro in Chicago;
  • Elaine trial records, NAACP defense files, affidavits, and Moore v. Dempsey materials;
  • photographs, postcards, maps, fire records, hospital files, and insurance claims;
  • church, business, fraternal, women's club, and neighborhood organization records;
  • oral histories, family records, cemetery evidence, and descendant testimony; and
  • modern memorial, archaeological, digital-mapping, and community-history projects.

Research Method

Red Summer research must compare official records with Black newspapers, organizational investigations, and family testimony.

Police and newspaper terms such as “riot,” “insurrection,” and “sniper” may encode the perspective of authorities rather than establish neutral fact.

Casualty numbers should be attributed and uncertainty stated explicitly.

Researchers should identify who initiated violence, which institutions had legal authority, whose property was destroyed, who was arrested, and whose testimony was accepted.

Common Misconceptions

“Red Summer Was One Riot”

It was a nationwide wave of distinct outbreaks, massacres, lynchings, and conflicts occurring in many communities during 1919.

“The Name Referred Primarily to Communism”

Red Scare politics overlapped with the violence, but James Weldon Johnson's term referred principally to bloodshed.

“The Violence Occurred Only in the South”

Major events occurred in Washington, Chicago, Knoxville, Omaha, Longview, Elaine, Charleston, and many other locations across regions.

“Race Riot Means Both Sides Were Equally Responsible”

Many events began with white mob attacks. Black residents sometimes fought back, but self-defense does not erase the initiating aggression or unequal institutional power.

“Black Veterans Caused the Violence by Becoming Militant”

Veterans demanded rights and sometimes defended communities. White resistance to their equality, not military service itself, produced the racial conflict.

“Economic Competition Alone Caused Red Summer”

Labor and housing conflict mattered, but white supremacy determined why Black workers and residents became targets.

“The Great Migration Created Racism in Northern Cities”

Migration changed demographics and exposed existing discrimination. Black movement did not create white supremacy.

“Chicago's Violence Was Only About a Beach Accident”

Eugene Williams's killing was the immediate trigger. Housing segregation, gangs, labor conflict, police bias, and political machines created the larger conditions.

“Elaine Was a Black Insurrection”

Black farmers were organizing for fair cotton accounting. White authorities used claims of insurrection to justify massacre, mass arrest, torture, and prosecutions.

“The Elaine Death Toll Is Known Precisely”

It is not. Reliable estimates range from dozens into the hundreds because officials never conducted an adequate accounting.

“Federal Troops Always Protected Black Communities”

Troops sometimes stopped mobs, but in other places they disarmed Black defenders, reinforced hostile authorities, or participated in coercion.

“The Government Punished the Mobs”

Prosecution was generally limited and unequal. Black residents were often arrested in large numbers while white attackers avoided conviction.

“The Tulsa Race Massacre Was Part of Red Summer”

Tulsa occurred in 1921. It belonged to the wider postwar era of anti-Black mass violence but not the 1919 Red Summer.

“Black Self-Defense Began in 1919”

African Americans had defended families and communities throughout slavery, Reconstruction, and Jim Crow. Red Summer made organized urban and veteran self-defense especially visible nationally.

“Red Summer Ended White Mob Violence”

Major attacks continued during the early 1920s, including Ocoee, Tulsa, and Rosewood.

Key Distinctions

Red Summer
The nationwide wave of racial violence occurring principally during spring through fall 1919.
Race riot
A historical label for collective racial violence; the term may conceal unequal aggression and power.
Anti-Black riot
Collective violence in which white attackers target Black people or communities.
Racial massacre
Large-scale killing of a racial community, often accompanied by state failure or participation.
Lynching
Extrajudicial killing or terror carried out by a group claiming authority outside lawful due process.
Black self-defense
Individual or collective action protecting people, homes, businesses, or neighborhoods against attack.
Great Migration
The mass movement of African Americans from the South to other regions during the twentieth century.
New Negro
A postwar expression of Black racial pride, assertiveness, cultural development, and political demand.
First Red Scare
The postwar fear and repression directed at radicals, labor movements, immigrants, and political dissent.
White Citizens' Council
A later twentieth-century form of public white supremacist organization; it should not be confused with the mobs and local associations operating in 1919.
Restrictive covenant
A property agreement used to prohibit sale or occupancy by specified racial groups.
Mob domination
A legal process controlled by threats or public violence rather than independent courts and due process.
Moore v. Dempsey
The 1923 Supreme Court case allowing federal review of state convictions produced through mob-dominated proceedings.
Tulsa Race Massacre
The 1921 destruction of Tulsa's Greenwood district, related to the broader postwar pattern but outside Red Summer.

Why Red Summer Matters

Red Summer demonstrated that white supremacy was a national system rather than a Southern regional exception.

The violence emerged wherever African Americans challenged racial boundaries in employment, housing, public space, voting, farming, military status, or community organization.

Black veterans and civilians defended neighborhoods visibly, altering national expectations about how African Americans would respond to mob attack.

The Black press and NAACP connected local violence into one national account and challenged official narratives that blamed Black communities.

Elaine demonstrated the relationship among racial violence, agricultural labor exploitation, police power, coerced confession, and mob-dominated courts.

Chicago demonstrated how migration, segregation, gangs, labor division, policing, and neighborhood boundaries could produce sustained urban conflict.

Washington and Omaha showed that neither the national capital nor the Midwest stood outside the lynching and riot tradition.

Government failure strengthened the demand for federal anti-lynching and civil-rights legislation, even though Congress did not enact comprehensive protection at the time.

Red Summer contributed to the New Negro movement, the Harlem Renaissance, Black nationalism, labor activism, civil-rights organization, and a longer tradition of community self-defense.

Understanding 1919 is essential for interpreting later racial massacres, housing segregation, policing, federal civil-rights law, and the continuing struggle over how the United States remembers collective racial violence.

Summary

Red Summer was the nationwide wave of racial violence that spread across the United States in 1919. James Weldon Johnson of the NAACP used the name to describe the bloodshed.

World War I, demobilization, the Great Migration, labor conflict, housing segregation, lynching, white press agitation, and the First Red Scare shaped the setting. These conditions became violent because white institutions treated Black employment, migration, military service, union activity, land organization, and equal citizenship as threats.

White mobs attacked Black people and communities in Charleston, Longview, Washington, Chicago, Knoxville, Omaha, Elaine, and many other locations. Black veterans and civilians defended homes, streets, businesses, churches, and families in several cities.

Chicago's conflict began after white beachgoers attacked Black teenager Eugene Williams and he drowned. Thirty-eight people died, more than five hundred were injured, and approximately one thousand Black families were left homeless.

In Washington, white servicemen and civilians attacked Black pedestrians and neighborhoods, but organized Black residents fought back. In Omaha, a white mob burned the courthouse and lynched Will Brown. In Longview and Knoxville, Black residents mounted organized defense against white attacks.

Elaine was a rural massacre connected to Black sharecroppers' efforts to obtain fair cotton settlements. White posses killed an unknown number of Black residents—credible estimates range from dozens into the hundreds—and authorities arrested nearly three hundred Black people.

The Elaine prosecutions eventually produced Moore v. Dempsey, which allowed federal courts to review state convictions created through mob domination and denial of due process.

The NAACP, Black press, veterans, writers, and community organizations interpreted Red Summer as proof that racial oppression was national and required organized resistance. The events contributed to the New Negro movement, anti-lynching campaigns, Black political organization, the Harlem Renaissance, and traditions of community self-defense.

Red Summer did not end racial massacres. Tulsa in 1921 and Rosewood in 1923 belonged to the continuing postwar pattern. The history remains essential to understanding housing segregation, labor division, policing, mob violence, federal civil-rights failure, and the long struggle for equal citizenship.
