Presidential Reconstruction

Andrew Johnson's executive-led program for rapidly restoring civil government in the former Confederate states without requiring Black suffrage or comprehensive federal civil-rights guarantees.

What Was Presidential Reconstruction?

Presidential Reconstruction was the executive-led process through which President Andrew Johnson attempted to restore civil government and ordinary constitutional relations in the former Confederate states after the Civil War.

Johnson issued a broad amnesty proclamation, granted individual pardons, appointed provisional governors, directed federal departments to resume operations, and authorized loyal white voters to elect state constitutional conventions. Those conventions and the governments formed under them were expected to repudiate secession, abolish slavery, reject Confederate debt, and resume participation in the federal system.

The program did not require states to enfranchise African American men, establish equal civil rights, redistribute land, or exclude most former Confederates permanently from political authority.

Congress rejected Johnson's claim that executive restoration was sufficient to determine when the former Confederate states were entitled to congressional representation. The resulting conflict transformed Reconstruction into a major constitutional struggle over presidential power, congressional authority, citizenship, race, federalism, and the meaning of freedom after slavery.

Presidential Reconstruction and Presidential Restoration

Andrew Johnson commonly described his policy as restoration. That term reflected his view that the Southern states had never lawfully left the Union and therefore did not need to be created again as states.

Under this theory, rebellion had destroyed or displaced lawful state governments, but the states themselves remained within the constitutional Union. The president's task was to restore loyal civil administration and federal law.

Historians commonly use Presidential Reconstruction to distinguish Johnson's executive program from the Congressional or Military Reconstruction system that replaced it.

The terms should not imply that Congress had no role during 1865 and 1866. Congress retained constitutional authority over legislation, constitutional amendments, appropriations, and the admission and qualifications of its own members. The central dispute concerned which branch could determine the political status and governing conditions of the former Confederate states.

Lincoln's Wartime Reconstruction as a Precursor

President Abraham Lincoln issued a Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction on December 8, 1863. It offered pardons to many participants in the rebellion who took a loyalty oath and accepted federal emancipation policy.

Lincoln's plan allowed a new loyal state government to be organized when the number of participating voters reached at least ten percent of the votes cast in that state during the 1860 presidential election. This became known as the Ten Percent Plan.

Congress responded with the Wade–Davis Bill of 1864, which required a much larger body of loyal voters and a more demanding loyalty oath. Lincoln did not sign the measure before Congress adjourned, producing a pocket veto.

Johnson inherited the executive premise that the president could help reorganize Southern state government. His 1865 proclamations, however, did not simply reproduce Lincoln's ten-percent threshold. They relied on provisional governors, loyalty oaths, and voters qualified under pre-secession state law.

Johnson's program also developed after Confederate military defeat rather than during an ongoing war. It returned political authority and property to former Confederates more rapidly and provided fewer protections for freedpeople than many Republicans had expected.

Andrew Johnson Assumes the Presidency

Abraham Lincoln was assassinated on April 14, 1865, and died the next morning. Vice President Andrew Johnson took the presidential oath on April 15.

Johnson was a Tennessee Democrat who had remained in the United States Senate after his state seceded. Lincoln later appointed him military governor of Tennessee, and the National Union Party selected him as Lincoln's running mate in 1864.

Some congressional Republicans initially expected Johnson to impose strict penalties on leading Confederates. Johnson had publicly denounced the planter elite and spoken in severe terms about treason.

Once in office, however, he adopted a rapid restoration policy. Congress had adjourned in March and was not scheduled to reconvene until December, allowing Johnson to implement his program largely through executive action during the intervening months.

Johnson's Constitutional Theory

Johnson maintained that secession was legally void. The former Confederate states had remained states within the Union, even though rebellion had disrupted their lawful governments.

He relied on presidential responsibilities as chief executive and commander in chief, the duty to execute federal law, the pardon power, and the constitutional guarantee of a republican form of government.

Johnson argued that once loyal civil governments were organized, the states were entitled to exercise their constitutional functions and send representatives to Congress.

Congressional Republicans disputed this framework. They argued that Congress possessed decisive authority over reconstruction conditions, the recognition of republican state governments, and the seating of senators and representatives.

The disagreement was not merely procedural. Johnson's theory enabled rapid restoration without federal guarantees of Black political participation, while congressional plans increasingly tied restoration to national citizenship and civil-rights protection.

Amnesty and Pardon Proclamation of May 29, 1865

Johnson issued his principal amnesty proclamation on May 29, 1865. It offered amnesty and pardon to many people who had participated in the rebellion if they took and maintained a prescribed oath of allegiance.

A qualifying pardon restored political and property rights, except ownership claims in enslaved people and property already involved in certain federal confiscation proceedings.

The oath required the applicant to support and defend the Constitution and Union, obey federal laws and proclamations concerning emancipation, and faithfully perform the commitment.

The proclamation excluded fourteen categories from automatic amnesty. These included leading Confederate civil and military officials, certain former United States officers and officeholders, people accused of unlawful treatment of Black prisoners of war, and people whose taxable property exceeded $20,000.

Excluded individuals could apply directly to the president for a special pardon. Johnson granted thousands of individual pardons, including many to wealthy planters and former Confederate leaders.

Purpose and Effect of Presidential Pardons

Pardons served several functions. They encouraged formal submission to United States authority, required an oath of future loyalty, restored legal capacity, and enabled former Confederates to recover property and resume political and economic activity.

Johnson initially used the individual-pardon process to require members of the planter elite to petition him personally. This gave the president discretionary leverage over former Confederate leadership.

In practice, broad pardon grants restored much of the prewar white political and economic leadership. Former Confederate officials and officers returned to state office, legislatures, courts, local government, and congressional elections.

A presidential pardon did not itself compel either chamber of Congress to seat a member-elect. The House and Senate retained constitutional authority to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of their members.

Pardons also did not erase every legal consequence of rebellion, invalidate later constitutional disqualifications, or guarantee recovery of property already transferred through completed legal proceedings.

The North Carolina Proclamation as the Administrative Model

On May 29, 1865, Johnson appointed William W. Holden provisional governor of North Carolina. Similar proclamations were later issued for the other states governed through Johnson appointees.

Holden was directed to establish rules for electing a convention of loyal delegates. To vote or serve as a delegate, a man had to take the presidential amnesty oath and satisfy the voter qualifications in force under North Carolina law immediately before secession.

Because prewar state voting law excluded African American men, the process restored government through a white electorate. Johnson left later voter qualifications and officeholding rules largely to the conventions and state legislatures.

The proclamation directed the military to assist rather than obstruct the provisional governor. It also ordered the State, Treasury, Post Office, Justice, Navy, and Interior Departments to restore federal administration, courts, taxation, customs, postal service, and public-property control.

The structure illustrates that Presidential Reconstruction was more than a pardon program. It was an executive administrative plan for rebuilding state and federal operations through presidentially selected officials.

State Reorganization Process

Although details varied among states, the Johnson process generally followed these stages:

  1. The president appointed a provisional governor or recognized an existing loyal government.
  2. Federal departments resumed civil operations and military officers assisted the transition.
  3. Eligible white men took loyalty or amnesty oaths and registered under state procedures.
  4. Voters elected delegates to a state constitutional convention.
  5. The convention repealed or declared void the ordinance of secession.
  6. The state abolished slavery through its constitution or ordinance.
  7. The state repudiated Confederate debt.
  8. Voters elected governors, legislatures, and other officials under the revised state constitution.
  9. State legislatures considered ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment.
  10. The state elected senators and representatives and sought recognition in Congress.

Johnson treated completion of this process as sufficient restoration of state government. Congress did not accept that the president could determine congressional representation unilaterally.

Abolition and the Thirteenth Amendment

Johnson required or pressed the reorganizing states to abolish slavery. State conventions repealed slavery provisions and acknowledged the destruction of the institution.

The Thirteenth Amendment supplied a national constitutional rule that did not depend on the continuing validity of wartime emancipation measures or individual state action.

Several governments organized under Presidential Reconstruction ratified the amendment. Georgia's ratification on December 6, 1865, supplied the approval needed from three-fourths of the states then counted by the federal government.

Ratification abolished slavery but did not settle citizenship, suffrage, land ownership, labor rights, equal protection, or the political status of the reorganized state governments.

Exclusion of African Americans From the Electorate

Johnson did not require Black male suffrage. He maintained that voting qualifications were principally matters for the states and opposed federal imposition of African American political rights.

The state conventions and elections held under his program therefore relied overwhelmingly on white voters. Approximately four million formerly enslaved people had no direct vote in creating the governments that would regulate their freedom.

African Americans nonetheless organized conventions, petitions, public meetings, churches, schools, mutual-aid institutions, and political associations. They demanded voting rights, equal law, land, education, family security, and federal protection.

The exclusion of freedpeople from the reconstruction electorate became one of the central congressional objections to Johnson's program.

Civil Rights and State Authority

Presidential Reconstruction did not require states to enact a comprehensive code of equal civil rights. Johnson expected ordinary state government to regulate contracts, property, courts, policing, family law, labor, and voting.

This approach left freedpeople dependent on institutions controlled by former enslavers and other white officials. Many state courts restricted Black testimony, local police enforced discriminatory labor rules, and legislatures adopted separate legal standards.

Johnson opposed broad federal civil-rights intervention and argued that states should resume their traditional authority once loyal government had been restored.

Congress increasingly concluded that state control without federal minimum rights would preserve slavery's practical incidents under new legal forms.

Land Restoration

Johnson's pardons generally restored property rights other than ownership of enslaved people. This policy had major consequences for land occupied or administered by the Union Army and Freedmen's Bureau.

Thousands of freedpeople had settled on abandoned, confiscated, tax-sale, or military-controlled lands. Some families occupied coastal land under General William T. Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15 and expected to obtain independent farms.

Johnson directed that land be returned to pardoned former owners when they retained or recovered lawful title. Freedmen's Bureau Circular No. 15 implemented restoration policy in September 1865.

Many Black families were removed from land they had occupied or improved and were forced to negotiate labor arrangements with former enslavers. The policy weakened prospects for broad Black land ownership and contributed to tenancy and sharecropping.

Not every parcel was restored, and some Black families retained or purchased land under specific tax-sale, statutory, or private arrangements. The larger pattern, however, favored restoration to former white owners rather than redistribution to freedpeople.

Relationship to the Freedmen's Bureau

Congress established the Freedmen's Bureau in the War Department on March 3, 1865, before Johnson became president. The Bureau administered relief, labor contracts, schools, medical care, land, legal complaints, family matters, and related assistance.

Johnson appointed Major General Oliver O. Howard as commissioner but frequently disagreed with congressional efforts to expand the Bureau's authority.

His pardons and land-restoration policy directly affected Bureau administration. Agents were required to return much property to pardoned former owners even when freedpeople had occupied or cultivated it.

Johnson vetoed a major Bureau extension bill on February 19, 1866. A revised extension later became law over his veto on July 16, 1866.

The Bureau therefore operated during Presidential Reconstruction but was not simply an instrument of Johnson's political program. It became one of the principal institutions through which Congress attempted to protect freedpeople against the limitations of that program.

Return of Former Confederates to Political Power

Presidential pardons and the rapid election timetable enabled many former Confederate leaders to regain political authority during 1865.

Southern voters selected former Confederate officers and officials for governorships, legislatures, courts, local offices, and Congress. Alexander H. Stephens, former vice president of the Confederacy, was elected to the United States Senate by the Georgia legislature.

To congressional Republicans, these elections suggested that the defeated ruling class was being restored without adequate evidence of loyalty or acceptance of emancipation.

Johnson viewed the participation of the reorganized states as necessary to restore the constitutional Union. Congress viewed the elected delegations as evidence that executive restoration had failed to transform political power.

Black Codes

State legislatures organized under Presidential Reconstruction enacted Black Codes during 1865 and 1866. The laws differed among states but commonly regulated African American labor, movement, residence, contracts, occupations, apprenticeships, property, weapons, courts, and criminal punishment.

Vagrancy laws allowed officials to arrest people who lacked approved employment or residence. Contract rules punished workers who left employers, and apprenticeship systems could place Black children under white control.

Black Codes demonstrated that formal abolition did not prevent states from building coercive systems designed to preserve plantation labor and white political control.

African Americans resisted through migration, labor action, petitions, court complaints, community organization, military service networks, conventions, and appeals to federal authorities.

Northern reports of the Black Codes substantially weakened support for Johnson's restoration policy.

Congress Reconvenes in December 1865

The Thirty-Ninth Congress convened on December 4, 1865. Delegations elected under Johnson's reconstructed governments arrived seeking seats.

Congress refused to seat the Southern delegations while it investigated the status of the former Confederate states and the conditions facing freedpeople and Unionists.

On December 13, Congress created the Joint Committee on Reconstruction, composed of nine representatives and six senators. The committee collected testimony from military officers, Southern officials, formerly enslaved people, and other witnesses.

The committee concluded that the former Confederate states lacked governments and constitutional relations sufficient for normal representation and that stronger national protection was necessary.

Congress's refusal to recognize Johnson's completed governments established the central institutional conflict of Presidential Reconstruction.

The Dispute Over Representation

Johnson argued that loyal governments had been restored and that continued exclusion of Southern representatives denied states their constitutional rights.

He also contended that Congress was incomplete and politically illegitimate when eleven Southern states lacked representation.

Congressional leaders answered that each chamber had explicit authority to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of its members. They maintained that Congress could determine whether states possessed loyal republican governments entitled to representation.

The dispute concerned not only whether individual members qualified, but also whether governments elected under white-only restoration processes could represent populations that now included millions of free citizens.

Freedmen's Bureau Veto

Congress passed legislation in early 1866 to extend and expand the Freedmen's Bureau. Supporters expected Johnson might approve the measure because it was sponsored by moderate Republican Senator Lyman Trumbull.

Johnson vetoed the bill on February 19, 1866. He argued that the Bureau was unnecessary after the war, created expensive federal dependency, intruded on state authority, and conferred special benefits on freedpeople.

Congress initially failed to override the veto. The episode broke the remaining expectation that Johnson and moderate Republicans would cooperate on Reconstruction.

A revised Bureau bill later passed both chambers and became law over Johnson's second veto on July 16, 1866.

Conflict Over the Civil Rights Act of 1866

Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1866 to declare national citizenship and protect equal legal capacity in contracts, courts, evidence, property, personal security, and punishment.

Johnson vetoed the bill on March 27, 1866. He questioned congressional authority, objected to federal intervention in state civil law, and argued that the measure favored African Americans.

The Senate overrode the veto on April 6, and the House completed the override on April 9. The act became the first major federal legislation enacted over a presidential veto.

The override demonstrated that Congress could conduct Reconstruction policy despite presidential opposition. It also established civil rights and national citizenship as federal responsibilities.

The Fourteenth Amendment

Congress proposed the Fourteenth Amendment on June 13, 1866. It constitutionalized national citizenship and prohibited states from abridging privileges or immunities, denying due process, or denying equal protection.

The amendment also addressed representation, rebellion-related officeholding, public debt, and congressional enforcement authority.

Johnson opposed the amendment and encouraged Southern states to reject it. Tennessee ratified and regained representation in Congress in July 1866, but the other former Confederate states initially rejected the proposal.

Rejection confirmed congressional concerns that the Johnson governments would not voluntarily accept national civil-rights conditions. Ratification later became a requirement under the Reconstruction Acts.

Racial and Political Violence

Violence against African Americans and white Unionists intensified the conflict over Presidential Reconstruction.

In Memphis in May 1866, white police officers, firefighters, civilians, and others attacked Black residents, killing dozens and destroying homes, churches, and schools.

In New Orleans on July 30, 1866, police and white civilians attacked a state constitutional convention and its Black supporters. The violence killed and wounded numerous people.

Federal investigations described local officials as participants in or facilitators of violence. These events showed that reconstructed civil governments could not or would not protect freedpeople and Republican political activity.

Johnson did not create these attacks, but his policies and public alignment with white Southern restoration reduced congressional confidence in his willingness to confront racial violence effectively.

The “Swing Around the Circle”

During August and September 1866, Johnson undertook a speaking tour commonly called the “Swing Around the Circle.” He campaigned for congressional candidates who supported his restoration policy.

Johnson attacked Radical Republicans, defended the legitimacy of the reconstructed states, and portrayed congressional civil-rights policy as destructive of constitutional government.

His confrontational speeches, exchanges with crowds, and personal attacks damaged his public standing. Rather than building support for Presidential Reconstruction, the tour strengthened Republican arguments that Johnson was unfit to direct national reconstruction.

Congressional Elections of 1866

Reconstruction became the central issue in the 1866 congressional elections. Republicans presented the Civil Rights Act and Fourteenth Amendment as necessary protections against the restoration of Confederate power.

Johnson campaigned for a coalition supporting immediate restoration and opposing congressional conditions.

Voters returned overwhelming Republican majorities to Congress. The new balance gave congressional Republicans secure power to override presidential vetoes and impose a reconstruction program without Johnson's approval.

The election effectively ended Presidential Reconstruction as the controlling national policy, although the formal replacement occurred when Congress enacted the Reconstruction Act in March 1867.

End of Presidential Reconstruction

On March 2, 1867, Congress enacted the First Reconstruction Act over Johnson's veto. The statute divided ten former Confederate states into five military districts and treated their existing civil governments as provisional.

The new framework required federally supervised voter registration without racial exclusion, constitutional conventions elected by qualified Black and white men, new state constitutions, and ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The act did not erase every government, official, or legal action created under Johnson's program. It subordinated those governments to congressional and military authority and required a new reconstruction process.

Johnson remained president and continued resisting congressional policy, but Presidential Reconstruction no longer governed the terms of state restoration.

Relationship to Johnson's Impeachment

Presidential Reconstruction ended before Johnson's impeachment, but the conflict it created led directly toward that constitutional crisis.

Congress enacted the Tenure of Office Act and command legislation intended to restrict Johnson's ability to remove officials and redirect military enforcement.

Johnson attempted to remove Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton, a supporter of Congressional Reconstruction. The House impeached Johnson on February 24, 1868.

The Senate acquitted him in May 1868 by one vote short of the two-thirds majority required for conviction. The trial concerned specific alleged legal violations and abuses of authority, not merely disagreement over Reconstruction policy.

Impeachment should therefore be understood as an outgrowth of the executive-legislative struggle initiated during Presidential Reconstruction, not as part of the 1865 state-restoration procedure itself.

Limited Achievements

Presidential Reconstruction helped restore federal postal, customs, tax, judicial, and administrative operations in the former Confederacy.

State conventions repudiated secession, abolished slavery in state law, rejected Confederate debt, and organized functioning civil institutions.

Governments organized during this period contributed state ratifications necessary for adoption of the Thirteenth Amendment.

The pardon and oath process encouraged formal submission to United States authority and enabled the demobilization of a vast wartime system.

These accomplishments restored basic governmental operations, but they did not establish an adequate settlement of citizenship, civil rights, political representation, land, labor, or racial violence.

Major Failures and Limitations

Exclusion of Freedpeople

The people most affected by emancipation were excluded from voting in the conventions and elections that reconstructed state government.

Restoration of Former Confederate Leadership

Broad pardons and rapid elections returned much of the former ruling class to political and economic power.

Black Codes

State governments used law to restrict Black freedom and preserve coercive labor relationships after formal abolition.

Land Restoration

Returning property to former enslavers displaced Black families and weakened economic independence.

Inadequate Civil-Rights Protection

The program supplied no binding federal minimum of equal citizenship, court access, personal security, or voting rights.

Failure to Secure Congressional Legitimacy

Johnson attempted to complete political restoration without agreement from the branch constitutionally responsible for seating members and enacting national law.

Failure to Prevent Violence

Reconstructed state and local institutions frequently failed to protect African Americans, Unionists, and Republican organizers from organized violence.

Destruction of Executive-Legislative Cooperation

Johnson's vetoes, public rhetoric, and refusal to compromise converted a policy dispute into a sustained constitutional confrontation.

Historical Assessment

Presidential Reconstruction succeeded at rapid administrative restoration but failed as a durable civil and political settlement.

Its central design assumed that loyal white-controlled state governments could regulate emancipation with limited federal intervention. The resulting governments demonstrated that abolition alone did not ensure equal citizenship or lawful freedom.

Johnson's use of pardons and provisional governors was legally significant and administratively effective in rebuilding formal institutions. The deeper problem was the standard by which restoration was judged complete.

By accepting repudiation of secession and slavery without requiring Black suffrage, equal civil rights, reliable protection, or transformation of land and labor power, the program restored government more quickly than it reconstructed the social order created by slavery.

Relationship to Related Subjects

Presidential Reconstruction and Reconstruction

Presidential Reconstruction was one phase of the larger Reconstruction era, not a synonym for the entire postwar process.

Presidential Reconstruction and Military Reconstruction

Presidential Reconstruction relied on executive restoration and white-controlled state conventions. Military Reconstruction placed ten states under statutory Army supervision and enfranchised qualified Black men.

Presidential Reconstruction and the Reconstruction Acts

The Reconstruction Acts were congressional statutes enacted to replace the state-restoration system created under Johnson.

Presidential Reconstruction and the Black Codes

The Black Codes were enacted by state and local governments restored under the presidential framework and became major evidence of its failure.

Presidential Reconstruction and the Freedmen's Bureau

The Bureau was a separate War Department agency created by Congress. It operated during Johnson's presidency but often became the institutional counterweight to state policies permitted by Presidential Reconstruction.

Presidential Reconstruction and the Thirteenth Amendment

State governments organized under Johnson helped ratify abolition, but the amendment did not itself determine citizenship, voting rights, or state representation.

Why Presidential Reconstruction Matters

Presidential Reconstruction established the first postwar framework for restoring state and federal government after the defeat of the Confederacy.

It demonstrated the institutional power of presidential pardons, appointments, military command, proclamations, and federal administration in a national emergency.

It also revealed the limits of restoring government without reconstructing citizenship and political power. State institutions could formally abolish slavery while preserving racial control through labor law, criminal law, land ownership, and political exclusion.

The program produced the decisive constitutional conflict between the executive and legislative branches over who controlled Reconstruction and what conditions legitimate republican government required.

Congress's rejection of Presidential Reconstruction led directly to the Civil Rights Act of 1866, Fourteenth Amendment, Reconstruction Acts, Black male suffrage, and Military Reconstruction.

Its history remains central to questions about executive power, federalism, pardons, postwar governance, civil rights, transitional justice, institutional legitimacy, and the difference between formal reunion and substantive freedom.

Summary

Presidential Reconstruction was Andrew Johnson's executive-led program for restoring civil government in the former Confederate states between 1865 and 1867. Johnson issued pardons, appointed provisional governors, restored federal administration, and authorized loyal white voters to elect conventions and governments.

The reconstructed states repudiated secession, abolished slavery, rejected Confederate debt, and contributed to ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment. The program did not require Black suffrage, comprehensive civil rights, broad land redistribution, or long-term federal supervision.

Former Confederates rapidly returned to power, state legislatures enacted Black Codes, pardons restored plantation property, and freedpeople remained excluded from formal political reconstruction.

Congress refused to recognize the program as a sufficient basis for representation. Conflict over the Freedmen's Bureau, Civil Rights Act, Fourteenth Amendment, racial violence, and state legitimacy culminated in the Republican electoral victory of 1866 and enactment of the Reconstruction Act on March 2, 1867.

Presidential Reconstruction restored governmental form more quickly than it secured equal citizenship. Its failure became the principal justification for Congressional and Military Reconstruction.
