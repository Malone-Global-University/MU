Secession

The attempted withdrawal of eleven slave states from the United States in 1860–1861, the creation of the Confederate States of America, and the constitutional crisis that became the American Civil War.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Constitutional crisis, political movement, state convention process, attempted national separation, rebellion, and immediate cause of the Civil War.
General definition
Secession is the claimed withdrawal of a political unit from a larger political body in order to become independent or join another political entity.
Principal United States event
The attempted withdrawal of eleven Southern slave states from the United States between December 20, 1860, and June 8, 1861.
First state
South Carolina, December 20, 1860.
First wave
South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas seceded before the attack on Fort Sumter.
Second wave
Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee seceded after Fort Sumter and President Abraham Lincoln's call for 75,000 militia.
States remaining in the Union
Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained United States states despite slavery, internal division, and Confederate efforts to claim Kentucky and Missouri.
Immediate political trigger
Abraham Lincoln's election on November 6, 1860, as the first Republican president.
Central institutional cause
Fear that Republican control would restrict slavery's expansion, weaken slaveholding political power, resist fugitive-slave demands, and place slavery on a path toward eventual extinction.
Claimed constitutional theory
Secessionists argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states and that a state could withdraw when the compact was violated.
United States position
The Union was perpetual; unilateral state ordinances were legally void; resistance to federal authority was insurrection or rebellion rather than lawful national withdrawal.
Confederate government created
Delegates from the first seven seceding states convened at Montgomery, Alabama, on February 4, 1861, and established the Confederate States of America.

Confederate forces attacked Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor on April 12, 1861.

In Texas v. White (1869), the Supreme Court held that unilateral secession ordinances were legally null and described the Union as indissoluble except through revolution or consent of the states.
Historical significance
Secession converted the long political struggle over slavery into armed rebellion. It tested whether an election defeat permitted states to dissolve the constitutional government, whether the Union could defend itself, and whether slaveholding interests could create a separate republic.

What Is Secession?

Secession is the claimed act of withdrawing from a political union.

A secession movement ordinarily seeks independence, a new confederation, or incorporation into another state.

Secession should be distinguished from ordinary political opposition, nullification of a particular law, territorial partition within a country, and successful revolution.

In United States history, “Secession” most commonly refers to the Southern crisis of 1860–1861.

Attempted Secession and Successful Independence

The eleven state conventions declared that their states had left the Union.

The United States rejected that claim and continued to treat the states and their residents as subject to the Constitution and federal law.

The Confederacy exercised substantial de facto control over territory and conducted war, diplomacy, taxation, conscription, and government.

It never secured internationally recognized independence and was defeated militarily.

“Attempted secession” therefore describes the legal and historical outcome more accurately than assuming the states became foreign nations.

Secession and Nullification

Nullification is a claim that a state may declare a federal law invalid within its territory.

Secession is a broader claim that a state may withdraw from the Union entirely.

John C. Calhoun and other states' rights theorists developed compact arguments useful to both doctrines.

The Nullification Crisis of 1832–1833 ended through compromise and federal pressure without South Carolina leaving the Union.

Secession and Revolution

A constitutional right of secession would permit withdrawal under the existing legal order.

A right of revolution claims moral or political authority to overthrow or escape an existing government outside ordinary constitutional procedure.

Some secessionists used both arguments, comparing Southern withdrawal to the American Revolution.

Unionists responded that revolution might succeed through force but was not a lawful power granted to one state by the Constitution.

Compact Theory

Secessionists described the Constitution as a compact ratified by sovereign peoples acting through states.

If other states or the federal government materially violated the compact, a state allegedly could resume powers it had delegated.

State conventions were presented as the highest expression of the state's sovereign people.

Opponents argued that the Constitution created a government acting directly on individuals, not merely a revocable league among governments.

Perpetual Union Theory

Unionists emphasized that the Articles of Confederation had described the Union as perpetual.

The Constitution sought “a more perfect Union” rather than a weaker one dissolvable by one member.

New states entered on equal footing within an existing national constitutional order.

Lincoln argued that no government could contain an implied power for a minority of its members to terminate it unilaterally.

Constitutional Silence

The Constitution contains no clause expressly authorizing a state to secede.

It also contains no clause using the word “secession” to prohibit withdrawal.

Antebellum arguments therefore relied on structure, ratification history, sovereignty, federal supremacy, reserved powers, and political theory.

The Civil War and Texas v. White later established the controlling national legal position against unilateral secession.

Ratification-Era Reservations

New York, Virginia, and Rhode Island used language during ratification reserving powers to the people.

Secessionists later cited such language as evidence of a retained withdrawal power.

Unionists argued that all states entered the same constitutional relationship and that no unilateral exit condition was accepted by the other states.

Ratification debates did not produce one explicit, universally accepted secession clause.

Earlier Disunion Threats

Americans discussed disunion before 1860 during conflicts over foreign policy, expansion, tariffs, slavery, and sectional power.

New England Federalists met at the Hartford Convention during the War of 1812 but did not adopt a secession ordinance.

South Carolina threatened resistance during the Nullification Crisis.

Abolitionist William Lloyd Garrison later advocated dissolution of a Union he considered corrupted by slavery.

None of these earlier movements created an eleven-state rival government and continental war.

John C. Calhoun

Calhoun developed an influential theory of state sovereignty, concurrent majorities, and constitutional protection for sectional minorities.

His political objective was protection of the slaveholding South against a potentially antislavery national majority.

Calhoun usually emphasized nullification and constitutional safeguards rather than immediate secession during the Nullification Crisis.

Later secessionists used his compact reasoning to justify withdrawal.

Long Sectional Crisis

Secession emerged from decades of conflict over slavery's expansion, fugitive enforcement, representation, party power, abolitionism, and federal territories.

The Missouri Compromise, Compromise of 1850, Kansas–Nebraska Act, Dred Scott decision, and violence in Kansas failed to produce a durable settlement.

Each crisis raised the question of whether slavery would remain locally protected, expand nationally, or be placed on a path toward restriction.

Fugitive-Slave Conflict

Slave states demanded federal recovery of people escaping bondage.

Northern personal-liberty laws and resistance were portrayed as violations of constitutional obligations.

South Carolina's declaration of immediate causes devoted extensive attention to Northern hostility toward slave property and fugitive enforcement.

The dispute shows that secessionists invoked states' rights while also demanding strong federal action against free states and individuals.

Slavery in the Territories

Expansion into western territory determined the future balance between slave and free states.

Slaveholders demanded equal access to federal territories with enslaved property and, increasingly, federal protection for slavery there.

Republicans opposed extension of slavery into the territories while generally denying federal power to abolish it immediately within existing states.

Secessionists viewed territorial restriction as political containment and eventual destruction of slavery.

Dred Scott v. Sandford

The Supreme Court ruled in 1857 that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories.

The majority also denied federal citizenship to Black people descended from enslaved Africans under its interpretation.

Republicans rejected the decision as controlling national policy beyond the immediate case.

Southern demands for acceptance of the decision deepened the sectional crisis.

John Brown's Raid

John Brown attacked the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry in October 1859 in an effort to initiate armed resistance to slavery.

United States forces captured Brown, and Virginia executed him.

White Southerners feared wider slave revolt and Northern support for violence.

Northern commemoration of Brown convinced many slaveholders that slavery was unsafe within the Union.

Division of the Democratic Party

The Democratic national convention met at Charleston in April 1860 and divided over federal protection of slavery in territories.

Southern delegates withdrew when the convention would not adopt their platform.

Northern Democrats nominated Stephen A. Douglas, while Southern Democrats nominated John C. Breckinridge.

The split reduced the possibility of a unified Democratic victory against the Republicans.

Constitutional Union Party

Former Whigs and moderates formed the Constitutional Union Party and nominated John Bell.

The party emphasized the Constitution, Union, and enforcement of law while avoiding a detailed sectional platform on slavery.

Bell carried Virginia, Kentucky, and Tennessee in the presidential election.

The result demonstrated significant Upper South resistance to immediate secession before Fort Sumter.

Election of 1860

Abraham Lincoln won the presidency on November 6, 1860.

He received approximately 40 percent of the popular vote and a majority of the Electoral College.

Lincoln's name did not appear on the ballot in most Deep South states.

His victory resulted from strong Northern support and division among Democratic and Constitutional Union opponents.

He campaigned against extension of slavery rather than immediate abolition within existing states.

Why Lincoln's Election Triggered Secession

Republicans had captured the presidency without electoral support from the slaveholding Deep South.

Secessionists argued that this demonstrated permanent sectional minority status and loss of national protection.

They expected Republican appointments, territorial policy, patronage, postal administration, and moral opposition to weaken slavery.

The election did not abolish slavery, but it convinced secessionists that the future direction of federal power had changed.

Immediate Secessionists, Cooperationists, and Unionists

Immediate secessionists
Favored rapid unilateral action by each state after Lincoln's election.
Cooperationists
Favored coordinated action among slave states, additional negotiations, or delay before withdrawal.
Conditional Unionists
Preferred remaining in the Union unless the federal government used force or refused specified guarantees.
Unconditional Unionists
Rejected secession and remained loyal to the United States despite state action.

These positions shifted rapidly as conventions met, federal property was seized, compromise failed, and war began.

South Carolina Secession

South Carolina's legislature called a convention after Lincoln's election.

Delegates assembled at Columbia and moved to Charleston because of a smallpox outbreak.

On December 20, 1860, the convention unanimously adopted an ordinance declaring the state's 1788 ratification of the Constitution repealed and the Union dissolved.

South Carolina became the first state to claim withdrawal.

South Carolina Declaration of Immediate Causes

On December 24, the convention adopted a statement explaining its action.

It argued that states had violated constitutional obligations concerning fugitive slaves and had denounced slavery as sinful.

It identified Republican organization and Lincoln's election as evidence that the federal government would become hostile to slaveholding interests.

The declaration presented states' rights through grievances centered on slavery.

Federal Property in South Carolina

State forces seized or controlled federal facilities around Charleston while Major Robert Anderson moved his United States garrison from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter.

South Carolina treated federal property as belonging to an independent state and demanded evacuation.

The United States maintained that forts, arsenals, customhouses, and other federal sites remained national property.

This conflict transformed constitutional claims into an immediate military crisis.

Star of the West

President James Buchanan's administration sent the civilian steamship Star of the West to reinforce and supply Fort Sumter.

South Carolina batteries fired on the ship on January 9, 1861, and it withdrew.

The incident occurred the same day Mississippi adopted its ordinance.

Open general war did not begin, but armed resistance to United States authority had already occurred.

Mississippi Secession

Mississippi adopted its ordinance on January 9, 1861.

Its declaration of causes stated directly that the state's position was thoroughly identified with slavery.

It connected slave labor to cotton, international commerce, racial ideology, territorial expansion, and opposition to the Republican Party.

Mississippi became the second state to claim withdrawal.

Florida Secession

Florida's convention adopted an ordinance on January 10, 1861.

State forces seized several federal facilities, while Fort Pickens near Pensacola remained under United States control.

Florida had a small white population but strategic ports, forts, and Gulf coastline.

Alabama Secession

Alabama's convention adopted an ordinance on January 11, 1861.

Voting revealed geographic division, with stronger Unionist or cooperationist sentiment in northern counties.

Alabama invited delegates from seceding states to meet at Montgomery and form a new government.

Montgomery became the first Confederate capital.

Georgia Secession

Georgia's convention adopted an ordinance on January 19, 1861.

Immediate secessionists defeated cooperationists who preferred collective action or further guarantees.

Alexander H. Stephens argued against immediate withdrawal before voting with his state and later becoming Confederate vice president.

Georgia's declaration of causes emphasized Republican efforts to restrict and ultimately end slavery.

Louisiana Secession

Louisiana's convention adopted an ordinance on January 26, 1861.

New Orleans, the Mississippi River, customs revenue, sugar, cotton trade, and federal military facilities gave the state exceptional strategic value.

State authorities seized the United States Mint, customs facilities, arsenals, and other property.

Representative John Edward Bouligny remained in the United States House and rejected secession.

Texas Secession

A convention adopted Texas's ordinance on February 1, 1861.

Voters approved the ordinance in a referendum on February 23, and it took effect on March 2 under the convention's schedule.

The declaration of causes defended African slavery, white supremacy, and slaveholding access to federal territory.

Texas became the seventh state to join the first secession wave.

Sam Houston's Opposition

Governor Sam Houston opposed secession and warned that war would bring devastation.

He accepted the referendum's result as political fact but refused to take the oath required by Confederate authority.

State officials removed him from office.

Houston's removal demonstrates that state action did not represent unanimous white opinion.

Secession Commissioners

Seceding states sent commissioners to other slave states to urge them to join.

Commissioners addressed legislatures, conventions, governors, and public meetings.

Their speeches warned that Republican power threatened slavery, racial hierarchy, property, and Southern security.

These contemporary appeals are major evidence for understanding secessionist purpose.

Declarations of Causes

South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas issued detailed public declarations explaining secession.

All four defended slavery or slaveholding rights prominently.

They discussed fugitive-slave enforcement, territorial slavery, racial hierarchy, abolitionism, Republican politics, and alleged constitutional violations.

Not every seceding state issued a separate detailed declaration, so evidence also comes from convention debates, commissioners, newspapers, resolutions, and leaders' speeches.

Slavery as the Central Cause

The first seven states were heavily committed to slaveholding agriculture and seceded after an antislavery-expansion party won the presidency.

Their official explanations repeatedly identified slavery, Black status, fugitive recovery, and slaveholding equality in territories.

Secession did not occur because Congress had abolished slavery; no such abolition had taken place.

It occurred because secessionists feared the future consequences of Republican national power.

States' Rights

Secessionists framed withdrawal as an exercise of state sovereignty.

The rights they most urgently claimed involved slavery, slave property, territorial access, and resistance to antislavery state laws.

They also demanded federal enforcement of fugitive-slave obligations against Northern states.

“States' rights” therefore describes the constitutional mechanism asserted, but it does not identify the underlying policy interest unless the specific right is named.

Tariffs and Economic Grievances

Tariffs had produced major sectional conflict during the Nullification Crisis and remained part of Southern political rhetoric.

Southern producers also criticized Northern industry, shipping, finance, and federal economic policy.

The detailed 1860–1861 declarations placed far greater emphasis on slavery than on the existing tariff.

Tariff conflict should therefore be treated as historical background and a secondary grievance rather than a substitute for the documentary record on slavery.

Racial Ideology

Secessionist documents defended a political and social order based on white supremacy.

Texas's declaration explicitly described white and African-descended people as unequal races and defended African servitude.

Confederate leaders presented racial hierarchy as natural, divinely sanctioned, and necessary for social order.

This ideology linked slavery to citizenship, suffrage, labor, land, and national identity.

President Buchanan's Position

James Buchanan denied that states possessed a constitutional right to secede.

He also argued that the federal government lacked clear constitutional power to coerce a state back into submission.

This combination produced an ineffective response while federal property was seized and the Confederacy organized.

Buchanan attempted to preserve federal forts and pursue compromise without initiating general war.

Seizure of Federal Property

State and Confederate forces seized arsenals, forts, customhouses, mints, revenue facilities, ships, weapons, and other United States property.

Some federal officers surrendered facilities; others destroyed supplies or held strategic posts.

The seizures supplied the new Confederacy with weapons and infrastructure.

They also demonstrated that secession was not merely a paper declaration but an attempt to displace United States sovereignty.

Montgomery Convention

Delegates from the first seven seceding states met in Montgomery, Alabama, beginning February 4, 1861.

They acted as a provisional Confederate Congress.

The convention adopted a provisional constitution on February 8 and selected Jefferson Davis as provisional president and Alexander H. Stephens as vice president.

Davis was inaugurated on February 18.

Confederate Constitution

The permanent Confederate Constitution was adopted on March 11, 1861.

It closely resembled the United States Constitution but explicitly protected slavery and property in enslaved people.

It required protection of slavery in Confederate territories and prohibited laws impairing slave property.

These provisions reveal the institution the new government was designed to secure.

Confederate Diplomacy

Confederate leaders sought peaceful recognition as an independent republic.

Commissioners approached the United States government and foreign powers.

The Lincoln administration refused to treat Confederate commissioners as representatives of a lawful foreign nation.

Foreign governments later recognized Confederate belligerency but never granted formal diplomatic recognition of independence.

Crittenden Compromise

Kentucky senator John J. Crittenden proposed constitutional amendments and resolutions in December 1860.

The plan would have extended the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific and protected slavery south of it.

It also sought stronger guarantees concerning slavery and fugitive recovery.

Republicans rejected concessions permitting slavery's territorial expansion, while secession continued.

Senate Committee of Thirteen

The Senate created a special committee to consider compromise proposals.

It included leading Republicans, Democrats, and Constitutional Unionists.

The committee failed to agree on a plan acceptable to both Republicans and secessionist interests.

Its failure reflected the central dispute over whether slavery would be protected in federal territories.

Washington Peace Conference

Former president John Tyler chaired a peace conference in Washington during February 1861.

Delegates from participating states proposed constitutional protections intended to prevent further secession.

The plan did not win the required congressional support.

Several states already outside the Union did not participate as ordinary members seeking compromise.

Corwin Amendment

Congress proposed a constitutional amendment in March 1861 stating that no amendment could authorize Congress to abolish or interfere with domestic institutions within a state, including persons held to labor.

The proposal attempted to reassure slave states that the federal government would not abolish slavery where it already existed.

Lincoln stated in his inaugural address that he had no objection to making the existing principle express and irrevocable.

The amendment was not ratified and did not stop secession.

Why Compromise Failed

Republicans believed their electoral victory would be meaningless if they surrendered the central platform against slavery's expansion.

Immediate secessionists believed Republican government itself was unacceptable and that delay endangered Southern unity.

Proposals protecting slavery within existing states did not satisfy demands for territorial expansion and federal slave protection.

Each new secession and federal-property seizure reduced trust and increased the cost of reversing course.

Lincoln's First Inaugural Address

Lincoln took office on March 4, 1861.

He repeated that he had no lawful intention to interfere with slavery in states where it existed.

He declared the Union perpetual and state secession ordinances legally void.

He promised to hold, occupy, and possess federal property and collect federal revenue while avoiding unnecessary invasion.

He placed responsibility for initiating violence on those resisting national authority.

Fort Sumter Crisis

Fort Sumter's garrison had limited food and supplies.

Lincoln decided to send provisions and notified South Carolina that the mission would supply the fort rather than land troops or weapons unless resisted.

Confederate leaders demanded evacuation and concluded that allowing resupply would weaken their claim to sovereignty.

Confederate batteries opened fire before the relief expedition arrived.

Attack on Fort Sumter

Confederate forces began firing at approximately 4:30 a.m. on April 12, 1861.

Major Robert Anderson's garrison returned fire but lacked sufficient supplies and ammunition for prolonged defense.

Anderson agreed to surrender on April 13 and evacuated on April 14.

No soldier was killed during the bombardment itself, although an accidental explosion during the surrender salute caused deaths.

Lincoln's Call for 75,000 Militia

On April 15, Lincoln called for 75,000 militia to suppress combinations obstructing federal law.

Governors were assigned troop quotas.

Upper South leaders now had to choose whether to provide troops against the Confederacy or join it.

The call transformed conditional Unionists into secessionists in Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina.

Upper South Secession

The Upper South contained strong Unionist sentiment and initially rejected immediate secession.

These states were slaveholding societies, but their economies, geography, party traditions, and military exposure differed from the Deep South.

Fort Sumter and the troop call reframed the decision as whether to participate in coercion against fellow slave states.

Slavery remained part of the underlying sectional conflict even when the immediate second-wave trigger was federal military mobilization.

Virginia Secession

Virginia's convention initially rejected secession on April 4.

After Fort Sumter and Lincoln's troop call, delegates adopted an ordinance on April 17 by a vote of 88 to 55.

Voters ratified the ordinance on May 23.

Virginia transferred military resources to Confederate cooperation before the popular vote and became the Confederacy's most important industrial and strategic state.

Richmond as Confederate Capital

Confederate authorities moved the national capital from Montgomery to Richmond after Virginia joined.

Richmond offered population, industry, railroads, government buildings, and symbolic prestige.

Its proximity to Washington made Virginia the principal eastern battlefield.

Western Virginia Unionism

Most delegates from northwestern Virginia opposed secession.

Unionists organized Wheeling conventions and created the Restored Government of Virginia recognized by the United States.

With consent from that recognized government and Congress, western counties formed West Virginia.

West Virginia entered the Union on June 20, 1863.

Arkansas Secession

Arkansas voters and convention delegates initially resisted immediate withdrawal.

After Lincoln's troop call, the convention reconvened and adopted an ordinance on May 6, 1861.

Only a small minority of delegates resisted the final action.

Northern Arkansas and other areas retained substantial Unionist sentiment.

Tennessee Secession

Tennessee voters rejected calling a secession convention in February 1861.

After the troop call, the legislature entered a military league with the Confederacy and adopted a declaration of independence and ordinance for popular approval.

Voters approved separation on June 8.

East Tennessee remained strongly Unionist and supplied thousands of soldiers to the United States.

North Carolina Secession

North Carolina voters narrowly rejected a convention in February.

The state refused Lincoln's troop request and seized federal military facilities after the crisis escalated.

A convention assembled at Raleigh and adopted an ordinance on May 20, 1861.

Mountain and coastal regions contained persistent Unionist resistance.

The Eleven-State Confederacy

  1. South Carolina — December 20, 1860;
  2. Mississippi — January 9, 1861;
  3. Florida — January 10, 1861;
  4. Alabama — January 11, 1861;
  5. Georgia — January 19, 1861;
  6. Louisiana — January 26, 1861;
  7. Texas — February 1, 1861, subject to referendum and scheduled effect;
  8. Virginia — April 17, 1861, subject to referendum;
  9. Arkansas — May 6, 1861;
  10. North Carolina — May 20, 1861; and
  11. Tennessee — approved by voters June 8, 1861.

Border Slave States

Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the United States.

Their location, population, rivers, railroads, industry, and access to Washington made them strategically critical.

Residents divided between Union, Confederate, and neutrality positions.

Remaining in the Union did not mean immediate abolition or unanimous support for Lincoln.

Delaware

Delaware's legislature rejected secession.

The state had the smallest enslaved population among slave states and strong economic ties to the North.

Delaware remained loyal while slavery continued legally until the Thirteenth Amendment.

Maryland

Maryland surrounded Washington on three sides and controlled vital rail links.

A pro-secession mob attacked Massachusetts soldiers in Baltimore on April 19, 1861.

The legislature did not call a secession convention, and the state remained in the Union.

Federal arrests, military occupation, suspension of habeas corpus, political pressure, and strong Unionist support all shaped the outcome.

Kentucky

Kentucky initially declared neutrality and rejected demands from both governments.

Confederate movement into the state in September 1861 undermined neutrality, and the recognized government aligned with the Union.

A rival Confederate convention created a provisional government that the Confederacy admitted as representing Kentucky.

Kentucky never adopted a valid statewide secession ordinance through its recognized government.

Missouri

Missouri's elected convention rejected secession.

Conflict between Union forces and pro-Confederate governor Claiborne Fox Jackson produced civil war within the state.

The recognized Unionist government remained associated with the United States.

A rival government passed a secession ordinance and was admitted to the Confederacy, but it never exercised stable control over the state as a whole.

Why Confederate Flags Sometimes Had Thirteen Stars

The Confederacy admitted rival governments claiming Kentucky and Missouri.

This produced a claimed thirteen-state Confederacy in symbolism and representation.

Kentucky and Missouri remained legally and institutionally divided, and their recognized state governments remained in the United States.

Thirteen stars should not be interpreted as proof that thirteen states successfully seceded.

Southern Unionism

Hundreds of thousands of white Southerners opposed secession, supported the United States, resisted Confederate authority, or changed allegiance during the war.

Strong Unionist regions included East Tennessee, western Virginia, northern Alabama, western North Carolina, the Texas Hill Country, and parts of Arkansas and Louisiana.

Unionists served in United States forces, guided armies, hid deserters, organized local governments, and resisted conscription.

Their existence disproves the claim that every resident of a seceding state supported Confederate independence.

Black People and the Secession Crisis

Enslaved and free Black people were excluded from secession conventions and voting.

They were the population whose status and labor were most directly at issue.

Black communities gathered information, anticipated war, resisted forced labor, escaped, and later joined United States forces.

Secession was conducted in the name of state sovereignty while denying political consent to millions of enslaved residents.

Women and Secession

Women could not vote in state secession elections or serve as convention delegates.

They participated through petitions, correspondence, public ceremonies, fundraising, journalism, household influence, and political networks.

White women divided among secessionist, cooperationist, and Unionist positions.

Enslaved women confronted intensified surveillance, forced labor, family danger, and opportunities for self-emancipation as war approached.

State Conventions and Democratic Legitimacy

Secessionists claimed special conventions represented the sovereign people more directly than ordinary legislatures.

Voting was restricted primarily to white men under state law.

Several states submitted ordinances to referenda, while others did not.

Election timing, intimidation, limited debate, unequal representation, and rapid military events affected public choice.

A convention vote cannot be treated as consent from the entire population.

Geographic Voting Patterns

Plantation and slaveholding regions commonly gave secession stronger support than many mountain, small-farm, and border regions.

Patterns differed by state and cannot be reduced to one formula.

Transportation, party history, slaveholding rates, local elites, economic ties, and fear of war influenced voting.

County-level analysis reveals internal civil conflict hidden by state-level maps.

Withdrawal From Congress

Senators and representatives from seceding states left Washington at different times and under different procedures.

Some gave farewell speeches; some resigned; others simply stopped attending.

Congress later declared seats vacant or expelled members considered engaged in rebellion.

Southern withdrawal reduced opposition to Republican wartime legislation.

Consequences for Federal Legislation

Secession removed many slave-state representatives who had blocked Republican economic and antislavery measures.

Wartime Congress enacted the Homestead Act, Pacific Railway Act, Morrill Land-Grant Act, national banking legislation, confiscation laws, District of Columbia emancipation, and territorial abolition.

Secession intended to protect slavery but helped create the congressional conditions for national policies hostile to it.

Secession and Federal War Powers

Lincoln treated resistance as an insurrection requiring suppression.

He called militia, expanded armed forces, proclaimed a blockade, suspended habeas corpus in specified contexts, and convened Congress.

Congress later authorized and funded the war while debating the constitutional basis of emergency actions.

The crisis expanded national administrative and military capacity.

Blockade and Belligerency

Lincoln proclaimed a blockade of Confederate ports beginning in April 1861.

A blockade ordinarily operates in international war, but the United States continued to deny Confederate sovereignty.

Courts and governments recognized that a civil war could create belligerent conditions without legal recognition of independence.

Britain and other powers recognized Confederate belligerency but not nationhood.

Confederate Claim of Independence

Confederate leaders maintained that states had lawfully withdrawn and created a separate nation.

They described United States military action as invasion and subjugation.

Confederate law treated loyalty to the United States within claimed territory as treasonous or disloyal.

The United States treated Confederate civil and military authority as rebellion against lawful national government.

Secession and the Cause of the Civil War

Slavery was the central sectional issue producing secession.

Secession was the political act that created a rival government and attempted to remove federal authority.

The Confederate attack on Fort Sumter initiated open general warfare.

These are connected but distinct levels of explanation: slavery produced the sectional crisis; secession converted crisis into attempted disunion; armed conflict determined whether disunion would succeed.

Secession and Emancipation

The United States initially fought officially to preserve the Union.

Enslaved people entered United States lines, withdrew labor, supplied intelligence, and demanded freedom.

Confiscation laws, the Emancipation Proclamation, Black military service, and the Thirteenth Amendment transformed the war.

The secession movement designed to protect slavery instead created the war that destroyed it.

Defeat of Secession

Confederate armies surrendered separately during April–June 1865.

Richmond fell, Jefferson Davis was captured, and Confederate civil administration collapsed.

No foreign government recognized Confederate independence before defeat.

United States victory established that the 1860–1861 withdrawal attempt had failed.

Postwar Repeal or Repudiation of Ordinances

During Presidential Reconstruction, former Confederate states held conventions and addressed their secession ordinances.

Some repealed ordinances; others declared them null, void, or without legal effect.

Congress also required repudiation of Confederate debt and ratification of constitutional changes as part of restoration.

Terminology mattered because declaring an ordinance void supported the position that lawful statehood had never ended.

Secession and Reconstruction

The United States treated former Confederate states as continuing states whose lawful governments had been displaced or corrupted by rebellion.

Presidential and congressional leaders disagreed over when and how representative government could be restored.

Readmission to congressional representation required new governments and, eventually, ratification of Reconstruction amendments under federal conditions.

Reconstruction addressed the constitutional and social consequences of failed secession and slavery's destruction.

Texas v. White

The Supreme Court decided Texas v. White in 1869.

The dispute concerned United States bonds transferred by Confederate-era Texas authorities.

Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase's majority opinion held that Texas had remained a state despite rebellion.

The Court described admission to the Union as an indissoluble relationship and held the secession ordinance legally null.

It stated that separation could occur through revolution or consent of the states, not unilateral constitutional withdrawal.

Scope of Texas v. White

The decision is the leading Supreme Court statement on unilateral state secession.

It arose after the Civil War rather than serving as a prewar adjudication accepted by all parties.

Its reference to revolution describes successful extraconstitutional change, not an ordinary legal procedure available to a state government.

Its reference to consent does not define one automatic modern process but rejects unilateral withdrawal.

Treason and Prosecution

Confederate officials and soldiers had acted against the United States, but large-scale treason trials did not follow the war.

Jefferson Davis was imprisoned and indicted but never brought to a completed treason trial.

Paroles, pardons, amnesty, political reconciliation, evidentiary problems, and concern about constitutional litigation shaped policy.

Absence of widespread trials did not constitute legal recognition of secession.

Pardons and Amnesty

Presidents Lincoln and Andrew Johnson issued pardon and amnesty policies under specified conditions.

Congress imposed political disabilities through Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment on certain former oath-bound officials.

The Amnesty Act of 1872 removed most such disabilities.

Reconciliation restored political participation without validating the legality of secession.

Lost Cause Memory

Lost Cause interpretation reframed secession as a principled defense of states' rights, constitutional liberty, and local self-government.

It minimized slavery and racial ideology in official declarations and speeches.

It portrayed Confederate defeat as resulting from overwhelming numbers rather than strategic, political, economic, and institutional failure.

Veterans' organizations, monuments, textbooks, memorial groups, and films spread this interpretation.

The “States' Rights” Memory Debate

Secessionists did argue for a state right to withdraw.

Historical explanation must identify why they sought to exercise that claimed right in 1860–1861.

The declarations of causes and commissioners' speeches identify protection of slavery and racial order as the principal policy objective.

Treating “states' rights” as a complete cause without naming the contested rights removes the central subject from the record.

Modern Legal Status

No state possesses a recognized unilateral constitutional right to leave the United States.

Political advocacy for independence is protected in many forms by freedoms of speech and association, but an ordinance alone would not legally remove a state from the Union.

Partitioning an existing state to create a new state is a different process governed by Article IV and requires specified state and congressional consent.

Secession should also be distinguished from local boundary changes or withdrawal from voluntary organizations.

Historical Records

Important sources include:

  • state secession ordinances;
  • declarations of immediate causes;
  • convention journals, debates, votes, and correspondence;
  • state legislative and gubernatorial records;
  • popular referendum returns and county election data;
  • secession commissioners' speeches and letters;
  • newspapers, pamphlets, broadsides, sermons, and political cartoons;
  • presidential messages, proclamations, and cabinet papers;
  • congressional debates, compromise proposals, and committee reports;
  • federal military, customs, treasury, postal, and property records;
  • Confederate provisional and permanent constitutions;
  • diplomatic correspondence;
  • private diaries, letters, petitions, and memoirs;
  • Unionist convention and military records;
  • Reconstruction convention proceedings; and
  • the Supreme Court opinion in Texas v. White.

Using the Sources

Ordinances establish the claimed legal action but often contain little explanation.

Declarations of causes explain official reasoning but exist for only four first-wave states.

Convention debates reveal disagreement but may be incomplete or reconstructed.

Commissioners' speeches show how secessionists persuaded other slave states and therefore provide especially direct evidence of political purpose.

Later memoirs should be compared with documents written before and during the crisis.

Common Misconceptions

“Secession and the Civil War Were Exactly the Same Event”

Secession was the attempted political withdrawal; the Civil War was the armed conflict determining whether it would succeed.

“The Constitution Explicitly Granted States a Right to Leave”

It contains no express secession clause, and the United States legal position rejects unilateral withdrawal.

“Every Southern State Seceded Immediately After Lincoln's Election”

Seven states seceded before Fort Sumter; Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee waited until after the troop call.

“All Slave States Seceded”

Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the Union.

“Kentucky and Missouri Clearly Became Confederate States Like South Carolina”

Rival governments joined the Confederacy, but the recognized state governments remained in the United States.

“Thirteen Stars Prove Thirteen States Lawfully Seceded”

The additional stars represented Confederate claims to Kentucky and Missouri.

“Every White Resident of a Seceding State Supported Secession”

Unionists remained numerous in several regions and supplied soldiers and governments to the United States.

“Secession Was Caused Principally by the Tariff of 1860”

Tariffs were part of sectional history, but the secession declarations centered slavery and slaveholding rights.

“States' Rights and Slavery Are Mutually Exclusive Explanations”

Secessionists used a states' rights theory chiefly to protect slavery and racial hierarchy.

“Lincoln Was Elected on a Platform of Immediate Abolition in Every State”

Republicans opposed slavery's expansion but did not claim federal power for immediate abolition within existing states in 1860.

“The Federal Government Refused Every Possible Slavery Guarantee”

Congress proposed the Corwin Amendment protecting slavery from federal constitutional interference within states, but it did not stop secession.

“Fort Sumter Was the First Confederate Action Against Federal Authority”

State forces had already seized federal facilities, and South Carolina batteries fired on the Star of the West in January.

“Virginia Seceded Before Fort Sumter”

Its convention initially rejected secession and reversed course after the attack and troop call.

“The Union Recognized the Confederacy as a Foreign Nation”

It treated the conflict as a rebellion while acknowledging belligerent realities necessary for war and blockade law.

“Appomattox Alone Legally Ended Every Secession Government”

Confederate armies and departments surrendered separately, while civil authority collapsed over several weeks.

“Failure to Try Every Confederate Leader Proved Secession Was Constitutional”

Pardons and reconciliation reflected policy choices, not legal validation.

“Texas v. White Created an Ordinary Procedure for a State to Leave With One Vote”

The decision rejected unilateral secession and referred only generally to revolution or consent of the states.

Key Distinctions

Secession
Claimed withdrawal from a larger political union.
Disunion
The breaking apart or threatened dissolution of the United States.
Nullification
A claim that a state may invalidate a particular federal law within its territory.
Revolution
Extraconstitutional overthrow or separation achieved outside ordinary law.
Rebellion
Organized resistance against lawful governmental authority.
Ordinance of secession
A state convention's formal instrument claiming withdrawal.
Declaration of causes
A document explaining the reasons offered for secession.
Secession convention
A specially elected or summoned state body claiming authority to decide the state's relationship to the Union.
Immediate secessionist
An advocate of rapid withdrawal after Lincoln's election.
Cooperationist
An advocate of coordinated Southern action, delay, or further guarantees before withdrawal.
Conditional Unionist
A Union supporter whose loyalty depended on federal policy or avoidance of military coercion.
Southern Unionist
A resident of a slave state or seceded state who remained loyal to the United States.
Border state
A slave state remaining in the Union during the Civil War.
Confederate belligerency
Recognition that organized war existed without diplomatic recognition of Confederate independence.
Readmission
Restoration of congressional representation and normal federal relations after rebellion.

Why Secession Matters

Secession tested whether a constitutional election could be nullified by states whose preferred political interest lost national power.

It exposed the conflict between state-sovereignty theories and a national government acting directly on citizens.

The documentary record demonstrates that the central interest driving the first secession wave was protection of slavery and white supremacy.

The Upper South's later withdrawal demonstrates how military escalation and regional solidarity transformed conditional Unionism into rebellion.

Southern Unionists and border states show that geography, slavery, and political allegiance did not align perfectly.

By removing Southern representatives from Congress and initiating war, secession produced national legislation and military emancipation that destroyed the institution it sought to preserve.

United States victory and Texas v. White established that a state cannot leave the Union by unilateral ordinance.

Debates over secession remain central to constitutional federalism, Civil War causation, national sovereignty, public memory, and the interpretation of states' rights.

Summary

Secession was the attempted withdrawal of eleven Southern slave states from the United States between December 1860 and June 1861.

Secessionists argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states and that a state could withdraw when the compact was violated.

Unionists argued that the Constitution created a perpetual national government and that no state could dissolve it by unilateral ordinance.

Decades of conflict over slavery's expansion, fugitive recovery, territorial government, abolitionism, and slaveholding political power created the crisis.

Lincoln's election on November 6, 1860, triggered immediate action because Republicans had won national power while opposing slavery's territorial expansion.

South Carolina seceded first on December 20, followed by Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas before Fort Sumter.

Official declarations from South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia, and Texas centered slavery, slave property, white supremacy, fugitive-slave enforcement, and territorial rights.

The first seven states met at Montgomery and established the Confederate States of America, whose constitution explicitly protected slavery.

Congress and interstate conferences considered the Crittenden proposals, Washington Peace Conference plan, and Corwin Amendment, but compromise failed over the future and protection of slavery.

State and Confederate forces seized federal property while Fort Sumter and Fort Pickens remained principal unresolved posts.

Lincoln declared in his First Inaugural Address that secession was legally void and that the government would hold federal property.

Confederate forces attacked Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861. Lincoln called for 75,000 militia on April 15.

Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee then joined the second secession wave, while Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the Union.

Kentucky and Missouri had rival Confederate governments, explaining thirteen-star Confederate symbolism, but their recognized governments did not lawfully secede.

Southern Unionists resisted secession throughout the Confederacy, especially in East Tennessee, western Virginia, northern Alabama, western North Carolina, Arkansas, and the Texas Hill Country.

Secession initiated the war that destroyed slavery through self-emancipation, confiscation, the Emancipation Proclamation, Black military service, Union victory, and the Thirteenth Amendment.

Confederate defeat ended the practical attempt at independence. Reconstruction restored lawful state governments and congressional representation under federal conditions.

In Texas v. White, the Supreme Court held that unilateral secession ordinances were legally null and that Texas had remained a state in the Union.

Later Lost Cause narratives emphasized abstract states' rights while minimizing the official documentary record identifying slavery and racial hierarchy as the principal purpose of secession.

Secession remains essential to understanding federalism, constitutional sovereignty, Civil War causation, national self-defense, slavery, Southern Unionism, Reconstruction, and historical memory.
