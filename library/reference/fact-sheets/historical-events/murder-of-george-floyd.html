Murder of George Floyd

The May 25, 2020, police killing in Minneapolis in which Derek Chauvin maintained an unlawful restraint upon George Floyd for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds, producing rare murder and federal civil-rights convictions and triggering worldwide protests against police violence and racial injustice.

What Was the Murder of George Floyd?

The murder of George Floyd was the unlawful police killing of an African American man during an arrest on May 25, 2020.

Minneapolis police officers responded to a report concerning a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill, handcuffed Floyd, attempted to place him in a police vehicle, and ultimately restrained him face down on the street.

Derek Chauvin maintained pressure across Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder area for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds.

J. Alexander Kueng and Thomas Lane physically restrained Floyd, while Tou Thao stood between the officers and bystanders who repeatedly demanded that the restraint end and that medical care be provided.

Floyd repeatedly stated that he could not breathe, called for his mother, said that he was going to die, stopped speaking and moving, and lost his pulse.

The officers continued restraining him after he became unresponsive. Criminal courts later determined that Chauvin murdered Floyd and that the other officers committed state or federal crimes through assistance, failure to intervene, or failure to provide medical aid.

Event and Biography Distinction

George Floyd's biography and the murder of George Floyd are related but distinct reference subjects.

A biography explains Floyd's family, childhood, education, athletics, music, employment, relationships, struggles, and life before May 25, 2020.

This page treats the bounded historical event: the police response, restraint, death, evidence, investigations, prosecutions, institutional findings, and consequences.

Keeping the subjects separate prevents Floyd's complete life from being reduced to the final minutes recorded outside Cup Foods.

Historical Context

Floyd's murder occurred within a long history of contested police force against Black people.

Earlier deaths involving Rodney King, Amadou Diallo, Oscar Grant, Eric Garner, Michael Brown, Freddie Gray, Philando Castile, Breonna Taylor, and many others had generated public protest and demands for reform.

Black Lives Matter had existed since 2013 and had developed organizing networks, digital practices, legal relationships, slogans, and public understanding that connected separate local cases.

The Minneapolis Police Department had faced earlier complaints concerning force, racial disparities, accountability, and community trust.

Floyd's death became an exceptional turning point because the public evidence was prolonged, clear, independently recorded, and accompanied by audible warnings from witnesses.

COVID-19 Context

The murder occurred during the first major phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Government restrictions had closed or reduced workplaces, restaurants, schools, public gatherings, and ordinary community activity.

Black communities were experiencing disproportionate infection, death, unemployment, housing insecurity, and frontline occupational exposure.

Floyd had lost security work as pandemic restrictions disrupted restaurants and nightlife.

The pandemic increased public attention to unequal institutional risk and left millions of people available to view, discuss, and respond to the video through digital media.

Cup Foods

Cup Foods was a neighborhood convenience store located at East 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

Floyd entered the store during the evening of May 25 and purchased cigarettes.

A teenage employee believed that the twenty-dollar bill used in the transaction was counterfeit.

Store workers approached Floyd, who was seated in a vehicle outside, and attempted to resolve the issue.

After Floyd did not return the cigarettes or otherwise satisfy the employees, a store worker called 911.

The call described Floyd as appearing intoxicated and stated that he was not acting in control of himself.

The Suspected Counterfeit Bill

The reported offense involved a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

Police had legal authority to investigate the allegation and, if supported by probable cause, to make an arrest.

The allegation did not authorize punishment at the scene, unnecessary force, or continued restraint after control had been achieved.

Whether Floyd knew that the bill was counterfeit was never determined in a criminal proceeding.

Presenting knowing counterfeiting as an established fact would therefore exceed the available adjudicated record.

Arrival of Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng

Officers Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng were the first Minneapolis police officers to arrive.

They located Floyd in the driver's seat of a nearby vehicle.

Lane approached, drew his firearm, ordered Floyd to show his hands, and later holstered the weapon.

The officers removed Floyd from the vehicle and handcuffed him.

Floyd initially sat on the ground and spoke with the officers.

The officers then walked him toward a police vehicle for transport.

Attempt to Place Floyd in the Police Vehicle

Floyd resisted entering the rear of the police vehicle.

He stated that he was claustrophobic and expressed fear and difficulty breathing.

His resistance included physical movement and refusal to remain seated in the vehicle.

Police were permitted to use objectively reasonable force to complete a lawful arrest.

The later criminal cases did not depend upon a finding that Floyd had obeyed every instruction.

The central legal issue was whether the force continued after Floyd had been handcuffed, brought to the ground, controlled, and no longer presented a threat requiring the prolonged restraint.

Arrival of Derek Chauvin and Tou Thao

Derek Chauvin and Tou Thao arrived while Lane and Kueng were attempting to place Floyd in the police vehicle.

Chauvin was the most experienced officer at the scene.

The officers pulled Floyd from the vehicle and brought him to the pavement.

Floyd remained handcuffed behind his back.

Chauvin assumed the dominant restraint position, Kueng and Lane controlled other portions of Floyd's body, and Thao took a position facing the growing group of witnesses.

Positions and Roles of the Four Officers

Derek Chauvin
Applied sustained pressure with his knees across Floyd's neck, back, shoulder, and arm area and refused to end the restraint as Floyd became unresponsive.
J. Alexander Kueng
Restrained Floyd's back and lower body, checked unsuccessfully for a pulse, and failed to intervene or provide medical care.
Thomas Lane
Restrained Floyd's legs, questioned whether Floyd should be rolled onto his side, and failed to provide effective medical aid.
Tou Thao
Controlled and argued with bystanders, observed the prolonged restraint, and failed to intervene or assist Floyd.

The officers did not perform identical acts, which is why their state and federal charges and convictions differed.

Nine Minutes and Twenty-Nine Seconds

Evidence presented in the federal prosecution established that Chauvin maintained the restraint for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds.

Earlier public descriptions commonly stated eight minutes and forty-six seconds. Prosecutors later revised the calculation after analyzing synchronized video evidence.

The revised duration became the accepted official figure used in the federal criminal case.

The exact number matters because it documents how long the restraint continued through multiple observable stages of medical decline.

Stages of Floyd's Medical Decline

Floyd initially spoke, pleaded, moved, and attempted to breathe.

He repeatedly stated that he could not breathe and that parts of his body hurt.

He called for his mother and said that the officers were going to kill him.

His speech became less frequent and less coherent.

Floyd stopped moving and became unresponsive.

Kueng checked for a pulse and stated that he could not find one.

Chauvin nevertheless maintained the restraint until emergency medical personnel directed that Floyd be moved.

“I Can't Breathe”

Floyd repeatedly told the officers that he could not breathe.

His words echoed Eric Garner's statements during a fatal New York police chokehold in 2014.

Police and defense witnesses later debated whether a person able to speak could still be in serious respiratory distress.

Medical testimony established that the ability to utter words does not prove adequate breathing or oxygenation.

The phrase became a major protest slogan because it expressed both Floyd's immediate medical crisis and the wider experience of institutional pressure described by demonstrators.

Floyd's Call for His Mother

During the restraint, Floyd called for his mother.

His mother, Larcenia “Cissy” Jones Floyd, had died before the encounter.

The call became one of the video's most emotionally powerful moments.

It demonstrated Floyd's distress and contributed to the public understanding that the person under the officers was not an abstract suspect but a son, father, brother, and community member.

Bystander Intervention

A group of residents and passersby gathered as the restraint continued.

They told officers that Floyd could not breathe, was no longer responsive, and needed medical attention.

Witnesses asked officers to check his pulse, place him in a recovery position, and begin resuscitation.

Several bystanders possessed medical or practical knowledge sufficient to recognize the emergency.

Thao prevented them from approaching Floyd and argued with them while Chauvin continued the restraint.

The witnesses could document, speak, and summon attention, but they did not possess lawful authority to physically displace armed officers without exposing themselves to arrest or force.

Darnella Frazier

Darnella Frazier was seventeen years old when she encountered the arrest while walking with a younger relative.

She recorded the restraint with her cellphone for approximately ten minutes.

The video captured Floyd's voice, Chauvin's posture, the other officers' roles, the warnings of bystanders, and Floyd's loss of consciousness.

Frazier later testified in Chauvin's trial about what she witnessed and the emotional effect of being unable to stop the killing.

The Pulitzer Prize Board awarded her a special citation in 2021 for courageously recording the murder and demonstrating the importance of citizens documenting government conduct.

Other Eyewitnesses

Witnesses included Cup Foods employees, neighborhood residents, passersby, a mixed-martial-arts practitioner, and an off-duty firefighter trained as an emergency medical technician.

Their testimony established that ordinary observers recognized the restraint as dangerous and repeatedly asked officers to act.

The witnesses differed in age, profession, knowledge, and relationship to Floyd.

Their accounts converged on the progression from restraint to medical emergency.

Body-Camera and Surveillance Evidence

Police body cameras recorded the approach, handcuffing, struggle near the police vehicle, restraint, and portions of the medical response.

Store and street surveillance cameras provided additional views.

These recordings supplied context not visible in Frazier's video, including Floyd's initial interaction with Lane and Kueng.

The complete evidentiary record showed both Floyd's resistance near the police vehicle and the officers' prolonged continuation of force after he was under control.

Accurate analysis requires the complete sequence rather than selecting only the portion most favorable to one argument.

The Initial Police Statement

Minneapolis police initially described the event as a man dying after a medical incident during a police interaction.

The statement reported that officers had encountered a suspect who appeared to be under the influence and had physically resisted.

It did not disclose that Chauvin maintained a knee restraint for more than nine minutes, that Floyd repeatedly stated he could not breathe, or that the restraint continued after he lost consciousness and pulse.

Frazier's recording contradicted the incomplete framing and forced public officials to respond to independently documented evidence.

The contrast became a major lesson concerning the need for external evidence, public records, and independent review when police agencies describe their own conduct.

Emergency Medical Response

Emergency medical personnel arrived while Floyd remained beneath the officers.

Paramedics assessed him and found that he lacked a pulse.

They moved him to an ambulance and began resuscitation efforts.

The ambulance left the immediate scene partly because the crowd and conditions complicated treatment.

Floyd was transported to Hennepin County Medical Center and pronounced dead.

The medical response could not reverse the consequences of the prolonged restraint and delay in effective aid.

Hennepin County Medical Examiner

The Hennepin County medical examiner classified Floyd's manner of death as homicide.

The official cause was cardiopulmonary arrest complicating law-enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression.

Other significant conditions included arteriosclerotic and hypertensive heart disease, fentanyl intoxication, and recent methamphetamine use.

The medical term homicide means that the actions of another person contributed to the death.

It does not independently determine whether the conduct was criminal, justified, accidental, murder, or manslaughter.

Criminal responsibility was determined through separate state and federal proceedings.

Independent Autopsy

Floyd's family retained independent physicians to examine the death.

Those experts described the death as a homicide caused by asphyxia associated with pressure and restraint.

The county and independent examinations used different medical terminology.

Both connected Floyd's death to the police restraint and classified the death as homicide.

Trial testimony later gave the jury a more complete medical record than either short public summary alone.

Toxicology and Health Conditions

Toxicology identified fentanyl and methamphetamine in Floyd's body.

He also had significant cardiovascular disease and had previously tested positive for COVID-19.

Chauvin's defense argued that these conditions, rather than the restraint, caused or created reasonable doubt concerning Floyd's death.

Prosecution experts explained that underlying illness and drug exposure may increase vulnerability without eliminating the causal role of police force.

The jury rejected the defense theory and found Chauvin criminally responsible beyond a reasonable doubt.

Medical and Legal Causation

Medical causation asks what physiological processes and contributing conditions produced death.

Legal causation asks whether prohibited conduct was a substantial and legally sufficient cause under the governing criminal law.

A defendant generally takes a victim as the defendant finds that person, including existing vulnerabilities.

The prosecution did not have to prove that Floyd was in perfect health or that restraint was the only condition present.

It had to prove that Chauvin's unlawful force caused Floyd's death within the meaning of Minnesota law.

The jury found that burden satisfied.

Dismissal of the Officers

Minneapolis fired Chauvin, Kueng, Lane, and Thao on May 26, 2020.

The dismissal occurred one day after the killing and after the public video began circulating.

Employment termination did not determine criminal guilt.

State and federal agencies separately investigated possible violations of Minnesota criminal law and federal civil-rights law.

Investigating Agencies

The Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension conducted the principal state criminal investigation.

The Federal Bureau of Investigation investigated potential federal civil-rights violations.

Prosecutors reviewed cellphone video, body-camera recordings, surveillance video, medical evidence, police training, dispatch communications, witness accounts, and officer statements.

Parallel state and federal investigations were legally distinct and examined different criminal statutes.

Initial Charge Against Chauvin

Hennepin County prosecutors arrested and charged Chauvin on May 29, 2020.

The initial complaint included third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

Public criticism focused on the time required to arrest Chauvin, the absence of charges against the other officers, and whether the initial charges adequately reflected the conduct shown on video.

Minnesota Attorney General Takes the Case

Governor Tim Walz assigned Attorney General Keith Ellison to lead the prosecution in cooperation with the Hennepin County Attorney's Office.

Ellison assembled a prosecution team including public attorneys and outside lawyers with criminal-trial, appellate, and civil-rights experience.

On June 3, prosecutors added second-degree unintentional murder against Chauvin and charged Kueng, Lane, and Thao with aiding and abetting second-degree murder and manslaughter.

Second-Degree Unintentional Murder

Minnesota's second-degree unintentional murder charge did not require proof that Chauvin intended to kill Floyd.

Prosecutors alleged that Chauvin caused Floyd's death while committing third-degree assault.

The underlying assault consisted of intentionally applying unlawful force that inflicted substantial bodily harm.

The jury found Chauvin guilty of this offense.

It was the most serious conviction governing his state sentence.

Second-Degree Manslaughter

The manslaughter count alleged that Chauvin created an unreasonable risk and consciously took a chance of causing death or great bodily harm.

This count required proof of culpable negligence rather than intent to kill.

The jury also found Chauvin guilty of this offense.

Third-Degree Murder Count

The trial court initially dismissed and later reinstated a third-degree-murder charge.

The jury returned a guilty verdict on that count in April 2021.

A later Minnesota Supreme Court interpretation of the third-degree-murder statute held that the offense did not apply when conduct was directed with particularity at one person in the manner alleged in Floyd's death.

The third-degree conviction was therefore vacated.

That action did not disturb Chauvin's second-degree-murder or manslaughter convictions or reduce the state sentence imposed on the second-degree-murder count.

Pretrial Issues

Chauvin sought dismissal, severance, and transfer of the trial to another location.

The defense argued that extensive publicity, public protest, security measures, and political statements made a fair trial in Hennepin County impossible.

Judge Peter Cahill denied transfer and conducted extensive jury questioning concerning exposure to publicity and ability to decide the case from courtroom evidence.

Chauvin was tried separately from the other officers because of scheduling and courtroom limitations associated with the pandemic.

Civil Settlement During Jury Selection

During jury selection, Minneapolis announced a $27 million settlement with Floyd's family.

The defense argued that the timing could prejudice prospective jurors by implying that the city had accepted responsibility.

The court questioned jurors concerning exposure to the announcement and removed or retained them according to their responses.

Chauvin later raised the settlement as part of his appeal, but appellate courts upheld the trial's fairness.

Trial of Derek Chauvin

Jury selection began in March 2021, and opening statements occurred on March 29.

The prosecution presented eyewitnesses, police officials, use-of-force experts, emergency personnel, medical experts, forensic evidence, and video recordings.

Senior Minneapolis police officials testified that Chauvin's conduct violated training and department policy.

Medical witnesses explained how prone restraint and pressure prevented adequate breathing and produced cardiopulmonary arrest.

The defense emphasized Floyd's resistance, drug exposure, cardiovascular disease, and the stressful environment surrounding the officers.

The trial was broadcast publicly, creating an unusually accessible record of testimony and evidence.

Police Use-of-Force Testimony

Minneapolis Police Chief Medaria Arradondo testified against Chauvin.

Other officers and training officials explained that force must remain proportionate to the threat and resistance actually present.

They testified that an officer must reassess force as conditions change.

A force that may be justified at one stage of an arrest can become unreasonable after a suspect is handcuffed, controlled, unconscious, or without a pulse.

This principle became central to the jury's evaluation of the prolonged restraint.

Medical Trial Testimony

Pulmonary, cardiac, emergency-medicine, pathology, and toxicology experts testified.

Prosecution experts described Floyd's death as the result of low oxygen caused by the manner in which officers restrained him.

Defense experts emphasized drug exposure, heart disease, possible carbon-monoxide exposure, and other alternative mechanisms.

The jury was responsible for evaluating the competing opinions together with the video, autopsy, physical evidence, and officer conduct.

State Verdict

The jury began deliberating on April 19, 2021.

On April 20, it returned guilty verdicts on second-degree unintentional murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

The court revoked Chauvin's bail and remanded him into custody.

The verdict was widely viewed as an unusual instance of a police officer being convicted of murder for force used while on duty.

The later vacation of the third-degree count did not alter the principal second-degree-murder judgment.

State Sentencing

Judge Cahill found aggravating circumstances permitting a sentence above the ordinary guideline range.

The court identified Floyd's particular vulnerability while handcuffed and prone, Chauvin's abuse of a position of trust and authority, the presence of children, and particular cruelty.

On June 25, 2021, Chauvin received a sentence of twenty-two and one-half years.

Minnesota law requires part of a state sentence to be served in prison and part under supervised release, subject to applicable rules and later legal developments.

Direct Appeal

Chauvin appealed, arguing that pretrial publicity, the trial location, juror issues, evidentiary rulings, prosecutorial conduct, and other alleged errors denied him a fair trial.

The Minnesota Court of Appeals unanimously affirmed the principal murder and manslaughter convictions in April 2023.

The Minnesota Supreme Court declined further review.

The United States Supreme Court declined to hear Chauvin's direct appeal in November 2023.

These decisions left the state judgment in force.

Postconviction Litigation

Chauvin later pursued collateral challenges arguing that new medical opinions, alleged prosecutorial misconduct, jury instructions, and other claimed defects required a new trial or reversal.

A renewed postconviction petition filed in 2025 was denied by a Minnesota district judge in 2026.

Chauvin appealed that denial.

As of August 1, 2026, no court had vacated his second-degree-murder or manslaughter judgment.

A pending appeal is a request for relief, not a finding that the original judgment was invalid.

Federal Civil-Rights Indictment

In May 2021, a federal grand jury indicted Chauvin, Kueng, Lane, and Thao under the federal criminal civil-rights statute.

The charges alleged that the officers acted under color of law and willfully deprived Floyd of constitutional rights.

The federal prosecution was separate from Minnesota's murder and manslaughter cases.

State and federal governments prosecuted violations of different sovereign laws arising from the same course of conduct.

Chauvin's Federal Guilty Plea

Chauvin pleaded guilty in December 2021 to willfully depriving Floyd of the right to be free from unreasonable force.

He admitted that he maintained unreasonable force after Floyd was handcuffed, compliant, and unresponsive.

He admitted that his conduct resulted in bodily injury and death and reflected callous and wanton disregard for Floyd's life.

Chauvin also admitted violating the civil rights of a fourteen-year-old boy in a separate 2017 encounter.

In July 2022, the federal court sentenced him to 252 months.

Federal Trial of Kueng, Lane, and Thao

Kueng, Lane, and Thao proceeded to a federal jury trial.

The evidence established that all three were trained in first aid, use of force, and intervention responsibilities.

The jury found Kueng and Thao guilty of willfully failing to intervene against Chauvin's unreasonable force.

It found Kueng, Lane, and Thao guilty of willfully failing to provide aid for Floyd's serious medical need.

The verdicts established that officers can incur criminal responsibility through deliberate inaction when they possess a duty and realistic opportunity to act.

Federal Sentences of the Other Officers

Thomas Lane received a federal sentence of thirty months.

J. Alexander Kueng received a federal sentence of thirty-six months.

Tou Thao received a federal sentence of forty-two months.

Sentencing differences reflected the officers' specific conduct, relative roles, legal findings, and judicial evaluation.

State Cases Against Kueng, Lane, and Thao

Lane pleaded guilty to aiding and abetting second-degree manslaughter and received a three-year state sentence.

Kueng pleaded guilty to the same offense and received a forty-two-month state sentence.

Thao submitted his case to a judge on stipulated evidence and was convicted of aiding and abetting second-degree manslaughter.

He received a state sentence of fifty-seven months.

By 2023, every officer involved in restraining or guarding Floyd had been convicted in state court, federal court, or both.

Duty to Intervene

Police officers are not permitted to remain passive when another officer uses clearly unlawful force and they possess a realistic opportunity to stop it.

The federal convictions of Kueng and Thao made this responsibility central to the case.

Intervention can include verbal commands, physical action, summoning a supervisor, repositioning a restrained person, or ending participation in the force.

Rank, seniority, loyalty, or fear of professional consequences does not automatically excuse willful failure to protect a person from unconstitutional force.

Duty to Provide Medical Aid

A person in police custody depends upon officers for access to medical treatment.

Floyd displayed a serious and increasingly obvious medical need.

He repeatedly reported breathing difficulty, stopped communicating, became motionless, and lacked a detectable pulse.

Kueng, Lane, and Thao were trained in emergency care but failed to provide timely assistance.

Their federal convictions established that deliberate indifference to a detainee's serious medical need can constitute a criminal civil-rights violation.

Floyd Family Civil Lawsuit

Floyd's family filed a federal civil-rights action against Minneapolis and the four officers.

The lawsuit alleged unconstitutional force, failure to provide medical care, and municipal responsibility for policies, customs, training, and supervision.

Civil litigation sought compensation and institutional accountability rather than imprisonment.

$27 Million Civil Settlement

Minneapolis approved a $27 million settlement with Floyd's family on March 12, 2021.

At the time, it was among the largest pretrial settlements in a police-death civil-rights case.

The agreement included $500,000 for the community surrounding 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

The settlement resolved civil claims without replacing the separate criminal prosecutions.

Immediate Minneapolis Protests

Demonstrations began on May 26 near the site of the murder and Minneapolis police facilities.

Protesters demanded arrest of the officers, release of video evidence, and broader police reform.

Police used chemical agents and less-lethal weapons during confrontations.

Looting, arson, and attacks spread through portions of Minneapolis and Saint Paul.

On May 28, officers abandoned the Third Precinct building, which was entered and set on fire.

Minnesota deployed the National Guard and imposed emergency restrictions.

National and Global Protest Wave

Protests spread to every state, the District of Columbia, and countries throughout the world.

Demonstrators connected Floyd's murder with Breonna Taylor, Ahmaud Arbery, Eric Garner, Michael Brown, and other victims of police or racial violence.

Most protest events did not involve violence or destructive activity by demonstrators.

Some cities nevertheless experienced serious looting, arson, shootings, attacks, police violence, curfews, mass arrest, and National Guard or federal deployment.

Floyd and his family did not organize or control the conduct of every person participating in the wider protest period.

Relationship to Black Lives Matter

Black Lives Matter began in 2013, seven years before Floyd's murder.

The movement had already organized around the deaths of Trayvon Martin, Michael Brown, Eric Garner, Freddie Gray, Sandra Bland, Philando Castile, and others.

Floyd's death activated existing language, networks, organizers, legal resources, and public understanding.

The 2020 protest wave was therefore an expansion of an established movement rather than its founding event.

Policy Demands Arising from the Murder

Protesters, lawmakers, civil-rights organizations, and families advanced varied demands.

  • Criminal accountability for officers who use unlawful force.
  • Clear duties to intervene and render medical assistance.
  • Restrictions on chokeholds, neck restraints, and prolonged prone restraint.
  • Independent investigation of deaths involving police.
  • Public misconduct databases and stronger officer-decertification systems.
  • Changes to qualified immunity and civil-liability rules.
  • Body-camera requirements, data reporting, and transparent disciplinary records.
  • Civilian oversight and enforceable pattern-or-practice reform.
  • Alternative responses for mental health, homelessness, traffic, and other nonviolent situations.
  • Reallocation of public resources toward housing, health, education, employment, and violence prevention.

George Floyd Justice in Policing Act

Members of Congress introduced major federal policing legislation bearing Floyd's name.

Provisions addressed use of force, chokeholds, no-knock warrants, qualified immunity, misconduct records, racial profiling, body cameras, federal prosecution standards, data, and grant conditions.

The House of Representatives passed versions in 2020 and 2021.

The Senate did not enact the bill, and bipartisan negotiations failed to produce a final statute.

The result demonstrated that widespread public condemnation of Floyd's murder did not create national agreement concerning police regulation.

Federal Executive Action

President Donald Trump issued a policing-related executive order in June 2020 addressing credentialing, information sharing, chokeholds, and social-service responses.

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. issued Executive Order 14074 on May 25, 2022.

The later order addressed federal force policy, body cameras, chokeholds, no-knock entries, misconduct records, accreditation, data, and grant standards.

Executive orders can direct federal agencies and influence grant recipients but cannot independently rewrite every state and local police law.

Federal Pattern-or-Practice Investigation

The United States Department of Justice opened a civil investigation of Minneapolis and its police department in April 2021.

In June 2023, the department found reasonable cause to believe that Minneapolis police engaged in patterns or practices of excessive force and racial discrimination.

It also identified violations involving protected speech and people with behavioral-health disabilities.

The findings addressed institutional conduct rather than merely the criminal responsibility of the four officers.

The department and city announced a proposed federal consent decree in January 2025.

The Justice Department dismissed the federal case and proposed decree in May 2025 after a change in presidential administration.

Minnesota Human Rights Settlement

The Minnesota Department of Human Rights conducted a separate civil-rights investigation.

Minneapolis entered a court-enforceable settlement agreement with the state in 2023.

The agreement addresses force, stops, searches, supervision, accountability, training, data, and community engagement.

Unlike the dismissed federal proposal, the state agreement remained active after 2025.

The different outcomes illustrate how overlapping federal, state, and municipal reform systems can continue or change independently.

Police Policy Changes

Minneapolis and other jurisdictions revised policies concerning neck restraints, chokeholds, prone restraint, intervention, medical care, body cameras, crowd control, warrants, and use-of-force reporting.

State legislatures adopted or considered officer-licensing, decertification, transparency, and force measures.

Some cities transferred limited responsibilities to mental-health teams or other unarmed responders.

Implementation varied, and later political changes altered some programs or funding decisions.

A written policy does not become operational reform without training, supervision, discipline, data, and enforcement.

George Floyd Square

The intersection where Floyd was murdered became a memorial, gathering place, protest site, and community space known as George Floyd Square.

People placed flowers, signs, art, candles, and names of people killed by police.

Residents organized mutual aid, food distribution, political education, memorial events, and informal security.

The space also generated disputes concerning traffic, public safety, business access, city authority, preservation, and redevelopment.

Minneapolis planned major street and infrastructure construction beginning in 2026 while stating that the redesign would honor Floyd and retain community and memorial space.

Memorial Services and Funeral

Memorial services were held in Minneapolis, North Carolina, and Houston.

Family members, clergy, civil-rights leaders, public officials, entertainers, athletes, and residents attended.

Floyd was buried in Texas near his mother.

The memorials combined personal grief with public demands for accountability and institutional change.

Public Memory and Archiving

Museums, libraries, universities, journalists, and community archives collected protest signs, murals, photographs, oral histories, digital records, and memorial objects.

Floyd's name appeared on streets, scholarships, foundations, public art, legislation, demonstrations, and institutional programs.

Memorialization preserved public response while also producing debate over commercialization, political symbolism, and whether remembrance was accompanied by measurable reform.

Misinformation and Revisionist Claims

Claims that Floyd died only from an overdose, heart disease, COVID-19, or an unrelated medical emergency have circulated widely.

Those claims conflict with the official homicide finding, the complete trial record, the state murder conviction, and Chauvin's federal guilty plea admitting that unreasonable force resulted in Floyd's death.

Medical conditions and toxicology were relevant to causation analysis but did not legally excuse the restraint.

Chauvin has continued to challenge the judgment through lawful appellate and postconviction procedures.

A claim in a petition or public campaign is not equivalent to a judicial finding.

Floyd's Conduct and Constitutional Protection

Floyd resisted being placed into the police vehicle and may have been impaired.

Officers were entitled to control and transport him using objectively reasonable force.

His conduct did not authorize force after he was handcuffed, prone, controlled, unresponsive, and without a pulse.

Past convictions, substance use, poor health, suspected counterfeiting, or imperfect compliance do not remove constitutional protection.

Police authority is measured by legal necessity and proportionality, not by whether a detained person is morally faultless.

Individual and Institutional Accountability

Individual accountability asks whether particular officers committed crimes or civil wrongs.

Institutional accountability asks whether training, supervision, policy, discipline, culture, hiring, complaint review, union systems, and government oversight allowed dangerous conduct to persist.

The criminal cases addressed individual responsibility.

The civil settlement and federal and state investigations addressed broader municipal and departmental responsibility.

Both forms of accountability are necessary because convicting one officer does not automatically reform the institution in which the conduct occurred.

Major Outcomes

Chauvin was convicted of murder and manslaughter and separately admitted a federal civil-rights offense.

Kueng, Lane, and Thao were convicted or pleaded guilty for their roles.

Floyd's family obtained a major civil settlement.

Minneapolis entered an enforceable state police-reform agreement.

Legislatures and police agencies changed policies across the country.

Millions of people participated in demonstrations, and the murder became a global reference point in debates over racism and policing.

The principal federal policing bill nevertheless failed to become law, and police killings and institutional disputes continued.

Limits of the Response

Criminal convictions provided accountability in one exceptionally documented case but did not establish a consistent national system for prosecuting police violence.

Reform varied among jurisdictions and could be reversed, underfunded, or weakly enforced.

The proposed federal Minneapolis consent decree was dismissed before court enforcement began.

Public support for movement demands changed as debates over crime, police staffing, protest disorder, and political polarization intensified.

The persistence of later police killings demonstrated that legal and policy changes had not eliminated the underlying problem.

Key Distinctions

George Floyd
The individual person whose life extended far beyond the final police encounter.

The bounded historical and criminal event occurring on May 25, 2020.

The mass demonstrations triggered by the murder.

The broader movement founded in 2013 that expanded after Floyd's death.
Homicide
A death caused by another person; the classification does not alone establish a particular crime.
Second-degree unintentional murder
The principal Minnesota offense for which Chauvin remains convicted, based on causing Floyd's death while committing felony assault.
Second-degree manslaughter
The Minnesota offense based upon culpable negligence creating an unreasonable risk of death or great bodily harm.

The Minnesota cases involving murder, manslaughter, and aiding-and-abetting liability.
Federal prosecution
The United States cases charging willful deprivation of constitutional rights under color of law.
Failure to intervene
An officer's willful failure to stop another officer's clearly unreasonable force despite knowledge and opportunity.
Deliberate indifference
Willful disregard of a person's serious medical need while under government control.

Compensation and resolution of damages claims, separate from criminal punishment.

An institutional civil-rights inquiry into repeated unlawful conduct by a law-enforcement agency.
Direct appeal
Review of alleged errors in the conviction and trial record.
Postconviction petition
A later collateral request for relief based on specified legal grounds, new evidence, or claimed constitutional violations.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Floyd was not arrested for a violent offense. The police response concerned a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

Whether Floyd knowingly used counterfeit currency was never adjudicated and should not be presented as proven.

Floyd resisted entry into the police vehicle, but resistance does not justify force after a person is handcuffed, prone, controlled, unconscious, or without a pulse.

The accepted restraint duration is approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds. The earlier eight-minute-and-forty-six-second estimate came from preliminary calculation.

Darnella Frazier's recording was independent cellphone video, not police body-camera footage.

The official cause of death included law-enforcement restraint and neck compression. Drug exposure and heart disease were listed as other significant conditions, not as a replacement for the homicide finding.

A medical homicide classification and a criminal murder conviction are different determinations produced by different institutions.

Chauvin did not need to intend Floyd's death to be convicted of Minnesota's second-degree unintentional murder offense.

The third-degree-murder count was vacated, but the second-degree-murder and manslaughter judgments remained valid.

Kueng, Lane, and Thao did not perform identical actions and therefore did not receive identical charges or sentences.

State and federal prosecutions addressed separate offenses under separate sovereign laws.

Most protests following Floyd's murder were peaceful, although serious violence and property destruction occurred in some places.

Peaceful protesters, rioters, looters, police officers, counterprotesters, and unaffiliated criminal actors should not be treated as interchangeable categories.

A postconviction petition or appeal does not invalidate a judgment unless a court grants relief.

Why the Murder of George Floyd Matters

The murder became one of the most thoroughly documented police killings in modern history.

It demonstrated that independent witnesses can create evidence capable of contradicting an incomplete official account.

The case established that force must be reassessed continually and cannot remain justified merely because some force was lawful earlier in an encounter.

Chauvin's conviction showed that an officer can be held criminally responsible for murder committed through unlawful force during an arrest.

The other officers' convictions demonstrated that criminal responsibility may arise from failure to intervene and failure to provide medical care.

The murder connected individual conduct to institutional questions concerning training, supervision, culture, discipline, data, racial disparity, and public trust.

The worldwide protests showed how a locally recorded event could activate established movement networks and produce global political response.

The policy consequences demonstrated both the capacity and limitations of protest: laws, policies, prosecutions, and settlements changed, but the principal federal legislation failed and institutional disputes continued.

The event also established a durable principle of public memory: constitutional protection does not depend upon a person's wealth, health, sobriety, criminal record, or perfect compliance.

George Floyd's murder remains a central historical reference point for evaluating police authority, government accountability, citizen documentation, racial justice, and the difference between symbolic recognition and enforceable reform.

Summary

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police responded to a report that George Floyd had used a suspected counterfeit twenty-dollar bill. Officers handcuffed Floyd, attempted to place him in a police vehicle, and restrained him face down on the street.

Derek Chauvin maintained pressure across Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder area for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds. Floyd repeatedly stated that he could not breathe, became unresponsive, and lost his pulse while J. Alexander Kueng, Thomas Lane, and Tou Thao failed to stop the force or provide effective medical aid. Darnella Frazier's video documented the killing and contradicted the initial police account.

Chauvin was convicted of second-degree unintentional murder and manslaughter and pleaded guilty to a federal civil-rights offense. Kueng, Lane, and Thao were also convicted or pleaded guilty for their roles. The murder triggered worldwide protests, a major civil settlement, institutional investigations, police reforms, legislation, memorialization, and continuing legal and political debate. Chauvin's principal judgments remained in force as of August 1, 2026.
