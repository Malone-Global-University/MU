Little Rock Nine

The nine African American students who desegregated Little Rock Central High School in 1957, confronting state defiance, white mob violence, sustained harassment, and a constitutional crisis that required federal military enforcement.

Who Were the Little Rock Nine?

The Little Rock Nine were nine African American teenagers who enrolled at the previously all-white Little Rock Central High School during the 1957–1958 academic year.

They became the first Black students to attend classes at Central High under a federal court-approved desegregation plan.

Their enrollment tested whether Arkansas officials, the Little Rock School District, the federal courts, and the executive branch would implement the Supreme Court's decisions prohibiting racial segregation in public education.

The students did not enter an ordinary academic dispute. They entered a political and constitutional confrontation in which state officials used military power to obstruct a federal court order and white segregationists used mob action, threats, and sustained harassment to resist integration.

Historical Context

Public education in Arkansas, like public education throughout the Jim Crow South, was organized through compulsory racial separation.

Black and white children attended different schools, and the institutions assigned to Black students commonly received fewer resources, restricted academic offerings, older buildings, and less political support.

African American families, teachers, churches, and civic organizations built strong educational institutions despite unequal public funding and exclusion from white schools.

By the 1950s, the NAACP had developed a national legal campaign challenging the constitutional foundation of segregated education.

Brown v. Board of Education

On May 17, 1954, the United States Supreme Court held in Brown v. Board of Education that state-required racial segregation in public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Court rejected the claim that separate public schools could satisfy the Constitution merely by appearing equal in buildings, salaries, or other measurable resources.

In 1955, the Court issued a second decision, commonly called Brown II, directing federal district courts to supervise implementation with “all deliberate speed.”

The absence of a fixed national deadline allowed many school districts and state governments to delay, restrict, or resist meaningful desegregation.

Arkansas Before the Central High Crisis

Arkansas was not initially viewed as the most resistant Southern state.

The University of Arkansas law school had admitted a Black student in 1948, and several state colleges later desegregated.

Little Rock had also desegregated certain public facilities before the Central High confrontation.

These developments did not eliminate Jim Crow or indicate general acceptance of integrated public schools.

School desegregation directly affected children, neighborhoods, social relationships, public budgets, and local political authority, making it a particularly powerful focus for organized white resistance.

The Blossom Plan

On May 24, 1955, the Little Rock School Board adopted a phased desegregation program developed under Superintendent Virgil T. Blossom.

The plan proposed beginning at Central High School in 1957 and extending desegregation gradually to junior high and elementary schools.

It was a limited plan rather than an immediate district-wide integration program.

New school construction helped preserve substantial racial separation. Hall High School opened for white students in western Little Rock, while Horace Mann High School served Black students in eastern Little Rock.

The plan also relied on screening and assignment procedures that limited the number of Black students transferred to Central.

Although gradual, the Blossom Plan became enforceable through federal court orders. State officials therefore could not lawfully obstruct its implementation.

The NAACP Legal Campaign

Arkansas NAACP leaders petitioned the Little Rock School Board for immediate desegregation soon after the Brown decision.

When the district continued operating segregated schools and rejected Black students seeking admission, families pursued federal litigation.

Attorney Wiley Branton and other NAACP lawyers represented Black students and parents.

Thurgood Marshall and the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund supported the litigation and later defended the students' right to remain at Central High.

Legal action provided an enforceable foundation, but litigation alone could not protect the students from crowds, police failures, political retaliation, or attacks inside the school.

Aaron v. Cooper

In February 1956, twelve Black parents filed a federal lawsuit on behalf of thirty-three students seeking the desegregation of Little Rock's public schools.

The case was commonly styled Aaron v. Cooper, using the names of student John Aaron and school-board president William Cooper.

A federal district court initially approved the school board's gradual plan, and the federal appellate court upheld it.

Because the federal court retained jurisdiction, implementation of the Blossom Plan became a judicial obligation rather than a discretionary promise by local officials.

Selection of the Students

The school district invited Black students from Horace Mann High School and Dunbar Junior High School to volunteer for transfer to Central.

More students initially expressed interest than ultimately entered the school.

Prospective students and their families faced extensive restrictions and pressure.

They were informed that transfer students could be excluded from extracurricular activities, including athletics, clubs, choir, drama, and social events.

Some families received employment threats or feared economic retaliation.

By the time the students entered Central on September 23, the group had narrowed to nine.

Daisy Bates

Daisy Gatson Bates was president of the Arkansas State Conference of NAACP Branches and one of the principal organizers of the Central High desegregation effort.

With her husband, L. C. Bates, she published the Arkansas State Press, a Black newspaper that investigated racial injustice and advocated civil rights.

Bates communicated with the students and their parents, coordinated transportation, met with lawyers, handled press inquiries, relayed information, and provided a central gathering place at her home.

She also served as an adviser and source of emotional support during a year in which the students faced daily threats and abuse.

The Bates home became a target of surveillance, economic retaliation, threats, and attacks because of its role in the campaign.

Organized Segregationist Resistance

Opposition to Central High's desegregation was organized through political officials, white citizens' councils, the Mothers' League of Central High School, attorneys, clergy members, and other segregationist networks.

These groups filed lawsuits, circulated petitions, organized demonstrations, spread racial fears, pressured public officials, and sought to prevent the students from entering the school.

Segregationist rhetoric frequently invoked states' rights, local control, public safety, parental authority, and opposition to federal power.

The operational objective was to preserve racial separation despite the controlling federal court order.

Governor Orval Faubus

Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus became the principal state official resisting the desegregation of Central High.

On September 2, 1957, he announced that he had ordered the Arkansas National Guard to Central High, claiming the deployment was necessary to prevent violence.

The Guard did not merely separate hostile crowds or protect the students.

Acting under Faubus's direction, guardsmen blocked African American students from entering the school while white students were permitted to attend.

The state therefore used military power to obstruct a federally supervised desegregation plan.

September 4, 1957

September 4 was the first attempted day of integration at Central High.

Ten African American students approached the school that morning.

Seven students met near the campus and approached with an interracial group of ministers arranged by Daisy Bates. Terrence Roberts walked from his nearby home, and Melba Pattillo arrived with her mother.

Elizabeth Eckford arrived alone because her family did not have a telephone and did not receive notice of the meeting arrangement.

Arkansas National Guard troops refused entry to all ten students.

Jane Lee Hill later returned to Horace Mann High School. The remaining nine students became known as the Little Rock Nine.

Elizabeth Eckford's Walk

Elizabeth Eckford attempted several times to pass the National Guard lines before realizing that the soldiers had been ordered to exclude her.

She then walked toward a bus stop while a white crowd followed, shouted racial insults and threats, spat at her, and demanded violence.

Photographs showed Eckford maintaining her composure while surrounded by hostility.

Grace Lorch, a white resident, eventually helped her board a city bus.

The images circulated nationally and internationally, making the confrontation a visible symbol of state-supported resistance to school desegregation.

Federal Court Response

Federal District Judge Ronald N. Davies ordered the Little Rock School Board to proceed with desegregation.

The United States government entered the proceedings to seek an injunction against Faubus and the Arkansas National Guard.

On September 20, Davies ruled that the Guard had been used to obstruct rather than preserve lawful implementation of the court order.

He enjoined Faubus and Guard officials from preventing the Black students from attending Central High.

Faubus then removed the Guard from the school, leaving local police responsible for public order.

September 23: Entry and Emergency Removal

On September 23, the Little Rock Nine entered Central High through a side entrance under local police protection.

A crowd of more than one thousand white protesters gathered outside.

Several Black journalists, including Alex Wilson, were attacked by members of the crowd while reporting on the students' arrival.

As the crowd became more violent and police feared the school could be overrun, authorities removed the Nine through a side door approximately three and one-half hours after their entry.

The incident established that a court order and local police presence were insufficient to guarantee safe attendance.

Mayor Woodrow Mann

Little Rock Mayor Woodrow Wilson Mann concluded that local authorities could not control the organized crowd.

He sent telegrams to President Eisenhower requesting federal assistance and warning that the situation was beyond the capacity of local police.

Mann's request helped establish the factual and political basis for direct federal intervention.

His action also demonstrated that the crisis was not a simple disagreement between Arkansas and Washington. Local officials within Arkansas differed over whether state resistance or federal enforcement should control.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower

President Eisenhower initially sought a negotiated resolution and met with Faubus on September 14.

Continued obstruction and mob violence made voluntary compliance increasingly unlikely.

On September 23, Eisenhower issued Proclamation 3204 ordering persons obstructing justice to disperse.

When the order failed to restore lawful conditions, he issued Executive Order 10730 on September 24.

The order federalized the Arkansas National Guard and authorized the Department of Defense to use military forces to enforce federal court orders.

The 101st Airborne Division

Eisenhower deployed approximately one thousand soldiers from the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock.

Federal troops established security around Central High, controlled access to the surrounding area, and escorted the students.

On September 25, soldiers accompanied the Little Rock Nine through the school's front entrance for their first full day of classes.

The deployment was not an ordinary school-security measure. It was the use of federal military power to overcome state obstruction and enforce constitutional rights recognized by the federal courts.

During the fall, federalized Arkansas National Guard units gradually assumed much of the protective role. Guard personnel remained for the school year.

Federalism and Constitutional Authority

The Little Rock crisis became a major confrontation over the division of authority among state governments, federal courts, and the president.

Arkansas officials argued that the state possessed authority over education and public order.

The federal government responded that state authority could not be exercised in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment or in defiance of federal court orders.

Federalism does not permit a state governor to nullify constitutional rights or use state forces to defeat judgments binding upon the state.

The crisis therefore concerned both school integration and the enforceability of the federal Constitution.

Inside Central High School

Entry into the building did not end the crisis.

The students faced racial insults, threats, isolation, spitting, tripping, shoving, kicks, punches, thrown objects, damaged property, and other forms of harassment.

Guards could escort them through hallways but could not remain with them inside classrooms, restrooms, locker rooms, or every other school space.

Some white students assisted or befriended members of the Nine, but those students could also face threats and social retaliation.

School administrators disciplined some attackers, but enforcement was uneven and could not eliminate the hostile environment.

Academic and Social Restrictions

The Black students entered Central for access to educational opportunities unavailable at their segregated schools, including a wider range of courses and facilities.

At the same time, transfer conditions excluded them from many extracurricular and social activities.

Their participation in athletics, clubs, performances, and school social life was restricted even while they carried the burden of demonstrating that desegregation could operate.

Formal admission therefore did not provide full equality within the institution.

Minnijean Brown's Suspension and Expulsion

Minnijean Brown became a major target of physical and verbal harassment.

In December 1957, she dropped chili on white boys who had blocked and harassed her in the cafeteria. School authorities suspended her.

In February 1958, Brown was again assaulted and responded by calling a white student “white trash.”

The school board expelled Brown for the remainder of the academic year.

Segregationist students circulated cards reading “One Down and Eight to Go.”

Brown's removal demonstrated how disciplinary systems could evaluate a Black student's response separately from the prolonged pattern of abuse that produced it.

Ernest Green's Graduation

Ernest Green was the only senior among the Little Rock Nine.

On May 27, 1958, he became the first African American student to graduate from Central High School.

Federal troops and local police remained present during the commencement ceremony.

Martin Luther King Jr. attended the graduation as a guest of the Green family.

Green's diploma marked a concrete institutional result: despite state obstruction, mob violence, and a year of harassment, Central High had been desegregated and a Black student had completed its academic program.

The Students' Families

The Nine did not bear the consequences alone.

Parents and relatives received threatening calls and letters, faced surveillance, lost employment, encountered economic retaliation, and feared attacks on their homes.

Families had to decide whether to continue sending their children into a dangerous environment while also recognizing the larger importance of the legal struggle.

The pressure demonstrates that school desegregation was enforced not only through rules governing students but also through economic and social coercion directed at Black households.

The Black Community

Black churches, civil-rights groups, attorneys, journalists, neighbors, and local residents provided support.

The crisis also created fear within the community because white retaliation could extend beyond the students and their families.

Some Black residents worried that the confrontation would endanger jobs, homes, businesses, and personal safety.

These concerns did not reflect indifference to equality. They reflected the unequal distribution of risk in a system where segregationists possessed extensive political, economic, and coercive power.

The Press and Public Record

Black newspapers, local newspapers, national publications, radio, television, wire services, and photographers documented the crisis.

The Arkansas State Press advocated desegregation and reported from the perspective of the Black community.

The Arkansas Gazette supported compliance with federal law and later received Pulitzer Prizes for public service and editorial writing.

Photographs of Elizabeth Eckford, the surrounding mob, armed troops, and the students entering under military escort became central evidence of the conflict.

The attack on Black journalists on September 23 also demonstrated that segregationists attempted to intimidate the people documenting their actions.

Cold War and International Attention

The United States presented itself internationally as a defender of democracy, constitutional government, and human freedom.

Images of Black children being excluded from school by soldiers and threatened by white mobs contradicted that message.

Foreign governments, international newspapers, anticolonial movements, and Soviet propaganda used the crisis to question American claims of democratic leadership.

International embarrassment did not create the constitutional duty to desegregate schools, but it increased the strategic cost of allowing state resistance and mob violence to prevail.

Cooper v. Aaron

After the 1957–1958 school year, the Little Rock School Board sought permission to delay further desegregation.

A federal district judge granted a delay until 1961, reasoning that disorder and hostility had made continued implementation difficult.

The federal appellate court reversed that decision.

On September 12, 1958, the Supreme Court unanimously required Little Rock to continue its desegregation plan.

In Cooper v. Aaron, the Court held that state officials were bound by the Constitution and by the Court's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment in Brown.

Officials could not use violence, disorder, public opposition, or state law as reasons to suspend constitutional rights.

Constitutional Supremacy

Cooper v. Aaron became one of the Supreme Court's most important statements concerning constitutional supremacy.

The Court emphasized that state legislators, governors, school officials, and judges could not wage resistance against the federal Constitution while claiming to exercise ordinary state authority.

The ruling did not grant federal courts unlimited power over every educational decision.

It established that state control of education must remain subject to constitutional rights and binding federal judgments.

The Lost Year

Governor Faubus responded to the Supreme Court's 1958 ruling by closing Little Rock's four public high schools.

The closures remained in effect throughout the 1958–1959 academic year and affected approximately 3,700 students.

Some white students entered private schools or studied elsewhere. Black students had fewer replacement options and often had to leave the city, use correspondence courses, or lose substantial educational time.

The closures demonstrated that segregationist officials were willing to deprive both Black and white students of public education rather than operate integrated schools.

The period became known as the Lost Year.

The Women's Emergency Committee and STOP

The Women's Emergency Committee to Open Our Schools formed in September 1958 to advocate reopening Little Rock's public high schools.

Its members included white women who opposed the destruction of public education even when they held different views concerning the pace or principle of integration.

In 1959, the committee joined business leaders in Stop This Outrageous Purge, commonly known as STOP.

STOP organized a recall campaign after segregationist school-board members attempted to remove forty-four teachers and administrators accused of supporting desegregation.

Voters removed the three segregationist board members in May 1959.

Reopening the Schools

Federal courts invalidated Arkansas's school-closing system.

Little Rock's public high schools reopened on August 12, 1959.

Segregationists again marched toward Central High, but local police and firefighters dispersed the crowd and made arrests.

Carlotta Walls and Jefferson Thomas returned to Central and graduated in 1960.

The reopening ended the immediate school-closing crisis but did not produce rapid, comprehensive integration throughout the district.

What the Little Rock Nine Achieved

The students converted the constitutional promise of Brown into an actual test of school admission and attendance.

Their persistence forced local, state, and federal institutions to reveal whether they would obey or resist federal law.

The crisis produced direct executive enforcement through the federalization of a state National Guard and deployment of Army troops.

Ernest Green's graduation established that Black students could complete Central High's academic program despite organized resistance.

Cooper v. Aaron strengthened constitutional protections by rejecting state attempts to evade Supreme Court decisions.

The Little Rock Nine also provided a model of youth participation in the Civil Rights Movement before the sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and student voter-registration campaigns of the 1960s.

Limits of the Victory

The admission of nine students did not immediately desegregate the entire Little Rock School District.

The Blossom Plan was gradual, narrowly administered, and designed to limit the number of Black students entering white schools.

Formal attendance did not protect the Nine from harassment or guarantee equal participation in school life.

The Lost Year demonstrated that state resistance could continue after a major federal intervention and Supreme Court ruling.

Substantial school integration in Little Rock required additional litigation, federal oversight, local organizing, and years of institutional change.

The Personal Cost of Desegregation

Public accounts sometimes describe the Nine as symbols of courage without fully explaining what their courage required.

They attended school in an environment where classmates and adults threatened their lives, attacked them physically, excluded them socially, and attempted to provoke disciplinary responses.

Their families faced economic and physical retaliation, while the students lost ordinary educational and adolescent experiences.

Desegregation therefore imposed extraordinary personal costs upon Black children because public institutions had failed to protect rights that the Constitution already recognized.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

The Little Rock Nine did not initiate the national legal attack on school segregation. Their actions implemented a constitutional struggle developed through decades of Black education advocacy and NAACP litigation.

The Little Rock School Board announced an intention to comply with Brown, but its Blossom Plan was gradual and preserved extensive racial separation.

Governor Faubus described the National Guard deployment as a public-safety measure, but the Guard's operational action was to exclude Black students while allowing white students to attend.

Federal troops did not create the students' constitutional rights. They enforced rights already recognized by the Supreme Court and protected by federal court orders.

The crisis did not end on September 25, 1957. Harassment continued throughout the school year, and state resistance produced the Lost Year.

Minnijean Brown's expulsion should not be presented without the prolonged harassment and physical attacks that preceded her responses.

Ernest Green's graduation was a major institutional milestone, but one graduate did not represent complete desegregation of the school district.

The Little Rock crisis was not merely a dispute over local school administration. It was a test of whether state officials could defy the Constitution and federal courts.

Why the Little Rock Nine Matter

The Little Rock Nine transformed school desegregation from an abstract constitutional rule into an institutional test conducted before the nation and the world.

Their attempt to attend school revealed that local and state officials could publicly promise compliance while using assignment systems, litigation, military forces, school closures, and political pressure to restrict or defeat implementation.

The crisis demonstrated that a Supreme Court decision is not self-executing. Courts require officials, administrators, police, and sometimes the executive branch to enforce their judgments.

Eisenhower's deployment of federal troops established that a president could use executive and military authority to protect federal court orders when state and local enforcement failed.

Cooper v. Aaron made the crisis a lasting constitutional precedent by rejecting the principle that state officials could decide whether to obey the Supreme Court.

The students' experience exposed the difference between formal admission and actual equality. They were present within Central High while remaining subject to harassment, exclusion, restricted participation, and unequal protection.

The role of Daisy Bates, the NAACP, lawyers, families, journalists, and community organizations demonstrates that individual courage depended upon organized institutional support.

The Lost Year showed that massive resistance could damage public institutions and deny education to thousands of children in an effort to preserve racial hierarchy.

The Little Rock Nine also established an important tradition of youth leadership within the Civil Rights Movement.

Their actions remain a central example of the personal sacrifices required when children and families are compelled to enforce constitutional principles that public authorities have refused to honor.

Summary

The Little Rock Nine were nine African American students who desegregated Central High School during the 1957–1958 academic year. Their enrollment followed the Supreme Court's rejection of public-school segregation and a federal court order approving Little Rock's gradual desegregation plan.

Governor Orval Faubus used the Arkansas National Guard to block the students, while white mobs and hostile classmates attempted to prevent or undermine their attendance. President Dwight D. Eisenhower federalized the Guard and deployed the 101st Airborne Division to enforce the court order. The students nevertheless endured sustained physical, verbal, social, and psychological abuse throughout the school year.

The crisis established that constitutional rights require institutional enforcement. It produced the major Supreme Court ruling Cooper v. Aaron, exposed the destructive consequences of massive resistance, and made the courage of Black students and families a defining part of the Civil Rights Movement.
