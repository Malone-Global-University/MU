Minneapolis Third Precinct Fire

The May 28, 2020 breach and arson of the Minneapolis Police Department's Third Precinct headquarters after police evacuated the building during the protests and civil unrest following the murder of George Floyd.

What Was the Minneapolis Third Precinct Fire?

The Minneapolis Third Precinct Fire was the deliberate burning of a police headquarters after officers withdrew from the building during the third night of demonstrations and civil unrest following George Floyd's murder.

The event included several related but distinct actions: a command decision to evacuate, the collapse of the perimeter, entry and theft by members of the crowd, multiple acts of arson, delayed firefighting because of dangerous conditions, and later criminal investigation and prosecution.

The fire was not a single spontaneous accident and was not committed by every person who protested near the station.

Federal cases established the criminal responsibility of specific individuals for conspiracy to commit arson, while other participants in the crowd engaged only in protest, observation, property damage, looting, celebration, documentation, medical assistance, or efforts to discourage violence.

Scope of the Event

The canonical event begins with the escalating confrontation around the Third Precinct on May 28 and extends through the evacuation, breach, fires, immediate emergency response, and initial damage assessment on May 29.

The protests beginning May 26 provide necessary context.

Federal prosecutions, government reviews, precinct relocation, and redevelopment of the building form the institutional aftermath.

The event is distinct from the complete history of the George Floyd protests, the broader Minneapolis–Saint Paul civil unrest, the murder of George Floyd, and the later political campaign to replace or reorganize the Minneapolis Police Department.

The Third Precinct

The Minneapolis Police Department divides the city into geographic precincts.

The Third Precinct serves much of South Minneapolis, including the area around East Lake Street, Minnehaha Avenue, and the intersection of 38th Street and Chicago Avenue where George Floyd was murdered.

The station at 3000 Minnehaha Avenue functioned as a workplace, equipment center, roll-call location, administrative office, public-service point, and visible symbol of police authority.

Its location near a major commercial corridor placed it close to neighborhoods with long histories of immigration, Black and Indigenous residence, working-class life, activism, business development, police contact, and conflict over public safety.

Police-Community Relations Before 2020

Distrust of the Minneapolis Police Department did not begin with George Floyd's death.

Black, Indigenous, Latino, immigrant, homeless, and low-income residents had reported racial disparities, unnecessary force, disrespect, weak discipline, aggressive street enforcement, and inadequate accountability.

Police officers and supporters emphasized violent crime, emergency response, officer injuries, staffing demands, and the difficulty of working in neighborhoods with serious public-safety problems.

The Third Precinct therefore represented different things to different residents.

To some it was a source of emergency service and neighborhood security.

To others it represented coercive power, racialized policing, and repeated institutional failure.

Murder of George Floyd

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police officers arrested George Floyd outside Cup Foods at 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

Derek Chauvin pressed his knee against Floyd's neck and upper back for approximately nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds while Floyd was handcuffed and repeatedly said that he could not breathe.

Darnella Frazier recorded the restraint, and the video circulated nationally.

The police department's incomplete initial statement contrasted sharply with the visible evidence.

Public anger focused upon the killing itself, the officers' conduct, the delayed arrest of Chauvin, prior complaints against police, and longstanding racial injustice.

May 26: First Major Demonstration at the Precinct

Demonstrators gathered at the site of Floyd's death and marched to the Third Precinct.

The demonstration included speeches, chants, signs, grieving, demands for prosecution, and criticism of Minneapolis police.

Some individuals damaged police vehicles, windows, fencing, and other property.

Police used chemical agents and less-lethal projectiles to push people away from the station.

The confrontation established the precinct as the principal physical focus of Minneapolis protest during the first days after Floyd's death.

May 27: Escalation

Crowds returned to the precinct and Lake Street area on May 27.

Peaceful protest continued while other people broke windows, looted stores, set fires, attacked property, or confronted officers.

Police repeatedly deployed chemical agents and less-lethal munitions.

Businesses near the precinct suffered extensive theft and damage.

A construction site, retail properties, and other structures burned.

Minneapolis officials requested Minnesota National Guard assistance, but local and state accounts later disputed whether the request was sufficiently detailed and how quickly state resources were mobilized.

Police Preparations Before the Evacuation

By May 28, the building had already sustained broken windows and substantial exterior damage.

Police vehicles and officers' personal vehicles had been damaged in the parking area.

Commanders removed weapons, computers, memorabilia, personal property, and other high-value items.

Vehicles were moved away from the station.

The building no longer operated normally as a neighborhood precinct.

Third Precinct officers parked elsewhere, answered emergency calls from dispersed locations, or entered the building in groups assigned to defend it.

May 28: Crowd and Perimeter

Hundreds of people gathered around the Third Precinct during the evening.

Participants included peaceful demonstrators, neighborhood residents, journalists, medics, observers, people damaging property, looters, arsonists, and individuals who had travelled to Minneapolis for ideological or criminal purposes.

Officers defended the building from behind fencing and barriers.

Police and members of the crowd exchanged projectiles, chemical agents, fireworks, and less-lethal munitions during portions of the confrontation.

The situation became increasingly difficult to control as fires and property destruction spread through the surrounding commercial area.

Minnesota National Guard Activation

Governor Tim Walz signed an executive order activating the Minnesota National Guard on May 28.

Guard personnel were not positioned at the Third Precinct in sufficient numbers to prevent its evacuation and breach that evening.

Later reviews identified failures in the speed, clarity, scale, and coordination of requests and deployments among Minneapolis, the Minnesota Department of Public Safety, state leadership, police, fire services, and the Guard.

City officials argued that they had requested help earlier.

State officials argued that local requests initially lacked operational detail.

The precinct fire became the event after which state leadership recognized that local authorities had been overwhelmed.

Decision to Evacuate

Minneapolis police initially planned to replace a larger group of exhausted officers with a smaller tactical team capable of defending the building.

Commander Scott Gerlicher later testified that police leadership received direction at approximately 10:00 p.m. not to use additional chemical agents or less-lethal projectiles solely to protect the station.

Without those tools, commanders concluded that the remaining team could not hold the building safely.

Police fully evacuated at approximately 10:15 p.m.

Testimony identified the directive as passing from Mayor Jacob Frey through Police Chief Medaria Arradondo.

Both men later defended the withdrawal as a decision to protect human life rather than sacrifice officers or civilians for a building.

Disputes over Command Responsibility

The evacuation produced years of political dispute.

Police-union representatives, state legislators, city officials, state officials, and commanders offered different accounts of who decided to withdraw and whether National Guard assistance arrived too late.

Some critics described the decision as surrender that encouraged further destruction.

Others argued that defending a damaged building would have required potentially lethal force against a large crowd and could have caused many deaths.

The surviving record supports the conclusion that the withdrawal was a command decision rather than an accidental collapse, while responsibility for the wider emergency-management failure was shared and disputed.

Police Withdrawal

Officers left the precinct in vehicles and on foot under tense conditions.

They carried available equipment and moved through routes that avoided direct entrapment by the crowd.

The withdrawal left the building without police protection.

It also removed a concentrated police target from the scene, reducing the immediate risk of a direct armed confrontation inside or immediately around the station.

Breach of the Precinct

After officers departed, people tore down or moved security fencing and entered the property.

Some participants broke doors and windows, moved through offices and corridors, removed equipment, damaged furniture and records, and carried police property outside.

The crowd outside included people cheering, chanting, filming, or watching as others entered.

Presence at the scene did not establish participation in arson.

Criminal liability depended upon the actions and intent of particular individuals.

Starting and Accelerating the Fires

Investigators found evidence of multiple separate fires.

Fires burned in or near the entrance and in interior areas.

Federal plea records described participants lighting an incendiary device, carrying it into the building, throwing combustible material, and pushing a barrel into an existing entrance fire to accelerate it.

Other unidentified participants also started or fed fires.

The existence of several ignition points and deliberate acceleration established arson rather than accidental spread from a nearby property.

Incendiary Device

Surveillance evidence showed Bryce Michael Williams holding a Molotov cocktail near the entrance.

Davon De-Andre Turner helped light the wick and carried the device into the station, where it was used to start or intensify a fire.

Williams later threw a box onto an existing fire outside the entrance.

A Molotov cocktail is an improvised incendiary weapon consisting generally of a breakable container filled with flammable liquid and fitted with an ignitable wick.

Its use in an occupied or recently occupied building creates a substantial risk of death, burns, smoke inhalation, structural collapse, and uncontrolled fire spread.

Theft of Police Property

People removed uniforms, protective equipment, ammunition, radios, handcuffs, batons, documents, and other police property.

Branden Wolfe later admitted entering the building and stealing numerous items, including body armor, a duty belt, ammunition, a police radio, a riot helmet, and other equipment.

Stolen police clothing and equipment created additional public-safety risks because they could be used to impersonate officers or support later violence.

Theft and arson were related parts of the breach but remained legally distinct acts.

Growth of the Fire

Flames spread through the entrance and interior as combustible materials ignited.

Smoke and fire became visible from surrounding streets and were broadcast live.

People continued entering or standing near the building despite the growing danger.

Fire damaged structural and mechanical systems, offices, interior finishes, records, equipment, windows, doors, and the building envelope.

Water used later to suppress the fire added further damage.

Why Firefighters Did Not Immediately Enter

Minneapolis fire commanders concluded that crews could not safely approach the precinct while the crowd remained uncontrolled and violence continued.

Firefighters elsewhere during the unrest encountered threats, attacks, stolen equipment, blocked routes, and rapidly changing hazards.

Fire engines require space, water access, protective security, and safe withdrawal routes.

Sending firefighters into an unsecured crowd around a burning police station could have exposed them to assault, gunfire, entrapment, or loss of emergency vehicles.

Delayed response allowed the fire to cause greater damage but reflected an immediate life-safety judgment rather than a conclusion that the building had no value.

Gunfire into the Building

Ivan Harrison Hunter travelled from San Antonio, Texas, to Minneapolis intending to participate in civil unrest.

Hunter identified himself with the Boogaloo Bois, a loose anti-government movement whose adherents anticipated or sought violent civil conflict.

On May 28, he fired thirteen rounds from an AK-47-style semiautomatic rifle into the entrance of the Third Precinct.

Other people believed to be looters remained inside.

Federal prosecutors attributed approximately $85,000 in building damage to the gunfire.

Hunter's actions demonstrate that the unrest attracted armed ideological actors whose objectives differed from racial-justice protest.

No Single Crowd Identity

The crowd should not be described as one organization acting under one command.

Participants held different motives and engaged in different conduct.

Some protested police brutality.

Some lived nearby and watched events affecting their neighborhood.

Some provided medical care, food, water, or documentation.

Some damaged or stole property.

Some committed arson.

Some celebrated the destruction as symbolic rebellion.

Some attempted to discourage fires or protect neighboring property.

Some outside extremists sought wider disorder.

Accurate history separates these groups rather than assigning every act to all protesters or to a single movement organization.

Relationship to Black Lives Matter

The gathering occurred within the wider Black Lives Matter protest wave following Floyd's murder.

The Black Lives Matter movement was decentralized and did not exercise operational control over everyone present at the precinct.

A person could invoke racial justice while engaging in conduct rejected by local organizers or other protesters.

Federal convictions for arson established the responsibility of named defendants, not the criminal responsibility of the movement as a whole.

Likewise, describing the fire only as unrelated criminality can obscure why the station became such a powerful political target.

Symbolism of the Station

Police stations represent state authority, emergency service, detention, investigation, and the organized capacity to use force.

To many demonstrators, the Third Precinct symbolized the department responsible for Floyd's death and a wider history of police violence.

To officers, families, victims of crime, and many residents, it symbolized emergency protection and the local presence of government.

Burning the building therefore produced both celebration and fear.

The same act could be interpreted as rebellion against abusive authority, destruction of public safety, criminal arson, political spectacle, or evidence of government collapse.

Historical explanation should identify these interpretations without treating them as legally equivalent.

Protest, Civil Disobedience, Riot, and Arson

Peaceful protest uses speech, assembly, march, vigil, petition, performance, or similar protected conduct.

Civil disobedience intentionally violates a law to challenge policy and commonly accepts the possibility of arrest.

Riot generally involves a group participating in or threatening serious public violence under applicable law.

Arson is the intentional or malicious burning of property and is a serious violent offense because fire spreads unpredictably and endangers occupants and responders.

The Third Precinct event contained protected protest, unlawful assembly, property crime, and arson in the same geographic setting.

One category should not erase the others.

Damage to the Building

Federal prosecutors described the station as nearly destroyed.

The city later described it as extensively damaged by fire and water.

The building remained vacant for years behind fencing and security barriers.

Damage included burned interiors, broken windows and doors, damaged service systems, debris, graffiti, weather exposure, smoke residue, water damage, and the loss or destruction of equipment and contents.

The structure was not completely demolished, which later made adaptive reuse possible.

Damage beyond the Precinct

The precinct fire occurred amid extensive destruction along Lake Street and elsewhere in Minneapolis and Saint Paul.

Small businesses, restaurants, pharmacies, grocery stores, service providers, nonprofit organizations, residences, construction sites, and other properties were looted, burned, or damaged.

Many affected businesses belonged to immigrants, Black residents, Indigenous residents, and other communities already facing economic insecurity during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The precinct became the most recognizable building fire, but it was not the only or necessarily the most consequential loss for neighborhood residents.

Live Media and Public Perception

Television stations, professional journalists, livestreamers, residents, and participants recorded the evacuation, breach, fires, celebrations, and surrounding destruction.

Images of flames emerging from a police station circulated nationally and internationally.

The visual event became shorthand for Minneapolis losing control and for the scale of anger following Floyd's murder.

Repeated focus upon the fire sometimes displaced attention from Floyd, peaceful demonstrations, local organizing, business losses, and the specific people who committed arson.

Video also became essential evidence for federal investigators identifying participants.

Immediate Aftermath

The fire transformed the emergency response.

State officials expanded National Guard deployment, and Minnesota moved toward a larger unified public-safety operation.

Curfews, arrests, road closures, Guard patrols, state police deployments, and protected fire operations followed.

Protest continued, while officials focused increasingly upon restoring order and preventing attacks upon additional precincts, government buildings, businesses, and residential areas.

The event also intensified national discussion of whether police departments could maintain legitimacy and whether cities were capable of protecting both constitutional protest and public safety.

A Turning Point in the Unrest

Government reviews and testimony described the fall of the Third Precinct as the moment officials recognized that ordinary local response had failed.

Police officers interpreted the loss as evidence that no facility was secure.

Some residents feared that emergency services could no longer protect neighborhoods.

Some protesters interpreted the evacuation as proof that sustained mass action could force police to retreat.

The event encouraged both further protest mobilization and further destructive activity, while prompting a much larger government deployment.

Arson Investigation

The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, Federal Bureau of Investigation, Minneapolis Police Department, and Minnesota State Fire Marshal Division investigated the fire.

Investigators used building surveillance footage, public video, social-media posts, witness information, recovered property, fire-scene evidence, travel records, and later admissions.

The number of participants and the destruction of the scene made it impossible to identify or prosecute every person who entered, damaged, looted, or burned the building.

Federal cases therefore established responsibility for particular defendants without claiming that those prosecutions represented every person involved.

Federal Arson Prosecution

Federal law criminalizes arson affecting property used in interstate or foreign commerce or in activities affecting such commerce.

Conspiracy liability applies when people agree to commit a federal offense and take action to advance the agreement.

A conspirator need not personally ignite every fire to be responsible for jointly planned or assisted arson.

The four principal defendants each pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson rather than proceeding to trial.

Guilty pleas are convictions based upon admissions and judicial acceptance, not merely accusations.

Dylan Shakespeare Robinson

Robinson entered the precinct area after the perimeter was breached.

He assisted an unidentified participant in lighting an object that was thrown toward the building to start or fuel a fire.

Robinson pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson.

In April 2021, the federal court sentenced him to forty-eight months in prison, two years of supervised release, and $12 million in restitution.

Branden Michael Wolfe

Wolfe pushed a barrel into an entrance fire with the intent to accelerate it.

He entered the building and stole police equipment, ammunition, protective gear, and other property.

Police later arrested him after finding him with items connected to the precinct.

Wolfe pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson.

In May 2021, he received forty-one months in prison, two years of supervised release, and $12 million in restitution.

Davon De-Andre Turner

Turner worked with Bryce Williams to light an incendiary device.

Turner carried the device into the precinct, where it was used to start or accelerate a fire.

He pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson.

In May 2021, he received thirty-six months in prison, two years of supervised release, and $12 million in restitution.

Bryce Michael Williams

Surveillance video showed Williams holding the incendiary device while Turner lit it.

Williams later placed a box onto an existing fire outside the entrance.

He pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson.

In June 2021, he received twenty-seven months in prison, two years of supervised release, and $12 million in restitution.

Understanding the $12 Million Restitution Orders

Federal sentencing announcements stated that each of the four arson defendants was ordered to pay $12 million in restitution.

This does not mean that the government calculated four separate $12 million buildings or a total loss of $48 million.

Restitution in a conspiracy may make several defendants responsible for the same underlying loss, subject to the court's judgment and collection rules.

News summaries should therefore describe the loss as approximately $12 million and the defendants as individually subject to restitution orders tied to that loss.

Ivan Hunter and Boogaloo Extremism

Hunter's case was separate from the four-person arson conspiracy.

He travelled from Texas, met other Boogaloo adherents, obtained a rifle, and fired into the precinct while people remained inside.

He later made statements celebrating the violence and communicating with members of the same extremist milieu.

Hunter pleaded guilty to a federal riot offense.

In April 2022, he received fifty-two months in prison and three years of supervised release.

His conduct complicates claims that every violent actor at the precinct was a local racial-justice protester.

Local Participants and “Outside Agitator” Claims

Officials and commentators initially made broad claims that most destructive actors came from outside Minnesota.

Available cases do not support a simple outsider-only explanation.

The four principal arson defendants were Minnesota residents from Brainerd, Saint Paul, and Staples.

Hunter travelled from Texas and represented a genuine outside extremist presence.

Other participants came from Minneapolis, nearby communities, other states, or remain unidentified.

Geographic origin alone does not establish motive, organization, or criminal responsibility.

After-Action Reviews

City, state, legislative, and law-enforcement reviews examined the response to the unrest.

Reviews identified inadequate preparation for large-scale civil disorder, unclear command structures, delayed resource deployment, inconsistent emergency procedures, poor communication, incomplete intelligence sharing, and confusion regarding responsibility.

They also examined police use of chemical agents and less-lethal weapons, the protection of firefighters, National Guard mobilization, and failure to support affected residents and businesses.

Different reviews reflected different political perspectives and did not agree on every assignment of blame.

They broadly supported the conclusion that Minneapolis and Minnesota lacked a sufficiently coordinated response during the critical first days.

Life-Safety Rationale

A police department is not ordinarily required to defend every government building regardless of the human cost.

Commanders must consider crowd size, available personnel, weapons, fire, escape routes, risk to nearby residents, and whether holding a position requires lethal force.

Arradondo later emphasized that officers could be replaced but lives could not.

This rationale explains the withdrawal without proving that every preceding emergency decision was sound.

A tactically justified evacuation may still occur within a broader failure of planning, reinforcement, communication, and public protection.

Criticism of the Evacuation

Critics argued that the city surrendered a major symbol of lawful authority and encouraged further arson and looting.

They contended that earlier National Guard positioning, a stronger perimeter, a coordinated fire plan, arrests of violent offenders, or different rules of engagement might have prevented the breach.

Police officers reported feeling abandoned by political leadership.

Residents and business owners reported that emergency services disappeared when they were needed most.

These concerns address the cumulative response rather than proving that officers should have remained inside once evacuation became necessary.

Effects on Businesses and Residents

The station fire occurred within a neighborhood rather than an isolated government complex.

Residents experienced smoke, gunfire, blocked streets, loss of pharmacies and grocery access, damaged housing, fear of further fires, interruption of transit, and reduced emergency response.

Business owners faced theft, fire damage, insurance disputes, cleanup costs, lost employment, reduced customer access, and long rebuilding periods.

Some supported the protests while condemning destruction.

Others believed government had protected a police institution more consistently before May 28 than it protected neighborhood businesses afterward.

Public-Safety Gap

Loss of the station did not eliminate police jurisdiction over the Third Precinct area.

Officers continued answering calls from alternative facilities and mobile assignments.

The absence of a permanent neighborhood station complicated roll call, evidence handling, public access, investigations, staff coordination, and community relationships.

Residents disagreed over whether rebuilding a conventional precinct would restore safety or reproduce a distrusted institution.

Relationship to Defund and Abolition Debates

After Floyd's murder, activists demanded reductions in police power and investment in non-police safety systems.

Some used “defund the police” to describe shifting funds toward housing, health, violence prevention, mental-health response, and community programs.

Police abolition advocates sought more fundamental replacement of policing and punishment institutions.

The fire was not a formal city policy and should not be described as implementation of either proposal.

Its symbolism nevertheless influenced public expectations about whether the Third Precinct should return to the same site and form.

Debate over Rebuilding the Precinct

Minneapolis considered restoring police operations at 3000 Minnehaha Avenue or selecting another location.

Some residents wanted a visible and accessible police presence restored quickly.

Others regarded return to the burned building as provocative, traumatic, or inconsistent with new approaches to public safety.

The city conducted meetings, surveys, and engagement with neighborhood and cultural communities during 2023.

Community opinions remained divided, and many participants criticized the available choices or the engagement process.

New Third Precinct Site

In November 2023, Minneapolis selected 2633 Minnehaha Avenue for the future Third Precinct station.

The station will form part of the South Minneapolis Community Safety Center rather than returning to 3000 Minnehaha Avenue.

The broader center is intended to include police and other community-safety functions.

This decision separated the future of the police precinct from the future of the burned building.

Minneapolis Democracy Center

Minneapolis designated the former Third Precinct building as the Minneapolis Democracy Center.

Planned uses include Elections and Voter Services offices, an early-voting center, a polling place, storage and flexible election space, and a large ground-floor area for a community partner.

The reuse transforms a building associated with police authority, Floyd's murder, civil unrest, and arson into a center for voting and civic participation.

Supporters view the change as constructive adaptive reuse.

Critics may question whether renaming and renovation adequately acknowledge the site's contested history.

Cleanup and Stabilization

The building remained vacant after the 2020 fire.

City crews removed interior debris, trash, signs, graffiti, and hazardous materials while conducting security and condition checks.

Minneapolis allocated $1.5 million in 2024 for additional cleanup and stabilization.

Work included engineering updates, repairs to service components, replacement of windows and doors, restored glazing, and reconstruction of the front entry.

Fencing was moved away from the sidewalk, and razor wire was removed.

Status through August 2026

The city officially referred to the property as the Minneapolis Democracy Center.

Design work was completed in May 2026.

Construction bidding began in summer 2026.

Major renovation construction was scheduled to begin in fall 2026.

The city expected to seek a community partner for the public space through a later request for proposals.

The building had therefore moved from long-term vacancy and stabilization into active redevelopment, but the completed democracy center was not yet fully operating as of August 1, 2026.

Adaptive Reuse and Historical Meaning

Adaptive reuse preserves a building while assigning it a new function.

Reuse can retain embodied history, reduce demolition waste, restore a damaged site, and support neighborhood activity.

It can also obscure history if renovation removes evidence without interpretation.

The Democracy Center project raises questions about whether the fire, Floyd's murder, protest history, police-community conflict, and neighborhood losses will be explained within the completed site.

A civic-use conversion is historically significant precisely because the former function remains part of the building's identity.

Legal and Moral Distinctions

Understanding why people hated or feared the police station does not legally justify arson.

Condemning the arson does not require defending the police conduct that caused the protests.

Recognizing a symbol of rebellion does not erase the danger to people inside, firefighters, neighbors, and surrounding businesses.

Recognizing the dangers of the fire does not make all demonstrators responsible.

Historical accuracy requires holding these conclusions together rather than selecting only the one most convenient to a political position.

Public Memory

Images of the burning precinct became a defining visual record of 2020.

Supporters of the uprising sometimes represented it as proof that oppressive institutions could be challenged.

Critics represented it as proof of lawlessness, failed leadership, and hostility toward police.

Neighborhood residents often remembered a more complicated event involving grief, fear, political hope, property loss, mutual aid, danger, and abandonment.

Redevelopment of the building ensures that public disagreement over its meaning will continue.

“Protest,” “Riot,” “Uprising,” and “Rebellion”

Different terms emphasize different aspects of the 2020 events.

“Protest” emphasizes collective expression and political demands.

“Civil unrest” describes disorder without determining its legitimacy.

“Riot” emphasizes group violence and criminal conduct and also has specific legal meanings.

“Uprising” emphasizes resistance to institutional oppression and the wider political character of events.

“Rebellion” emphasizes rejection of state authority.

The Third Precinct fire may be situated within all of these broader descriptions, but the act of intentionally burning the building is accurately classified as arson.

Major Outcomes

Minneapolis police evacuated and lost use of the Third Precinct headquarters.

Arson caused approximately $12 million in loss and left the building vacant for years.

Four defendants received federal conspiracy-to-commit-arson convictions and prison sentences.

A separate armed extremist received a federal riot conviction for firing into the building.

The event accelerated state and National Guard response to the wider unrest.

Government reviews documented serious failures in planning, command, communications, deployment, and emergency coordination.

The Third Precinct operated without a permanent neighborhood headquarters for years.

Minneapolis selected a different site for the future precinct and community safety center.

The burned building was renamed and planned for conversion into the Minneapolis Democracy Center.

Limits and Unresolved Questions

Investigators did not identify or prosecute every person who started, fed, or assisted the fires.

Public video does not capture every action inside the building.

Official accounts continue to differ regarding responsibility for delayed National Guard support and the precise chain of political decision-making.

The counterfactual question of whether different tactics could have saved the building without causing deaths cannot be answered conclusively.

Restitution orders do not establish how much money defendants were able to pay.

The long-term effectiveness of the new community safety center and Democracy Center remained unknown in August 2026.

The completed redevelopment may change how the site presents or suppresses its 2020 history.

Misinformation and Common Errors

The precinct burned on May 28, three days after Floyd was murdered.

Police evacuated before the crowd entered and set major fires; officers were not defending the interior while it burned.

The building was heavily damaged but not reduced completely to an empty foundation.

Investigators found multiple deliberate fires. The event was arson, not an accidental building fire.

No evidence establishes that every demonstrator participated in or supported the arson.

The four principal federal arson defendants were Minnesota residents, while the armed Boogaloo participant came from Texas.

The presence of an outside extremist does not prove that outsiders committed all destruction.

The $12 million restitution orders should not be added together as four independent building-loss estimates.

Hunter was convicted of rioting for firing into the precinct; he was not one of the four defendants convicted in the arson conspiracy.

The fire did not formally abolish, defund, or disband the Minneapolis Police Department.

The Third Precinct continued serving its geographic area from other locations.

The future Third Precinct station is planned for 2633 Minnehaha Avenue, not the burned building.

The former station is being redeveloped as the Minneapolis Democracy Center, but it was not yet a fully completed operating center as of August 1, 2026.

Key Distinctions

Third Precinct
The geographic police command serving part of South Minneapolis.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Use “fire” for the event title but identify the cause as deliberate arson.

Do not describe the building as fully demolished. It was heavily damaged, remained standing, and is being reused.

Do not describe every person near the station as an arsonist, rioter, Black Lives Matter member, or outside agitator.

Do not erase the peaceful protests that occurred alongside criminal acts.

Do not omit the danger that arson, gunfire, theft, and delayed firefighting created for residents, participants, and emergency personnel.

Do not claim that all destructive actors came from outside Minnesota. The four convicted arson conspirators were Minnesota residents.

Do not claim that no outsiders participated. Hunter travelled from Texas and fired into the station.

Distinguish the four arson-conspiracy convictions from Hunter's separate riot conviction.

Do not add the four $12 million restitution orders into a $48 million loss estimate.

Do not state that the precinct ceased operating as a police district. The headquarters was lost, but officers continued serving the area.

Do not state that the city has already opened a completed Democracy Center. Renovation remained underway in 2026.

Do not claim that the evacuation was caused exclusively by one official without acknowledging the chain of command and disputed emergency context.

Do not treat understanding the political symbolism of the fire as legal justification for arson.

Why the Minneapolis Third Precinct Fire Matters

The fire became the most visible destruction of a police facility during the 2020 George Floyd protests.

It showed the depth of public anger produced by Floyd's murder and longstanding distrust of Minneapolis police.

It also showed how quickly a protest site can become a lethal environment when arson, firearms, crowd pressure, police force, and emergency-response failure converge.

The evacuation demonstrated that government may decide to surrender property rather than defend it through potentially deadly force.

The destruction demonstrated that a life-safety withdrawal can create wider risks when no coordinated force is available to protect residents, businesses, firefighters, and public infrastructure afterward.

The criminal cases demonstrated how video, social media, surveillance systems, and recovered property can identify particular offenders within a large and decentralized crowd.

Hunter's case demonstrated that anti-government extremists attempted to exploit racial-justice unrest for separate violent objectives.

The event exposed failures of communication and command among city, state, police, fire, emergency-management, and National Guard institutions.

Years of disagreement over the precinct's location demonstrated that police facilities possess symbolic as well as operational meaning.

Conversion of the building into a democracy center represents an effort to replace a site associated with coercive authority and arson with voting and civic participation.

The success of that transformation will depend upon whether the completed site acknowledges the full history rather than treating redevelopment as erasure.

Summary

On May 28, 2020, hundreds of people gathered around the Minneapolis Police Department's Third Precinct during the third night of protest and civil unrest following George Floyd's murder. After two days of damage, confrontation, police chemical agents, looting, and fires, city and police leaders ordered officers to evacuate the station at approximately 10:15 p.m. because commanders concluded that holding it would create unacceptable risks to life.

Members of the crowd breached the perimeter, entered and looted the station, and deliberately started or accelerated multiple fires. Firefighters delayed entry because the area was unsafe. Four Minnesota men later pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit arson and received federal prison sentences ranging from twenty-seven to forty-eight months. A separate anti-government extremist who travelled from Texas received a fifty-two-month sentence after firing thirteen rifle rounds into the building while people remained inside.

The fire caused approximately $12 million in loss, displaced the Third Precinct, and became a turning point in the government response to the unrest. It also became a lasting symbol interpreted as rebellion against police abuse, destructive lawlessness, emergency-management failure, or some combination of those meanings. Minneapolis later chose a different location for the future Third Precinct and began converting the burned building into the Minneapolis Democracy Center. As of August 1, 2026, design was complete, bidding was underway, and major renovation was scheduled to begin in fall 2026.
