Surrender at Appomattox

The April 1865 surrender of Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant, which ended the principal Confederate army's resistance in Virginia, accelerated the collapse of the Confederacy, and helped secure Union victory and emancipation.

What Was the Surrender at Appomattox?

The Surrender at Appomattox was the negotiated military capitulation of General Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia to Lieutenant General Ulysses S. Grant on April 9, 1865.

Lee and Grant met in the McLean House at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, after Union forces blocked Lee's retreat and made further organized resistance by his army militarily untenable.

Grant established terms that required the Confederate army to surrender public weapons and military property while permitting officers and soldiers to return home on parole.

The agreement was not a peace treaty between two sovereign nations, a surrender of every Confederate army, or a political settlement determining Reconstruction. It was a military surrender of a specific field army.

Appomattox Court House: Name and Location

In nineteenth-century Virginia, a county-seat village was often identified by the name of the county followed by “Court House.”

Appomattox Court House referred to the village that served as the county seat, not merely to one courthouse building.

Lee and Grant did not sign the surrender inside the courthouse. They met in the nearby home of Wilmer McLean.

The historic village is also distinct from the present Town of Appomattox, which developed around the railroad location formerly known as Appomattox Station.

Military Context

By early 1865, the United States had gained decisive military and economic advantages over the Confederacy.

Union armies controlled the Mississippi River, occupied major Southern territory, disrupted Confederate railroads and supply systems, and threatened the remaining Confederate armies in Virginia and the Carolinas.

Lee's Army of Northern Virginia had defended Richmond and Petersburg through a prolonged campaign, but its manpower, food, transportation, and replacement capacity had deteriorated.

The Confederate government remained committed to resistance, yet its ability to sustain conventional war depended heavily on Lee's army.

Petersburg and Richmond

Petersburg was a major railroad and supply center supporting Richmond, the Confederate capital.

From June 1864 into April 1865, Union forces extended their lines around Petersburg, cut railroads, and pressured Confederate defenses.

The Union victory at Five Forks on April 1 threatened the remaining South Side Railroad and the Confederate right flank.

Grant ordered a general assault on April 2. Lee concluded that Richmond and Petersburg could no longer be defended and directed their evacuation.

Evacuation of Richmond and Petersburg

Confederate troops withdrew from the Richmond–Petersburg defenses during the night of April 2–3, 1865.

Confederate government officials, including President Jefferson Davis, left Richmond as Union forces approached.

Lee intended to concentrate his scattered columns at Amelia Court House, obtain food, move south along the Richmond and Danville Railroad, and join General Joseph E. Johnston's army in North Carolina.

Union forces entered Petersburg and Richmond on April 3, while Grant began an aggressive pursuit designed to prevent Lee from escaping or joining another Confederate army.

Lee's Retreat

The retreating Confederate army moved west and southwest through central Virginia while Union infantry and cavalry followed parallel routes.

Lee's columns included combat units, artillery, wagons, wounded soldiers, animals, and administrative personnel.

Hunger, exhaustion, damaged roads, swollen waterways, broken equipment, and Union attacks weakened the army.

Confederate soldiers fell out of formation to search for food, became separated from their commands, surrendered, deserted, or were captured.

The Delay at Amelia Court House

Lee expected food supplies to be available at Amelia Court House on April 4.

The needed rations were not present, forcing the army to halt while wagons searched the surrounding countryside.

The delay consumed time that Lee needed to place distance between his army and Grant's pursuing forces.

Union troops used the opportunity to move into positions blocking the direct route toward Danville.

Sailor's Creek

On April 6, Union forces attacked separated Confederate columns near Sailor's Creek.

Nearly 8,000 Confederate soldiers were killed, wounded, or captured, and several Confederate generals became prisoners.

The defeat destroyed a substantial part of Lee's remaining organized force and exposed the Army of Northern Virginia's continuing disintegration.

After observing the defeated columns and captured troops, Lee recognized that his army was approaching collapse.

Grant's Demand for Surrender

On April 7, Grant wrote to Lee stating that recent events demonstrated the hopelessness of further resistance and asking for the surrender of the Army of Northern Virginia.

Lee did not immediately agree that his army's position was hopeless, but he asked Grant what terms would be offered.

The two commanders exchanged messages on April 7, 8, and 9 while their armies continued moving and fighting.

Grant treated the exchange as a military negotiation. He did not claim authority to negotiate a general political peace or determine the constitutional restoration of the seceded states.

Appomattox Station

Lee hoped to reach supplies transported by rail to Appomattox Station and then continue toward Lynchburg or turn south.

On April 8, Union cavalry under Major General Philip Sheridan reached the area first.

Major General George A. Custer's division captured Confederate supply trains, artillery, and prisoners near the station.

Union troops established positions west and south of Appomattox Court House, closing the routes Lee needed for escape.

Lee's Final Council of War

On the evening of April 8, Lee met with senior officers and considered the army's remaining options.

The Confederate command decided to attempt a breakout on the morning of April 9.

Major General John B. Gordon's troops would attack the Union cavalry blocking the road, while other Confederate forces attempted to hold off pursuing Union infantry.

The plan depended on the assumption that only cavalry stood between the Confederates and an open route westward.

Battle of Appomattox Court House

Confederate forces attacked before 8:00 a.m. on April 9 and initially pushed back portions of the Union cavalry line.

The arrival of Union infantry from the Army of the James and other commands revealed that Lee's route was blocked by a larger force.

Gordon reported that his command could not advance unless supported and protected from attack in the rear.

Union forces were also closing from the east. Lee concluded that his army could not escape as an organized field force.

The Decision to Surrender

Lee directed that a truce be requested and sent a message asking Grant for an interview.

Fighting ceased gradually as orders reached the opposing units.

Lee's decision prevented another large assault against a surrounded and exhausted army, though combat and casualties had already occurred that morning.

The decision surrendered military control of the Army of Northern Virginia to United States authority.

The McLean House

Lieutenant Colonel Charles Marshall of Lee's staff was sent to locate a suitable meeting place.

Wilmer McLean agreed to make the parlor of his house available.

Lee arrived at approximately 1:00 p.m. Grant and members of his staff arrived about thirty minutes later.

The surrender terms were written, accepted, and exchanged in the parlor.

Participants in the Meeting

Lee was accompanied principally by Lieutenant Colonel Charles Marshall.

Grant arrived with several staff officers and other United States officers, including representatives of commands participating in the campaign.

Lieutenant Colonel Ely S. Parker, a Seneca officer serving as Grant's military secretary, prepared the formal copy of Grant's terms.

The meeting was a command-level military negotiation rather than a public ceremony involving the full armies.

The Surrender Meeting

Grant and Lee began with brief conversation about their earlier service in the United States Army during the Mexican–American War.

Lee then directed the discussion toward the terms of surrender.

Grant wrote terms based on the substance of the correspondence already exchanged.

Lee reviewed the terms and accepted them in a separate written response.

The meeting ended at approximately 3:00 p.m. The commanders departed after agreeing that subordinate officers would administer the detailed surrender process.

The Surrender Documents

There was no single jointly signed document resembling a modern peace treaty.

The April 9 surrender was completed through Grant's written terms and Lee's separate letter accepting them.

Grant's terms required rolls of officers and men, individual or command paroles, surrender of public military property, and release of the Confederate personnel to return home.

On April 10, commissioners appointed by both commanders completed supplementary administrative agreements governing the movement of troops, surrender of equipment, retention of authorized property, and treatment of wounded personnel.

Principal Terms of Surrender

  • Confederate officers and men would be listed on duplicate rolls.
  • Officers would give individual paroles not to take up arms against the United States until properly exchanged.
  • Company or regimental commanders would sign paroles covering the men under their commands.
  • Public arms, artillery, flags, and military property would be stacked, parked, or delivered to designated United States officers.
  • Confederate officers could retain side arms, private horses, and personal baggage.
  • Officers and soldiers could return home after the required surrender and parole procedures.
  • United States authorities would not disturb the paroled personnel while they observed their paroles and applicable law.

Horses, Mules, and Spring Planting

Lee informed Grant that many enlisted cavalrymen and artillerymen personally owned the horses and mules they were using.

Grant did not rewrite the formal terms but agreed to direct his officers to allow men claiming private ownership to keep the animals.

The provision recognized that many soldiers would need animals for agricultural work after returning home.

The exception applied to privately owned animals, not to all public Confederate livestock or transportation property.

Food and Humanitarian Relief

Lee's army had suffered severe food shortages throughout the retreat.

During the meeting, Grant offered approximately 25,000 rations for the surrendered Confederates.

United States forces distributed food while officers completed surrender and parole procedures.

The food provision reduced immediate suffering and supported the orderly dispersal of the defeated army.

Military Parole

Civil War military parole was a conditional release of a captured or surrendered combatant based on a pledge not to resume fighting until formally exchanged or otherwise released from the obligation.

It was not the same as modern criminal parole.

Grant's terms converted officers and soldiers of the Army of Northern Virginia into paroled prisoners of war who could return home rather than enter prison camps.

Violation of parole could expose a former soldier to renewed military detention or other legal consequences.

Parole Passes

United States Army printers produced large numbers of blank parole forms at the Clover Hill Tavern in Appomattox Court House.

Confederate officers used unit rolls to enter each soldier's name, state, company, regiment, or other identifying information.

The passes demonstrated that the bearer had surrendered under the Appomattox agreement rather than deserted or remained an active combatant.

Parole passes could also support access to food and free transportation on United States-controlled railroads, steamboats, and stage routes.

Parole Was Not a General Pardon

The Appomattox terms protected surrendered soldiers from immediate military detention and promised noninterference while they obeyed their paroles and the law.

The agreement did not automatically grant every Confederate a presidential pardon, restore citizenship rights, return confiscated property, or resolve possible civil liability.

Federal amnesty and pardon policy remained a separate presidential and political matter.

Robert E. Lee later applied for amnesty under President Andrew Johnson's policy, demonstrating that the Appomattox parole and political pardon were legally distinct.

Surrender Commissioners

Grant and Lee each designated officers to administer the surrender.

The commissioners determined procedures for marching Confederate units to designated locations, stacking arms, delivering flags and public property, identifying privately owned animals, handling transportation, and processing wounded soldiers.

This administrative work converted the general terms agreed upon by Grant and Lee into a functioning surrender process.

The formalities continued for several days after the April 9 meeting.

Stacking of Arms

On April 12, approximately 22,000 Confederate infantrymen marched along the Richmond–Lynchburg Stage Road through Appomattox Court House.

Under Major General John B. Gordon, the Confederates surrendered rifles, flags, and other military equipment.

United States troops under Brigadier General Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain supervised the ceremony.

The stacking of arms was the visible public completion of the infantry surrender, but the controlling agreement had already been accepted on April 9.

Military Courtesy and Restraint

Accounts of the April 12 ceremony describe Chamberlain ordering United States soldiers to shoulder arms as the Confederate column passed.

Gordon responded by directing a reciprocal military acknowledgment.

Grant also ordered an end to celebratory artillery firing after news of the surrender spread through Union lines.

These acts represented military restraint and an effort to prevent humiliation from provoking disorder. They did not erase the causes of the war, excuse rebellion, or settle the status and rights of formerly enslaved people.

How Many Soldiers Surrendered?

Historical counts differ because the Army of Northern Virginia included soldiers present for duty, wounded men, detached personnel, cavalry that escaped the immediate encirclement, stragglers, prisoners, and men who had left the columns during the retreat.

The National Park Service maintains an Appomattox parole list containing more than 28,000 names.

Approximately 22,000 Confederate infantrymen participated in the April 12 stacking of arms.

These figures should not be reduced to a single unsupported count for every category of soldier associated with Lee's army.

United States Colored Troops at Appomattox

Seven United States Colored Troops infantry regiments participated in the final operations around Appomattox.

The 8th, 29th, 31st, 41st, 45th, 116th, and 127th United States Colored Infantry together represented more than 5,000 Black soldiers.

These regiments marched rapidly during the pursuit and helped close the routes available to Lee's army.

On April 9, Black and white United States infantry advanced against Confederate forces attempting to break through the encirclement.

Their service placed African American soldiers directly within the military operations that forced the Confederacy's most prominent army to surrender.

Meaning of Black Military Service

The presence of Black soldiers at Appomattox carried direct political and historical significance.

The Confederacy had been founded to protect a slaveholding order and denied that Black people could participate as equal citizens in national government.

At Appomattox, African American soldiers served in the victorious United States Army and helped defeat the military institution defending that order.

Military service strengthened Black claims to freedom, citizenship, equal protection, suffrage, public recognition, and federal responsibility during Reconstruction.

Victory did not automatically secure those rights, but it supplied a powerful factual and moral basis for demanding them.

Appomattox and Emancipation

The Emancipation Proclamation had declared enslaved people free in designated areas of rebellion beginning January 1, 1863.

Its practical enforcement depended upon United States military success.

Lee's surrender removed the principal Confederate field army defending Virginia and made the restoration of Confederate slavery through military victory impossible in the region.

For enslaved people near Appomattox and across Virginia, the arrival and victory of United States forces gave immediate practical force to emancipation.

National constitutional abolition was completed later in 1865 through ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment.

Hannah Reynolds

Hannah Reynolds was an enslaved woman living near the battlefield at Appomattox Court House.

During the fighting on April 9, an artillery shell wounded her.

A United States Army surgeon amputated her injured arm, but she died three days later.

The surrender occurred before her death, and the National Park Service describes her as dying a free woman.

Her experience demonstrates that the event remembered as a command meeting also affected enslaved civilians whose freedom and survival were determined by the outcome of the war.

What Lee Surrendered

Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia as an organized Confederate field army.

The agreement covered officers, enlisted men, units, arms, artillery, flags, and public property subject to the surrender procedures.

Lee did not surrender every Confederate soldier throughout the South and West.

He did not sign a treaty recognizing a separate Confederate nation or transferring political sovereignty to Grant.

Confederate President Jefferson Davis and other civil officials were not parties to the Appomattox agreement.

Why the War Did Not End Everywhere on April 9

Other Confederate armies and military departments remained in the field after the Army of Northern Virginia surrendered.

Communications moved slowly, commanders exercised independent authority, and some forces operated far from Virginia.

Confederate President Jefferson Davis initially hoped resistance could continue.

Appomattox nevertheless demonstrated that the Confederacy's strongest and most celebrated army had been defeated and that further large-scale resistance was unlikely to change the result.

Subsequent Confederate Surrenders

Appomattox established a military model for the surrenders that followed.

  • On April 26, General Joseph E. Johnston surrendered the principal Confederate forces in the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida to Major General William T. Sherman near Durham, North Carolina.
  • On May 4, Lieutenant General Richard Taylor surrendered Confederate forces in Alabama, Mississippi, and East Louisiana to Major General Edward R. S. Canby.
  • On May 26, representatives of General Edmund Kirby Smith agreed to surrender the Trans-Mississippi Department.
  • On June 2, Kirby Smith formally approved the surrender arrangements.
  • Smaller commands, irregular units, naval forces, and isolated personnel ended resistance through additional agreements and individual actions.

Collapse of the Confederate Government

Jefferson Davis and members of the Confederate government fled Richmond before Lee's surrender.

Davis attempted to maintain a functioning government while moving south.

As additional armies surrendered and officials dispersed, the Confederate government lost any remaining capacity to direct national military resistance.

United States forces captured Davis near Irwinville, Georgia, on May 10, 1865.

Lincoln's Assassination

President Abraham Lincoln was shot on April 14, five days after the Appomattox meeting, and died on April 15.

His death transferred the presidency to Andrew Johnson during the transition from war to peace.

The assassination intensified uncertainty about punishment, amnesty, federal occupation, restoration of the Southern states, and protection of freedpeople.

It did not invalidate Grant's military agreement with Lee, but it changed the political leadership responsible for Reconstruction.

The Formal End of the Civil War

No single date ended every legal, military, and political aspect of the Civil War.

Appomattox ended the Army of Northern Virginia's organized resistance and is commonly treated as the war's decisive symbolic conclusion.

Major Confederate surrenders continued through the spring of 1865.

President Andrew Johnson later issued proclamations addressing the end of armed resistance in different regions and formally declared peace throughout the United States on August 20, 1866.

From Surrender to Reconstruction

Military surrender answered who controlled the battlefield, but it did not determine the complete political settlement after the war.

The United States still had to decide how former Confederate states would return to constitutional government, how civil authority would be restored, and how emancipation would be protected.

Formerly enslaved people sought family reunification, wages, land, education, legal recognition, political participation, personal security, and control over their labor.

White Southern resistance, Black Codes, racial violence, labor coercion, and political conflict demonstrated that Confederate military defeat did not automatically produce racial equality.

Appomattox was therefore both an ending and a beginning: the end of Lee's army and the beginning of a prolonged struggle over the meaning of freedom.

Military Significance

The surrender eliminated the Confederate army that had defended Richmond, invaded United States territory, and served as the Confederacy's principal military symbol.

It prevented Lee from joining Johnston and attempting to continue conventional war in another region.

The agreement allowed tens of thousands of soldiers to disperse without a final destructive battle or prolonged imprisonment.

Its terms became the practical standard for later surrenders after broader political provisions proposed elsewhere were rejected.

Political and Constitutional Significance

Union victory preserved the United States and defeated the attempt to establish an independent Confederacy.

The surrender confirmed that secession would not succeed through armed force.

It strengthened the federal government's ability to enforce emancipation and proceed toward constitutional abolition.

It did not itself define national citizenship, equal protection, voting rights, or the constitutional conditions for restoring former Confederate states.

Those questions became central to the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments and to the conflict between presidential and congressional Reconstruction.

Significance in African American History

The Confederacy's military collapse removed the principal armed institution protecting slavery in Virginia.

Black soldiers participated directly in the campaign and final battle, demonstrating that African Americans were agents of Union victory rather than passive recipients of emancipation.

Enslaved people had weakened the Confederacy throughout the war by escaping, withdrawing labor, supplying intelligence, aiding United States forces, protecting families, and joining the military.

Appomattox made freedom more enforceable but left landownership, labor rights, citizenship, voting, education, public accommodations, and protection from racial violence unresolved.

The event belongs within African American history not only because it defeated a slaveholding rebellion, but also because it opened the political struggle to determine whether emancipation would become substantive freedom.

Historical Memory

Appomattox has often been remembered as a scene of national reconciliation between honorable white soldiers.

The military restraint shown during surrender was real, but a reconciliation-only interpretation can obscure slavery as the central cause of secession, the role of Black soldiers, the actions of enslaved people, and the violent resistance to Black freedom after the war.

Lost Cause interpretations frequently presented Confederate defeat as the unavoidable result of superior United States numbers and resources while minimizing Confederate political aims and the importance of emancipation.

A complete interpretation must hold together military surrender, preservation of the Union, destruction of slavery, Black participation, and the unfinished work of Reconstruction.

Wilmer McLean and the “Beginning and End” Story

Wilmer McLean had previously lived near the First Battle of Bull Run in 1861 before moving to Appomattox County.

His connection to both locations produced the popular statement that the war began in his front yard and ended in his front parlor.

The saying communicates an unusual historical coincidence, but the war did not literally begin on McLean's property and did not end everywhere at the moment of the Appomattox meeting.

The phrase should be treated as historical memory rather than a precise description of the war's legal and military chronology.

Common Misconceptions

“Lee Surrendered the Entire Confederacy”

Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia. Other Confederate armies and departments required separate surrenders.

“The Civil War Legally Ended on April 9, 1865”

Appomattox was the decisive symbolic and military turning point, but fighting, surrenders, and federal declarations continued afterward.

“The Surrender Occurred Inside the Courthouse”

The meeting occurred in the McLean House within the village called Appomattox Court House.

“Grant and Lee Signed One Formal Surrender Treaty”

Grant wrote terms, Lee accepted them in a separate letter, and commissioners later completed supplementary administrative arrangements.

“Lee Surrendered Under an Apple Tree”

Lee waited near an apple tree while messages were exchanged, but the surrender meeting took place in the McLean House.

“Every Confederate Soldier Became Fully Pardoned”

The soldiers received military parole and conditional protection. Presidential pardon and restoration of political rights were separate matters.

“Grant Allowed Confederates to Keep All Military Property”

Public arms, artillery, flags, animals, and military property were surrendered. Limited exceptions applied to officers' side arms, personal baggage, private horses, and personally owned animals.

“Only White United States Soldiers Forced the Surrender”

More than 5,000 soldiers in seven United States Colored Troops regiments participated in the final Appomattox operations.

“Appomattox Immediately Created Racial Equality”

The surrender secured military victory and advanced emancipation, but civil rights, political rights, economic independence, and physical protection required further constitutional and political struggle.

“Reconciliation Was the Only Meaning of Appomattox”

Military conciliation was one feature of the surrender. The event also represented the defeat of secession, the collapse of a government created to protect slavery, and the beginning of Reconstruction.

Key Distinctions

Surrender
The yielding of a military force to an opposing authority under stated terms.
Capitulation
A formal agreement setting the conditions under which a military force surrenders.
Parole
A military pledge by a captured or surrendered combatant not to resume fighting until properly released or exchanged.
Pardon
Executive forgiveness of an offense or relief from specified legal consequences; not automatically supplied by the Appomattox parole.
Amnesty
A broader governmental act extending pardon or legal relief to a class of people under stated conditions.
Armistice
An agreement to stop fighting, usually without requiring the complete surrender of one force.
Peace treaty
A political agreement formally ending war between recognized governments; the Appomattox agreement was not such a treaty.
Army of Northern Virginia
The principal Confederate field army operating in Virginia and nearby regions under Robert E. Lee.
Appomattox Campaign
The final pursuit and series of battles from the fall of Richmond and Petersburg to Lee's surrender.

The April 9 meeting in the McLean House at which Grant issued and Lee accepted the surrender terms.
Stacking of arms
The April 12 ceremony and administrative process during which Confederate infantry surrendered weapons and flags.
Emancipation
The process by which enslaved people became free through self-emancipation, military action, federal policy, state action, and constitutional abolition.
Reconstruction
The postwar process of restoring civil government, defining freedom and citizenship, rebuilding institutions, and determining federal protection in the former Confederate states.

Why the Surrender at Appomattox Matters

Appomattox destroyed the Confederacy's most prominent army and made continued large-scale Confederate resistance increasingly impossible.

It preserved the military result of Union victory: the seceded states had not established permanent independence, and the United States remained one nation.

It strengthened the practical enforcement of emancipation by eliminating the principal Confederate army protecting slavery in Virginia.

The participation of United States Colored Troops demonstrated the active role of Black soldiers in defeating the rebellion and securing freedom.

Grant's terms enabled a large defeated army to disarm and disperse without mass imprisonment or another major battle.

The event also exposed the limits of military surrender. It did not determine land distribution, labor relations, citizenship, voting, education, public safety, or racial equality.

Appomattox therefore marks the transition from the Civil War's military struggle to Reconstruction's political struggle over the meaning and enforcement of freedom.

Summary

The Surrender at Appomattox was the April 9, 1865 capitulation of Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant. It followed the fall of Richmond and Petersburg, a rapid Union pursuit, Confederate supply failure, major losses at Sailor's Creek, the capture of supplies at Appomattox Station, and the defeat of Lee's final breakout attempt.

Grant required the Confederate army to surrender public arms and military property while allowing officers and soldiers to return home on parole. Officers retained side arms and private property, enlisted men could keep personally owned horses and mules, and United States forces supplied food to the hungry army. The formalities continued through April 12, when Confederate infantry stacked arms and surrendered flags.

Lee surrendered only the Army of Northern Virginia, not every Confederate force. Other surrenders followed through the spring of 1865, and the United States formally declared peace later. Nevertheless, Appomattox destroyed the Confederacy's principal field army and became the decisive symbol of Union victory.

More than 5,000 soldiers in seven United States Colored Troops regiments participated in the final Appomattox operations. Their presence demonstrated African American agency in defeating the slaveholding rebellion and securing emancipation. The surrender made freedom increasingly enforceable but did not settle citizenship, voting, land, labor, education, or protection from racial violence. Those unresolved questions became central to Reconstruction.
