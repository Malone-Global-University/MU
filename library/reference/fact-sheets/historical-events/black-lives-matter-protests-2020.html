Black Lives Matter Protests

The nationwide and international wave of demonstrations that expanded after the May 25, 2020, murder of George Floyd and demanded accountability for police violence, protection of Black life, institutional reform, and broader racial justice.

Fact Sheet Overview

Formal subject
The Black Lives Matter and racial-justice protest wave that began in late May 2020 following the murder of George Floyd.
Common names
Black Lives Matter protests, George Floyd protests, racial-justice protests, George Floyd uprising, and 2020 anti-racism protests.
Subject type
Decentralized mass-protest wave, civil-rights mobilization, anti-police-brutality campaign, political movement, and international solidarity movement.
Principal period
Late May through the summer of 2020, with demonstrations, vigils, organizing, litigation, and policy campaigns continuing through 2021 and afterward.
Immediate triggering event
The May 25, 2020, murder of George Floyd by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin while Floyd was handcuffed and restrained on the ground.
Broader 2020 context
The killings of Breonna Taylor and Ahmaud Arbery, earlier deaths involving police and racial violence, continuing criminal-justice disparities, and the unequal health and economic effects of the COVID-19 pandemic.
Movement origins
The phrase and movement Black Lives Matter emerged in 2013 after George Zimmerman's acquittal in the killing of Trayvon Martin and expanded during the 2014 Ferguson protests and related campaigns.
Principal geographic scope
All fifty states and the District of Columbia, with solidarity demonstrations in cities and countries throughout the world.
Scale
Researchers documented thousands of demonstrations in thousands of cities and towns. Survey-based estimates suggested that 15 million to 26 million people participated in the United States during the first major weeks, although participation estimates vary substantially by methodology.
Peaceful-demonstration finding
ACLED classified more than 93 percent of demonstrations associated with Black Lives Matter during the principal summer period as involving no violence or destructive activity by demonstrators.
Important statistical caution
The ACLED percentage describes demonstration events, not the percentage of individual protesters, and its broader violence category included clashes, vandalism, looting, and other destructive activity.
Principal demands
Accountability for police killings, limits on force, independent investigation, greater transparency, changes to police budgets and responsibilities, protection of voting and protest rights, investment in Black communities, and elimination of institutional racism.
Organizational structure
Highly decentralized. Demonstrations were organized by local Black Lives Matter groups, civil-rights organizations, families of victims, student groups, labor unions, religious institutions, community coalitions, unaffiliated residents, and newly formed networks.
Major protest methods
Marches, vigils, rallies, die-ins, kneeling, memorials, street murals, occupation of public space, strikes, boycotts, petition campaigns, public testimony, online organizing, and civil disobedience.
Government responses
Police crowd-control operations, emergency curfews, mass arrests, National Guard deployments, federal law-enforcement operations, prosecutions, investigations, legislation, executive orders, and local policy changes.
Property destruction and violence
Some cities experienced looting, arson, vandalism, assaults, shootings, and destruction of businesses, public buildings, police vehicles, and other property. There is no single authoritative national casualty or damage total because sources use different definitions and time periods.
Federal legislative result
The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House of Representatives in 2020 and again in 2021 but did not become federal law.
Federal executive result
Presidents issued policing-related executive orders, including Executive Order 14074 in 2022 concerning federal law-enforcement accountability, use of force, data, accreditation, and grant conditions.
Criminal accountability
Derek Chauvin was convicted of state murder and manslaughter offenses and pleaded guilty to federal civil-rights charges. The other three officers present were also convicted of federal civil-rights violations and faced separate state proceedings.
Historical significance
The protests created one of the broadest mass mobilizations in United States history, transformed public discussion of policing and structural racism, produced institutional reforms and cultural change, and exposed persistent disagreement over public safety, protest, property, race, and government authority.

What Were the Black Lives Matter Protests?

The Black Lives Matter protests were a decentralized wave of demonstrations against police violence and racial injustice that expanded across the United States after George Floyd was murdered in Minneapolis on May 25, 2020.

Bystander video showed police officer Derek Chauvin maintaining pressure upon Floyd's neck, back, and shoulder area for more than nine minutes while Floyd was handcuffed, restrained, and increasingly unresponsive.

The recording circulated rapidly through news media and social-media platforms. Within days, demonstrations occurred in large cities, suburbs, small towns, rural communities, and state capitals.

Protesters demanded accountability for Floyd's death and connected it to a longer history of police killings, unequal law enforcement, racial profiling, incarceration, housing inequality, employment discrimination, and institutional racism.

The protest wave included peaceful demonstrations and organized civil disobedience as well as separate or overlapping incidents of looting, arson, assault, vandalism, and armed conflict.

Black Lives Matter, George Floyd Protests, and Racial-Justice Protests

The term Black Lives Matter protests connects the demonstrations to the wider movement and slogan opposing violence and discrimination against Black people.

The term George Floyd protests emphasizes the killing that triggered the 2020 mobilization.

The term racial-justice protests includes demonstrations that addressed policing together with housing, employment, education, healthcare, voting, monuments, and other institutions.

The term George Floyd uprising emphasizes the movement's scale, disruption, and resistance to institutional authority.

These labels overlap but are not identical. Not every participant belonged to a Black Lives Matter organization, and not every protest used the same name or adopted the same demands.

Movement, Slogan, Network, and Organizations

Black Lives Matter may refer to a moral and political statement, a decentralized social movement, local organizations, or specifically named nonprofit entities.

The broader movement included people and organizations that were not members of the Black Lives Matter Global Network Foundation.

Local chapters and independent groups exercised substantial autonomy.

Families of people killed by police, established civil-rights organizations, labor unions, student groups, religious institutions, mutual-aid networks, and unaffiliated protesters organized many events.

No central organization possessed operational control over all demonstrations held under Black Lives Matter language.

Origins of Black Lives Matter

Seventeen-year-old Trayvon Martin was killed in Sanford, Florida, on February 26, 2012.

On July 13, 2013, a jury acquitted George Zimmerman of second-degree murder and manslaughter charges arising from Martin's death.

Alicia Garza wrote a public message affirming the value of Black life. Patrisse Cullors used the hashtag #BlackLivesMatter, and Opal Tometi helped develop the digital and organizational infrastructure through which the phrase spread.

The phrase became both a declaration and an organizing framework against racial violence, criminal-justice inequality, and the devaluation of Black people.

Ferguson and the Movement's National Expansion

On August 9, 2014, police officer Darren Wilson shot and killed eighteen-year-old Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri.

Protests developed over Brown's death, the treatment of his body, police use of force, municipal-court practices, and the relationship between a predominantly Black community and its government.

Militarized police equipment, tear gas, arrests of journalists, and confrontations with demonstrators attracted national attention.

The Ferguson protests expanded the use of Black Lives Matter as a national movement identity.

Demonstrations also developed after the death of Eric Garner in New York and the grand jury's decision not to indict the officer who placed him in a prohibited chokehold.

Movement Development, 2015–2019

Black Lives Matter activism continued through protests, elections, policy campaigns, community organizing, art, online communication, and support for families affected by police violence.

Demonstrations followed the deaths of Freddie Gray, Sandra Bland, Alton Sterling, Philando Castile, and other Black people in police encounters or custody.

Activists challenged police use of force, cash bail, jail conditions, prosecutorial decisions, surveillance, school policing, and municipal funding priorities.

Movement organizations also addressed gender, sexuality, disability, immigration, poverty, and the particular vulnerability of Black women and LGBTQ+ people.

By 2020, the movement possessed established slogans, networks, legal relationships, media experience, and organizing practices that could be activated rapidly.

The COVID-19 Context

The 2020 protests occurred during the first major phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Government restrictions had closed schools, workplaces, businesses, churches, and public gathering places.

Black communities experienced disproportionate infection, hospitalization, death, unemployment, housing insecurity, and frontline occupational exposure.

The pandemic intensified discussion of structural inequality while also creating health risks for mass gatherings.

Many protest organizers distributed masks and sanitizer, encouraged distancing where possible, and used outdoor spaces. Public-health officials debated how to balance infection control with the health consequences of racism and police violence.

Ahmaud Arbery

On February 23, 2020, Ahmaud Arbery was chased and killed while running in a Georgia neighborhood.

The men involved were not police officers, but the delayed arrests and involvement of a former law-enforcement officer raised questions concerning racial profiling and unequal criminal accountability.

Video of the killing became public in May and produced demonstrations shortly before Floyd's death.

Arbery's name became part of the wider demand that racial violence against Black people receive serious investigation and prosecution.

Breonna Taylor

On March 13, 2020, Louisville police officers shot and killed Breonna Taylor during a nighttime raid at her apartment.

Taylor was a twenty-six-year-old emergency medical worker.

Her death raised questions concerning search warrants, no-knock entries, police intelligence, officer identification, gunfire into occupied homes, medical response, and accountability.

The phrase “Say Her Name” emphasized the risk that police violence against Black women would receive less public attention.

Protests for Taylor intensified alongside the George Floyd demonstrations.

The Murder of George Floyd

On May 25, 2020, Minneapolis police officers responded to a report that George Floyd had used a counterfeit twenty-dollar bill.

Officers removed Floyd from a vehicle, handcuffed him, and attempted to place him in a police car.

Derek Chauvin restrained Floyd in a prone position with pressure across his neck and upper body while officers J. Alexander Kueng and Thomas Lane assisted and Tou Thao controlled bystanders.

Floyd repeatedly stated that he could not breathe. Bystanders urged the officers to stop and provide medical assistance.

Chauvin continued the restraint after Floyd stopped responding. Floyd died as a result of the police encounter.

Bystander Video and Digital Evidence

Seventeen-year-old Darnella Frazier recorded the restraint on her telephone.

Her video provided a continuous public record that contradicted the initial police statement describing Floyd's death as occurring after a medical incident.

Additional surveillance, body-camera, and bystander recordings later documented other parts of the encounter.

The rapid circulation of the video demonstrated how mobile cameras and social media could challenge official narratives and mobilize national protest.

Video evidence did not remove the need for investigation, trial, expert testimony, and public interpretation, but it made the event directly visible to millions of people.

Minneapolis and the First Days

Protesters gathered near the location of Floyd's death and outside Minneapolis police facilities on May 26.

Demonstrations included memorials, marches, speeches, and demands for the officers' arrest.

Confrontations developed between demonstrators and police, who used chemical agents and less-lethal weapons.

Looting, property destruction, and fires spread through commercial areas.

On May 28, protesters entered the Minneapolis Police Department's Third Precinct building after officers abandoned it, and the building was set on fire.

Minnesota activated the National Guard as the emergency expanded.

National Spread

Demonstrations expanded rapidly beyond Minneapolis.

Events occurred in every state, the District of Columbia, tribal and territorial communities, suburbs, university towns, and communities with relatively small Black populations.

The movement's geographic reach distinguished it from protest waves concentrated mainly in major cities.

Some locations held one march or vigil. Others experienced months of recurring demonstrations, encampments, occupations, public hearings, and confrontations with police.

Local events often combined national demands with the names and cases of people killed or injured by law enforcement in that community.

Scale and Participation

Protest scale is difficult to measure because no national authority registered every event or participant.

The Crowd Counting Consortium documented nearly 9,700 George Floyd-related protests in more than 3,050 cities and towns during approximately ten weeks from late May through July 2020.

Survey analysis cited by Harvard researchers estimated that between 15 million and 26 million people in the United States participated during the first major period.

Event-based counts produce lower totals because many demonstrations had no reliable crowd estimate and individuals may have attended more than one event.

The available evidence supports describing the 2020 mobilization as one of the largest and geographically broadest protest movements in United States history.

International Protests

Demonstrations occurred in cities across Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Latin America, and the Caribbean.

International protesters expressed solidarity with George Floyd and Black Americans while connecting the movement to local histories of policing, colonialism, slavery, migration, discrimination, and treatment of Indigenous and African-descended populations.

Some demonstrations targeted monuments, national symbols, or institutions associated with colonial rule and the slave trade.

The international response showed that the phrase Black Lives Matter had become a global framework rather than solely an American organizational label.

Who Participated?

Black organizers, families affected by police violence, students, workers, clergy members, attorneys, medical personnel, artists, athletes, labor unions, neighborhood associations, and civil-rights organizations participated.

Large numbers of white, Latino, Asian American, Native American, immigrant, LGBTQ+, disabled, and multiracial participants joined demonstrations.

Many people were attending their first political protest.

Participation did not establish agreement with every slogan, organization, or policy proposal associated with Black Lives Matter.

The protest coalition was broad precisely because people entered it with different experiences, priorities, and theories of change.

Black Leadership and Coalition Participation

The protests concerned the protection of Black life and emerged from Black organizing traditions.

Black women, queer activists, young organizers, families of victims, clergy members, attorneys, and neighborhood groups provided substantial leadership.

Interracial participation increased the movement's scale but could also produce disputes over visibility, leadership, risk, and the meaning of solidarity.

Effective coalition participation required non-Black supporters to oppose racial injustice without displacing Black organizers or treating Black communities as politically uniform.

Major Demands

There was no single national list accepted by every organizer or participant.

Common demands included:

  • Arrest and prosecution of officers responsible for unlawful killings or serious misconduct.
  • Independent investigation of police use of force and deaths in custody.
  • Restrictions or bans on chokeholds, neck restraints, no-knock warrants, and unnecessary military equipment.
  • Clear duties to intervene and provide medical assistance.
  • Public reporting of stops, searches, force, complaints, discipline, and officer misconduct.
  • Stronger civilian review, inspector-general authority, and community oversight.
  • Changes to qualified immunity and other legal barriers to civil accountability.
  • Reduced police involvement in mental-health crises, homelessness, schools, traffic enforcement, and low-level disputes.
  • Reallocation of public money toward housing, healthcare, education, violence prevention, transportation, employment, and community services.
  • Protection of voting rights, protest rights, and Black political participation.

“Defund the Police”

“Defund the police” became one of the most prominent and disputed slogans of the protest wave.

For some advocates, it meant reducing police budgets and transferring specific responsibilities and resources to housing, mental-health, healthcare, education, transportation, youth services, and community-based violence prevention.

For others, it formed part of a longer-term strategy to replace conventional policing and incarceration with alternative institutions.

Some protesters supported reform without supporting major budget reductions or abolition.

Opponents interpreted the slogan as a proposal to eliminate public safety or leave communities without protection.

The phrase therefore identified a family of positions rather than one uniform policy.

Police Reform and Police Abolition

Reform advocates sought changes within existing police institutions, including training, discipline, body cameras, force restrictions, accreditation, supervision, prosecution, and civilian oversight.

Abolitionist organizers argued that repeated reform had not prevented violence and that public safety should be reorganized around housing, care, conflict resolution, community resources, and institutions not centered upon armed police or imprisonment.

Intermediate proposals sought to remove particular functions from police, reduce armed responses, create unarmed crisis teams, or limit police contact.

These positions overlapped in some campaigns but differed substantially in long-term objective.

Protest Methods

Organizers used marches, rallies, candlelight vigils, memorials, die-ins, kneeling, moments of silence, street murals, art installations, strikes, petitions, public testimony, boycotts, and online campaigns.

Demonstrators occupied streets, bridges, parks, government plazas, police precinct areas, and commercial districts.

Some groups organized bail funds, legal-observer teams, medical support, food distribution, protective equipment, transportation, and communication networks.

Social media allowed local organizers to announce events rapidly but also allowed false information, rumors, impersonation, and unverified claims to spread.

The Predominantly Peaceful Character of the Protest Wave

Most Black Lives Matter demonstrations did not involve violence or destructive activity by protesters.

ACLED recorded more than 7,750 BLM-associated demonstrations in more than 2,440 locations between May 26 and August 22, 2020.

Its analysis found that more than 93 percent involved no violence or destructive activity by demonstrators.

The statistic should not be used to deny the serious destruction that occurred in particular cities.

It establishes that those incidents were not representative of the majority of demonstration events.

Civil Unrest, Looting, and Arson

Minneapolis, Saint Paul, Kenosha, Portland, Seattle, Los Angeles, New York, Chicago, Philadelphia, Washington, and other cities experienced significant civil disorder at different times.

Businesses were looted, police vehicles and stations were damaged, buildings were burned, and public monuments and property were defaced or removed.

Some destructive acts occurred near demonstrations, while other looting and criminal activity took place after organized protests had ended or in different locations.

Participants included people expressing political anger, opportunistic thieves, organized groups, individuals seeking confrontation, and people whose motives remain unknown.

It is inaccurate to attribute every destructive act to the Black Lives Matter movement or to treat all property destruction as unrelated to the protest environment.

Violence, Injuries, and Deaths

People were killed or injured in incidents occurring during the wider period of protest and unrest.

Victims included protesters, bystanders, motorists, business owners, police officers, federal personnel, armed participants, and people involved in unrelated or opportunistic criminal activity.

Violence included shootings, vehicle attacks, assaults, arson, police use of force, and clashes involving protesters, counterprotesters, or other groups.

There is no single authoritative national casualty count because databases use different dates, locations, and standards for determining whether a death was caused by a protest, riot, police action, criminal act, or unrelated event.

Responsible analysis should identify individual incidents rather than assigning one undifferentiated total to the movement.

Property and Economic Loss

Civil disorder caused major insured and uninsured losses in multiple cities.

Small businesses, national retailers, restaurants, housing, pharmacies, grocery stores, government buildings, vehicles, and community institutions were damaged.

Property destruction eliminated jobs and services in some neighborhoods already affected by pandemic closures and economic hardship.

National estimates vary because some sources combine many cities, dates, types of damage, and insurance claims while excluding uninsured losses.

Property loss should be neither minimized nor used to treat property as equivalent to human life.

Police Response to Demonstrations

Police responses varied by city and event.

Some departments facilitated marches, redirected traffic, communicated with organizers, or joined symbolic gestures.

Other agencies used batons, chemical irritants, kinetic-impact projectiles, sound devices, mounted units, mass arrest, kettling, curfews, and dispersal orders.

Protesters, journalists, medics, legal observers, bystanders, and police officers reported injuries.

Lawsuits and official reviews questioned whether force was necessary, proportionate, properly authorized, and consistent with First Amendment protections.

Arrests and Curfews

Thousands of people were arrested nationwide during the first weeks of protest and civil unrest.

Charges included curfew violations, failure to disperse, unlawful assembly, obstruction, burglary, theft, arson, assault, and weapons offenses.

Arrest totals vary because no unified official national database recorded every jurisdiction and because some published counts covered only the earliest days.

Curfews gave authorities broad power to arrest people who were not committing violence or property crimes.

Many low-level protest charges were later dismissed, but arrest itself imposed detention, legal costs, employment consequences, and potential exposure to COVID-19.

National Guard Deployment

Governors deployed National Guard personnel to support police, protect property, control traffic, enforce curfews, and respond to fires and civil disorder.

By June 3, the Department of Defense reported that governors in twenty-eight states had activated Guard forces in response to the protests and unrest.

The District of Columbia National Guard operated under a different command structure because the district is not a state.

Guard deployments sometimes provided logistical and security assistance without direct enforcement contact.

Their presence also intensified debate over the militarization of domestic public-order operations.

Washington, D.C.

Washington became one of the principal national protest centers.

Demonstrators gathered near the White House, United States Capitol, Lincoln Memorial, Department of Justice, and other federal sites.

The federal government deployed personnel from numerous departments and agencies.

The Government Accountability Office later found that at least twelve federal agencies deployed up to approximately 9,300 personnel per day in Washington between May 26 and June 15.

Multiple agencies used less-lethal force, but federal reporting and after-action review were inconsistent.

Lafayette Square

On June 1, federal and local law-enforcement personnel dispersed protesters from Lafayette Square and surrounding streets near the White House.

Officers used physical force, chemical agents, smoke, mounted units, and other crowd-control methods.

President Donald Trump later walked through the cleared area to St. John's Episcopal Church for a photographed appearance.

The Interior Department inspector general found that the United States Park Police had decided to clear the area for installation of security fencing before officials knew of the president's planned walk.

Later oversight reports nevertheless identified serious coordination, planning, communication, and use-of-force questions involving the multiple agencies operating in Washington.

Black Lives Matter Plaza

On June 5, the District of Columbia government painted the words “BLACK LIVES MATTER” in large yellow letters on Sixteenth Street near the White House.

Mayor Muriel Bowser designated the area Black Lives Matter Plaza.

The mural became a prominent symbol and gathering point.

Some activists welcomed the recognition, while others criticized it as symbolic action insufficiently connected to police budgets, law, or institutional change.

Portland and Federal Courthouse Operations

Portland, Oregon, experienced recurring demonstrations for months.

Some protesters targeted the federal courthouse and other government property, while federal officers used barriers, chemical agents, impact munitions, arrests, and street operations.

Federal deployment produced disputes concerning agency identification, detention procedures, jurisdiction, use of force, and the effect of federal intervention upon crowd size and conflict.

GAO found that at least five federal agencies deployed up to approximately 325 personnel per day during the examined period and reported more than 700 less-lethal force incidents.

The report also found inconsistent policies, reporting, and review across agencies.

Journalists, Medics, and Legal Observers

Journalists documented police operations, protester conduct, fires, arrests, speeches, and community response.

Reporters were arrested, struck, pepper-sprayed, or hit with projectiles in several cities despite identifying themselves as press.

Volunteer medics treated injuries, while legal observers recorded arrests and force.

Their presence helped create evidence for litigation and historical archives.

Attacks upon identifiable observers raised concerns about freedom of the press, accountability, and the ability to document government conduct.

Counterprotests and Armed Participation

Some demonstrations attracted armed counterprotesters, militia groups, white nationalists, and people claiming to protect businesses or monuments.

Armed individuals also appeared in support of racial-justice protests.

Vehicles were driven into crowds in multiple incidents, sometimes intentionally and sometimes under disputed circumstances.

Armed participation increased the danger of misidentification, escalation, and lethal conflict.

Authorities and media sometimes attributed violence before reliable information concerning the responsible person or organization was available.

Surveillance and Facial Recognition

Federal, state, and local agencies used social-media monitoring, aerial observation, video, license-plate data, and other investigative tools during and after the protests.

The Government Accountability Office reported that six federal agencies used facial-recognition searches on images associated with unrest, riots, or protests following Floyd's death.

Agencies described the searches as directed toward suspected criminal activity.

Civil-liberties advocates warned that powerful surveillance tools could identify peaceful protesters, discourage protected assembly, and produce racial or technical errors.

The dispute concerned not only whether a tool could identify suspects but also what limits, warrants, auditing, and public reporting should govern its use.

Public Opinion and Political Polarization

Public support for Black Lives Matter and concern about racial discrimination increased sharply during the first weeks after Floyd's death.

Businesses, universities, professional associations, sports organizations, and government agencies issued statements and announced diversity or racial-equity initiatives.

Support later declined from its initial peak, and public attitudes remained divided by race, political party, ideology, geography, and interpretation of the unrest.

Opponents emphasized crime, property destruction, police safety, and criticism of movement organizations.

Supporters emphasized police violence, unequal justice, structural racism, and the overwhelmingly peaceful character of most demonstrations.

Monuments, Names, and Public Memory

Protesters challenged Confederate monuments, memorials to enslavers, colonial symbols, and names associated with racial oppression.

Governments, universities, military institutions, businesses, and private organizations removed or renamed some monuments, buildings, products, teams, and programs.

Some monuments were removed through government procedures, while others were pulled down or damaged by protesters.

Supporters described removal as correction of public honor and historical interpretation.

Opponents described it as destruction, erasure, or politically driven revision.

The controversy demonstrated that public memory is connected to institutional authority and decisions concerning whom government chooses to honor.

State and Local Policy Responses

State legislatures, city councils, mayors, police departments, prosecutors, and ballot initiatives adopted or considered numerous measures.

Changes included restrictions on chokeholds and no-knock warrants, duties to intervene, body-camera rules, public misconduct databases, revised force policies, civilian oversight, decertification procedures, and alternative crisis-response programs.

Some jurisdictions reduced or reallocated police budgets, while others later restored funding or increased it.

Policy language did not always produce consistent practice, and unions, courts, state preemption laws, administrative resistance, staffing, and funding affected implementation.

The reform record therefore varied considerably among jurisdictions.

George Floyd Justice in Policing Act

Members of Congress introduced the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act in 2020.

The proposal addressed federal civil-rights prosecution, qualified immunity, misconduct databases, use-of-force rules, no-knock warrants, chokeholds, racial profiling, training, data collection, and federal grants.

The House of Representatives passed a version in June 2020.

The House passed a revised version, H.R. 1280, on March 3, 2021.

The Senate did not enact the House bill, and bipartisan negotiations did not produce a final statute.

The result demonstrated the difficulty of creating one national standard for thousands of state and local law-enforcement agencies.

Federal Executive Action

President Trump issued an executive order in June 2020 addressing police credentialing, information sharing, mental-health and homelessness responses, and restrictions concerning chokeholds except where deadly force was authorized.

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. issued Executive Order 14074 on May 25, 2022.

The 2022 order established requirements and policies for federal law enforcement and directed federal agencies to use grants, accreditation, data collection, misconduct records, force standards, body cameras, and other tools to influence policing.

Executive orders can govern federal agencies and specified federal programs but cannot independently rewrite every state and local law.

Criminal Cases Against the Minneapolis Officers

Minnesota prosecuted Derek Chauvin for George Floyd's death.

On April 20, 2021, a jury convicted Chauvin of second-degree unintentional murder, third-degree murder, and second-degree manslaughter.

Chauvin also pleaded guilty to a federal civil-rights offense for willfully depriving Floyd of his constitutional rights.

A federal jury convicted J. Alexander Kueng, Thomas Lane, and Tou Thao of civil-rights violations arising from failure to intervene or provide medical assistance.

The three officers also faced separate state charges resolved through trial or guilty pleas.

The prosecutions provided individual accountability but did not by themselves resolve the institutional issues raised by the protests.

Federal Investigation of Minneapolis Policing

The United States Department of Justice opened a pattern-or-practice investigation of the City of Minneapolis and its police department.

In 2023, the department reported reasonable cause to believe that the city and police department engaged in patterns or practices of excessive force and racial discrimination and violated rights involving speech and people with behavioral-health disabilities.

The investigation distinguished responsibility for Floyd's murder from broader institutional practices.

Pattern-or-practice enforcement seeks structural changes through agreements, court orders, monitoring, policy revision, training, supervision, data, and accountability systems.

Labor, Strikes, and Workplace Action

Labor unions participated in marches and issued statements concerning racial justice.

Workers organized walkouts, stoppages, and the July 20, 2020, Strike for Black Lives.

Athletes and sports teams suspended games or declined to play after police shot Jacob Blake in Kenosha, Wisconsin.

Workplace actions connected police violence to wages, healthcare, workplace discrimination, union representation, and investment in Black communities.

Police unions frequently opposed reforms they believed weakened officer protections, due process, staffing, or public safety.

Institutional and Corporate Responses

Corporations, universities, foundations, museums, media companies, professional associations, and cultural institutions announced racial-equity initiatives.

Responses included donations, diversity programs, revised hiring practices, curriculum reviews, collection projects, public art, and changes to organizational names or symbols.

Some commitments produced continuing programs.

Others were criticized as temporary branding, symbolic statements, or promises without measurable accountability.

The distinction between public declaration and institutional implementation became a major part of the movement's legacy.

Opposition and Criticism

Critics objected to protest disruption, property destruction, attacks upon police, occupation of streets, removal of monuments, and demands to reduce police budgets.

“Blue Lives Matter,” “All Lives Matter,” and law-and-order campaigns challenged Black Lives Matter language and priorities.

Some opponents argued that the movement generalized unfairly about police or minimized violent crime within Black communities.

Movement supporters responded that affirming Black lives did not deny the value of other lives and that unequal state violence required specific attention.

Political debate often merged the conduct of peaceful protesters, movement organizations, rioters, vandals, and unrelated criminal actors into one category.

Internal Debates

Participants disagreed concerning reform, abolition, electoral politics, nonprofit leadership, property destruction, armed self-defense, coalition participation, corporate donations, and relations with the Democratic Party.

Some organizers prioritized immediate prosecution and legislation.

Others emphasized community organization, mutual aid, local budgets, political education, labor action, or long-term transformation of policing and prisons.

Decentralization allowed rapid expansion and local adaptation but made unified negotiation, accountability, and public representation more difficult.

These disagreements demonstrate that Black Lives Matter was a movement field rather than one centralized command structure.

Major Achievements

The protests placed police violence and structural racism at the center of national and international public debate.

They produced unprecedented documentation, historical collecting, public testimony, litigation, local legislation, policy review, and community organizing.

The officers involved in Floyd's death faced state and federal criminal accountability.

Governments revised force rules, reporting systems, oversight structures, and crisis-response programs in numerous jurisdictions.

The protests broadened political participation among young people and communities with little previous involvement in demonstrations.

They also expanded public recognition of earlier victims whose cases had received limited attention.

Limits and Unresolved Issues

Congress did not enact the principal national policing bill proposed in George Floyd's name.

State and local reforms varied, and some were delayed, weakened, reversed, or difficult to enforce.

Police killings, racial disparities, and community distrust continued.

Property destruction and violence weakened public support in some constituencies and imposed costs upon affected neighborhoods.

Institutional statements did not necessarily change budgets, leadership, ownership, or measurable outcomes.

Disputes involving nonprofit governance and finances damaged trust in some organizations but did not define every local group or the broader movement.

Historical Documentation

Museums, libraries, universities, journalists, photographers, and community archives collected protest signs, murals, oral histories, digital records, photographs, video, protective equipment, and organizational documents.

The Library of Congress created the Protests Against Racism Web Archive.

Smithsonian museums collected objects and testimony from Lafayette Square, Washington, Los Angeles, and other locations.

This collecting activity recognized the protests as a major historical event while participants and policy disputes were still active.

Digital preservation remains important because websites and social-media records can be deleted, altered, or separated from their original context.

Key Distinctions

Black Lives Matter movement
The broad decentralized movement opposing violence and institutional discrimination against Black people.
Black Lives Matter Global Network Foundation
A specifically constituted nonprofit organization associated with the movement but not identical to every chapter, protest, supporter, or use of the phrase.
George Floyd protests
The mass demonstrations beginning after Floyd's murder, many but not all of which used Black Lives Matter language.
Peaceful protest
Protected assembly, speech, petition, marching, vigil, or civil disobedience that does not involve violence or destructive activity.
Civil disobedience
Deliberate nonviolent violation of a law or order to expose injustice or compel institutional response.
Riot or civil disorder
Collective public violence, arson, looting, assault, or destructive activity that may occur near a protest but is not synonymous with protest itself.
Police reform
Changes intended to improve existing law-enforcement institutions through policy, training, oversight, discipline, prosecution, data, or organizational design.
Police abolition
A long-term project to replace policing and incarceration with other systems of safety, care, prevention, and conflict response.
Defunding
Reducing or reallocating police funding or responsibilities; the scale and intended destination of the resources vary among proposals.
Qualified immunity
A judicial doctrine affecting when government officials may be held personally liable for damages in federal civil-rights lawsuits.
National Guard
State military forces normally controlled by governors unless placed under federal authority.
Federal law-enforcement deployment
Use of personnel from federal agencies to protect federal property, investigate federal crimes, support public order, or perform other authorized functions.

Interpretive and Accuracy Cautions

Black Lives Matter is a broad movement and slogan, not one organization directing every protest.

George Floyd's murder was the immediate trigger of the 2020 wave, but the movement developed from years of organizing and many earlier deaths and cases.

The great majority of demonstration events were peaceful. This does not erase serious violence, arson, looting, or property loss in particular locations.

Destructive acts occurring near a protest should not automatically be attributed to an identified Black Lives Matter organization without evidence.

The terms protester, rioter, looter, counterprotester, bystander, and criminal actor are not interchangeable.

“Defund the police” did not possess one universally accepted meaning.

Protest participation estimates vary according to surveys, event databases, crowd reports, dates, and whether repeat participation is counted.

No single authoritative national count exists for arrests, deaths, injuries, or property damage associated with the entire protest period.

The federal criminal convictions of the Minneapolis officers addressed individual violations. They did not establish that every movement demand had been satisfied.

Criticism of financial or governance practices within a particular nonprofit entity should not be generalized automatically to every local organizer, demonstration, or supporter using Black Lives Matter language.

Why the Black Lives Matter Protests Matter

The 2020 protests constituted one of the largest and most geographically widespread mobilizations in United States history.

They demonstrated how bystander video, social media, local organizing, and established movement networks could transform one police killing into a national and international political crisis.

The demonstrations placed police accountability, racial disparities, qualified immunity, public budgets, incarceration, and alternative systems of safety before institutions that had often treated them as specialized or local issues.

The movement brought together millions of people across racial, geographic, generational, religious, labor, and political boundaries.

The accompanying unrest demonstrated the severe social and economic consequences that follow when organized protest, opportunistic criminal activity, government force, and accumulated grievance converge.

Federal and local responses exposed unresolved questions concerning First Amendment rights, curfews, surveillance, less-lethal weapons, military deployment, press freedom, and intergovernmental command.

The protests produced criminal accountability in Floyd's case and policy change in numerous jurisdictions, but the principal federal legislation did not become law and implementation remained uneven.

Museums and archives collected materials immediately, recognizing that the movement had become a defining historical event before its political consequences were settled.

The central institutional lesson is that public legitimacy depends upon more than formal rules. It requires credible accountability, transparent enforcement, equal protection, functioning community institutions, and the demonstrated capacity to correct abuse.

Summary

The Black Lives Matter protests were a decentralized national and international mobilization that expanded after Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd on May 25, 2020. The protests connected Floyd's death to Breonna Taylor, Ahmaud Arbery, Trayvon Martin, Michael Brown, and a longer history of police violence and institutional discrimination.

Millions of people participated in thousands of demonstrations. Most events were peaceful, although some cities experienced serious looting, arson, assault, shootings, police violence, property loss, curfews, mass arrests, National Guard deployment, and federal intervention.

The movement produced criminal accountability in Floyd's case, state and local policy changes, executive action, historical documentation, and a lasting transformation of public debate. Its limitations included uneven implementation, continued police violence, political polarization, destructive unrest, organizational controversy, and the failure of the principal federal policing bill to become law.
