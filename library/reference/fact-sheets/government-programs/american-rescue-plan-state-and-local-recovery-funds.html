American Rescue Plan State and Local Recovery Funds

The $350 billion federal fiscal-recovery program that supplied flexible assistance to states, territories, Tribal governments, counties, metropolitan cities, and smaller local governments during and after the COVID‑19 emergency.

Terminology Note

The official program name is the Coronavirus State and Local Fiscal Recovery Funds, commonly abbreviated SLFRF.

The program consists of two statutory funds: the Coronavirus State Fiscal Recovery Fund under section 602 of the Social Security Act and the Coronavirus Local Fiscal Recovery Fund under section 603.

“American Rescue Plan State and Local Recovery Funds” is a descriptive name. It should not be confused with the Coronavirus Relief Fund created by the CARES Act, the Capital Projects Fund, the Local Assistance and Tribal Consistency Fund, Emergency Rental Assistance, the Homeowner Assistance Fund, or general state and local aid.

SLFRF recipients are governments. Households, businesses, nonprofits, workers, and other organisations can receive assistance as beneficiaries, contractors, or subrecipients but did not receive direct statutory formula allocations from Treasury.

The program’s obligation deadline passed on December 31, 2024. Most valid obligations must be expended by December 31, 2026. Surface Transportation and Title I projects use a September 30, 2026 expenditure deadline.

Overview

SLFRF was the principal flexible state, local, territorial, and Tribal fiscal-assistance program in the American Rescue Plan.

Congress designed it to prevent public-service cuts, support public-health response, address economic harm, compensate essential workers, and finance infrastructure.

Treasury distributed formula awards rather than requiring governments to compete nationally.

Recipients selected projects within federal rules and remained responsible for eligibility, procurement, subrecipients, reporting, civil rights, audit, and repayment of misspent funds.

The 2022 final rule greatly expanded flexibility, especially through a $10 million standard revenue-loss allowance and broader enumerated public-health and economic-recovery uses.

Congress expanded the program again in December 2022 to permit natural-disaster relief, transportation, and community-development projects.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because it is a federal formula-grant system governed by statute, Treasury regulations, financial-assistance agreements, the Uniform Guidance, reporting, audits, compliance review, and recoupment.

The American Rescue Plan Act and Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 belong under Laws and Legislation.

Treasury, state governments, Tribal governments, counties, municipalities, and subrecipient organisations belong under Institutions.

Revenue loss, obligation, expenditure, beneficiary, subrecipient, premium pay, and federal award closeout are related concepts.

Pandemic Fiscal Context

COVID‑19 generated public-health costs, business closures, unemployment, service disruption, and uncertainty about state and local revenue.

Governments faced demands for testing, vaccination, emergency response, housing assistance, small-business aid, food support, remote services, ventilation, broadband, and public-sector staffing.

State and local governments cut more than 1.5 million jobs during the early pandemic period.

Congress sought to avoid the prolonged state and local austerity that followed the Great Recession.

CARES Act Coronavirus Relief Fund

The CARES Act created the $150 billion Coronavirus Relief Fund in March 2020.

CRF primarily covered necessary pandemic-related expenditures not included in government budgets and used a shorter spending period.

SLFRF was broader, longer, and expressly included revenue replacement, economic recovery, premium pay, and infrastructure.

American Rescue Plan Act

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. signed the American Rescue Plan Act on March 11, 2021.

Section 9901 added sections 602 and 603 to the Social Security Act and appropriated $350 billion.

The program was designed as direct federal aid to governmental recipients rather than pass-through aid limited to states.

Coronavirus State Fiscal Recovery Fund

Section 602 financed states, the District of Columbia, territories, and Tribal governments.

The state and District allocation totalled approximately $195.3 billion.

Territories received $4.5 billion, and Tribal governments received $20 billion.

Coronavirus Local Fiscal Recovery Fund

Section 603 financed counties, metropolitan cities, and non-entitlement units of local government.

Counties received approximately $65.1 billion, metropolitan cities $45.6 billion, and smaller local governments $19.53 billion.

Local governments were direct federal recipients even when a state distributed funds to non-entitlement units.

State Allocation Method

The statute combined a minimum allocation with a distribution based largely on each state’s share of national unemployment.

Each state and the District of Columbia received at least $500 million.

The unemployment-based formula directed greater funds to states experiencing more severe labour-market disruption.

Territories

Puerto Rico, Guam, United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and Northern Mariana Islands shared the $4.5 billion territorial allocation.

Treasury distributed the funds primarily according to population.

Territories were subject to program reporting and the statutory tax-offset restriction.

Tribal Governments

Congress reserved $20 billion for eligible Tribal governments.

Treasury used a multi-part formula including an equal allocation and components based on Tribal enrollment, employment, and government expenditures.

The award represented the largest single federal fiscal infusion into Indian Country at the time.

Tribal governments used funds for housing, water, broadband, health, public employment, economic development, food security, and essential-worker pay.

Counties

Counties received approximately $65.1 billion, generally based on population.

The program represented an unusually broad direct federal payment to county governments.

Special statutory rules applied where a county was not a unit of general local government.

Metropolitan Cities

Metropolitan cities received approximately $45.57 billion.

Treasury relied on the Community Development Block Grant formula structure used by the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

Eligible cities received funds directly from Treasury.

Non-Entitlement Units of Local Government

Non-entitlement units, or NEUs, are generally municipalities with populations below 50,000 that do not receive direct CDBG entitlement grants.

Congress allocated $19.53 billion for NEUs, distributed through states and territories.

An NEU’s allocation could not exceed 75 percent of its most recent budget.

NEUs remained direct Treasury recipients for compliance and reporting purposes rather than state subrecipients.

Payment Tranches

Many recipients received their awards in two tranches approximately twelve months apart.

Treasury accelerated or adjusted payments in circumstances allowed by statute.

The two-tranche structure permitted continued federal oversight while providing immediate fiscal support.

2021 Interim Final Rule

Treasury issued an interim final rule in May 2021 while recipients were beginning to receive funds.

The rule interpreted statutory eligible uses, revenue loss, premium pay, infrastructure, restrictions, deadlines, and compliance.

Recipients made early project decisions under this framework while Treasury received public comments.

2022 Final Rule

Treasury published the final rule on January 6, 2022, with an effective date of April 1, 2022.

The final rule broadened presumed impacted populations, enumerated eligible uses, affordable-housing options, public-sector capacity, capital expenditures, broadband, and revenue replacement.

Recipients could elect to use the final rule before its effective date.

Eligible-Use Framework

The program ultimately contained seven principal eligible-use categories.

  1. Public health and negative economic impacts.
  2. Premium pay for eligible workers.
  3. Revenue replacement and government services.
  4. Water, sewer, and broadband infrastructure.
  5. Emergency relief from natural disasters.
  6. Surface Transportation projects.
  7. Title I community-development projects.

Public-Health Response

Recipients could finance programs responding to COVID‑19 and its broader health effects.

  • Vaccination, testing, contact tracing, and public-health communications.
  • Medical care, emergency response, and public-health staffing.
  • Behavioural-health and substance-use services.
  • Violence prevention and community health.
  • Ventilation and infection control.
  • Public-health data, laboratories, and administration.

Negative Economic Impacts

Recipients could assist households, small businesses, nonprofits, impacted industries, and the public sector when programs responded to pandemic harm.

Treasury identified presumed eligible populations and allowed recipients to document other impacted or disproportionately impacted classes.

Assistance had to be related and reasonably proportional to the identified harm.

Assistance to Households

  • Food, rent, mortgage, utility, and emergency cash assistance.
  • Childcare, early learning, and education services.
  • Housing navigation, homelessness prevention, and supportive services.
  • Job training and workforce support.
  • Internet access and digital literacy.
  • Health, mental-health, and community-violence programs.
  • Home repairs and lead remediation where eligible.

Disproportionately Impacted Communities

The final rule recognised that the pandemic intensified pre-existing health and economic disparities.

Recipients could provide broader responses to disproportionately impacted households and communities.

Qualified Census Tracts, low-income households, Tribal communities, and other documented populations could receive presumptions or tailored services.

Small-Business Assistance

Eligible assistance included grants, loans, technical assistance, counselling, business planning, rehabilitation, and support for pandemic-related operating or capital needs.

Programs had to identify economic harm and provide assistance reasonably proportional to that harm.

General economic-development subsidies without a valid eligible-use basis were not automatically permitted.

Nonprofit Assistance

Nonprofits could receive direct assistance as beneficiaries or administer programs as subrecipients.

Recipients supported food banks, community health, housing providers, childcare, arts organisations, legal services, and other community institutions.

The recipient had to classify the organisation correctly because beneficiary and subrecipient status carry different federal requirements.

Impacted Industries

Tourism, travel, hospitality, and other industries experiencing severe pandemic disruption could receive assistance.

Eligible programs could support payroll, operations, rehabilitation, safety investments, and economic recovery.

Assistance could not substantially exceed the industry’s pandemic harm.

Public-Sector Capacity

The final rule allowed recipients to restore and strengthen public employment and administrative capacity.

  • Rehiring employees cut during the pandemic.
  • Hiring above pre-pandemic baselines within rule limits.
  • Retention incentives and workforce development.
  • Modernising data, technology, procurement, and public administration.
  • Improving public-health and service-delivery systems.

Capital Expenditures

Capital projects could qualify when they responded to an identified public-health or economic harm.

Projects of at least $1 million generally required a written justification retained by the recipient.

Projects of at least $10 million generally required submission of the justification to Treasury through reporting.

The justification addressed the harm, why the project was appropriate, and whether less costly alternatives were considered.

Affordable Housing

Treasury permitted construction, rehabilitation, preservation, and financing of qualifying affordable housing.

Recipients could use established federal housing-program standards or demonstrate that a project served eligible households and remained affordable for an appropriate period.

Uses included rental housing, supportive housing, homeownership assistance, preservation, and conversion of properties.

Premium Pay

SLFRF could provide additional compensation to eligible workers performing essential work during the COVID‑19 public-health emergency.

Premium pay could not exceed $13 per hour or $25,000 for one worker over the entire period of performance.

It had to be in addition to ordinary wages or remuneration and could be paid directly by a recipient or through a grant to an eligible employer.

Eligible Essential Workers

Eligible sectors included healthcare, public health, emergency response, sanitation, food production and delivery, childcare, education, transportation, grocery work, social services, and other critical sectors.

Essential work generally required regular in-person interaction or physical handling of items handled by others.

Volunteers could not receive statutory premium pay as eligible workers.

Premium-Pay Targeting

Treasury encouraged priority for low- and moderate-income workers who faced health risk.

Premium pay was presumed responsive when the worker was nonexempt from Fair Labor Standards Act overtime or below the applicable wage threshold.

Higher-paid exempt workers required written justification explaining why the payment responded to pandemic essential work.

End of Qualifying Essential Work

The federal COVID‑19 national emergency ended on April 10, 2023.

Premium pay could not be earned for work performed after that date, although a validly obligated retrospective payment for earlier eligible work could be paid later.

Revenue Replacement

Recipients could use SLFRF to provide government services up to the amount of pandemic-related revenue loss.

Revenue replacement became the largest spending category because it allowed governments to finance a broad range of lawful services without tying each expenditure to a specific pandemic harm.

Revenue-loss funds retained their federal character and remained subject to program restrictions and applicable federal requirements.

Ten-Million-Dollar Standard Allowance

The 2022 final rule allowed every recipient to elect a standard revenue-loss allowance of up to $10 million or its entire award, whichever was less.

A recipient using the allowance did not have to perform the full revenue-loss calculation.

The election was especially significant for small cities, towns, villages, and Tribal governments.

Revenue-Loss Formula

Recipients not relying exclusively on the standard allowance could calculate loss by comparing actual general revenue with a counterfactual baseline.

The formula used a pre-pandemic base year, a growth adjustment, measurement dates, and Treasury-defined general-revenue rules.

The calculated amount limited government-services spending under this category.

Government Services

Government services generally included services traditionally provided by the recipient government.

  • Public safety, fire, courts, and emergency services.
  • Roads, parks, libraries, and public buildings.
  • Education, health, housing, and social services.
  • Government administration and payroll.
  • Pay-go infrastructure and maintenance.
  • Environmental, cultural, and community programs.

Water and Sewer Infrastructure

Recipients could finance projects eligible under the Clean Water and Drinking Water State Revolving Funds and certain additional projects identified by Treasury.

  • Drinking-water treatment and distribution.
  • Wastewater treatment and collection.
  • Stormwater and flood-resilience systems.
  • Lead-service-line replacement.
  • Septic systems and residential wells.
  • Water conservation, reuse, and cybersecurity.

Broadband Infrastructure

Broadband projects could address inadequate or unaffordable reliable service.

Treasury generally expected scalable infrastructure capable of symmetrical 100 Mbps service where practicable.

Recipients had to address affordability, service areas, provider commitments, and location-level reporting.

Middle-mile and last-mile projects could qualify under applicable standards.

Administrative Costs

Reasonable direct and indirect costs of administering SLFRF programs were allowable.

Costs could include staffing, consultants, legal review, procurement, data systems, reporting, subrecipient monitoring, audit, translation, community engagement, and closeout.

Administrative costs had to be properly allocated, documented, and obligated within program rules.

Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023

Congress amended sections 602 and 603 in December 2022.

The amendments added emergency relief from natural disasters, Surface Transportation projects, and Title I community-development projects.

Treasury implemented these authorities through a 2023 interim final rule.

Emergency Relief from Natural Disasters

Recipients could use funds for emergency relief addressing the physical or negative economic effects of a natural disaster.

The disaster had to occur or be threatened during the statutory period and require an emergency response.

Eligible uses could include temporary shelter, debris removal, emergency protective measures, damaged public infrastructure, household assistance, and economic relief.

Assistance had to be related and reasonably proportional to the disaster’s effects.

Surface Transportation Projects

The 2023 amendments created three pathways for eligible transportation uses.

Pathways incorporated specified Department of Transportation programs, a streamlined framework for certain projects, and nonfederal matching support for listed federal transportation programs.

Projects remained subject to applicable transportation, environmental, labour, project-approval, and reporting requirements.

Title I Community-Development Projects

Recipients could fund activities eligible under section 105(a) of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974.

These activities overlap with Community Development Block Grant and Indian Community Development Block Grant uses.

Potential projects included housing, public facilities, neighbourhood improvements, clearance, rehabilitation, economic development, and public services.

Environmental review and other Title I requirements could apply.

Transportation and Title I Funding Cap

Combined spending for Surface Transportation and Title I projects could not exceed the greater of $10 million or 30 percent of the recipient’s SLFRF award.

The combined amount could never exceed the recipient’s total award.

Additional restrictions prohibited supplanting certain other committed funds.

Eligible Cost Periods

For uses governed by the 2022 final rule, recipient costs generally had to be incurred from March 3, 2021 through December 31, 2024 and paid by the applicable expenditure deadline.

The three uses added in 2023 generally applied to recipient costs incurred from December 29, 2022 through December 31, 2024.

Beneficiary costs could predate March 3, 2021 in limited assistance programs when the recipient incurred its assistance obligation during the eligible period.

Obligation

An obligation is an order placed for property or services, a contract, subaward, similar transaction requiring payment, or another recognised commitment under Treasury’s rule.

Budgeting, appropriating, planning, or announcing a project did not necessarily create a federal obligation.

The recipient had to incur the obligation by December 31, 2024.

2023 Obligation Interim Final Rule

Treasury amended and clarified the obligation definition in November 2023.

The rule addressed contracts, subawards, interagency agreements, personnel costs, change orders, contingencies, and certain legal and administrative expenses.

It did not extend the December 31, 2024 statutory obligation deadline.

Personnel-Cost Estimates

Recipients could use reported estimates for qualifying personnel costs after the obligation deadline when positions were established and filled by December 31, 2024.

Estimates had to be calculated and reported under Treasury guidance.

The flexibility did not permit creation of new SLFRF-funded positions after the obligation deadline.

Change Orders and Contingencies

Recipients could reserve reasonable estimates for certain contract change orders and contingencies under the Obligation IFR.

The underlying contract had to be a valid obligation by December 31, 2024.

Unused estimates could not become authority to create entirely new obligations after the deadline.

Post-Deadline Legal and Administrative Costs

Recipients could estimate and obligate certain necessary costs of reporting, compliance, subrecipient monitoring, audit, recordkeeping, and closeout to be incurred after December 31, 2024.

The estimate had to follow Treasury guidance and remain limited to administering the existing award.

Unobligated Funds

Funds not validly obligated by December 31, 2024 generally must be returned to Treasury.

Recipients reported final obligation amounts in January or April 2025 according to reporting tier.

Treasury issued return instructions to recipients reporting unobligated balances.

GAO reported that states and localities had returned $13.7 million of $111.4 million in reported unobligated funds by November 2025.

Reclassification after the Obligation Deadline

A recipient cannot create a new obligation after December 31, 2024.

Treasury permits reclassification between projects where the receiving project had a valid obligation by the deadline.

Total reported obligations generally cannot exceed the amount established in the obligation-deadline report.

Reclassification does not cure an invalid or untimely obligation.

General Expenditure Deadline

Most SLFRF obligations must be liquidated and funds expended by December 31, 2026.

The deadline applies to public-health, economic-impact, premium-pay, revenue-replacement, infrastructure, natural-disaster, and administrative expenditures unless a specialised rule applies.

Unspent balances after the deadline are subject to return.

Special September 2026 Deadline

Surface Transportation and Title I project funds must be expended by September 30, 2026.

The earlier deadline reflects the statutory amendment and federal fiscal-year structure.

Recipients must distinguish these projects from similar activities funded through revenue replacement or another eligible category.

Restrictions on Use

Federal flexibility did not make every governmental expenditure eligible.

  • Deposits into pension funds were prohibited.
  • States and territories could not use funds to offset specified reductions in net tax revenue.
  • Debt service and replenishment of financial reserves were prohibited.
  • Payments satisfying judgments or settlements were generally prohibited unless they required otherwise eligible services.
  • Projects had to comply with applicable federal law and award conditions.

State and Territorial Tax-Offset Restriction

States and territories could not directly or indirectly use SLFRF to offset a reduction in net tax revenue resulting from a change in law, regulation, or administrative interpretation.

Treasury established a step-by-step process examining tax changes, revenue, allowable offsets, and de minimis effects.

The restriction did not apply to local or Tribal governments.

Pension-Fund Prohibition

Recipients could not deposit SLFRF into a pension fund.

Ordinary payroll costs, including normal pension contributions associated with eligible employee compensation, could be allowable when part of an eligible expense.

Extraordinary deposits intended to reduce unfunded pension liabilities were prohibited.

Debt, Borrowing, and Reserves

SLFRF could not directly pay principal or interest on outstanding debt or replenish rainy-day and reserve funds.

Revenue-replacement flexibility did not override these restrictions.

Recipients could finance pay-go capital projects and lawful government services rather than using the award as debt repayment.

Nonfederal Match

Revenue-replacement funds could generally meet nonfederal matching or cost-share requirements of other federal programs unless the other program prohibited it.

SLFRF could not be used as state Medicaid or CHIP matching funds under the applicable waiver framework.

The 2023 amendments authorised specialised matching pathways for certain transportation and Title I projects.

Transfers to Other Entities

Recipients could transfer funds to certain governmental units, nonprofit organisations, public-benefit corporations involved in transportation, special-purpose units, and other authorised entities.

Transfer did not relieve the original recipient of federal responsibility.

The receiving entity had to use funds within an eligible category and comply with incorporated requirements.

Beneficiary, Subrecipient, and Contractor

A beneficiary receives assistance as an end user, such as a household, small business, nonprofit, or impacted industry.

A subrecipient carries out part of the federal program on behalf of the recipient.

A contractor provides goods or services for the recipient’s own use through procurement.

The classification determines procurement, reporting, monitoring, audit, and federal-transparency obligations.

Subrecipient Monitoring

Pass-through recipients had to identify each subaward, communicate federal requirements, assess risk, monitor performance, review audits, and require corrective action.

Subrecipients generally needed a Unique Entity Identifier.

The recipient remained responsible to Treasury for misuse by a subrecipient.

Procurement

Procurement generally had to comply with 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.317–200.327 and applicable state, local, or Tribal law.

Requirements included competition, written procedures, cost or price analysis where applicable, conflict-of-interest standards, contract provisions, and documentation.

Emergency circumstances did not automatically justify permanent noncompetitive contracting.

Property Management

Real and personal property purchased with SLFRF remained subject to federal property standards and Treasury’s alternative-use period.

Recipients had to maintain inventories, safeguards, maintenance, insurance where required, disposition records, and continuing eligible use.

Sale or changed use could trigger compensation or repayment obligations.

Program Income

Income generated by an SLFRF-supported activity remained governed by the award and Uniform Guidance.

Program income earned before the expenditure deadline could support eligible obligations incurred by December 31, 2024.

Recipients had to report and account for income, revolving loans, repayments, fees, and other proceeds.

Civil-Rights Compliance

Non-Tribal recipients were subject to applicable federal nondiscrimination requirements, including Title VI, Section 504, Title IX, and the Age Discrimination Act.

Programs had to provide meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency and effective access for people with disabilities.

Treasury collected compliance information and could investigate complaints or impose remedies.

Tribal Sovereignty and Civil-Rights Conditions

Treasury’s pandemic award terms did not impose civil-rights obligations on Tribal governments beyond those otherwise applicable under federal law.

Tribal governments remained sovereign recipients rather than state subrecipients.

Reporting and audit obligations applied according to the award and federal grant rules.

Environmental and Infrastructure Requirements

The National Environmental Policy Act did not automatically apply to every ordinary SLFRF project solely because Treasury supplied funds.

Environmental review could apply through another federal permit, funding source, transportation pathway, Title I authority, or project-specific law.

Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, historic-preservation, prevailing-wage, and other requirements depended on project facts and authority.

Labour Practices

Treasury encouraged strong labour standards for large infrastructure projects.

Recipients reported workforce practices for specified projects of at least $10 million.

Davis–Bacon prevailing-wage requirements applied when independently triggered by another federal law or program but not universally to every original SLFRF use.

Reporting Framework

Reporting frequency depended on recipient type, population, and award size.

Reports included project names, descriptions, obligations, expenditures, subawards, demographic information, performance measures, infrastructure data, and compliance certifications.

Treasury used the information for oversight and public dashboards.

Interim Report

States, territories, metropolitan cities, counties, and Tribal governments submitted an initial interim report in 2021.

The report identified award status, early expenditures, revenue replacement, and basic program information.

NEUs used different initial reporting arrangements because states distributed their awards.

Project and Expenditure Reports

Project and Expenditure reports form the central compliance record.

Quarterly reporters included states, territories, Tribal governments with larger awards, and larger cities and counties.

Smaller cities, counties, NEUs, and smaller Tribal recipients generally reported annually.

Final reporting extends through April 30, 2027.

Recovery Plan Performance Reports

States, territories, and metropolitan cities and counties with populations above 250,000 generally prepared annual public Recovery Plans.

Plans described strategy, engagement, equity, projects, performance, and intended outcomes.

Treasury published the plans to support public transparency and comparison.

Public Transparency

Treasury publishes recipient-level data, project records, award information, and Recovery Plans.

State and local governments also used budgets, council resolutions, websites, public hearings, open-records systems, and dashboards.

Public reporting can lag current financial systems and should be read with reporting-period dates.

Performance Measurement

Recipients reported outputs and outcomes for specified expenditure categories.

Measures included households served, jobs supported, housing units, workers receiving premium pay, broadband locations, water projects, and public-health services.

Flexible revenue-replacement spending was harder to associate with one national outcome.

Internal Controls

Recipients needed written policies for eligibility, allowable costs, cash management, procurement, conflicts, subrecipient monitoring, records, reporting, and fraud response.

Controls were especially important for small governments receiving federal awards far larger than their ordinary grants.

Leadership certification did not replace supporting documentation.

SAM.gov and Unique Entity Identifiers

Recipients had to maintain active System for Award Management registration during the performance period.

Subrecipients generally needed a Unique Entity Identifier, although beneficiaries and contractors did not need one solely because of those roles.

Treasury used identifiers to connect awards, subawards, reporting, and audit records.

Recordkeeping

Recipients generally must retain financial and program records for five years after all funds are expended or returned.

Records include eligibility, contracts, invoices, payroll, procurement, property, subawards, civil rights, reports, audits, and corrective actions.

Litigation, audit, investigation, or unresolved findings can extend the retention period.

Single Audit

Recipients and subrecipients exceeding the federal expenditure threshold are subject to the Single Audit Act and 2 C.F.R. Part 200, Subpart F.

For fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024, the threshold is more than $1 million in total federal awards expended.

The Compliance Supplement identifies SLFRF requirements auditors should test.

Eligible smaller recipients could use an alternative compliance examination under specified conditions.

Treasury Monitoring

Treasury conducts desk reviews, data analysis, information requests, compliance reviews, and corrective-action monitoring.

Review can focus on reporting failure, ineligible projects, unsupported revenue loss, tax offsets, procurement, premium pay, subawards, or deadlines.

Treasury can require additional documentation before accepting a recipient’s position.

Treasury Office of Inspector General

Treasury OIG audits and investigates SLFRF and other pandemic-relief programs.

It can examine recipient controls, misuse, fraud, reporting, contractor conduct, and Treasury administration.

Award records must be made available to OIG and authorised representatives.

Government Accountability Office Oversight

GAO monitored allocations, obligations, expenditures, reporting, Treasury compliance procedures, and recipient progress.

GAO found that nearly all state and local funds had been allocated but identified weaknesses in reporting compliance and Treasury enforcement processes.

Its February 2026 update documented high obligation rates and continuing expenditure through 2026.

Reporting Noncompliance

Some recipients failed to submit timely Project and Expenditure reports.

Treasury issued notices, withheld action, requested correction, and developed procedures for chronic noncompliance.

Failure to report can lead to a finding, repayment demand, award suspension, or closeout complication.

Fraud and Abuse Risks

  • False beneficiary eligibility or duplicate assistance.
  • Bid rigging, kickbacks, and conflicts of interest.
  • Fictitious vendors or inflated invoices.
  • Misclassified contractors and subrecipients.
  • Unsupported payroll or premium pay.
  • Personal use of public funds.
  • Property diversion or unauthorised disposal.
  • False obligation documentation created after the deadline.
  • Manipulated revenue-loss calculations.
  • Cyber theft and payment redirection.

Improper and Ineligible Expenditures

A payment can be improper because the activity, beneficiary, amount, period, procurement, documentation, or funding category was incorrect.

Improper payment does not always involve fraud.

Recipients must identify errors, correct records, recover funds where appropriate, and repay Treasury when the federal award cannot absorb the cost.

Recoupment

Treasury can recoup funds used in violation of sections 602 or 603, the regulations, or award terms.

Treasury generally provides notice and an opportunity for the recipient to submit a request for reconsideration.

Final debts can be collected under federal debt-collection law.

Recipient Reconsideration

A recipient receiving an initial recoupment notice can present legal arguments, facts, and supporting documents within the specified period.

Treasury reviews eligibility, calculation, documentation, and corrective action.

Reconsideration is an administrative process rather than a household-level benefits appeal.

Early Closeout

Treasury created an early-closeout process for recipients that spent their full award and were invited to close.

Eligible recipients could submit a closeout report through a reporting cycle or Treasury portal prompt.

Early closeout did not eliminate later audit, recordkeeping, property, fraud, or debt obligations.

Standard Closeout

The ordinary award period ends December 31, 2026, except for the September deadline applying to transportation and Title I expenditures.

Recipients then complete final reconciliation, reporting, property records, subrecipient resolution, and return of unspent balances.

Final Project and Expenditure reporting is due April 30, 2027.

Closeout Is Not a Release from Liability

Treasury can reopen issues involving fraud, audit findings, disallowed costs, property, records, or material reporting error after administrative closeout.

Recipients remain responsible for long-term loans, property restrictions, subrecipient recovery, and record retention.

State Spending through March 2025

States and the District of Columbia reported spending $156.3 billion, approximately 80 percent of their awards, as of March 31, 2025.

Revenue replacement accounted for 53 percent of reported state spending.

Negative economic impacts accounted for 31 percent, with public health and infrastructure each accounting for about 6 percent.

Local Spending through March 2025

Cities, counties, and NEUs reported spending $107.2 billion, approximately 84 percent of local awards.

Revenue replacement accounted for about 67 percent of reported local spending.

Local spending rates varied among cities, counties, and smaller municipalities.

Dominance of Revenue Replacement

The standard allowance allowed many governments to classify their full award as revenue replacement.

This reduced administrative burden and permitted broad government-services spending.

It also made national analysis less specific because projects such as roads, payroll, parks, public safety, and administration could be grouped under one flexible category.

Public-Health Investments

Recipients financed vaccination, testing, health departments, behavioural health, community health workers, ventilation, data systems, emergency response, and violence prevention.

The program helped governments retain staff and modernise public-health capacity.

Outcomes varied according to local project design and the changing pandemic.

Housing Investments

Governments used SLFRF for rent and mortgage assistance, homelessness services, supportive housing, affordable-housing construction, rehabilitation, home repair, and housing navigation.

Treasury expanded guidance to make long-term affordable-housing projects easier to structure.

Housing development remained constrained by land, permitting, construction costs, financing, and the 2026 expenditure deadline.

Workforce Investments

Recipients supported job training, apprenticeships, childcare, public employment, workforce intermediaries, and small-business assistance.

Premium pay compensated some workers for pandemic exposure.

Public-sector capacity projects sought to reverse staffing losses and improve service delivery.

Infrastructure Investments

Water, sewer, stormwater, lead-removal, broadband, roads, public facilities, and community-development projects became major long-term uses.

Infrastructure could produce benefits after the pandemic but faced procurement, engineering, permitting, inflation, labour, and deadline risks.

Recipients frequently combined SLFRF with other federal, state, local, and private funds.

Impact in Tribal Communities

Tribal governments used SLFRF to address longstanding infrastructure, housing, public-health, employment, and economic-development needs exposed by COVID‑19.

Flexible direct funding strengthened Tribal self-determination relative to narrowly prescribed grants.

Small administrative systems and remote project conditions created implementation and reporting challenges.

Equity and Disproportionate Impact

Treasury encouraged recipients to direct resources toward communities suffering disproportionate health and economic harm.

The final rule created presumptions and broader services for low-income and underserved populations.

Local political choices determined whether flexible aid reduced disparities or primarily supported general government budgets.

Inflation and Administrative Capacity

Construction inflation, supply-chain disruption, labour shortages, and higher interest rates reduced the purchasing power of planned projects.

Smaller governments often lacked grants-management, procurement, engineering, legal, and reporting staff.

Technical assistance and the revenue-loss allowance reduced but did not eliminate these barriers.

Federalism

SLFRF combined federal financing with state, local, territorial, and Tribal project selection.

Direct payments reduced state control over counties and cities while creating a federal compliance relationship with tens of thousands of governments.

The program demonstrated both the flexibility and administrative complexity of large-scale intergovernmental grants.

Program Achievements

  • Delivered $350 billion to more than 30,000 governments.
  • Prevented or reduced public-service cuts during the pandemic recovery.
  • Provided the first comparable direct federal fiscal support to every county.
  • Supplied an unprecedented $20 billion Tribal allocation.
  • Supported public health, households, businesses, nonprofits, and essential workers.
  • Restored public-sector employment and administrative capacity.
  • Financed water, sewer, broadband, housing, and community infrastructure.
  • Allowed small governments to use a simplified $10 million revenue-loss allowance.
  • Expanded flexibility for disasters, transportation, and community development.
  • Generated extensive recipient-level public data.
  • Supported a faster state and local fiscal recovery than after the Great Recession.

Limitations and Failures

  • Rules changed repeatedly after recipients began planning.
  • More than 30,000 recipients created a major oversight burden.
  • Some small governments lacked federal grants-management capacity.
  • Reporting noncompliance persisted.
  • Revenue replacement reduced project-level transparency.
  • Some projects had weak connections to pandemic harms.
  • Infrastructure inflation reduced purchasing power.
  • Obligation and expenditure deadlines created completion risk.
  • GAO identified weaknesses in Treasury compliance procedures.
  • Fraud, procurement, and conflict-of-interest risks accompanied rapid spending.
  • Flexible aid did not guarantee equitable local distribution.
  • Temporary funds could create services that governments could not sustain.

Major Controversies

Fiscal Need versus Over-Allocation

Critics argued that improved state revenues made parts of the $350 billion unnecessary, while supporters emphasised uncertainty, local variation, and long-term service needs.

Federal Flexibility versus Accountability

Broad eligible uses allowed local adaptation but made national outcome measurement and misuse prevention more difficult.

Tax Cuts

States challenged Treasury’s tax-offset interpretation, arguing that Congress had intruded on state taxing authority.

Revenue Replacement

The $10 million allowance simplified administration but allowed many recipients to classify nearly every ordinary government service as recovery spending.

Long-Term Projects with Temporary Funds

Housing and infrastructure investments could yield durable benefits but faced deadlines and future operating costs.

Equity

Treasury encouraged equitable recovery, but elected governments retained broad discretion over priorities.

Historical Significance

SLFRF was one of the largest direct federal transfers to state, local, territorial, and Tribal governments in United States history.

It marked a deliberate departure from the slower and more austere state and local recovery after the Great Recession.

The program broadened federal fiscal support beyond reimbursement for emergency costs to include revenue replacement, equity, public-sector capacity, affordable housing, and long-term infrastructure.

Its direct relationship with more than 30,000 governments created a lasting model—and warning—for future national emergency grants.

Research Cautions

  • The official name is SLFRF. “State and Local Recovery Funds” is descriptive shorthand.
  • There are two statutory funds. Section 602 covers states, territories, and Tribes; section 603 covers local governments.
  • The obligation deadline has passed. New obligations generally cannot be created after December 31, 2024.
  • Most spending ends December 31, 2026. Transportation and Title I spending ends September 30, 2026.
  • Budgeting was not necessarily obligation. A valid contract, subaward, order, or similar commitment was generally required.
  • Revenue replacement is an eligible-use category, not proof of an actual budget deficit. Many recipients elected the standard allowance.
  • Beneficiaries are not subrecipients. Classification affects audit and monitoring requirements.
  • NEUs received funds through states but are Treasury recipients.
  • Public data are retrospective. Always identify the reporting date.
  • Obligated and expended are different. A committed contract is not the same as a paid invoice.
  • Closeout does not eliminate liability. Audit, property, fraud, and records obligations continue.
  • The CARES Act Coronavirus Relief Fund is a different program.

An order, contract, subaward, or similar transaction requiring payment under Treasury’s rule.

Additional compensation of up to $13 per hour and $25,000 total for eligible essential work.
Program income
Income generated by an SLFRF-supported activity or property.

A recipient’s calculated or standard-allowance reduction in general revenue used to authorise government-services spending.

Coronavirus State and Local Fiscal Recovery Funds.

Government-wide grant rules codified principally at 2 C.F.R. Part 200.

Summary

The Coronavirus State and Local Fiscal Recovery Funds program was created by the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021.

Congress appropriated $350 billion for states, the District of Columbia, territories, Tribal governments, counties, metropolitan cities, and smaller municipalities.

Treasury distributed approximately $195.3 billion to states and the District, $65.1 billion to counties, $45.6 billion to metropolitan cities, $19.53 billion to NEUs, $20 billion to Tribal governments, and $4.5 billion to territories.

Original eligible uses addressed public health and economic harm, premium pay, revenue replacement, and water, sewer, and broadband infrastructure.

The 2022 final rule added a standard revenue-loss allowance of as much as $10 million and broadened household, housing, business, public-sector, and capital-project flexibility.

Congress later added natural-disaster relief, Surface Transportation projects, and Title I community-development projects.

All funds generally had to be obligated by December 31, 2024. Unobligated balances must be returned.

Most obligations must be expended by December 31, 2026, while Surface Transportation and Title I projects must be expended by September 30, 2026.

Final reporting extends through April 30, 2027, and audit, property, records, fraud, and recoupment responsibilities can continue after closeout.

As of March 31, 2025, states reported spending $156.3 billion and localities $107.2 billion, with revenue replacement accounting for the largest share.

SLFRF helped prevent public-service cuts, supported economic recovery, and financed long-term infrastructure, but its flexibility, scale, changing rules, uneven local capacity, and heavy use of revenue replacement complicated transparency and outcome evaluation.
