Drug-Free Communities Support Program

The federal matching-grant program created in 1997 to build twelve-sector community coalitions that use local data, positive youth engagement, environmental prevention, policy, services, and long-term collaboration to prevent and reduce youth substance use.

What Is the Drug-Free Communities Support Program?

The DFC Support Program provides federal grants to community coalitions that organize local institutions and residents around youth substance-use prevention.

The program does not prescribe one curriculum or intervention. It funds coalition capacity, assessment, planning, coordination, implementation, policy change, communication, environmental strategies, evaluation, and sustainability.

Each coalition is expected to identify the substances and conditions most important in its community and develop a comprehensive, long-term response.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s creation, statutory purposes, federal administration, coalition eligibility, twelve-sector model, fiscal agents, grant cycles, matching rules, rural provisions, strategic planning, seven community-change strategies, data requirements, evaluation evidence, oversight findings, current funding, fiscal year 2026 recompete, controversies, and related programs.

It distinguishes DFC from treatment grants, school-only curricula, law-enforcement drug programs, the CARA Local Drug Crises Program, the STOP Act grant program, and the High Intensity Drug Trafficking Areas Program.

Geographic Scope

DFC coalitions operate throughout the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, the United States Virgin Islands, tribal communities, rural counties, suburbs, cities, towns, and multijurisdictional regions.

A coalition must define the community it represents. The boundary can be a municipality, county, reservation, school district, neighborhood, or other locally meaningful area.

More than one coalition can represent the same general community only when the coalitions demonstrate collaboration and each independently satisfies federal eligibility requirements.

Historical Context

Congress created DFC during renewed national concern about adolescent drug use in the 1990s.

Policymakers argued that local substance use was shaped not only by individual knowledge but also by family expectations, peer norms, retail access, school policy, law enforcement, media, community institutions, and the physical environment.

The program therefore emphasized broad coalitions and community-level change rather than reliance on one school curriculum or public-awareness campaign.

Community Anti-Drug Coalition Movement

Local anti-drug coalitions had developed before the federal statute through parent groups, schools, health departments, law enforcement, businesses, civic organizations, and prevention agencies.

The 1997 act sought to strengthen existing coalitions, encourage new coalitions, and provide national training and evaluation infrastructure.

Drug-Free Communities Act of 1997

President Bill Clinton signed Public Law 105-20 on June 27, 1997.

The law amended the National Narcotics Leadership Act of 1988 and placed the program under the direction of ONDCP.

The statute treated community coalitions as catalysts capable of coordinating prevention across institutions and sustaining work beyond federal funding.

Congressional Findings

Congress found that substance use among youth was preventable, that community coalitions were developing comprehensive strategies, and that federal, state, local, tribal, and private cooperation was critical.

Some original findings reflected the terminology and assumptions of 1990s drug policy and have since been amended from “substance abuse” to “substance use and misuse.”

Eight Statutory Purposes

  1. Reduce youth substance use and misuse and, over time, adult use and misuse.
  2. Strengthen collaboration among communities and federal, state, local, and tribal governments.
  3. Enhance intergovernmental cooperation and coordination.
  4. Catalyze citizen participation and collaboration across community sectors.
  5. Provide technical assistance, guidance, and financial support to communities demonstrating long-term commitment.
  6. Disseminate timely information about effective practices and initiatives.
  7. Enhance rather than supplant local initiatives.
  8. Encourage creation and support of community anti-drug coalitions.

Two Operational Program Goals

  1. Establish and strengthen collaboration among communities, nonprofit agencies, and federal, state, local, and tribal governments.
  2. Reduce substance use among youth and, over time, adults by addressing community risk factors and strengthening protective factors.

Office of National Drug Control Policy

ONDCP administers DFC as part of the Executive Office of the President.

ONDCP sets policy, oversees funding, develops notices of funding opportunity, approves awards, manages national evaluation and technical-assistance relationships, and reports to Congress.

The program’s placement in ONDCP connects local prevention coalitions to the National Drug Control Strategy.

DFC Program Administrator

Federal law directs the ONDCP Director to appoint an Administrator responsible for carrying out the program.

The Administrator is responsible for grants, standards, technical assistance, data, compliance, and general administration.

OJJDP Management, Fiscal Years 1998–2004

The Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention within the Department of Justice managed day-to-day grant administration during the program’s early years.

This arrangement reflected DFC’s original emphasis on youth prevention and juvenile policy.

SAMHSA Management, Fiscal Years 2005–2020

ONDCP transferred day-to-day management to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration beginning in fiscal year 2005.

SAMHSA’s Center for Substance Abuse Prevention administered grant competitions, continuations, and federal oversight until the 2020 transition.

Transition to CDC in 2020

ONDCP transferred program management from SAMHSA to CDC in fiscal year 2020.

ONDCP and CDC have operated the program through an annually updated interagency agreement.

ONDCP oversees strategy and funding, while CDC performs day-to-day grant management, recipient communication, program review, reporting support, and related administration.

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

CDC manages funding opportunities, grants, technical guidance, recipient communication, performance reporting, coalition listings, and program resources.

DFC is located within CDC’s overdose-prevention and injury-prevention structure, although the program’s statutory focus remains broad youth substance-use prevention.

Community Anti-Drug Coalitions of America

CADCA provides coalition-development training, technical assistance, planning tools, sustainability support, and workforce development.

Congress supports this function through the National Community Anti-Drug Coalition Institute.

Technical assistance is intended to improve coalition capacity rather than dictate one local strategy.

National Community Anti-Drug Coalition Institute

Congress established the Institute through the 2001 reauthorization.

The Institute develops and disseminates training, technical assistance, evaluation support, and coalition-building resources.

Fiscal year 2026 appropriations reserve $2.5 million for this national training function.

Community Coalition

A coalition is a formal arrangement for cooperation and collaboration among groups or sectors within a defined community.

The coalition itself can be a nonprofit organization or can operate through an eligible fiscal agent such as a local government, school district, health department, hospital, youth organization, or other legal entity.

Fiscal Agent

A coalition without independent legal or tax-exempt status can partner with an eligible organization that applies for and receives the award.

The fiscal agent assumes federal responsibility for financial management, reporting, compliance, procurement, records, audit, and grant administration.

The coalition remains responsible for implementing the community strategy.

Six-Month Existence Requirement

A coalition must have been in existence for at least six months before applying.

The requirement is intended to ensure that DFC funds support a functioning community partnership rather than create a coalition solely to obtain a grant.

Principal Mission

The coalition’s principal mission must be comprehensive, long-term reduction of substance use and misuse, with a primary focus on youth.

A general health, education, policing, or social-service organization does not qualify merely because it conducts one prevention project.

Multiple-Substance Requirement

DFC coalitions must address more than one substance.

The requirement discourages coalitions from operating as single-drug campaigns and recognizes that youth exposure, risk, and local markets change over time.

The Twelve Required Sectors

  1. Youth.
  2. Parents.
  3. Businesses.
  4. Media.
  5. Schools.
  6. Youth-serving organizations.
  7. Law enforcement.
  8. Religious or fraternal organizations.
  9. Civic or volunteer groups.
  10. Healthcare professionals or organizations.
  11. State, local, or tribal government agencies with substance-use expertise.
  12. Another organization involved in reducing substance use.

One Person Per Sector

An individual can represent only one statutory sector at a time.

This rule prevents one person from satisfying multiple categories and preserves the program’s requirement for broad institutional participation.

Youth Sector

The youth representative must be eighteen or younger.

Meaningful participation can include voting, planning, data interpretation, communication, peer outreach, youth-led research, and strategy design.

Youth should not be included merely as speakers or symbols.

Parent Sector

The parent sector represents people legally responsible for children, grandchildren, foster children, or other qualifying youth.

Parent participation can shape communication, family policy, monitoring, support, and prevention access.

Business Sector

Businesses can contribute employment, sponsorship, communication channels, retail-policy cooperation, workplace support, and local influence.

A participating business should not operate in conflict with DFC goals.

Media Sector

Media representatives help distribute local information, correct misperceptions, publicize services, and support campaigns.

Media activity should avoid sensationalism, stigma, unsupported claims, and disclosure of confidential youth information.

School Sector

Schools provide access to youth, prevention programming, student surveys, policy, counselling, family communication, and early identification.

School participation is also critical because many coalitions collect core outcome data from middle- and high-school students.

Youth-Serving Organization Sector

Youth-serving organizations can include clubs, mentoring programs, recreation, child welfare, after-school programs, and community centres.

They can reach youth who may not engage through schools or government agencies.

Law-Enforcement Sector

The law-enforcement representative must be an active sworn officer under current guidance.

Law enforcement can provide local drug-market information, retail compliance, diversion prevention, prescription take-back support, safety, and enforcement of laws affecting youth access.

Enforcement should be balanced with prevention, public health, civil rights, and avoidance of youth criminalization.

Religious or Fraternal Sector

Faith and fraternal organizations can provide trusted adults, youth activity, meeting space, family support, volunteers, and community norms.

Federal funds cannot be used for inherently religious activity, coercion, or unlawful discrimination.

Civic or Volunteer Sector

Civic and volunteer organizations can contribute leadership, volunteers, fundraising, public events, and connections across the community.

The representative must come from an organization other than the applicant coalition itself.

Healthcare Sector

Healthcare participation can include physicians, pharmacists, nurses, behavioural-health providers, hospitals, clinics, and public-health professionals.

Relevant roles include prevention, screening, prescribing education, overdose response, treatment referral, data, and family support.

Government Substance-Use Sector

The required government-sector representative must come from a state, local, or tribal agency with expertise in substance-use prevention, treatment, or recovery support.

This sector connects the coalition with public funding, policy, epidemiology, treatment systems, and state prevention infrastructure.

Other Substance-Use Organization Sector

The twelfth sector provides flexibility for organizations not captured by the other categories.

Examples can include recovery organizations, treatment providers, universities, housing organizations, libraries, cultural institutions, or other prevention partners.

Elected Officials

When feasible, federal law also calls for participation by elected officials or their representatives from relevant federal, state, local, or tribal governments.

Elected-official participation can help connect coalition recommendations to policy and budget decisions.

Maintaining Sector Participation

Coalitions must maintain active participation from all twelve sectors throughout the grant.

Vacancies, inactive members, staff turnover, and nominal representation can place a coalition out of compliance.

GAO reported in 2026 that ONDCP had not consistently enforced this requirement.

Defined Community

Applicants identify the population, geography, institutions, and local identity of the community served.

A clear definition prevents duplication, supports data collection, establishes accountability, and guides coalition membership.

Overlap and Mutual Cooperation

A coalition cannot overlap another DFC-funded coalition without a letter of mutual cooperation.

The rule prevents duplicative federal funding and encourages coordinated service when boundaries intersect.

Initial Five-Year Grant Cycle

A successful new applicant receives a first-year award and can receive four annual continuation awards if it remains compliant and funding is available.

Continuation is not automatic in the sense of an entitlement; recipients must submit required applications, reports, matching documentation, and assurances.

Additional Five-Year Grant Cycle

After completing the first five years, a coalition can compete for an additional grant beginning in year six.

The coalition receives no statutory preference merely because it previously held a DFC grant.

Successful additional-grant recipients can receive years six through ten.

Ten-Year Funding Limit

A coalition cannot receive more than ten years of ordinary DFC funding.

The limit reflects the program’s expectation that federal grants build self-sustaining local prevention infrastructure.

Annual Award Cap

The maximum federal award is $125,000 per coalition per fiscal year.

Congress increased the cap from $100,000 to $125,000 in 2006.

Inflation has reduced the real purchasing power of the fixed cap over time.

Match During Years One Through Five

The federal award cannot exceed the amount of nonfederal support raised by the coalition.

A coalition requesting $125,000 therefore generally must document at least $125,000 in allowable nonfederal match.

Match During Years Six and Seven

During the first two renewal years of the additional grant cycle, nonfederal support must equal at least 125 percent of the federal award.

A $125,000 federal award therefore requires at least $156,250 in allowable nonfederal support.

Match During Years Eight Through Ten

During the final three years, nonfederal support must equal at least 150 percent of the federal award.

A $125,000 federal award therefore requires at least $187,500 in allowable nonfederal support.

Cash Match

Cash match can include eligible nonfederal contributions used for coalition staff, activity, supplies, services, facilities, data, training, or other approved costs.

Match cannot be counted simultaneously toward another federal award unless specifically authorized.

In-Kind Match

Allowable in-kind support can include volunteer time, donated space, professional services, equipment use, supplies, media, and other documented contributions.

Valuation must be reasonable, supported, necessary to the approved project, and consistent with federal cost principles.

Tribal Match Provision

Federal funds associated with coalition participation by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, Indian Health Service, or a tribal government agency with substance-use expertise can be counted as nonfederal match under the special statutory rule.

This provision recognizes the distinct federal funding structure of tribal communities.

Temporary COVID-19 Match Relief

Congress authorized limited match relief for fiscal years 2020–2022 when coalitions could not raise agreed nonfederal support because of the COVID-19 public-health emergency.

The relief did not eliminate the permanent statutory matching structure for later years.

Sustainability Requirement

Applicants must demonstrate nonfederal support and a strategy for obtaining substantial future resources.

Sustainability can include institutional budgets, local grants, philanthropy, volunteers, partnerships, contracts, fundraising, policy adoption, and integration of coalition functions into permanent organizations.

Rural Coalition Grants

Federal law authorizes special grants for coalitions representing counties with populations not exceeding 30,000.

The Administrator can waive specified eligibility requirements when appropriate to stimulate coalition development in sparsely populated areas.

The $125,000 cap and matching structure remain applicable.

Priority for Economically Disadvantaged Areas

The statute gives priority in initial awards to coalitions serving economically disadvantaged areas.

Disadvantaged communities can face greater substance-use risk while possessing less fundraising, staffing, data, and grant-writing capacity.

Application Requirements

Applicants must document coalition history, sectors, mission, community boundary, substance-use problems, existing programs, service gaps, strategic plan, nonfederal match, sustainability, evaluation capacity, fiscal controls, and federal assurances.

A technically complete application still competes on quality and available funding.

Community Needs Assessment

Coalitions collect local quantitative and qualitative information concerning substance use, access, attitudes, consequences, policies, services, demographics, protective factors, and risk factors.

Assessment should include youth who are absent from school, disconnected, unhoused, justice involved, in foster care, or otherwise missing from ordinary school surveys where practicable.

Programs and Service-Gap Analysis

Applicants inventory existing prevention and treatment activity and identify gaps.

DFC is primarily a prevention and coalition-capacity grant, but understanding treatment, recovery, overdose, mental health, and family-service gaps is necessary for coordinated planning and referral.

Strategic Plan

The coalition develops a comprehensive, long-term strategic plan addressing youth substance use.

The plan identifies measurable objectives, priority substances, local conditions, target populations, partners, strategies, implementation responsibility, timelines, and evaluation.

Community Consensus

Coalitions are expected to develop agreement concerning local priorities rather than impose a plan from one agency.

Consensus does not require unanimity, but the process should document meaningful participation and disagreement.

Local Problems, Local Solutions

DFC is built on the principle that substance-use patterns and contributing conditions vary by community.

A rural methamphetamine problem, suburban alcohol access problem, urban cannabis-retail issue, tribal fentanyl threat, or youth-vaping pattern can require different coalitions and interventions.

Risk Factors

Risk factors are conditions associated with an increased likelihood of youth substance use.

Examples include favourable norms, easy retail or social access, low perceived risk, family conflict, trauma, school disengagement, peer use, community disorganization, and availability of substances.

Protective Factors

Protective factors reduce risk or support healthy development.

Examples include family connection, school connectedness, supportive adults, positive peer groups, meaningful youth participation, faith or cultural connection, safe recreation, economic opportunity, and clear community standards.

Environmental Prevention

Environmental strategies seek to change the context in which choices occur rather than rely only on individual information.

Coalitions can address retail access, social availability, policies, physical design, enforcement, incentives, community norms, and institutional practice.

Seven Strategies for Community Change

  1. Provide information.
  2. Enhance skills.
  3. Provide support.
  4. Change access or barriers.
  5. Change consequences, incentives, or disincentives.
  6. Change physical design.
  7. Modify or change policies.

Provide Information

Information strategies include public education, media, presentations, fact sheets, town halls, youth communication, parent education, and awareness of local trends.

Information alone is usually insufficient to create lasting population-level change.

Enhance Skills

Skill-building can include parenting, refusal skills, media literacy, youth leadership, prevention workforce training, retailer training, motivational interviewing, coalition management, and policy advocacy within legal limits.

Provide Support

Support strategies can include mentoring, youth clubs, family support, recovery support, school connection, referral systems, peer groups, and safe activities.

DFC funds should strengthen prevention infrastructure rather than become a substitute for comprehensive clinical treatment.

Change Access and Barriers

Coalitions can address retail sales to minors, social furnishing, unsecured medications, safe storage, disposal, counterfeit pills, vaping products, alcohol availability, cannabis access, and treatment or prevention barriers.

Change Consequences and Incentives

Strategies can alter incentives for retailers, schools, families, institutions, or youth.

Examples include retailer recognition, administrative enforcement, diversion, graduated school responses, positive youth recognition, and consistent policy implementation.

Change Physical Design

Physical-design strategies can improve lighting, supervision, recreation, storage, signage, safe gathering places, retail layout, medication security, and public spaces.

Changes should be connected to a documented substance-use condition rather than treated as general beautification.

Modify or Change Policies

Coalitions can support evidence-informed policy concerning schools, retailers, prescribing, safe storage, tobacco and nicotine, alcohol, cannabis, workplace practice, treatment access, and youth-serving institutions.

Federal grant funds remain subject to lobbying restrictions.

Positive Youth Engagement

Current CDC guidance emphasizes meaningful youth participation, leadership, connection, and positive development.

Youth can conduct assessments, design messaging, advise adults, lead projects, interpret data, and evaluate coalition decisions.

Alcohol Prevention

Alcohol remains one of the most commonly addressed substances because of underage use, social availability, impaired driving, poisoning, injury, and later dependence risk.

Strategies include parent communication, retailer compliance, social-host policy, school policy, norms campaigns, screening, and youth alternatives.

Tobacco and Nicotine

Coalitions address cigarettes, vaping devices, nicotine products, youth marketing, retail access, school policy, and social norms.

Rapid product change requires current surveillance and accurate communication.

Marijuana and Cannabis

Coalitions address youth access, perceived risk, impaired driving, high-potency products, edible products, advertising, storage, school policy, and adult-use market changes.

State legalization does not eliminate the federal program’s youth-prevention focus.

Prescription-Drug Misuse

Relevant strategies include safe prescribing education, medication storage, disposal, pharmacist participation, parent communication, counterfeit-pill warnings, and referral to care.

Opioids and Fentanyl

Coalitions increasingly address illicit fentanyl, counterfeit pills, opioid misuse, overdose risk, naloxone, treatment referral, stigma, and youth education.

Prevention messaging should distinguish prescribed medication, misuse, counterfeit products, illicit supply, substance-use disorder, and overdose response.

Methamphetamine

Local methamphetamine strategies can include youth education, community surveillance, treatment connection, family support, prevention of initiation, and coordination with public health and law enforcement.

Emerging Drug Threats

Coalitions can respond to substances such as xylazine, medetomidine, new synthetic opioids, counterfeit pills, novel stimulants, and changing nicotine or cannabis products.

Emerging-threat communication should be timely, evidence based, and designed to avoid panic or unintentional promotion.

School-Based Prevention

Schools are an important setting but DFC is not a school-only program.

Effective coalition work can combine curricula with policy, family engagement, student support, data, retailer action, health care, community norms, and environmental change.

Law Enforcement and Prevention

Law enforcement can support prevention through education, diversion, safe disposal, retail compliance, drug-market information, school partnerships, and overdose response.

Coalition participation should not convert confidential health, school, or youth information into criminal intelligence without lawful authority and clear safeguards.

Treatment and Recovery Connections

Federal law refers to prevention and treatment, but DFC grants principally build coalition and prevention capacity.

Coalitions can map services, reduce stigma, improve referral, support families, connect people to treatment and recovery, and identify gaps.

Overdose Prevention

Coalitions can support naloxone education and access, overdose awareness, safe storage, counterfeit-pill information, treatment referral, post-overdose support, and coordination with local surveillance.

Youth-use prevention and overdose prevention overlap but are not identical objectives.

Stigma and Language

Stigmatizing language can discourage treatment, recovery, family participation, honest data, and youth help-seeking.

Modern practice increasingly uses person-first language while preserving the program’s statutory prevention mission.

Four Core Measures

Coalitions report common indicators concerning:

  1. past thirty-day substance use;
  2. perception of risk or harm;
  3. perception of parental disapproval; and
  4. perception of peer disapproval.

Core Substances

Core measures historically cover alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, and prescription-drug misuse.

Coalitions can collect additional local measures for fentanyl, opioids, methamphetamine, vaping, or other priorities.

Initial Benchmark Survey

An initial applicant must conduct or use an available local benchmark survey or performance measure concerning youth substance use.

Baseline data allow later comparison but do not create a valid control group by themselves.

Biennial Youth Surveys

Federal law requires biennial surveys or incorporation of suitable existing surveys to measure progress and effectiveness.

The entity collecting or evaluating data must have relevant experience.

Annual Progress Report

Beginning in 2023, DFC coalitions moved from semiannual to annual progress reporting.

Reports describe coalition capacity, sector activity, strategies, reach, products, policy, local conditions, and implementation challenges.

Coalition Classification Tool

Coalitions use the Coalition Classification Tool to identify prevention assets established or strengthened through DFC funding.

The tool seeks to capture community infrastructure that can persist beyond individual events or grants.

National Cross-Site Evaluation

ONDCP contracts for a national evaluation using coalition reports, core measures, surveys, implementation data, and other sources.

The evaluation describes program reach, coalition activity, reported community change, and trends among participating coalitions.

The 2024 Evaluation Report

The report published in June 2025 analysed implementation from August 2023 through July 2024 and core measures submitted from 2002 through 2024.

It included progress reports from 745 fiscal year 2023 coalitions after five awards had been returned or suspended.

Reported Past-Thirty-Day Use Declines

The national evaluation reported statistically significant decreases in past-thirty-day use of alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, and prescription drugs among both middle- and high-school youth from coalitions’ first to most recent reports.

For all coalitions since inception, reported relative declines ranged from approximately 14.5 percent to 38.9 percent depending on substance and school level.

Fiscal Year 2023 Cohort Results

The most recent cohort also reported significant declines across the four core substances at both school levels.

Reported relative declines ranged from approximately 18.5 percent to 45.5 percent.

Comparison with National YRBS Data

The national report found that high-school youth in DFC communities reported lower 2023 past-thirty-day alcohol and marijuana use than the national Youth Risk Behavior Survey sample.

Differences between separate samples do not prove that DFC caused the lower rates.

Mixed Perception Measures

Results for risk perception, parental disapproval, and peer disapproval were mixed by substance, school level, and cohort.

High-school peer disapproval generally increased, while several middle-school measures moved contrary to the desired direction or showed no change.

Causality Limitation

The cross-site evaluation does not establish that DFC caused observed substance-use changes.

Coalitions are not randomly assigned, local survey methods vary, comparison groups are limited, and communities operate many concurrent prevention efforts.

The evaluation itself acknowledged that one activity cannot be linked clearly to an outcome across the comprehensive coalition model.

June 2026 GAO Review

GAO concluded that ONDCP did not have sufficient performance data to claim that the DFC program itself was reducing youth substance use.

GAO identified inconsistent data, unclear sources, variation allowed by statute, and inadequate methodological transparency.

GAO nevertheless found that available information provided useful insight into coalition activity and local prevention efforts.

GAO’s Six 2026 Recommendations

  1. Develop a strategy to identify relevant data for understanding program impact.
  2. Explore standardization of data collection for the four core measures.
  3. Publish complete evaluation methodology.
  4. Create performance goals and measures for collaboration.
  5. Enforce maintenance of all twelve sectors and help coalitions restore vacancies.
  6. Improve congressional disclosure of administrative carryover balances.

ONDCP concurred with all six recommendations, which remained open when GAO released the report.

Data-Standardization Problem

Coalitions use different surveys, grades, schedules, settings, administration methods, consent rules, and sample sizes.

Core wording is reviewed, but variation can affect comparability and representation.

School-Survey Limitation

Most coalition surveys are administered through schools.

Youth who are absent, have left school, are in detention, are unhoused, or are otherwise disconnected can be underrepresented despite potentially higher risk.

Consent and Survey Burden

Active parental-consent requirements, school workload, survey fatigue, staff turnover, and concern about instructional time can limit data collection.

These barriers can produce lower response rates or samples that differ from the entire youth population.

Performance and Impact

Program performance measures can document activities, collaboration, capacity, and trends.

Impact evaluation asks whether outcomes would have differed without the program.

The distinction is central to interpreting federal claims about success.

Types of DFC Grants

New grants
Openly competed awards for a coalition’s first or sixth year of funding.
Continuation grants
Annual in-cycle awards for years two through five or seven through ten.
Mentoring grants
Historical awards to established coalitions helping create new coalitions.
Mentoring continuations
Second-year continuation of a two-year mentoring grant.

Mentoring Grants

Federal law and prior practice allowed established coalitions to mentor developing communities.

GAO reported that mentoring grants had not been funded since management transferred to CDC in 2020.

Fiscal Year 2024 Grant Activity

GAO reported 192 new grants and 559 continuation grants in fiscal year 2024.

No mentoring or mentoring-continuation awards were made that year.

Fiscal Year 2025 Funding

Congress appropriated $109 million for DFC in fiscal year 2025.

GAO reported that 751 coalitions received funding during the fiscal year.

The appropriation allowed nearly $12.8 million for administrative expenses.

Fiscal Year 2025 New-Award Pause

GAO reported that no new grants were initially awarded in fiscal year 2025 because ONDCP paused the competition to align funding notices with administration policy priorities and executive orders.

Existing continuation awards remained a separate part of the program.

September 2025 Recompete Decision

ONDCP announced on September 26, 2025 that it would recompete fiscal year 2025 awards to review compliance with presidential executive orders.

The agency stated that appropriated money remained available.

The decision delayed awards and introduced a significant change to the ordinary grant cycle.

Fiscal Year 2026 Appropriation

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 provided $109 million for the Drug-Free Communities Program.

No more than $12.78 million was available for administrative expenses, and $2.5 million was reserved for national coalition training.

The enacted amount rejected the administration’s earlier proposal to reduce and transfer the program.

July 2026 New and Recompeted Awards

ONDCP published a five-page award list in July 2026 identified as fiscal year 2026 awards from the fiscal year 2025 recompete.

The list contains 133 application entries representing coalitions across states, Puerto Rico, the United States Virgin Islands, and tribal communities.

Fiscal Year 2026 Continuation Awards

GAO reported that ONDCP expected to award a cohort of 546 noncompeting continuation grants in July 2026.

New or recompeted awards and continuation awards should be counted separately when describing the current portfolio.

Current Program Reach

ONDCP describes the program as supporting more than 500 coalitions.

For 2025, ONDCP estimated that DFC communities included 62 million residents, 2.4 million middle-school youth, and 3.2 million high-school youth.

Approximately 42,600 community members, including 10,800 youth, were reported as mobilized in prevention activity.

Current Program Focus

Current federal materials emphasize positive youth engagement, community protective factors, abstinence from youth drug use, emerging drug threats, evidence-based strategies, faith and family involvement, and local data.

Coalitions continue to select local priorities within statutory and award requirements.

Administrative Funding

Administrative funds support grant review, federal staff, CDC management, contracts, evaluation, data systems, monitoring, training, and technical assistance.

GAO found that annual appropriations had permitted administrative amounts above the general statutory percentage and recommended greater transparency concerning carryover balances.

Financial Management

Recipients must maintain accounting records, internal controls, source documentation, match records, time-and-effort support, procurement files, and audit trails.

Federal funds and match must be necessary, reasonable, allocable, and connected to the approved coalition plan.

Nonsupplanting Purpose

One statutory purpose is to enhance rather than supplant local prevention initiatives.

Coalitions should use DFC funds to strengthen capacity, coordination, strategy, and community change rather than replace existing nonfederal commitments without justification.

Allowable Costs

Allowable costs can include coalition staff, coordination, assessment, data, training, technical assistance, communication, meetings, youth engagement, evaluation, travel, supplies, contractual services, and implementation of approved prevention strategies.

Each cost remains subject to the funding notice, federal cost principles, award conditions, and local plan.

Restricted and Unallowable Costs

DFC funds generally cannot support lobbying, unlawful discrimination, inherently religious activity, direct cash assistance unrelated to the grant, unsupported incentives, construction, treatment replacing other systems, or costs outside the approved prevention strategy.

Applicants must consult the current funding notice because restrictions can change.

Lobbying Restrictions

Coalitions can educate policymakers and provide evidence concerning substance-use conditions.

Federal funds cannot be used for prohibited lobbying or partisan political activity.

Coalitions should distinguish public education, policy implementation, and allowable technical input from attempts to influence legislation using federal money.

Religious Activity

Faith-based organizations are eligible partners and can serve as fiscal agents when otherwise qualified.

Grant-funded activity must be separate from worship, religious instruction, coercion, or proselytization.

Civil-Rights Compliance

Recipients must comply with federal nondiscrimination law in employment, services, outreach, meetings, data, and grant activity.

Coalition strategy should account for race, ethnicity, disability, language, sex, geography, income, and other factors affecting access and outcomes.

Language Access

Coalitions serving multilingual communities may need translation, interpretation, bilingual outreach, accessible surveys, and culturally competent communication.

English-only activity can exclude parents and youth essential to coalition legitimacy.

Disability Access

Meetings, surveys, communication, websites, youth activities, and services must be accessible to people with disabilities where required.

Prevention planning should include youth with physical, sensory, intellectual, developmental, and mental-health disabilities.

Health Equity

Substance-use risk and prevention access vary by income, race, geography, disability, housing, school resources, trauma, and healthcare access.

Equity analysis can identify whether coalition strategies reach those at greatest risk or primarily serve institutions and populations easiest to engage.

Tribal Communities

Tribal coalitions can address substance use through culturally grounded prevention, language, ceremony, family, elders, youth leadership, healthcare, schools, and tribal government.

Federal implementation must respect tribal sovereignty and distinct funding relationships.

Rural Communities

Rural coalitions can face long distances, small populations, limited treatment, workforce shortages, confidentiality concerns, transportation barriers, and dependence on volunteers.

Special statutory rural authority can provide flexibility, but matching and long-term sustainability remain difficult.

Urban Communities

Urban coalitions can address concentrated retail environments, diverse populations, multiple school systems, neighborhood variation, homelessness, violence, and overlapping government jurisdictions.

Citywide coalitions may need neighborhood-level data and participation.

Youth Privacy

Surveys and coalition activity can involve sensitive information about substance use, family, mental health, school, and justice-system contact.

Coalitions must protect confidentiality, limit identifiable data, obtain required consent, and avoid sharing prevention data for unrelated enforcement.

Parental Consent

State and local rules can require active or passive parental consent for student surveys.

Consent protects family rights but can reduce participation and create nonrepresentative samples when return rates are low.

Cannabis-Legalization Context

DFC’s youth-prevention mission can coexist with state adult-use legalization, medical cannabis, and decriminalization.

Coalitions should distinguish youth use, adult legal use, impaired driving, illicit markets, product safety, public health, and criminal justice.

Abstinence and Harm Reduction

DFC focuses on preventing and reducing youth substance use and reflects a drug-free-youth mission.

Communities also use harm-reduction tools such as naloxone, fentanyl education, safer storage, and treatment referral to prevent death and injury.

Coalition plans should explain how prevention and immediate safety strategies fit together.

Public Health and Law-Enforcement Balance

The twelve-sector structure includes both health and law enforcement.

This can improve coordination but can produce conflict concerning youth discipline, criminalization, confidentiality, cannabis, syringe services, treatment, and overdose response.

Coalitions require clear governance and role boundaries.

Community Participation and Power

Broad membership does not guarantee equal influence.

Agencies controlling staff, data, grants, or legal authority can dominate parents, youth, volunteers, and small community organizations.

Transparent voting, leadership rotation, compensation where allowable, youth authority, and conflict-of-interest rules can improve balance.

Volunteer Burden

DFC depends heavily on volunteer participation and in-kind support.

Matching and reporting requirements can place substantial burden on small communities and organizations with limited grant infrastructure.

Coalition Capacity

Coalition capacity includes leadership, governance, sector participation, data, communication, strategic planning, conflict management, implementation, finance, evaluation, and sustainability.

Capacity is itself a program outcome even when changes in youth use require longer periods to observe.

Leadership and Membership Turnover

Turnover among school staff, law enforcement, healthcare, youth, parents, and coalition employees can weaken relationships and interrupt strategy.

Succession plans, orientation, written procedures, and shared data reduce dependence on one leader.

Time Required for Community Change

Community-level prevention can take years to alter policies, norms, access, institutions, and youth outcomes.

Five- and ten-year grant cycles recognize this long horizon, but annual funding and leadership changes can disrupt continuity.

Changes in Federal Priorities

Although the statutory eligibility criteria are fixed, administrations influence funding notices, terminology, executive-order compliance, priority substances, technical assistance, and grant review.

The fiscal year 2025 recompete demonstrated how federal policy change can delay local prevention funding.

Prohibition on Additional Eligibility Criteria

Federal law prohibits the ONDCP Director from imposing eligibility criteria on new applicants or renewal recipients that are not provided in the DFC statute.

The rule was added in 2006 after concerns about administrative expansion of eligibility requirements.

Suspension and Appeal

A coalition that no longer satisfies eligibility requirements can face suspension or termination.

Federal law requires written notice and a fair, timely, independent appeal before suspension or termination.

Monitoring

CDC and ONDCP monitor applications, sectors, strategy, match, finance, reports, evaluation participation, and award conditions.

Monitoring can include document review, communication, technical assistance, corrective action, suspension, and audit.

GAO’s 2008 Management Review

GAO found weaknesses in internal controls, application review, monitoring, documentation, and grant-making management.

ONDCP and its management partner subsequently strengthened collaboration, controls, and procedures.

GAO’s 2017 Collaboration Review

GAO found improved ONDCP–SAMHSA collaboration but identified weaknesses in compliance and performance monitoring.

The recurring oversight theme is that a decentralized coalition program requires strong federal data and sector-participation controls.

Budget Transparency

Program appropriations include direct grants, administration, evaluation, training, and potentially carryover funding.

GAO recommended clearer disclosure to Congress of administrative balances and intended use.

Fraud and Misuse Risks

Risks include unsupported match, inflated volunteer values, conflicts of interest, duplicate costs, false membership records, unallowable lobbying, personal benefit, and inaccurate performance reports.

Remedies can include corrective action, disallowance, repayment, suspension, termination, debarment, civil action, or criminal prosecution.

Major Achievements

  • Created a stable national funding stream for local youth substance-use coalitions.
  • Required representation from twelve community sectors.
  • Supported coalitions in urban, rural, tribal, territorial, and suburban communities.
  • Provided up to ten years for long-term community change.
  • Required nonfederal matching support and sustainability planning.
  • Promoted local data, strategic planning, and environmental prevention.
  • Built national training and technical-assistance infrastructure.
  • Mobilized tens of thousands of community members and youth.
  • Reached an estimated 62 million residents in 2025.
  • Produced reported declines in youth use across multiple substances among participating coalitions.
  • Created local infrastructure adaptable to opioids, fentanyl, methamphetamine, vaping, and emerging threats.
  • Maintained bipartisan congressional support across multiple administrations.

Limitations and Failures

  • The annual award cap has remained $125,000 since 2006.
  • Matching requirements can disadvantage low-resource communities.
  • Sector membership can be nominal rather than active.
  • Volunteer burden and turnover can weaken sustainability.
  • Most outcome data come from locally varied school surveys.
  • Out-of-school and highly vulnerable youth can be underrepresented.
  • The national evaluation cannot establish causal program impact.
  • Reported perception outcomes are mixed.
  • Coalitions can emphasize information campaigns over stronger environmental strategies.
  • Federal priorities can delay or reshape awards.
  • Mentoring grants have not been funded since 2020.
  • GAO found continuing weaknesses in sector enforcement, methodology, performance data, and budget transparency.

Major Controversies

Effectiveness claims
ONDCP reports favourable trends, while GAO concluded that available data do not support claims that DFC caused the declines.
Drug-free framing
Supporters view abstinence-focused youth prevention as clear and protective; critics argue that messaging can become stigmatizing or disconnected from harm reduction and treatment.
Cannabis policy
Federal youth-prevention policy operates amid state legalization and changing public perceptions.
Law-enforcement participation
Law enforcement contributes essential local knowledge but can create confidentiality and youth-criminalization concerns.
Match burden
High nonfederal match demonstrates commitment but can favour affluent communities and institutions able to document in-kind support.
Community representation
Twelve sectors create breadth, but large agencies can exercise more power than youth, parents, and volunteers.
Federal political change
The 2025 recompete delayed awards to review executive-order compliance and changed the ordinary grant process.
Data privacy
Youth surveys and cross-sector partnerships can expose sensitive information without strong safeguards.

How the DFC Program Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the coalition, fiscal agent, defined community, grant year, funding cycle, match, twelve-sector participation, priority substances, strategies, survey methods, comparison data, policy changes, and outcomes.

Important distinctions include:

  • the coalition versus the fiscal agent;
  • new awards versus continuation awards;
  • years one through five versus years six through ten;
  • federal award amount versus matched total project resources;
  • coalition capacity versus youth substance-use outcomes;
  • reported trends versus causal impact;
  • local survey data versus national survey data;
  • school-enrolled youth versus all youth;
  • prevention versus treatment and recovery;
  • information campaigns versus environmental change;
  • DFC grants versus CARA Local Drug Crises grants;
  • appropriated funding versus administrative and carryover balances;
  • fiscal year 2025 funded coalitions versus fiscal year 2026 recompeted awards; and
  • statutory criteria versus administration-specific policy conditions.

Why the Drug-Free Communities Support Program Matters

DFC is the federal government’s principal long-term investment in local coalition infrastructure for youth substance-use prevention.

Its twelve-sector requirement treats prevention as a community responsibility shared by youth, parents, schools, health care, law enforcement, businesses, media, faith institutions, civic groups, government, and service organizations.

The program’s matching rules and ten-year limit seek to create locally owned institutions rather than permanent federal dependence.

DFC also illustrates the challenge of evaluating comprehensive community initiatives. Reported youth-use trends are favourable, but varied surveys and the absence of strong comparison designs limit claims about causation.

The June 2026 GAO review makes the program an important contemporary case in federal performance measurement, community representation, grant oversight, and evidence-based prevention.

Summary

The Drug-Free Communities Support Program is a federal matching-grant program supporting local coalitions that prevent and reduce youth substance use.

Congress created DFC through the Drug-Free Communities Act of 1997. ONDCP administers the program, and CDC has managed day-to-day grants through an interagency agreement since fiscal year 2020.

Every coalition must include representatives from twelve community sectors, define a community, document local substance-use problems, identify service gaps, develop a long-term strategic plan, maintain nonfederal support, and participate in evaluation.

Coalitions can receive up to $125,000 annually for five years and can compete for an additional five years. Match begins at one-to-one, rises to 125 percent in years six and seven, and rises to 150 percent in years eight through ten.

DFC emphasizes local conditions, risk and protective factors, positive youth engagement, environmental prevention, and seven strategies for community change.

The national evaluation reports significant declines in past-thirty-day alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, and prescription-drug use among participating middle- and high-school populations.

Those findings do not establish causation. In June 2026, GAO found that ONDCP lacked sufficient performance data to claim that DFC itself caused reductions and recommended stronger data, methodology, collaboration measures, sector enforcement, and budget transparency.

Congress provided $109 million in both fiscal years 2025 and 2026. GAO reported 751 funded coalitions in fiscal year 2025.

Following a fiscal year 2025 award pause and recompete, ONDCP published 133 new awards in July 2026. GAO separately reported that 546 continuation grants were expected that month.

DFC remains active as of August 3, 2026. Its central strength is locally tailored, multisector prevention infrastructure; its central challenge is demonstrating causal impact while maintaining broad, equitable, and sustained community participation.
