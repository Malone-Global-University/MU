Section 8 Housing Assistance

The federal rental-assistance framework that subsidizes eligible low-income households in privately owned housing through tenant-based vouchers, project-based contracts, and related housing-assistance programs.

Overview

Section 8 Housing Assistance is the collective name for rental-assistance programs authorized under Section 8 of the United States Housing Act of 1937. Congress created the original Section 8 framework through the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974. The program shifted a substantial part of federal housing policy away from direct public ownership and toward subsidies used in privately owned rental housing.

“Section 8” does not identify one uniform program. It includes several legally and administratively distinct forms of assistance. The largest is the Housing Choice Voucher Program, under which a public housing agency subsidizes an eligible household renting an approved private-market unit. Section 8 also includes Project-Based Vouchers administered by public housing agencies, Project-Based Rental Assistance contracts administered principally through HUD’s Office of Multifamily Housing Programs, legacy Moderate Rehabilitation contracts, and several specialized or preservation-related forms of assistance.

Tenant-based assistance generally follows the household when it moves to another eligible unit. Project-based assistance remains attached to a particular unit or property for the benefit of current and future eligible residents. In either form, the government ordinarily pays a housing assistance payment to the owner while the household pays an income-related share of rent and utilities.

Section 8 assistance has become a central component of the United States housing safety net. It helps millions of households obtain or retain housing, preserves privately owned affordable developments, and supports veterans, families with children, older adults, people with disabilities, young adults leaving foster care, and households displaced by housing conversion or government action.

The program also has significant limitations. Appropriations do not provide assistance to every eligible household; waiting lists may remain closed for years; voucher holders may have difficulty finding participating landlords; payment standards may not match local rents; and racial segregation, source-of-income discrimination, limited mobility, inspection delays, administrative complexity, and geographic inequality can restrict practical housing choice.

Definitions

Section 8
The statutory rental-assistance authority contained in Section 8 of the United States Housing Act of 1937. The term may refer broadly to several tenant-based and project-based programs rather than to one uniform benefit.
Housing Choice Voucher
Tenant-based Section 8 assistance administered by a public housing agency. An eligible household generally selects an approved privately owned unit, and the assistance can ordinarily move with the household.
Tenant-based assistance
Rental assistance assigned to an eligible household rather than permanently attached to one unit. The household may use the assistance in another eligible unit, subject to program rules.
Project-based assistance
Rental assistance attached by contract to specific units or a particular property. When one assisted household moves, the subsidy generally remains with the unit for the next eligible household.
Project-Based Voucher
A component of the Housing Choice Voucher Program through which a public housing agency commits voucher funding to specified units under a housing assistance payments contract. The abbreviation PBV is commonly used.
Project-Based Rental Assistance
Section 8 assistance administered principally by HUD’s Office of Multifamily Housing Programs through contracts between HUD or a contract administrator and a property owner. The abbreviation PBRA is commonly used.
Public housing agency
A state, regional, county, municipal, or other public body authorized to administer Housing Choice Vouchers, Project-Based Vouchers, public housing, or related federal housing programs. The abbreviation PHA is commonly used.
Housing assistance payment
The subsidy payment made to an owner on behalf of an eligible household under a Section 8 contract. The abbreviation HAP is commonly used.
Housing assistance payments contract
The contract establishing the obligations of HUD, a public housing agency, a contract administrator, and a property owner concerning assisted units, payments, inspections, rent, occupancy, and compliance.
Voucher
A document issued by a public housing agency authorizing an eligible household to search for housing under the Housing Choice Voucher Program for a specified period and unit size.
Payment standard
The amount established by a public housing agency as the maximum monthly assistance level used in calculating subsidy for a voucher household before subtracting the household’s required contribution.
Fair market rent
A HUD estimate of gross rent, including tenant-paid utilities, used as a benchmark in administering rental-assistance programs. The abbreviation FMR is commonly used.
Gross rent
The rent payable to the owner plus the public housing agency’s utility allowance for tenant-paid utilities.
Utility allowance
An estimate of reasonable monthly utility expenses paid directly by the household rather than included in the rent charged by the owner.
Total tenant payment
An income-based amount calculated under federal rules and used to determine the household’s share of rent and utilities.
Family rent to owner
The portion of the approved rent paid directly by the assisted household to the property owner.
Rent reasonableness
The requirement that the rent charged for an assisted unit be reasonable in comparison with rents for comparable unassisted units, taking account of location, size, type, quality, amenities, services, and utilities.
Portability
The ability of a Housing Choice Voucher household to use tenant-based assistance outside the jurisdiction of the public housing agency that initially issued the voucher, subject to statutory and administrative rules.
Initial public housing agency
The public housing agency that first issues a voucher to a household seeking to move to another jurisdiction.
Receiving public housing agency
The public housing agency serving the jurisdiction to which a voucher household moves under portability.
Special-purpose voucher
A voucher funded for a defined population or policy objective, such as veterans, families involved with child welfare, non-elderly people with disabilities, or households displaced from assisted housing.
Enhanced voucher
A form of tenant-protection assistance generally provided when specified project-based affordability restrictions end or an eligible preservation event occurs. It can permit qualifying residents to remain in the property despite rents above ordinary voucher payment standards, subject to statutory rules.
Moderate Rehabilitation
A legacy Section 8 program that subsidized privately owned units after an owner completed an approved level of rehabilitation under a project-based contract. New project authority was repealed, but remaining contracts may continue or convert under preservation programs.

Historical Development

Shift from Public Construction to Private-Market Assistance

Before Section 8, the federal government primarily supported low-income renters through public housing and earlier subsidized private-housing programs. By the early 1970s, policymakers sought a system that relied more heavily on existing privately owned housing, reduced the need for direct federal construction, and permitted eligible households to rent from private owners.

The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 created Section 8 as a replacement for several earlier approaches. It authorized HUD to enter into housing assistance payment arrangements covering existing housing, newly constructed housing, substantially rehabilitated housing, and other eligible properties.

Certificates and Project-Based Contracts

The original existing-housing certificate program allowed an eligible household to lease an approved private unit, generally within federal rent limits. At the same time, HUD entered into long-term project-based contracts with owners who constructed, substantially rehabilitated, or operated multifamily housing for eligible residents.

These project-based contracts encouraged private investment by guaranteeing a stream of rental subsidy. They also created long-term federal obligations to renew or restructure contracts so that assisted properties would remain financially viable and affordable.

Creation of the Voucher Model

During the 1980s, federal policy shifted further toward tenant-based vouchers. Unlike certificates, vouchers used a payment standard and allowed a household to rent a unit above that benchmark if the household paid the additional cost, subject to affordability rules at initial occupancy.

Congress ended new authority for Section 8 New Construction and Substantial Rehabilitation in 1983, although properties already developed under those programs remained assisted through existing and renewed contracts.

Merger into Housing Choice Vouchers

The Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998 merged the certificate and voucher programs. The resulting Housing Choice Voucher Program became the standard tenant-based form of Section 8 assistance.

Preservation and Repositioning

As original project-based contracts approached expiration, federal policy increasingly focused on preserving assisted properties, restructuring rents and federally insured debt, preventing displacement, and issuing tenant-protection or enhanced vouchers when assistance ended.

The Rental Assistance Demonstration later permitted eligible public housing and legacy assisted properties to convert to long-term Project-Based Voucher or Project-Based Rental Assistance contracts. This expanded Section 8’s role as a preservation platform for housing originally developed under other federal programs.

Major Forms of Section 8 Assistance

Housing Choice Vouchers

The Housing Choice Voucher Program is the principal tenant-based Section 8 program. HUD allocates funds to public housing agencies, which determine eligibility, maintain waiting lists, issue vouchers, approve units, calculate assistance, inspect housing, and make subsidy payments to participating owners.

The household searches for a unit in the private market. The owner retains ordinary tenant-screening authority, subject to applicable fair-housing, civil-rights, source-of-income, and local laws. The public housing agency separately determines whether the unit, rent, lease, and owner meet program requirements.

Project-Based Vouchers

A public housing agency may use an authorized portion of its voucher funding to enter into long-term contracts covering designated units. Assistance remains attached to those units rather than following the household immediately upon departure.

After satisfying applicable occupancy requirements, a resident of a Project-Based Voucher unit generally has access to a form of continued tenant-based assistance when available, allowing the resident to move without permanently losing subsidy. The timing and availability of that assistance depend on federal rules and public housing agency resources.

Project-Based Rental Assistance

Project-Based Rental Assistance covers privately owned multifamily properties operating under Section 8 contracts administered through HUD’s Office of Multifamily Housing Programs. The contract is generally between HUD or a designated contract administrator and the owner.

PBRA includes properties developed or preserved through several historical Section 8 authorities. Although HUD no longer makes ordinary new commitments under the original New Construction and Substantial Rehabilitation programs, it continues to renew existing contracts and can provide PBRA through authorized preservation or conversion mechanisms.

Moderate Rehabilitation

The Moderate Rehabilitation Program provided project-based assistance for existing privately owned housing requiring limited rehabilitation. Congress repealed authority for new projects, but remaining properties can continue under legacy contracts or convert through the Rental Assistance Demonstration.

Tenant-Protection and Enhanced Vouchers

Tenant-protection vouchers may be issued when public housing is demolished or disposed of, project-based assistance terminates, an owner opts out of a contract, a property undergoes an eligible preservation transaction, or another authorized displacement event occurs.

Enhanced vouchers can permit eligible residents to remain in an affected property while paying an income-based contribution, even when the owner’s new rent exceeds an ordinary payment standard, provided the statutory conditions are met.

Special-Purpose Vouchers

Congress and HUD have created voucher categories directed to particular populations or policy objectives. These generally operate under Housing Choice Voucher rules with additional eligibility, referral, service, or reporting requirements.

How Tenant-Based Assistance Works

Application and Waiting List

A household applies to a public housing agency when its waiting list is open. Because federal funding is limited, agencies commonly receive more eligible applications than they can serve. Agencies may use lotteries, application dates, local preferences, referrals, or combinations of these methods.

Eligibility Determination

The agency verifies income, assets, household composition, citizenship or eligible immigration status, Social Security information where required, and other program factors. Eligibility does not guarantee immediate assistance.

Voucher Issuance

When selected, the household receives a voucher identifying the authorized bedroom size and search period. The household may request an extension under the agency’s policy or a reasonable accommodation where required.

Housing Search

The household searches for a qualifying rental unit. Eligible housing can include apartments, townhouses, duplexes, detached homes, manufactured homes, and other approved housing types. The unit may be located within the issuing agency’s jurisdiction or, through portability, in another participating jurisdiction.

Owner Approval

The owner screens the household under lawful rental criteria and submits the proposed tenancy to the public housing agency. The owner is not an employee or agent of HUD merely because the property participates.

Unit Inspection

The public housing agency determines whether the unit meets applicable physical-condition standards. Deficiencies may require correction before assistance begins or within an authorized period, depending on severity and program rules.

Rent Approval

The agency determines whether the proposed rent is reasonable compared with similar unassisted units. It also calculates the household’s subsidy using the payment standard, gross rent, income, deductions, utility allowance, and applicable affordability limit.

Lease and HAP Contract

The household and owner execute a lease. The public housing agency and owner execute a housing assistance payments contract. The tenancy addendum required by HUD becomes part of the lease and controls when inconsistent lease terms conflict with federal requirements.

Monthly Payments

The household pays its required portion to the owner. The public housing agency pays the housing assistance payment directly to the owner. Failure by either party to comply with program duties can affect continued assistance or owner payments.

Annual and Interim Reviews

The agency periodically reviews household income, assets, deductions, composition, and continued eligibility. Certain changes must be reported between regular reviews. Rent and utility calculations may change when household circumstances or owner rents change.

Representative Voucher Calculation

The following simplified example illustrates the basic relationship among the payment standard, household contribution, rent, utilities, and housing assistance payment. Actual calculations depend on federal rules and public housing agency policy.

  • Monthly adjusted household income: $1,200
  • Approximate income-based contribution: $360
  • Public housing agency payment standard: $1,400
  • Approved rent to owner: $1,300
  • Tenant-paid utility allowance: $100
  • Gross rent: $1,400

In this simplified example, the gross rent equals the payment standard. The household is responsible for an income-based total tenant payment of approximately $360, including responsibility for the estimated $100 in tenant-paid utilities. The public housing agency would pay the remaining approved amount to the owner.

If the household selected a more expensive unit above the payment standard, it could be required to pay the difference. At initial occupancy, federal rules generally restrict the household’s total contribution to no more than 40 percent of adjusted monthly income when the gross rent exceeds the applicable payment standard.

Eligibility, Income Targeting, and Rent

Income Categories

Section 8 programs use local income limits established by HUD and adjusted for household size. Categories include low-income, very low-income, and extremely low-income households.

For Housing Choice Vouchers, federal law generally requires at least 75 percent of newly admitted households in a public housing agency’s fiscal year to be extremely low-income. Project-based programs have related but not always identical targeting rules.

Household Status

Eligibility may depend on whether applicants meet the statutory definition of a family and satisfy citizenship or eligible immigration-status rules. Families can include households with children, households without children, older adults, people with disabilities, displaced persons, and qualifying single persons.

Income and Assets

Public housing agencies and owners verify income and assets under federal rules. Earned income, benefits, pensions, child support, investment income, deductions, exclusions, and asset limitations can affect eligibility and rent.

Tenant Contribution

The income-based calculation generally begins with the highest applicable amount among 30 percent of adjusted monthly income, 10 percent of gross monthly income, a designated welfare rent, or an authorized minimum rent. Program-specific rules, utility allowances, flat deductions, hardship exemptions, and household choices can alter the amount paid to the owner.

Minimum Rent and Hardship

Public housing agencies and owners may impose an authorized minimum rent. Federal law requires hardship procedures for qualifying circumstances, including loss of assistance, impending eviction, income loss, death in the household, or other conditions recognized by the responsible agency or owner.

Over-Income and Termination Rules

Assistance can end when a household no longer requires a subsidy, violates material program obligations, provides false information, engages in specified prohibited conduct, or becomes otherwise ineligible. Termination generally requires notice and an opportunity for review under the procedures applicable to the program.

How Project-Based Assistance Works

Assistance Attached to the Unit

Project-based assistance is committed to designated units under a long-term contract. Eligible tenants occupy the units and pay an income-related share. The owner receives the balance through a housing assistance payment.

Owner and Contract Administrator

Under Project-Based Vouchers, a public housing agency contracts with the owner. Under Project-Based Rental Assistance, HUD or a performance-based contract administrator oversees the owner’s contract, subject to HUD requirements.

Waiting Lists and Referrals

Applicants may enter a property-specific, public housing agency, regional, or centralized waiting list, depending on program type and local administration. Some properties serve designated populations, such as older adults, people with disabilities, or formerly homeless households.

Occupancy and Rent

The owner determines applicant suitability under approved tenant-selection plans and fair-housing requirements. The responsible agency or owner verifies program eligibility, calculates rent, and completes recurring income reviews.

Property Standards

The owner must maintain the assisted units and common areas in accordance with applicable HUD physical-condition standards, the HAP contract, local codes, and other legal obligations. Serious or repeated noncompliance may produce payment abatement, enforcement, contract transfer, foreclosure-related intervention, or termination.

Contract Renewal and Preservation

Long-term affordability depends on renewal funding, owner participation, property finances, mortgage obligations, rent levels, and federal preservation law. HUD may renew, restructure, transfer, or terminate assistance under authorized procedures.

Special-Purpose Voucher Programs

HUD–Veterans Affairs Supportive Housing

HUD–VASH combines Housing Choice Voucher assistance with case management and clinical services provided through the Department of Veterans Affairs for eligible veterans experiencing homelessness.

Family Unification Program

Family Unification Program vouchers assist families for whom inadequate housing is a primary factor in the placement or delayed return of a child, and eligible young adults who have left foster care and face homelessness or housing instability.

Foster Youth to Independence

Foster Youth to Independence provides time-limited voucher assistance and supportive-service coordination for eligible young adults leaving foster care.

Mainstream Vouchers

Mainstream vouchers assist households that include a non-elderly person with a disability. They generally follow ordinary Housing Choice Voucher rules while operating through separately identified funding.

Emergency Housing Vouchers

Emergency Housing Vouchers were funded through the American Rescue Plan Act for households experiencing homelessness, at risk of homelessness, recently homeless, or fleeing domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, stalking, or human trafficking.

Tenant-Protection Vouchers

Tenant-protection vouchers respond to eligible displacement or loss of an existing federal housing subsidy. Depending on the event, they may be tenant-based, project-based, or enhanced.

Non-Elderly Disabled Vouchers

Several historical voucher initiatives have assisted non-elderly people with disabilities who face institutionalization, segregation, or displacement from designated housing.

Landlord Participation and Property Requirements

Voluntary and Mandatory Participation

Federal Section 8 law generally does not require every private landlord to accept tenant-based vouchers. State and local source-of-income laws may prohibit owners from rejecting applicants because they use lawful rental assistance. Other federal, state, or local obligations can also affect participation.

Owner Screening

Owners may screen voucher applicants for tenancy using lawful standards such as rental history, credit, references, and ability to comply with the lease. Screening cannot violate fair-housing, disability, retaliation, or other applicable protections.

Rent Reasonableness

A public housing agency cannot approve a tenant-based rent that exceeds a reasonable amount for comparable unassisted housing. Owners must disclose rents charged for comparable units where required.

Physical Condition

Assisted units must meet HUD physical-condition requirements. Owners are responsible for correcting deficiencies. Tenants remain responsible for damage or violations caused by household members or guests beyond ordinary wear.

Lease Enforcement

The owner enforces the lease and may pursue termination or eviction under applicable law. The public housing agency separately determines whether the household remains eligible for assistance.

Prohibited Payments

An owner may not collect side payments or rent amounts exceeding the approved rent. Unauthorized fees, undisclosed charges, or false claims can lead to repayment, termination, debarment, civil penalties, or criminal enforcement.

Resident Rights and Responsibilities

Rights

Depending on program type and circumstances, Section 8 applicants and participants may possess rights involving:

  • Nondiscrimination under federal civil-rights laws
  • Reasonable accommodations and accessibility protections
  • Written notice of eligibility, denial, termination, and rent decisions
  • An informal review or hearing for specified public housing agency actions
  • Accurate income, deduction, utility, and subsidy calculations
  • Housing meeting applicable physical-condition standards
  • Protection against unauthorized owner charges
  • Portability under the Housing Choice Voucher Program
  • Violence Against Women Act protections for covered survivors
  • Limited confidentiality of sensitive personal information
  • Protection from retaliation for exercising legal rights

Responsibilities

Households generally must:

  • Provide complete and accurate eligibility information
  • Report required changes in income, assets, household composition, and address
  • Use the assisted unit as the household’s principal residence
  • Permit authorized inspections and income reviews
  • Pay the required household portion of rent and utilities
  • Comply with the lease and program family obligations
  • Avoid unauthorized occupants or subleasing
  • Avoid serious or repeated lease violations and specified prohibited conduct
  • Notify the public housing agency before moving or terminating the lease as required

Racial and Civil-Rights Context

Access to Private Housing

Section 8 was partly justified as a means of giving low-income households access to a broader range of privately owned housing than was available through geographically fixed public housing developments. Tenant-based assistance can support movement across neighborhood and jurisdictional boundaries.

Continuing Segregation

Formal portability does not guarantee meaningful housing choice. Voucher households may confront landlord refusal, racial discrimination, source-of-income discrimination, limited payment standards, security-deposit barriers, transportation constraints, weak search assistance, exclusionary zoning, and low vacancy rates.

These barriers can reproduce residential segregation by concentrating voucher use in neighborhoods where rents are lower and owners are more willing to participate. African American voucher holders may experience both racial discrimination and discrimination based on the use of rental assistance.

Mobility Programs

Housing mobility initiatives seek to help families obtain housing in lower-poverty or higher-opportunity neighborhoods through counselling, landlord recruitment, flexible payment standards, security-deposit assistance, search support, regional administration, and post-move services.

Project-Based Preservation

Project-based Section 8 has preserved deeply affordable housing in many high-cost communities. It can also perpetuate geographic concentration when assisted developments are located primarily in racially isolated or high-poverty neighborhoods.

Fair-Housing Enforcement

HUD, the Department of Justice, state and local agencies, private plaintiffs, and fair-housing organizations enforce protections involving race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability, and other classifications recognized by applicable law.

Source-of-Income Protection

The federal Fair Housing Act does not independently establish a general nationwide protected class for voucher status. A growing number of states and local governments prohibit discrimination based on lawful source of income, including Housing Choice Vouchers.

Benefits and Limitations

Housing Affordability

Section 8 reduces the gap between household income and market rent, lowering the risk of eviction, overcrowding, homelessness, and severe rent burden.

Private-Market Choice

Tenant-based vouchers permit households to search among privately owned units and can support moves connected to employment, family, education, healthcare, disability access, or personal safety.

Affordable-Housing Preservation

Project-based contracts supply stable rental revenue that can preserve privately owned developments serving low-income tenants, older adults, and people with disabilities.

Insufficient Funding

Section 8 is not an entitlement for every income-eligible household. Congress appropriates a limited amount, and many qualified applicants receive no assistance.

Waiting Lists

Public housing agency and property waiting lists can be long, periodically closed, or accessible only through short application windows. Applicants may need to apply to multiple agencies or properties.

Voucher Search Failure

A voucher does not guarantee a lease. Households can lose the opportunity if they cannot locate an eligible unit and participating owner within the authorized search period.

Payment Standards and High-Cost Markets

Payment standards may be too low to secure housing in some neighborhoods. Higher standards can improve access but increase per-household subsidy costs and reduce the number of households an agency can assist within a fixed budget.

Administrative Fragmentation

Thousands of public housing agencies and multiple HUD offices administer different parts of Section 8. Local variation can affect application procedures, waiting-list preferences, payment standards, landlord relations, inspection timing, portability, and participant experience.

Owner Exit and Contract Expiration

Project-based affordability can be threatened when contracts expire, owners opt out where legally permitted, properties deteriorate, financing fails, or federal appropriations are uncertain.

Work Disincentive Debate

Because tenant contributions usually rise when income rises, critics argue that rental assistance can reduce the immediate financial benefit of increased earnings. Family Self-Sufficiency escrow accounts and income disregards have been used to reduce this effect for eligible participants.

Key Distinctions

Section 8 and Housing Choice Vouchers

Section 8 is the broader statutory framework. Housing Choice Vouchers are the principal tenant-based component of that framework. The terms are often used interchangeably in everyday speech, but they are not legally identical.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Project-Based Vouchers

A Housing Choice Voucher generally follows the household. A Project-Based Voucher is committed by a public housing agency to designated units and remains attached to those units when a resident moves.

Project-Based Vouchers and Project-Based Rental Assistance

Project-Based Vouchers are part of the Housing Choice Voucher Program and are administered by public housing agencies under 24 C.F.R. part 983. Project-Based Rental Assistance is administered through HUD’s multifamily housing system under separate Section 8 authorities and contracts.

Section 8 and Public Housing

Public housing is generally owned or controlled by a public housing agency and funded through the Public Housing Operating and Capital Funds. Section 8 generally subsidizes occupancy in privately owned housing, although public entities and converted former public housing properties may participate in project-based Section 8 structures.

Section 8 and the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit

Section 8 provides rental subsidy tied to a household or unit. The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit provides tax incentives to finance rent-restricted housing. A tax-credit unit may also receive Section 8 assistance.

Section 8 and Rent Control

Section 8 subsidizes eligible households or units. Rent control regulates permissible rent increases or rent levels for covered private housing under state or local law.

Section 8 and Affordable Housing

Affordable housing is a broad category. It includes Section 8 housing but also public housing, tax-credit developments, nonprofit housing, inclusionary units, cooperative housing, community land trusts, and unsubsidized lower-cost rentals.

Section 8 and Emergency Rental Assistance

Section 8 is generally ongoing rental assistance renewed through annual appropriations. Emergency rental assistance usually provides temporary help with arrears, utilities, or short-term housing instability.

Key Organizations

  • United States Department of Housing and Urban Development

    The federal department responsible for Section 8 funding, regulation, oversight, contract renewal, civil-rights compliance, and national policy.

  • Office of Public and Indian Housing

    The HUD office responsible for the Housing Choice Voucher Program, Project-Based Vouchers, public housing agency oversight, and related special-purpose vouchers.

  • Office of Multifamily Housing Programs

    The HUD office responsible for Project-Based Rental Assistance contracts and the oversight and preservation of assisted multifamily properties.

  • Public Housing Agencies

    State, regional, and local bodies that administer Housing Choice Vouchers, Project-Based Vouchers, waiting lists, inspections, rent calculations, portability, and owner payments.

  • Performance-Based Contract Administrators

    Entities contracted to perform specified Project-Based Rental Assistance oversight functions, including contract administration and property monitoring.

  • United States Department of Veterans Affairs

    The federal partner providing case management and clinical services for HUD–VASH participants.

  • Public Child Welfare Agencies

    State and local agencies that identify and refer eligible families and young adults for Family Unification Program and Foster Youth to Independence assistance.

  • Fair-Housing Organizations

    Public and private organizations that investigate discrimination, educate residents and owners, conduct testing, and pursue administrative or judicial enforcement.

  • Private and Nonprofit Property Owners

    Owners who lease units to voucher households or operate properties under project-based Section 8 contracts.

Geographic Scope

Section 8 assistance operates throughout the United States through local, regional, and state public housing agencies and through HUD-assisted multifamily properties. Programs also operate in the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and other participating United States jurisdictions.

Availability varies considerably. Some areas have multiple housing agencies with separate waiting lists and payment standards. Other areas are served by a statewide or regional agency. Certain rural communities have limited participating rental inventory even when vouchers are available.

Housing Choice Voucher portability permits movement across public housing agency boundaries, but administrative procedures, funding arrangements, search periods, jurisdictional rules, and housing-market conditions can affect the ease of relocation.

Project-based Section 8 properties are fixed in place. Their location can preserve affordability in high-cost communities or reinforce concentration in disadvantaged neighborhoods, depending on historical siting, land markets, zoning, owner participation, and preservation policy.

Legal Context

United States Housing Act of 1937

Section 8 is codified principally at 42 U.S.C. § 1437f. Related provisions governing income, rent, eligibility, public housing agency organization, family obligations, and assisted-housing administration appear elsewhere in the Act.

Housing and Community Development Act of 1974

Section 201 of this law created the Section 8 Housing Assistance Payments Program and established the original tenant-based and project-based framework.

Housing and Urban-Rural Recovery Act of 1983

This statute introduced the federal voucher option and ended new Section 8 commitments for ordinary new-construction and substantial-rehabilitation projects.

Housing and Community Development Act of 1987

This law made the voucher program permanent, expanded tenant-protection assistance, and created or revised preservation mechanisms for federally assisted housing.

Cranston–Gonzalez National Affordable Housing Act of 1990

This law amended Section 8, established the Family Self-Sufficiency Program, expanded housing policy for people with special needs, and created related affordable-housing programs.

Multifamily Assisted Housing Reform and Affordability Act of 1997

This law established the Mark-to-Market framework for restructuring rents and debt at many federally insured properties with project-based Section 8 contracts and governs important contract-renewal and preservation procedures.

Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998

This statute merged the certificate and voucher programs into the Housing Choice Voucher Program, established income-targeting requirements, revised project-based voucher authority, expanded public housing agency flexibility, and made broad changes to assisted housing.

Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act of 2016

HOTMA revised income calculations, asset rules, inspections, project-basing authority, and other requirements affecting Section 8 and public housing.

Fair Housing Act

The Fair Housing Act prohibits specified forms of discrimination in rental housing, advertising, financing, and related housing activity. It applies to most owners and housing providers participating in Section 8.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programs receiving federal financial assistance and applies to HUD-funded public housing agencies and other covered recipients.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination in federally assisted programs and imposes accessibility and reasonable-accommodation obligations on covered housing providers.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Titles II and III of the Americans with Disabilities Act may apply to public housing agencies, program offices, services, public accommodations, and other covered activities connected to Section 8 administration.

Violence Against Women Act

VAWA prohibits covered housing providers from denying or terminating assistance solely because an applicant or tenant is a victim of specified violence and establishes notice, confidentiality, emergency-transfer, and lease-bifurcation protections.

Selected Legal Cases

  • Hills v. Gautreaux (1976): Permitted metropolitan-area relief to remedy federal participation in racially discriminatory public housing administration, including the use of Section 8 opportunities beyond Chicago.
  • Cisneros v. Alpine Ridge Group (1993): Addressed HUD’s interpretation of annual rent-adjustment provisions in Section 8 project-based contracts.
  • Salute v. Stratford Greens Garden Apartments (1998): Held within the Second Circuit that federal disability-discrimination law did not generally require private landlords to participate in the voluntary tenant-based Section 8 program solely as a reasonable accommodation.

Related Concepts

  • Affordable Housing

    Housing maintained at a cost considered affordable to households within defined income ranges.

  • Housing Cost Burden

    The share of household income devoted to rent, utilities, mortgage payments, and related housing expenses.

  • Housing Voucher

    A government rental subsidy assigned to an eligible household for use in qualifying housing.

  • Housing Mobility

    Policies and services that help assisted households obtain housing across a broader range of neighborhoods.

  • Source-of-Income Discrimination

    The denial or unequal treatment of applicants because they use vouchers, benefits, wages, or another lawful source to pay rent.

  • Rent Reasonableness

    The comparison of a proposed assisted rent with rents for similar unassisted housing.

  • Fair Market Rent

    A HUD-calculated rental benchmark used in administering federal housing assistance.

  • Payment Standard

    The local subsidy benchmark used to calculate Housing Choice Voucher assistance.

  • Residential Segregation

    The geographic separation of racial, ethnic, or economic groups through law, discrimination, market practices, and institutional policy.

  • Concentrated Poverty

    The geographic concentration of households experiencing poverty in particular neighborhoods.

  • Project-Based Assistance

    Rental assistance contractually attached to designated housing units or properties.

  • Tenant-Based Assistance

    Rental assistance assigned to a household and ordinarily portable between eligible units.

Summary

Section 8 Housing Assistance is the principal federal statutory system for subsidizing low-income renters in privately owned housing. Created in 1974, it includes tenant-based Housing Choice Vouchers, Project-Based Vouchers, Project-Based Rental Assistance, legacy Moderate Rehabilitation contracts, tenant-protection assistance, and specialized voucher programs.

The system divides responsibility among HUD, public housing agencies, property owners, contract administrators, and assisted households. Government subsidy covers the approved difference between an income-related household contribution and an eligible rent, subject to payment standards, rent reasonableness, inspections, contracts, and continuing eligibility reviews.

Section 8 has provided housing stability and preserved affordable rental properties for millions of households. Its practical effectiveness remains constrained by limited appropriations, long waiting lists, landlord participation, payment-standard adequacy, administrative fragmentation, housing quality, discrimination, and uneven access to high-opportunity neighborhoods.

Understanding Section 8 requires distinguishing the broad statutory framework from the individual programs operating within it. Housing Choice Vouchers are tenant-based; Project-Based Vouchers and Project-Based Rental Assistance are attached to particular units or properties; and each form has distinct administrative, legal, and mobility consequences.
