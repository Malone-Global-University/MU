Works Progress Administration

The principal New Deal work-relief agency that employed millions of unemployed Americans on publicly sponsored construction, infrastructure, education, health, welfare, research, records, and cultural projects from 1935 through 1943.

Overview

The Works Progress Administration was created in 1935 to replace much of the federal government's earlier reliance on direct emergency relief with a national system of publicly useful employment.

Rather than distribute assistance solely through cash or commodities, the WPA assigned unemployed people to approved public projects and paid them wages. Its central policy assumed that work, wages, and the production of public assets were preferable to long-term dependence on a direct-relief “dole.”

The WPA differed from a permanent public-employment service. Workers had to be unemployed, certified as needy, occupationally classified, and assigned to available projects. Their jobs depended on federal appropriations, project approval, local sponsorship, continued eligibility, and the national level of unemployment.

Most WPA employment involved construction and engineering. Workers built or improved roads, bridges, schools, hospitals, airports, sewers, parks, public buildings, flood-control facilities, utilities, and recreation areas.

The agency also employed teachers, librarians, nurses, clerks, researchers, artists, musicians, actors, writers, historians, archaeologists, recreation workers, dietitians, seamstresses, cooks, and household aides. These service and professional projects distinguished the WPA from programs confined to heavy construction.

WPA operations were highly decentralised but federally controlled. State and local governments proposed and sponsored projects, contributed materials or services, and received the completed facilities. The federal agency reviewed proposals, supplied most relief labour costs, enforced eligibility and wage rules, and supervised administration.

The program operated through deep political and social contradictions. It extended federal employment to groups excluded from private jobs, but it worked through local welfare and political systems that often reproduced racial, gender, occupational, citizenship, and regional inequality.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because the fact sheet addresses the administration of work relief: eligibility, certification, wages, assignment, project sponsorship, public construction, community services, cultural employment, racial implementation, wartime conversion, funding, and outcomes.

The Emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1935, annual relief appropriations, federal reorganisation measures, and related executive orders belong primarily under Laws and Legislation.

The Works Progress Administration as a federal agency may also be cross-referenced under Institutions. The Great Depression and the New Deal belong under Historical Events and Concepts and Terminology.

The WPA must be distinguished from the Public Works Administration. The WPA directly administered work-relief projects designed to employ people certified as needy. The Public Works Administration financed larger construction through grants, loans, and contracts and generally relied on ordinary construction firms and labour markets.

The National Youth Administration was initially created within the WPA but became administratively separate in 1939. It should receive a separate government-program page because it served students and young adults through distinct aid, work, and training systems.

Historical Context

The Great Depression produced prolonged unemployment, bank failures, business collapse, mortgage distress, reduced public revenue, hunger, homelessness, and pressure on local charities and governments.

Before the New Deal, relief responsibility rested principally with families, private charities, municipalities, counties, and states. The scale of the Depression exceeded their financial and administrative capacity.

The Roosevelt administration created the Federal Emergency Relief Administration in 1933 to distribute federal grants for direct and work relief. It also created the Civil Works Administration for rapid winter employment and the Public Works Administration for larger capital construction.

By 1935, unemployment remained extremely high. The administration concluded that a longer-term federal work program was necessary for unemployed people whom private industry had not rehired.

The Emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1935 provided nearly $4.9 billion for a broad federal works program. Roosevelt then established the WPA to manage the principal nationwide work-relief component.

Program Purpose

Provide Work Instead of Direct Relief

WPA employment was designed to preserve work habits, dignity, occupational experience, and household income while reducing dependence on direct relief.

Support Families

Security wages were intended to sustain a worker's household, not merely compensate an isolated individual.

Create Public Assets

The program converted federal relief expenditures into roads, schools, airports, parks, hospitals, utilities, records, cultural works, and public services.

Employ Diverse Occupations

The WPA attempted to match project assignments to workers' established occupations and skills rather than place every unemployed person in manual construction.

Strengthen State and Local Government

Sponsors gained facilities, surveys, planning data, libraries, health services, school programs, and records that many communities could not otherwise afford.

Stabilise Communities

Local wages supported consumer spending and reduced the social instability associated with mass unemployment.

Prepare Workers for Private and Wartime Employment

Training, work experience, and later defence programs sought to move WPA workers into private industry, military support, and war production.

Legal and Executive Authority

Emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1935

Approved on April 8, 1935, the act appropriated emergency funds to the president for work relief, public works, rural rehabilitation, and related purposes. It provided the fiscal authority from which the WPA emerged.

Executive Order 7034

Issued on May 6, 1935, Executive Order 7034 established the Works Progress Administration and designated Harry Hopkins as administrator.

Executive Order 7046

Issued on May 20, 1935, this order established rules concerning wages, hours, and employment conditions under the emergency appropriation.

Executive Order 7060

Issued on June 5, 1935, this order addressed worker eligibility, relief certification, registration, referral, and assignment procedures.

Annual Emergency Relief Appropriation Acts

Congress continued, modified, and restricted the WPA through annual funding laws. These measures changed citizenship rules, sponsor contributions, work hours, continuous-employment limits, political-activity requirements, and other operating conditions.

Reorganisation Plan No. I of 1939

Federal reorganisation placed the agency within the Federal Works Agency and changed its name from Works Progress Administration to Work Projects Administration, effective July 1, 1939.

Hatch Act and Relief-Act Political Restrictions

Federal law restricted the use of WPA authority and employment for partisan political purposes and established investigative duties concerning prohibited political activity.

Wartime Appropriations and Directives

Later legislation and executive policy redirected projects toward national defence, vocational training, military facilities, airports, war services, childcare, nutrition, and the transfer of workers to essential employment.

Administrative Development

National Office

The central administration issued regulations, allocated employment and funds, reviewed major policy, coordinated federal agencies, supervised states, collected statistics, investigated complaints, and reported to the president and Congress.

Regional Offices

Regional structures coordinated groups of states, reviewed operations, communicated national policy, and addressed problems extending across state boundaries.

State Administrations

Each state WPA administration reviewed project proposals, allocated workers, supervised districts, maintained financial records, and coordinated with governors, welfare agencies, and sponsors.

District Offices

District personnel classified workers, made assignments, monitored projects, processed payrolls, heard complaints, and maintained relationships with local sponsors.

Project Administration

A project superintendent, foremen, timekeepers, safety personnel, clerks, and technical employees managed daily work. Some supervisory and specialised workers were hired without relief certification when required skills were unavailable among certified workers.

Federal Works Agency

After 1939, the WPA operated within the Federal Works Agency, which coordinated several major federal construction and public-facility institutions.

Harry Hopkins and Program Leadership

Harry Hopkins was a social worker and emergency-relief administrator who had directed the Federal Emergency Relief Administration before becoming the first WPA administrator.

Hopkins emphasised rapid employment, federal responsibility, administrative flexibility, and practical results. He rejected the claim that unemployed people should receive assistance without work when useful public projects could be organised.

His administration attempted to move millions of people from state relief rolls onto federal projects within months. The speed of this mobilisation produced administrative innovation but also uneven planning, supply delays, political conflict, and variation among states.

Francis C. Harrington succeeded Hopkins as commissioner during the 1939 reorganisation. Howard O. Hunter later directed the agency during the wartime transition and liquidation.

Worker Eligibility

WPA project workers were generally required to be both unemployed and certified as economically needy.

Eligibility rules commonly considered:

  • family income and resources;
  • employability and physical capacity;
  • current unemployment;
  • registration with the United States Employment Service;
  • residence within the relevant jurisdiction;
  • citizenship or later statutory alien restrictions;
  • whether another family member held WPA employment;
  • receipt of other public benefits;
  • occupational classification and project availability; and
  • special restrictions imposed by annual appropriations.

Local and state welfare agencies certified need and referred eligible people. This system allowed the WPA to use existing local knowledge but also exposed applicants to inconsistent standards, political influence, racial discrimination, and administrative delay.

Eligibility did not create an immediate entitlement to a job. A certified worker could remain unassigned when no suitable project, occupational opening, or appropriation was available.

One-Worker-per-Family Rule

WPA employment was ordinarily limited to one worker in a family. Officials expected one security wage to support the entire household.

Heads of families generally received preference in assignments. In practice, prevailing assumptions frequently treated men as the primary household workers even when women were unemployed, widowed, separated, or solely responsible for dependents.

The rule restricted the number of jobs available to households containing several unemployed adults. It also made disputes over family status, separation, dependency, and residence important to eligibility decisions.

Single people could qualify, but administrators scrutinised whether a young unmarried adult belonged to a larger family unit or had established an independent household.

Certification and Assignment Process

  1. Application for public assistance. The individual or household applied through a local welfare, relief, or employment office.
  2. Investigation of need. Officials examined income, property, dependents, employment, benefits, and household circumstances.
  3. Certification. The welfare agency certified that the applicant met federal and local need standards.
  4. Employment registration. The applicant registered with the designated employment service or WPA office.
  5. Occupational classification. Officials classified the worker as unskilled, intermediate, skilled, professional, technical, clerical, service, or another occupational category.
  6. Project referral. The district office matched the worker to an available project within reasonable travel distance when possible.
  7. Project assignment. The worker reported to the project, completed employment records, and entered the payroll system.
  8. Periodic review. Continued employment depended on need, conduct, project status, funding, work availability, and later statutory time limits.
  9. Separation or reassignment. A worker could leave for private employment, be dismissed, complete a project, transfer, or return to the pool awaiting another assignment.

Project Sponsorship

Most WPA projects originated with a public sponsor rather than with an individual worker or private commercial firm.

Sponsors included:

  • state departments and commissions;
  • counties and municipalities;
  • school districts and public universities;
  • public hospitals and health departments;
  • libraries, museums, parks, and recreation departments;
  • federal departments and agencies;
  • public authorities and special districts; and
  • approved nonprofit institutions acting through legally authorised arrangements.

A sponsor submitted a proposal describing the public need, location, design, labour requirements, materials, equipment, cost, schedule, and contribution.

The WPA evaluated whether the project was useful, feasible, safe, legally sponsored, sufficiently labour-intensive, and unlikely to compete improperly with private business or displace regular public employees.

Sponsors commonly supplied land, plans, engineering, heavy equipment, materials, transportation, technical staff, or cash. The federal government supplied most wages for certified project workers.

Sponsor contributions varied by project and state. Beginning in 1939, federal law required sponsor contributions to average at least 25 percent nationally, increasing pressure on financially weak communities.

Project Selection Principles

WPA administrators generally sought projects that:

  • served a recognised public purpose;
  • could begin within a reasonable period;
  • employed significant numbers of certified workers;
  • matched the occupations of available unemployed people;
  • used local materials where practical;
  • could be completed or divided into usable units;
  • did not replace ordinary maintenance or established public employees;
  • did not produce goods for ordinary commercial sale;
  • did not provide an improper private benefit;
  • met engineering, legal, financial, and safety requirements; and
  • included an adequate sponsor contribution.

These principles sometimes conflicted. A project designed to maximise labour could cost more or proceed more slowly than contractor-led construction using heavy machinery. A project best suited to local unemployed occupations might not be the sponsor's highest infrastructure priority.

Security Wages

The WPA did not ordinarily pay a single national wage or the full private-market wage for every occupation. It used monthly security-wage schedules.

Wage levels varied according to:

  • occupational skill classification;
  • regional wage conditions;
  • the population size of the worker's community;
  • period-specific federal regulations; and
  • whether the project received a defence or wartime exemption.

Early policy combined fixed monthly earnings with prevailing hourly rates. Skilled workers in areas with high prevailing rates could therefore complete their monthly wage in fewer hours than unskilled workers.

Congress required a generally uniform 130-hour work month after August 31, 1939. This policy abandoned much of the earlier prevailing-hourly-rate approach while retaining security-wage ceilings.

WPA wages were intended to be high enough to provide basic household support but low enough not to draw workers away from ordinary private employment. In practice, many families regarded the payments as inadequate, particularly in high-cost cities or large households.

Hours and Conditions of Employment

Work schedules depended on the period, occupation, project, weather, and wage regulations.

After the 1939 statutory change, project workers generally worked up to 130 hours per month, with daily and weekly limits. Defence and war projects later received authority for additional hours and earnings.

WPA workers were protected by federal safety programs, compensation systems, grievance procedures, and policies prohibiting discrimination based on race, religion, political affiliation, union membership, or union activity.

Workers retained the right to join or decline to join labour organisations. Unions and worker committees presented grievances concerning wages, hours, assignments, dismissals, discrimination, and working conditions.

The WPA was not a permanent civil-service employer. Project workers could be released when appropriations declined, projects ended, eligibility changed, or Congress imposed separation requirements.

The Eighteen-Month Separation Rule

The Emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1939 required most relief workers who had been continuously employed by the WPA for eighteen months or longer to be removed from project employment.

Veterans and certain other categories received exemptions. Dismissed workers could seek recertification and reassignment after a waiting period if they remained needy and otherwise eligible.

Nearly 783,000 workers were dismissed in July and August 1939 under the provision. A federal survey of dismissed urban workers found that only a small proportion had secured private employment soon afterward.

The rule demonstrated a conflict between the view that WPA jobs should be temporary and the reality that private labour markets could not absorb many older, less-skilled, disabled, racially excluded, or geographically isolated workers.

Construction and Engineering Projects

Construction and engineering accounted for most WPA employment, expenditures, and physical output.

Roads, Streets, and Bridges

Workers built and improved rural farm-to-market roads, urban streets, highways, curbs, gutters, drainage, culverts, guardrails, retaining walls, bridges, and viaducts.

The final federal report credited the WPA with construction or improvement of approximately 572,000 miles of rural roads, construction of about 78,000 new bridges and viaducts, and improvement of more than 46,000 existing structures.

Public Buildings

Projects built or improved schools, city halls, firehouses, courthouses, armories, hospitals, libraries, dormitories, community buildings, administrative offices, warehouses, and public institutions.

WPA workers constructed nearly 40,000 public buildings and improved more than 85,000 existing structures. These totals included more than 5,900 new school buildings, more than 2,170 additions, and approximately 31,000 renovated or modernised schools.

Airports and Airways

Workers constructed new airports, extended runways, graded and drained fields, built hangars, installed lighting, and improved airway facilities. During the entire program, the WPA constructed more than 480 airports, improved approximately 470, and built about 900 miles of runways.

Water, Sewer, and Sanitation

Projects installed or repaired water mains, sewers, treatment facilities, wells, drainage, disposal systems, public toilets, and sanitation infrastructure.

Flood and Water Control

Workers built levees, drainage channels, dams, reservoirs, erosion structures, stream improvements, and flood-protection facilities.

Parks and Recreation

Projects created playgrounds, athletic fields, swimming pools, beaches, park roads, trails, landscaping, recreation centres, auditoriums, gymnasiums, picnic facilities, and public campgrounds.

Conservation and Natural-Resource Work

WPA conservation projects supplemented the work of the Civilian Conservation Corps, Forest Service, Soil Conservation Service, state forestry agencies, and local governments.

Work included:

  • planting approximately 177 million trees in publicly owned forests;
  • constructing or improving more than 7,000 miles of firebreaks;
  • building or repairing ranger stations and lookout towers;
  • sealing abandoned mines to reduce hazards and water pollution;
  • controlling soil erosion through terraces, contour work, check dams, and vegetation;
  • building or expanding fish hatcheries;
  • restoring oyster beds;
  • protecting wildlife habitat;
  • controlling insects, plant disease, weeds, and pests under period conservation policies; and
  • conducting surveys and mapping for future land and water projects.

Some WPA environmental practices reflected the ecological knowledge and assumptions of the 1930s and would be modified under later conservation science.

Community and Public-Service Projects

Service projects employed people whose backgrounds did not fit heavy construction and supplied public services that communities could not otherwise finance.

Adult Education

Unemployed teachers conducted literacy, citizenship, vocational, homemaking, workers' education, correspondence, public-affairs, and general academic classes.

Nursery Schools

WPA nursery schools employed teachers, nurses, nutritionists, cooks, clerks, and custodians while providing meals, health supervision, rest, play, and education for young children.

Libraries

Library workers catalogued books, operated rural bookmobiles and travelling libraries, extended service into underserved counties, repaired materials, and helped establish permanent public-library systems.

Public Recreation

Recreation projects employed instructors, coaches, musicians, crafts workers, playground leaders, and organisers to operate sports, arts, camps, clubs, and community events.

Public Health

Nurses, clerks, technicians, and health aides supported vaccination, maternal and child health, tuberculosis work, sanitation, clinics, surveys, and public-health education.

Welfare and Household Assistance

Housekeeping aides provided emergency support in homes where illness or another crisis prevented the usual household work. Federal totals recorded more than 32 million home visits.

Museum and Records Work

Workers classified collections, prepared exhibits, constructed models, indexed historical and archaeological materials, surveyed government records, and preserved documentary resources.

Research and Statistical Projects

Professional and clerical workers conducted economic, labour, municipal, land-use, population, consumer, health, and planning studies for public agencies.

Sewing, Goods Production, and Household Projects

Sewing rooms became one of the largest sources of WPA employment for women. Workers produced clothing, bedding, hospital linens, school garments, and other goods for people receiving relief and for tax-supported institutions.

Goods were marked as WPA products and restricted from ordinary commercial sale. These rules sought to prevent federal relief production from competing with private manufacturers.

Other projects manufactured furniture, mattresses, toys, household goods, school materials, hospital supplies, and equipment needed by public agencies.

Critics argued that sewing reinforced traditional gender roles and provided limited pathways into better-paid private employment. Supporters emphasised that it created paid work for women whom local labour markets and welfare systems often excluded.

School Lunch, Gardening, and Food Preservation

WPA workers prepared and served meals to undernourished children through public and, in some localities, parochial schools.

The final federal report credited WPA workers with preparing more than 1.237 billion school lunches during the program's existence.

Food-production projects operated gardens, farms, kitchens, and canneries. Workers preserved nearly 85 million quarts of food and millions of pounds through drying and other methods.

The program demonstrated the educational and health value of regular school meals and helped establish administrative practices later incorporated into permanent school-lunch systems.

Federal Project Number One

Federal Project Number One, commonly called Federal One, was established in July 1935 to provide nationally coordinated employment for unemployed artists, musicians, actors, directors, writers, historians, archivists, and other cultural workers.

Unlike ordinary locally sponsored projects, Federal One used central federal sponsorship because many communities had little precedent for financing arts and cultural services.

Federal Art Project

Artists created murals, paintings, sculpture, prints, posters, photographs, community art centres, design work, art education, and indexes of American design.

Federal Music Project

Musicians performed orchestral, band, choral, chamber, opera, folk, and educational programs and provided music instruction.

Federal Theatre Project

Theatre workers produced classical, experimental, children's, regional, Black, immigrant-language, dance, circus, and “Living Newspaper” productions.

Federal Writers' Project

Writers and researchers prepared state and city guides, local histories, folklore, interviews, ethnic studies, travel materials, and the narratives commonly known as the WPA slave narratives.

Historical Records Survey

Workers inventoried, indexed, described, and preserved federal, state, county, municipal, church, and other historical records.

Federal One attracted controversy over cost, political content, race, labour organising, artistic experimentation, and alleged communist influence. Congress ended central Federal One sponsorship in 1939. The Federal Theatre Project was terminated, while several other cultural programs continued on reduced state-sponsored bases.

African American Cultural and Historical Work

WPA arts, writing, theatre, photography, education, and records projects created important professional opportunities for Black cultural workers.

African American writers and interviewers documented formerly enslaved people, folklore, work, religion, migration, neighbourhoods, music, family life, and regional culture.

Black theatre units staged works by and about African Americans and trained actors, directors, designers, musicians, and technical workers.

Artists created murals, posters, prints, sculpture, illustrations, and community art programs that depicted Black history and contemporary life.

These opportunities did not eliminate discrimination. Black professionals could be restricted to racially designated units, denied leadership, assigned to segregated facilities, or excluded from projects controlled by white sponsors.

Women in the WPA

Women obtained WPA employment as seamstresses, teachers, librarians, nurses, clerks, researchers, artists, writers, actors, musicians, cooks, dietitians, recreation workers, household aides, and health workers.

Through June 1940, women generally formed between 12 and 18 percent of WPA project employment, below their share of the national labour force.

In April 1938, approximately 56 percent of women workers were assigned to sewing and other goods-production projects, 41 percent to white-collar projects, and only 3 percent to construction or miscellaneous work.

Gendered occupational assignment reproduced existing labour-market divisions. Women with teaching, nursing, library, clerical, or artistic experience sometimes received suitable professional work, but local project shortages could place trained women in sewing rooms.

The one-worker-per-family rule, preference for household heads, public assumptions about male breadwinners, and the concentration of women in lower-paid occupations restricted access.

Women became an increasing share of the remaining WPA rolls during wartime because men left more rapidly for private industry and military service. Women constituted nearly 27 percent of employment in June 1942 and approximately 41 percent of the greatly reduced rolls in December 1942.

African American Participation

African Americans entered the Depression with fewer savings, higher unemployment, lower wages, restricted occupations, segregated public services, and widespread discrimination in private employment.

WPA employment became especially important because public jobs were sometimes available when white employers, unions, or customers excluded Black workers.

In selected national surveys, Black workers accounted for roughly 14 to 18 percent of certified WPA employment, substantially exceeding the African American share of the national population.

The proportion increased during later years partly because Black workers encountered greater difficulty leaving WPA employment for private jobs, including during the early defence expansion.

Black workers built roads, schools, hospitals, parks, airports, public housing improvements, drainage, utilities, and community facilities. They also worked as teachers, nurses, writers, artists, musicians, actors, researchers, librarians, clerks, cooks, seamstresses, and recreation workers.

WPA employment supplied household income, strengthened Black professional networks, and created records of African American history and culture that remain major research collections.

Racial Discrimination and Segregation

Certification and Referral

State and local welfare agencies could discriminate when investigating need, certifying applicants, classifying occupations, or referring workers.

Segregated Projects and Crews

In the Jim Crow South, construction crews, sewing rooms, schools, recreation facilities, hospitals, and service projects frequently operated on segregated bases.

Unequal Occupational Classification

Skilled and professional Black applicants could be classified according to lower-status occupations or assigned to manual and service work despite relevant experience.

Regional Wage Inequality

Security wages varied by region and community size. Lower Southern schedules disproportionately affected Black workers concentrated in that region.

Discriminatory Public Assets

WPA workers sometimes constructed segregated schools, parks, hospitals, recreation areas, and public facilities. A federally financed project could therefore expand public infrastructure while reproducing unequal access.

Limited Leadership

Black workers and professionals encountered restrictions in supervisory, administrative, and technical positions.

Federal Policy and Enforcement

WPA rules formally prohibited discrimination based on race. Enforcement depended on complaints, investigation, federal willingness to confront local authorities, and the practical availability of alternative assignments.

Political Advocacy

Black newspapers, civil-rights organisations, labour groups, community leaders, and the informal Federal Council on Negro Affairs pressed the administration for more equitable participation.

Citizenship and Alien Restrictions

The 1935 emergency appropriation did not initially impose a general citizenship requirement.

Congress progressively restricted noncitizen participation. The 1936 law prohibited knowing employment of people unlawfully present in the United States. The 1937 law limited employment to certain legally present noncitizens who had applied for citizenship.

Beginning with the 1939 appropriation, Congress prohibited WPA employment of aliens. Workers were required to execute citizenship affidavits.

Later wartime appropriations required affidavits concerning citizenship and membership in specified political organisations. These measures expanded federal investigation and could generate false, malicious, or politically motivated accusations.

At the same time, WPA adult-education projects provided English, literacy, naturalisation, and citizenship instruction to immigrants and refugees.

Disability, Age, and Employability

WPA policy focused on people classified as employable, but employability was broader than capacity for heavy manual labour.

Older workers and people with disabilities could receive assignments in clerical work, sewing, research, records, arts, libraries, education, workshops, or specially supervised projects.

Availability differed by locality. Communities with few service or professional projects could leave nonconstruction workers without suitable assignments.

Older, disabled, and long-term unemployed people experienced particular difficulty moving into private jobs. The eighteen-month separation rule exposed this group to renewed unemployment even when need continued.

Labour Relations and Worker Protest

WPA workers organised through unions, unemployed councils, project committees, professional associations, and local worker groups.

Disputes involved security wages, prevailing rates, the 130-hour month, dismissals, project safety, discrimination, political coercion, transportation, occupational assignment, and the eighteen-month rule.

Federal policy protected the right to join or decline to join labour organisations and prohibited discrimination based on union membership or activity.

Organised labour sometimes supported WPA employment because it reduced desperation and preserved wage standards. Unions also feared that low-paid federal projects could compete with private labour or replace regular government employees.

The WPA sought to balance relief employment with prevailing labour standards by limiting monthly earnings, using sponsor-paid union labour for certain skilled tasks, and restricting projects that displaced ordinary employment.

Politics, Patronage, and Investigation

Because the WPA controlled millions of jobs and billions of dollars, opponents charged that the administration used employment to reward political supporters and build Democratic electoral power.

Project selection and worker certification involved federal, state, and local officials, creating opportunities for political pressure, favouritism, retaliation, and misuse of public resources.

The agency established investigative units to address fraud, payroll abuse, theft, sponsor misconduct, political coercion, false eligibility claims, discrimination complaints, and statutory violations.

Congress imposed increasingly strict political-activity rules, including provisions associated with the Hatch Act.

Political allegations varied in credibility. Some documented real abuses; others formed part of broader ideological opposition to federal relief, labour organisation, arts programs, and the New Deal.

Difference from the Public Works Administration

Works Progress Administration
Directly administered labour-intensive work-relief projects and employed people certified as unemployed and needy.
Public Works Administration
Financed larger capital projects through federal construction, grants, loans, and contracts using ordinary contractors and wage labour.
WPA priority
Put unemployed people to work rapidly while producing useful public facilities and services.
PWA priority
Stimulate heavy industry and build major long-term infrastructure through planned capital investment.
Typical WPA project
Local road improvement, school renovation, sewer installation, park construction, sewing room, library service, or records survey.
Typical PWA project
Major dam, power plant, hospital, courthouse, large school, water system, bridge, or other contract-built public facility.

Relationship to the National Youth Administration

President Roosevelt established the National Youth Administration within the WPA on June 26, 1935.

The NYA provided student aid, part-time work, out-of-school youth employment, guidance, and vocational training. It served younger people through rules and project structures distinct from ordinary WPA relief employment.

The NYA separated from the WPA during the 1939 federal reorganisation and later moved into the Federal Security Agency and War Manpower Commission.

NYA participation and accomplishments should not be included automatically in WPA totals unless the source explicitly treats them as part of the broader federal works program.

Emergency and Disaster Response

WPA workers responded to floods, hurricanes, drought, fires, blizzards, earthquakes, epidemics, and other emergencies.

Emergency work included:

  • constructing and reinforcing levees;
  • filling and placing sandbags;
  • evacuating residents and livestock;
  • operating shelters and kitchens;
  • clearing debris and reopening roads;
  • repairing water, sewer, telephone, and electrical systems;
  • providing nursing and public-health assistance;
  • restoring public buildings and schools;
  • fighting forest and urban fires; and
  • conducting damage surveys and records work.

Emergency projects could be approved and operated under modified procedures when ordinary planning requirements would have delayed urgent action.

Safety and Workers' Compensation

Construction, transportation, forestry, quarry, flood, airport, and machinery projects exposed workers to serious hazards.

The WPA developed a national safety system involving inspections, accident reporting, protective equipment, first aid, fire drills, training, engineering review, and investigation.

Indoor projects were inspected for fire exits, ventilation, lighting, sanitation, floor loading, and other hazards.

Injured project workers could receive federal employees' compensation under applicable procedures. Administration of claims required coordination among project offices, state finance divisions, medical personnel, and the United States Employees' Compensation Commission.

Federal reports regarded the WPA's accident-prevention performance as a major administrative achievement, although injuries and fatalities continued across a workforce performing billions of hours of labour.

National Defence and Wartime Conversion

Beginning in 1940, the WPA redirected an increasing share of construction, training, research, and service work toward national defence.

Defence-related work included:

  • airports, runways, hangars, and airway facilities;
  • access roads to military bases and defence plants;
  • water, sewer, housing, and utilities in defence communities;
  • construction and renovation at Army and Navy installations;
  • maps, surveys, records, and clerical support;
  • vocational training for aircraft, shipbuilding, machining, welding, radio, and other essential occupations;
  • nursery schools for children of military and war-production families;
  • school lunches, health, recreation, and library services in rapidly expanding defence areas;
  • war-information and civilian-defence activities; and
  • transfer and placement of WPA workers in private war industry.

Between July 1940 and June 1943, WPA projects constructed 215 airports and reconditioned 160 as part of the defence and war effort.

As private and military demand expanded, younger and more employable workers left the WPA. Remaining rolls increasingly included women, older workers, people with disabilities, and workers facing racial or occupational barriers.

Liquidation and Termination

By 1942, war industries and the armed forces had absorbed millions of workers. WPA employment fell from millions to fewer than 700,000 in June 1942 and continued declining.

On December 4, 1942, President Roosevelt agreed that remaining WPA project operations should be liquidated as rapidly as possible.

Construction and training projects closed across the states during early 1943. Normal project operations ended by April 30.

The WPA officially ceased to exist on June 30, 1943. A liquidation division within the Federal Works Agency handled remaining property, contracts, records, claims, payrolls, and administrative obligations.

Some services—especially nursery schools, school lunches, libraries, and war-community programs—continued under local government, other federal appropriations, or successor agencies.

Program Scale and Selected Outputs

The WPA's final report and later federal summaries document a program of exceptional scale.

  • approximately 8.5 million different individuals employed;
  • peak annual-average employment exceeding three million workers;
  • approximately 18.8 billion hours of work performed on WPA-operated projects;
  • approximately $10.75 billion in federal WPA expenditures;
  • approximately $2.84 billion contributed by sponsors;
  • approximately 572,000 miles of rural roads constructed or improved;
  • approximately 78,000 new bridges and viaducts constructed;
  • more than 46,000 bridges and viaducts improved;
  • nearly 40,000 public buildings constructed;
  • more than 85,000 public buildings improved;
  • more than 480 airports constructed and approximately 470 improved;
  • approximately 177 million trees planted;
  • more than 1.237 billion school lunches served;
  • nearly 85 million quarts of food canned;
  • more than 32 million housekeeping-aide visits made; and
  • thousands of murals, performances, publications, record inventories, interviews, maps, posters, and cultural works produced.

These figures use different units and definitions. A project approval, operating project, project unit, structure, improvement, worker, assignment, and work year are not interchangeable measures.

Financing and Cost Sharing

Congress appropriated emergency-relief funds to the president, who allocated funds among the WPA and other federal work agencies.

The WPA's federal money paid the majority of relief-worker wages and portions of administration, materials, equipment, and specialised costs.

Sponsors contributed land, buildings, equipment, materials, engineering, professional services, transportation, and cash. Their contributions totalled approximately $2.84 billion.

The final federal report calculated approximately $10.14 billion in federal expenditure on WPA-operated project work, apart from administrative and other costs.

Administrative expenses remained a relatively small share of total expenditures. At peak operations, the final report placed administration at approximately 3.4 percent of WPA-operated program spending.

Cost-sharing requirements could disadvantage poor rural counties, cities with collapsed tax bases, and communities serving large low-income populations because they possessed fewer resources for equipment and materials.

Program Achievements

  • It provided wages to approximately 8.5 million unemployed people.
  • It supported millions of family members during prolonged economic crisis.
  • It produced extensive roads, bridges, airports, schools, hospitals, parks, utilities, and public buildings.
  • It brought public facilities to rural and low-income communities that had lacked them.
  • It employed manual, skilled, clerical, professional, educational, cultural, and service workers.
  • It expanded school lunches, nursery schools, libraries, health services, recreation, and adult education.
  • It preserved oral histories, government records, folklore, architecture, art, music, theatre, and local history.
  • It provided employment opportunities to African Americans and women when private discrimination remained severe.
  • It built federal, state, and local administrative capacity for project planning, public employment, safety, procurement, and records.
  • It supplied labour and training for national defence and wartime mobilisation.
  • It demonstrated that federal employment policy could be used to counter mass unemployment directly.

Limitations and Failures

  • The program did not provide employment to every unemployed or needy person.
  • Security wages were often inadequate for large families or high-cost communities.
  • Eligibility certification varied among state and local welfare systems.
  • The one-worker-per-family rule restricted household access and frequently favoured male workers.
  • Racial discrimination persisted in certification, occupation, pay region, assignment, supervision, and access to completed facilities.
  • Women were concentrated in sewing, service, and lower-status work.
  • Noncitizens were progressively excluded.
  • The eighteen-month separation rule removed workers who often could not secure private employment.
  • Local sponsor requirements produced geographic inequality.
  • Some projects were poorly planned, delayed, politically influenced, or left incomplete.
  • Work relief could not substitute for a fully recovered private economy or permanent social insurance.
  • Federal One and other programs were vulnerable to ideological attacks and congressional termination.
  • Some public assets reinforced segregated education, recreation, healthcare, and housing systems.
  • The program ended when wartime labour demand rose rather than through creation of a permanent employment guarantee.

Major Controversies

“Boondoggling”

Critics used the term to portray projects as wasteful, unnecessary, slow, or designed primarily to keep workers occupied. Supporters pointed to the enduring infrastructure and services produced.

Competition with Private Enterprise

Businesses objected when WPA production or services appeared to compete with commercial markets. The agency restricted commercial sale and closed or modified some projects after complaints.

Wage Competition

Employers argued that relief wages could discourage private hiring, while workers and unions argued that low security wages depressed labour standards.

Political Patronage

Opponents alleged that jobs were conditioned on Democratic support. Investigations documented some abuses but did not establish that every WPA assignment functioned as partisan patronage.

Federal One

Arts projects attracted criticism over public spending, race, labour organising, political content, experimental theatre, and alleged communist participation.

Federal versus Local Authority

State and local officials sought control over employment and projects, while the Roosevelt administration argued that direct federal administration was necessary to limit local political manipulation and unequal relief.

Historical Significance

The WPA institutionalised the principle that the federal government could respond to mass unemployment by directly financing useful work rather than leaving relief entirely to local charity and state government.

Its physical legacy remains embedded in roads, schools, hospitals, airports, parks, bridges, public buildings, water systems, murals, archives, guidebooks, and historical collections.

Its social legacy is more complex. The program expanded opportunity and public services but did not dismantle Jim Crow, gendered labour markets, local political power, or unequal access to public institutions.

The WPA became a reference point in later debates over public employment, infrastructure stimulus, national service, arts funding, school meals, childcare, economic rights, and a federal job guarantee.

The program's history demonstrates that public employment can create durable assets and services, but equitable implementation requires explicit enforcement, accessible sponsorship, fair wages, inclusive occupational assignment, and protection from local discrimination.

Records and Research Value

The principal federal records of the WPA are preserved by the National Archives in Record Group 69, Records of the Work Projects Administration.

Records include:

  • central administrative correspondence;
  • state and regional office records;
  • project applications, approvals, and reports;
  • employment, certification, assignment, and separation records;
  • payroll and wage records;
  • worker complaints and grievance files;
  • investigation and political-activity records;
  • safety, accident, and compensation records;
  • engineering drawings, maps, plans, and surveys;
  • construction and accomplishment reports;
  • photographs, films, posters, and publicity materials;
  • Federal One administrative and project files;
  • Federal Writers' Project manuscripts and interviews;
  • Historical Records Survey inventories;
  • research and statistical publications;
  • war-training and defence-project records;
  • property and liquidation files; and
  • records maintained by sponsoring agencies and successor governments.

WPA employment records are not always consolidated in a single individual personnel file. Researchers may need to identify the state, project, sponsor, occupation, company, work division, or approximate dates of service.

Research Cautions

  • WPA had two official names. It was the Works Progress Administration until July 1, 1939, and the Work Projects Administration thereafter.
  • WPA and PWA were different programs. Confusing them leads to incorrect claims about administration, employment, and project finance.
  • NYA totals may be separate. The National Youth Administration was initially within the WPA but maintained distinct programs and later became independent.
  • Worker and assignment totals differ. One person could leave, return, transfer, or receive multiple project assignments.
  • Project totals use different definitions. A nationwide program, local project, project unit, work order, completed structure, and improvement may be counted differently.
  • Federal One was not the entire WPA. Arts and cultural projects were highly visible but represented a small portion of total employment and expenditure.
  • Official nondiscrimination did not ensure equality. Local records must be examined for segregation, occupational assignment, wage region, supervision, and facility access.
  • Publicity materials were promotional. Photographs and reports may emphasise successful projects while minimising conflict, delay, discrimination, or incomplete work.
  • Surviving records are decentralised. Important project files may remain in state archives, municipal records, libraries, museums, universities, and sponsoring agencies.

The administrative determination that a household or worker met economic-need requirements for work relief.

The fixed monthly WPA earning schedule based on skill, geography, and community size rather than an unrestricted private-market wage.

A nonconstruction public activity involving education, health, libraries, welfare, recreation, research, arts, records, food, or other community services.

Public assistance delivered through government-supported employment on useful projects.

A relief-certified person employed on an approved WPA project, distinct from a regular federal civil-service administrative employee.

Geographic Scope

WPA projects operated throughout the forty-eight states, the District of Columbia, and United States territories.

Project design reflected local conditions:

  • urban areas emphasised streets, sewers, schools, hospitals, recreation, arts, clerical work, records, and public services;
  • rural counties emphasised farm-to-market roads, schools, sanitation, libraries, conservation, food production, and public health;
  • coastal and river regions used workers for flood, drainage, levee, harbour, and storm-recovery projects;
  • western states received airports, irrigation, roads, parks, public-land, survey, and conservation projects;
  • Southern states operated within Jim Crow systems that shaped employment, project segregation, wages, and facility access;
  • territories used WPA labour for roads, public buildings, health, education, conservation, and administrative development; and
  • defence communities received expanded housing-related utilities, childcare, roads, airports, and public services after 1940.

Summary

The Works Progress Administration—renamed the Work Projects Administration in 1939—was the central federal work-relief agency of the Second New Deal.

It employed approximately 8.5 million different people on publicly sponsored construction, infrastructure, conservation, education, health, welfare, research, records, food, sewing, recreation, and cultural projects between 1935 and 1943.

Workers were generally required to be unemployed, employable, and certified as economically needy. They received monthly security wages based on occupation and location. State and local sponsors proposed projects and contributed materials, equipment, services, land, or money.

The WPA built and improved hundreds of thousands of miles of roads, tens of thousands of bridges and public buildings, hundreds of airports, and extensive park, school, hospital, water, sewer, and recreation facilities. Service projects supplied more than a billion school lunches, expanded nursery schools and libraries, supported public health, and preserved major cultural and historical records.

Women and African Americans gained employment that private labour markets often denied them, but access remained restricted by the one-worker-per-family rule, gendered occupations, Jim Crow segregation, racial discrimination, unequal wage regions, local certification, and limited advancement.

The WPA ended in 1943 after wartime mobilisation reduced unemployment. Its infrastructure, public services, artwork, oral histories, archives, guidebooks, and administrative precedents remain central to the history of the New Deal and federal employment policy.
