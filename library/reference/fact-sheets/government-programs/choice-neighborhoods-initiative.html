Choice Neighborhoods Initiative

The federal place-based grant program that combines assisted-housing redevelopment, resident services, and neighborhood investment through locally developed Transformation Plans organized around Housing, People, and Neighborhood goals.

Overview

The Choice Neighborhoods Initiative is a competitive federal grant program administered by the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development. It supports locally driven strategies to replace or rehabilitate severely distressed public housing or other eligible HUD-assisted housing while improving conditions throughout the surrounding neighborhood.

Congress first funded Choice Neighborhoods in fiscal year 2010. HUD issued the initial funding notice in August 2010 and made the first Planning Grant awards in 2011. The first Implementation Grants supported comprehensive redevelopment in Boston, Chicago, New Orleans, San Francisco, and Seattle.

Choice Neighborhoods is the functional successor to the HOPE VI Program. HOPE VI focused principally on redevelopment of severely distressed public housing sites. Choice Neighborhoods retained mixed-income redevelopment, private financing, relocation, resident services, and physical transformation, but broadened the strategy to include certain privately owned HUD-assisted housing and the surrounding neighborhood.

The program is organized around three core goals:

  • Housing: Replace distressed public or assisted housing with high-quality, financially sustainable, mixed-income housing.
  • People: Improve employment, income, health, education, and related outcomes for residents of the target housing.
  • Neighborhood: Create conditions supporting public and private reinvestment, safety, schools, transportation, commercial activity, services, public assets, and neighborhood opportunity.

Local partners express these goals through a comprehensive neighborhood revitalization strategy known as a Transformation Plan. The plan identifies the target housing, defines the target neighborhood, establishes resident and community priorities, sets measurable outcomes, assigns responsibility among partners, assembles funding, and coordinates housing redevelopment with services and neighborhood investment.

The two principal grant forms are Planning Grants and Implementation Grants. Planning Grants support the development of a Transformation Plan, resident engagement, data analysis, organizational partnerships, and limited early-action activities where authorized. Implementation Grants provide larger awards for housing redevelopment, resident services, critical community improvements, relocation, administration, and related implementation.

The program relies on extensive partnerships. Public housing agencies, local governments, assisted-housing owners, residents, schools, police departments, workforce agencies, healthcare providers, transit agencies, nonprofit organizations, philanthropic institutions, universities, businesses, lenders, investors, and private developers may participate.

Choice Neighborhoods seeks to correct several controversies associated with HOPE VI. Current Implementation Grant requirements generally include one-for-one replacement of covered target housing units and an opportunity for lease-compliant original tenants to return to replacement housing. Grantees must plan for relocation, resident engagement, long-term affordability, and continued assistance for residents affected by redevelopment.

The program nevertheless remains controversial. Large-scale transformation can require demolition, temporary displacement, multiple financing transactions, extensive screening and lease compliance, long construction schedules, and major changes in neighborhood land use and governance. Redevelopment can improve housing and neighborhood conditions while also exposing residents and nearby households to disruption, rising property values, exclusion, or displacement pressure.

HUD’s July 2024 Implementation Grant list identified 59 awards totaling approximately $2.04 billion. A December 2024 evaluation of nine early grantees found that they generally redeveloped severely distressed housing, maintained one-for-one replacement, and produced mixed-income housing. Resident and neighborhood outcomes varied, and broader neighborhood changes could not always be attributed solely to the federal grant.

The program remained active in 2026. The fiscal-year 2025 Implementation Grant competition made approximately $75 million available and anticipated roughly four awards in August 2026. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 provided an additional $25 million for Choice Neighborhoods competitive grants.

The defined geographic area surrounding the target housing in which the Transformation Plan coordinates housing, resident, institutional, economic, and physical improvements.
Planning Grant
A smaller competitive award supporting resident engagement, data analysis, partnership development, housing and neighborhood planning, financial strategy, and completion of a Transformation Plan.
Implementation Grant
A larger competitive award supporting execution of an approved or implementation-ready Transformation Plan, including housing redevelopment, relocation, supportive services, critical community improvements, and program administration.
Planning and Action Grant
A historical grant form combining comprehensive planning with limited early-action neighborhood improvements intended to build momentum during the planning period.
Housing goal
The program objective of replacing or rehabilitating distressed housing with high-quality, financially viable, mixed-income housing responsive to resident and neighborhood needs.
People goal
The program objective of improving employment, income, health, education, stability, and related outcomes for residents of the target housing.
Neighborhood goal
The program objective of creating conditions for public and private reinvestment, safety, schools, services, transportation, economic activity, public assets, and neighborhood opportunity.

Public or assisted housing experiencing serious physical, financial, management, design, vacancy, crime, isolation, or social conditions meeting the requirements of the applicable funding notice and statutory authority.
Public housing

HUD-assisted multifamily housing
Privately or nonprofit owned rental housing receiving qualifying HUD project-based assistance, mortgage insurance, legacy subsidy, or other covered federal support.
Public housing agency

Co-applicant

A core organization with formal responsibility for one or more components of the Transformation Plan.
Resident engagement
Structured involvement of target-housing residents and neighborhood stakeholders in planning, implementation, monitoring, and decision-making.
One-for-one replacement

Replacement housing
Qualifying public housing, Project-Based Voucher units, Project-Based Rental Assistance units, other eligible assisted units, and limited tenant-based voucher assistance where an authorized exception applies.
Right to return
The program requirement that qualifying original tenants receive an opportunity and highest-level preference to occupy replacement housing when they satisfy applicable lease-compliance and continuing-eligibility requirements.

Mixed-income housing
A development containing housing serving multiple income levels, commonly including replacement public or assisted housing, other affordable units, workforce housing, market-rate rentals, and sometimes homeownership.
Critical Community Improvement

Case management
A coordinated coaching and service-delivery process through which staff help residents establish goals, connect to services, address barriers, and track progress.
Leverage
Funds, financing, services, land, facilities, or other resources committed by entities other than the Choice Neighborhoods grant.
Mixed-finance development
A housing transaction combining federal housing resources with tax credits, bonds, loans, grants, private equity, local funds, land, or other financing.
Neighborhood of opportunity
A neighborhood offering stronger access to housing quality, employment, education, transportation, healthcare, safety, services, and other resources associated with resident well-being.
Transformation
The coordinated replacement or rehabilitation of distressed housing together with changes in resident services, institutions, public assets, economic conditions, and neighborhood investment.

Origins and Relationship to HOPE VI

Lessons from HOPE VI

HOPE VI demonstrated that severely distressed public housing could be demolished or rebuilt through mixed-income design, private financing, resident relocation, new streets, community facilities, and stronger property management. It also demonstrated the risks of reducing public housing, displacing residents, limiting return, and evaluating success primarily through the appearance of the redeveloped site.

Broader Neighborhood Strategy

Choice Neighborhoods was designed to extend redevelopment beyond the property boundary. Federal policymakers argued that housing transformation would be insufficient when residents continued to face weak schools, unemployment, crime, poor health access, transportation barriers, vacant property, and neighborhood disinvestment.

Interagency Neighborhood Revitalization

The initiative emerged as part of an Obama administration effort to coordinate HUD with the Departments of Education, Justice, Treasury, Health and Human Services, and other federal partners. The strategy encouraged local coordination among institutions that ordinarily operated through separate funding and administrative systems.

Public and Privately Owned Assisted Housing

Unlike HOPE VI, Choice Neighborhoods could target certain privately owned HUD-assisted multifamily housing in addition to public housing. This expanded the program’s reach to distressed developments outside public housing agency ownership.

Formal Housing, People, and Neighborhood Framework

Choice Neighborhoods institutionalized three linked domains. Housing redevelopment remained central, but applicants also had to establish resident-outcome strategies and neighborhood investments connected to measurable local needs.

Stronger Replacement and Return Requirements

The program generally requires covered target units to be replaced and qualifying original residents to receive an opportunity to return. These requirements respond directly to HOPE VI controversies concerning unit loss and former-resident exclusion.

Three Core Goals

Housing

The Housing goal seeks to replace or rehabilitate distressed public or HUD-assisted housing with high-quality, well-managed, financially sustainable, mixed-income housing. Strategies may include demolition, rehabilitation, new construction, preservation, off-site replacement, acquisition, mixed-finance ownership, rental assistance, and homeownership.

The Housing Plan must address unit counts, bedroom sizes, affordability, resident return, relocation, financing, design, environmental conditions, management, long-term operating feasibility, and integration with the surrounding neighborhood.

People

The People goal focuses on residents of the target housing. Local strategies commonly address:

  • Employment and earned income
  • Job training and career advancement
  • Education from early childhood through adulthood
  • Physical and behavioral health
  • Childcare and youth development
  • Financial capability and asset development
  • Housing stability and relocation
  • Transportation and digital access
  • Safety and violence prevention
  • Case management and benefits coordination

Neighborhood

The Neighborhood goal seeks to improve the broader environment in which residents live. Strategies may involve:

  • Vacant-property reuse
  • Affordable and market-rate housing production
  • Commercial development and small-business support
  • Schools and early-learning institutions
  • Parks, recreation, and public space
  • Transit, streets, sidewalks, and pedestrian access
  • Healthcare and social-service facilities
  • Public safety and violence prevention
  • Environmental remediation and resilience
  • Broadband and digital infrastructure
  • Arts, culture, and historic preservation
  • Community ownership and organizational capacity

Integrated Strategy

The three goals are intended to operate together. A housing project without resident and neighborhood improvements may reproduce isolation. Resident services without stable housing and neighborhood investment may have limited effect. Neighborhood investment without affordability and return protections may displace the population the program is intended to benefit.

Grant Types

Planning Grants

Planning Grants support development of a Transformation Plan. Activities may include resident engagement, organizational planning, market and housing analysis, neighborhood data collection, environmental assessment, financial modelling, educational strategy, supportive-services planning, design, and identification of implementation partners.

Planning Grants do not guarantee a later Implementation Grant. Communities must build an independently viable plan and pursue other public, private, philanthropic, and institutional resources.

Implementation Grants

Implementation Grants support execution of an advanced Transformation Plan. Eligible costs can include housing redevelopment, relocation, resident services, critical community improvements, planning directly related to implementation, and administration.

Implementation awards are highly competitive and generally require substantial readiness, site control, development capacity, financing commitments, resident participation, local-government engagement, and evidence that the plan can produce measurable transformation.

Planning and Action Grants

HUD has used Planning and Action Grants in selected years to combine planning with limited early physical or community-development activity. Early action can demonstrate progress, address urgent conditions, and strengthen resident confidence while the larger plan is prepared.

Supplemental Grants

Congress and HUD have periodically permitted supplemental awards to existing Implementation Grantees facing construction-cost increases, financing gaps, changed market conditions, or additional implementation needs.

Technical Assistance

HUD can support grantees and applicants through technical assistance, data tools, peer learning, grant-management guidance, financial instruction, mapping, and implementation support.

Transformation Plan

Purpose

The Transformation Plan is the central governing document for Choice Neighborhoods. It aligns housing redevelopment, resident outcomes, and neighborhood investment within one long-term strategy.

Neighborhood Definition

Applicants define a target neighborhood that is locally recognized and supported by data. Boundaries should capture the target housing and the institutions, assets, barriers, markets, and population affected by the proposed strategy.

Needs Assessment

The planning team evaluates housing condition, resident demographics, income, employment, education, health, safety, transportation, environmental conditions, land use, property markets, vacancy, institutions, and public assets.

Vision and Outcomes

The plan establishes a shared vision and measurable outcomes. Effective measures distinguish activities—such as constructing a building or enrolling residents in a program—from results such as improved housing quality, employment, school attendance, health access, or neighborhood safety.

Housing Plan

The Housing Plan identifies unit counts, replacement obligations, tenure and income mix, phasing, development sites, design, financing, relocation, resident return, property management, affordability, and long-term viability.

People Strategy

The People strategy identifies target residents, service needs, partners, case-management systems, outcome measures, referral processes, data-sharing, funding, and plans for sustaining services.

Neighborhood Strategy

The Neighborhood strategy coordinates public facilities, schools, commercial activity, transportation, public safety, parks, vacant land, additional housing, infrastructure, environmental improvements, and institutional capacity.

Implementation Structure

The plan assigns responsibility among the lead applicant, co-applicant, housing implementation entity, principal team members, resident organizations, service providers, local departments, and other partners.

Financial Plan

The plan identifies the Choice Neighborhoods request, leverage, match, financing sources, timing, contingencies, operating assumptions, and funding gaps.

Resident and Stakeholder Participation

Residents and neighborhood stakeholders should participate through meetings, workshops, surveys, resident councils, advisory committees, design sessions, working groups, youth engagement, and other accessible processes.

Living Document

Implementation can require amendments when costs, sites, partners, schedules, environmental conditions, markets, or resident needs change. Material changes remain subject to HUD and other funder approval.

Housing Redevelopment

Rehabilitation and Preservation

Choice Neighborhoods does not require demolition in every case. A property can be rehabilitated when buildings, infrastructure, location, and financial conditions support preservation.

Demolition and New Construction

Severely obsolete or distressed buildings can be demolished and replaced. Plans may restore street networks, reduce isolation, change density, redesign buildings, remediate hazards, and create new public spaces.

On-Site and Off-Site Replacement

Replacement units may be developed on the original site, elsewhere in the target neighborhood, or in qualifying off-site locations. Distribution affects resident return, neighborhood integration, access to opportunity, and the physical composition of the redevelopment.

Mixed-Income Development

Projects commonly combine replacement units with other affordable rentals, workforce housing, market-rate rentals, and homeownership. The income mix is intended to support financial sustainability and reduce concentrated poverty.

Mixed-Finance Structure

Housing phases frequently combine Choice Neighborhoods grants with Low-Income Housing Tax Credit equity, Project-Based Vouchers, public housing funds, Project-Based Rental Assistance, HOME funds, Housing Trust Fund assistance, tax-exempt bonds, conventional debt, state and local grants, and private equity.

Design and Integration

Housing plans may emphasize connected streets, accessible units, energy efficiency, resilient construction, pedestrian access, multiple housing types, public space, and visual integration among income categories.

Long-Term Affordability

Federal appropriations generally require additional affordability of at least twenty years, while public housing, project-based rental assistance, tax credits, local covenants, and other funding sources can impose longer restrictions.

Property Management

The redevelopment plan must establish competent management, maintenance, leasing, security, financial controls, resident communication, and compliance across multiple subsidy and ownership structures.

One-for-One Replacement and Resident Return

One-for-One Requirement

Current Implementation Grant requirements generally require one-for-one replacement when covered public housing units are demolished or disposed of. Applicants must certify replacement plans and identify the number, bedroom distribution, subsidy form, location, and development schedule of replacement units.

Qualifying Replacement Forms

Replacement housing can include public housing units, Project-Based Vouchers, Project-Based Rental Assistance, and other qualifying project-based assisted units. Under a specified exception, tenant-based vouchers can count toward a limited share of replacement.

Bedroom Mix

The replacement plan should provide an appropriate bedroom distribution capable of serving original residents who choose to return. Changes in household size, accessibility, and local need must be addressed.

Right to Return

A tenant occupying target housing under a valid lease or occupancy arrangement on the grant-award date generally must receive an opportunity to return to a replacement unit if the tenant was lease compliant at departure and remains lease compliant during relocation.

Returning tenants receive the highest-level preference for replacement units under current program requirements.

Continuing Eligibility

Return remains subject to household eligibility, lawful occupancy requirements, unit availability, family composition, and lease compliance. The plan should not impose new barriers inconsistent with federal requirements or the resident-return commitment.

Split Households

When relocation causes a household to divide, current rules prioritize the original head of household. Additional qualifying households created through the split may receive later access to available replacement units or waiting-list preference under program procedures.

Meaning of Replacement

One-for-one replacement protects the number of assisted housing opportunities but does not necessarily guarantee that every unit remains on the original site, uses the same subsidy, has the same design, or is completed at the same time.

Enforcement

Replacement and return obligations are reflected in certifications, grant agreements, relocation plans, development documents, funding conditions, resident notices, and HUD oversight.

Resident Relocation

Relocation Planning

Grantees prepare a relocation strategy addressing notices, counselling, moving costs, temporary and permanent housing, vouchers, accessibility, school continuity, transportation, language access, resident tracking, return, and phased redevelopment.

Housing Options

Residents may move to:

  • Another public housing unit
  • A private-market unit using a Housing Choice Voucher
  • A Project-Based Voucher unit
  • Another assisted multifamily property
  • Temporary housing during rehabilitation
  • A completed replacement unit
  • Another housing option selected by the household where permitted

Uniform Relocation Act

Residents and other lawful occupants displaced by covered activities may receive protections under the Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act, including notices, advisory services, moving expenses, and replacement-housing assistance.

Phased Relocation

Large developments may relocate residents in stages so that completed units can receive returning residents before later buildings are demolished.

Resident Tracking

Grantees must maintain contact with relocated households, provide updated information, track service participation, communicate construction progress, and notify eligible households when replacement units become available.

School and Service Continuity

Moves can interrupt schooling, childcare, healthcare, employment, transportation, and social networks. Effective relocation plans coordinate these systems rather than treating relocation as only a real-estate transaction.

Temporary and Permanent Displacement

Some residents return after temporary relocation. Others permanently use vouchers or other assisted housing. Resident choice, eligibility, construction timing, unit availability, family circumstances, and neighborhood preference influence outcomes.

Relocation Risk

Even with formal protections, long construction periods and difficult housing markets can expose residents to instability, repeated moves, distant relocation, landlord discrimination, or loss of contact.

People Strategy

Resident-Centred Planning

The People strategy begins with the households living in the target housing. Services should reflect resident priorities and measured needs rather than a generic program model.

Case Management

Case managers work with residents to establish goals, coordinate referrals, address barriers, document progress, and connect housing stability with employment, education, health, and family outcomes.

Employment and Income

Strategies can include job readiness, occupational training, apprenticeships, employer partnerships, placement, career advancement, entrepreneurship, benefits counselling, transportation, and Section 3 employment.

Children’s Education

Grantees may coordinate early childhood education, school attendance, tutoring, literacy, after-school programs, youth development, college preparation, family engagement, and partnerships with school districts or charter operators.

Health

People strategies can address primary care, behavioral health, trauma, chronic disease, maternal health, environmental hazards, nutrition, substance-use treatment, disability services, and healthcare access.

Financial Stability

Services may include budgeting, credit repair, banking, savings, tax preparation, debt management, benefits access, homeownership preparation, and asset development.

Safety and Stability

Programs can address domestic violence, youth violence, reentry, legal services, eviction prevention, family support, and connections to public-safety partners.

Service Leverage

Choice Neighborhoods grants cannot finance every service over the full transformation period. Grantees rely on school systems, workforce agencies, health institutions, federal grants, local budgets, philanthropy, and nonprofit providers.

Outcome Measurement

Programs track participation and outcomes such as employment, earned income, school attendance, educational performance, healthcare access, and resident stability. Data-sharing must address privacy, consent, accuracy, and institutional capacity.

Neighborhood Strategy

Critical Community Improvements

Implementation Grants can finance selected neighborhood investments directly connected to the Transformation Plan. Projects may include parks, community facilities, streetscape improvements, commercial spaces, educational facilities, vacant-property reuse, public-safety infrastructure, or other critical assets.

Additional Housing

The Neighborhood strategy can support preservation and production beyond the target housing, including affordable rentals, homeownership, market-rate housing, rehabilitation, and conversion of vacant or foreclosed properties.

Commercial and Economic Development

Strategies may involve small-business support, retail recruitment, commercial corridors, workforce facilities, entrepreneurship, industrial redevelopment, and connections to regional employment centres.

Schools and Education

Choice Neighborhoods can align housing redevelopment with early-learning centres, schools, educational partners, school facilities, attendance strategies, and youth programs.

Transportation

Neighborhood plans can improve transit access, sidewalks, bicycle connections, street networks, traffic safety, and connections to employment and services.

Public Safety

Safety strategies may include environmental design, violence intervention, youth programs, community policing, lighting, vacant-property management, reentry services, and resident-led prevention.

Environmental Conditions

Projects can address contamination, lead, flooding, heat, energy use, stormwater, parks, tree canopy, indoor environmental quality, and climate resilience.

Institutional Capacity

Long-term neighborhood improvement depends on organizations capable of sustaining investment and accountability after the HUD grant ends.

Catalytic Theory

The federal grant is intended to catalyze larger investment rather than pay for every neighborhood activity. The strategy depends on aligning capital plans, institutions, private markets, philanthropy, and public budgets around a common geography.

Partnerships and Governance

Local Government and Housing Agency

Choice Neighborhoods requires close coordination between local government and the public housing agency when public housing is targeted. Either may serve as lead applicant under the applicable competition, but both must support the Transformation Plan.

Housing Owner

When the target property is privately owned HUD-assisted housing, the owner and local government participate in the application and redevelopment structure as required by the funding notice.

Housing Implementation Entity

A capable entity must manage the housing redevelopment. This can include a public housing agency, private developer, nonprofit developer, owner affiliate, joint venture, or mixed-finance partnership.

Education Partners

School districts, early-learning providers, charter schools, colleges, universities, libraries, and youth organizations may lead educational strategies.

Health and Human-Service Partners

Hospitals, clinics, public-health departments, mental-health agencies, workforce boards, childcare providers, and social-service organizations may support the People strategy.

Residents

Resident councils, tenant associations, block organizations, youth groups, elders, and other residents provide local knowledge and should participate throughout planning and implementation.

Private and Philanthropic Partners

Investors, lenders, developers, businesses, foundations, community-development financial institutions, and philanthropic organizations can provide financing, services, facilities, employment, and technical capacity.

Governance Structure

Effective plans establish decision-making authority, reporting, dispute resolution, resident representation, data-sharing, financial accountability, and responsibility for each outcome.

Accountability Risk

Large partnerships can diffuse responsibility. A plan may contain many commitments without a single entity able to compel performance by schools, employers, police, developers, or service providers.

Finance and Leverage

Federal Grant

The Choice Neighborhoods award finances only part of the total Transformation Plan. HUD limits grant use, requires budgets, establishes cost rules, and reviews housing-development proposals.

Matching Requirement

Annual appropriations generally require grantees to provide matching funds from state, local, federal, or private sources under the terms established by HUD.

Housing Leverage

Housing financing can include:

  • Low-Income Housing Tax Credit equity
  • Tax-exempt private-activity bonds
  • Public Housing Funds
  • Project-Based Vouchers
  • Project-Based Rental Assistance
  • HOME Investment Partnerships funds
  • Housing Trust Fund assistance
  • Community Development Block Grants
  • State and local housing funds
  • Conventional construction and permanent loans
  • Federal Home Loan Bank Affordable Housing Program funds
  • Philanthropic and mission-investment capital
  • Land and infrastructure contributions

People Leverage

Resident-service leverage can include school budgets, workforce grants, healthcare funding, childcare subsidies, transportation assistance, philanthropy, federal service programs, and in-kind institutional commitments.

Neighborhood Leverage

Neighborhood resources can include public works, parks, transit, schools, libraries, commercial development, private housing, public safety, broadband, infrastructure, and local capital budgets.

Leverage Benefits

Leverage expands the scale of transformation and connects the grant to institutions with continuing resources.

Leverage Risks

Uncommitted or delayed leverage can weaken the plan. Projects can be affected by interest rates, tax-credit pricing, construction costs, public budgets, market demand, partner withdrawal, or changes in political leadership.

Financial Complexity

Each housing phase may require separate ownership, closing, subsidy, environmental review, financing, procurement, and compliance. Complexity can extend the implementation period and increase transaction cost.

Implementation Process

Grant Agreement

After selection, HUD and the grantee execute a grant agreement establishing terms, performance period, budget, drawdown, reporting, replacement, affordability, resident, and compliance requirements.

Environmental Review

Projects must complete environmental review before undertaking actions that limit reasonable alternatives or create adverse environmental effects. Reviews can address contamination, flood risk, historic resources, noise, air quality, infrastructure, and cumulative effects.

Site Control

The housing implementation team must establish control over the target and replacement sites through ownership, lease, option, agreement, acquisition authority, or another HUD-approved mechanism.

Relocation

Residents receive notices and assistance before demolition, disposition, rehabilitation, or conversion. Phasing must coordinate resident movement with construction and replacement availability.

Housing Development Proposal

HUD reviews each housing phase for unit mix, replacement, financing, cost, ownership, subsidy, design, accessibility, management, and long-term viability.

Construction

Grantees procure or supervise construction, monitor labor requirements, manage contingencies, report progress, and coordinate infrastructure and vertical development.

Lease-Up

Replacement units are offered under resident-return preferences and applicable waiting-list, eligibility, income, lease, accessibility, and occupancy rules.

People and Neighborhood Activities

Service and community-improvement activities proceed alongside housing development. Their schedules may differ from construction and require independent partnerships and funding.

Reporting and Monitoring

Grantees report expenditures, unit production, relocation, return, services, outcomes, leverage, milestones, and challenges. HUD conducts financial, programmatic, civil-rights, environmental, and development oversight.

Closeout

Grant closeout requires completion or acceptable disposition of funded activities, cost certification, property and affordability documentation, resolution of findings, and continued compliance with long-term obligations.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Target-Neighborhood Demographics

Many Choice Neighborhoods target areas are predominantly African American or Latino because public and assisted housing has historically been concentrated in communities shaped by segregation, redlining, urban renewal, highway construction, discriminatory lending, exclusionary zoning, and public and private disinvestment.

Housing Preservation

One-for-one replacement can protect affordable housing within neighborhoods experiencing redevelopment and rising property values. Long-term affordability and resident-return rules are especially important where African American communities face displacement pressure.

Physical and Institutional Investment

The program can direct substantial housing, infrastructure, school, health, commercial, and public-space investment into neighborhoods that have historically received inadequate public and private resources.

Resident Return

Return rights seek to ensure that original residents can benefit from redevelopment rather than being permanently removed before neighborhood conditions improve.

Screening and Lease Compliance

Return can still be affected by lease compliance, household eligibility, criminal-history standards, unit size, occupancy, and relocation-period conduct. Because African American communities are disproportionately exposed to policing, eviction, income instability, and administrative surveillance, neutral rules can have unequal practical effects.

Neighborhood Revaluation

New housing and public investment can raise land values and attract private capital. Without preservation of nearby rentals, homeownership, businesses, and community institutions, revitalization can benefit incoming households more than long-term residents.

Local Hiring and Contracting

Section 3 and local economic-development strategies can connect residents and neighborhood businesses to construction, property management, services, and permanent employment. Results depend on enforcement, training, procurement, contractor capacity, and job quality.

Resident Power

Meaningful participation requires more than attendance at meetings. Residents need timely information, technical assistance, accessible processes, influence over major decisions, and mechanisms to hold implementing institutions accountable.

Fair-Housing Obligations

Site selection, relocation, replacement location, marketing, tenant selection, accessibility, displacement, and neighborhood investment must comply with applicable civil-rights law.

Outcomes and Evidence

Implementation Portfolio

HUD’s July 2024 list reported 59 Implementation Grants with approximately $2.037 billion in federal awards. Grants ranged from roughly $13 million to $50 million and covered communities across the country.

Housing Redevelopment

The 2024 HUD evaluation found that nine examined early grantees succeeded in redeveloping severely distressed public or assisted housing, maintained one-for-one replacement, and produced mixed-income housing.

Resident Outcomes

Residents in some studied communities experienced reductions in neighborhood poverty and meaningful increases in employment and income. Other resident findings were mixed, demonstrating that physical redevelopment does not produce uniform household outcomes.

Neighborhood Improvements

Grantees completed community improvements involving parks, schools, streets, facilities, economic development, public safety, and other assets. The evaluation concluded that broader neighborhood effects could not always be attributed solely to Choice Neighborhoods because many other public and private forces affected the same areas.

Leverage and Coordination

The program has attracted substantial additional housing, institutional, local-government, philanthropic, and private resources. Leverage is a central achievement but complicates causal evaluation because much of the transformation is financed outside the HUD grant.

Implementation Time

Major housing transformations can require many years. Long schedules reflect relocation, demolition, environmental review, financing, land acquisition, infrastructure, multiple development phases, and changing market conditions.

Measurement Challenges

Program evaluation must distinguish among outcomes for:

  • The original target housing site
  • Original residents who returned
  • Original residents who relocated elsewhere
  • New residents of replacement housing
  • The broader target neighborhood
  • Nearby households and businesses outside the formal program

Causal Attribution

Neighborhood change can result from housing markets, regional employment, public investment, private development, demographic change, schools, transit, crime trends, and broader policy. Choice Neighborhoods may catalyze change without being the sole cause.

Benefits and Limitations

Comprehensive Framework

The program recognizes that housing, employment, education, health, transportation, safety, and neighborhood institutions are interconnected.

One-for-One Replacement

Replacement requirements offer stronger protection against net loss of assisted housing than many HOPE VI redevelopments provided.

Resident Return

Return requirements create a formal mechanism for original residents to benefit from transformed housing and neighborhood conditions.

Neighborhood Investment

Choice Neighborhoods can align multiple capital systems around communities that have experienced long-term disinvestment.

Planning Capacity

Planning Grants help communities build data, partnerships, resident processes, and implementation strategies even when they do not later receive an Implementation Grant.

Limited Federal Scale

The number of distressed developments and neighborhoods exceeds the number that can receive competitive grants. Most potentially eligible communities do not receive a large Implementation award.

Complexity

The program requires advanced development, finance, partnership, data, service, relocation, and administrative capacity. Communities with the greatest need may lack the institutional capacity required to compete successfully.

Long Schedules

Residents can experience uncertainty and disruption for years before replacement housing and neighborhood improvements are complete.

Reliance on Partners

HUD cannot compel every school, employer, healthcare institution, transit authority, lender, or private developer to fulfil local commitments indefinitely.

Housing-Market Risk

Rising costs, interest rates, insurance, land prices, tax-credit conditions, and weak or overheated markets can disrupt implementation.

Displacement Pressure

Target-housing residents may receive formal protections while nearby unsubsidized renters, homeowners, and businesses remain vulnerable to rising costs.

Outcome Ambiguity

A transformed site can be physically successful while relocated residents experience mixed outcomes. Evaluation must not treat neighborhood appearance as a substitute for resident well-being.

Major Policy Debates

Comprehensive Transformation or Excessive Complexity

Supporters argue that concentrated poverty requires coordinated housing, service, and neighborhood intervention. Critics argue that one housing grant cannot effectively manage schools, health, policing, transportation, employment, and private markets.

Mixed Income and Resident Benefit

Mixed-income housing can improve financial stability, reduce stigma, and create a broader neighborhood market. It does not automatically create social interaction, employment, educational gains, or economic mobility for the poorest residents.

Redevelopment and Preservation

Some distressed housing requires major rehabilitation rather than demolition. Decisions should consider building condition, resident preference, cultural history, environmental cost, unit loss, financing, and long-term viability.

Return Rights and Lease Compliance

Return rights protect residents, but lease-compliance requirements can exclude households facing poverty, disability, trauma, policing, or administrative disputes. Policy debates concern due process, individualized assessment, and proportionality.

Neighborhood Revitalization and Gentrification

Public and private investment can improve opportunity while increasing rents and property values. Effective transformation requires preservation and anti-displacement measures beyond the target housing.

Competitive Grants and Geographic Equity

Competition can reward strong plans and implementation capacity. It can also direct resources toward applicants best able to write grants rather than toward communities with the greatest underlying distress.

Resident Participation and Institutional Control

Resident engagement is mandatory, but public agencies, developers, lenders, investors, and funders retain control over many major decisions. The scope of resident authority varies by site.

Federal Funding Stability

Because Choice Neighborhoods depends on annual appropriations rather than a large permanent authorization, grant availability and scale can change with federal budget policy.

Key Distinctions

Choice Neighborhoods and HOPE VI

HOPE VI focused primarily on severely distressed public housing redevelopment. Choice Neighborhoods applies a broader Housing, People, and Neighborhood framework and can include certain privately owned HUD-assisted housing and surrounding-neighborhood investment.

Planning Grants and Implementation Grants

Planning Grants develop a Transformation Plan and partnerships. Implementation Grants finance execution of an advanced plan, including housing, relocation, services, and community improvements.

Choice Neighborhoods and the Federal Public Housing Program

The Federal Public Housing Program is the ongoing national housing system. Choice Neighborhoods is a selective competitive grant program for transformation of particular distressed developments and neighborhoods.

Choice Neighborhoods and the Rental Assistance Demonstration

RAD converts eligible public housing or legacy assisted properties to long-term Section 8 contracts to support preservation or redevelopment. Choice Neighborhoods supplies competitive grant funding and requires a broader resident and neighborhood strategy. The programs can be combined.

Choice Neighborhoods and Section 18

Section 18 is a statutory demolition or disposition process for public housing. Choice Neighborhoods is a funding and transformation program. A Choice project can require related Section 18 or other HUD approvals.

Choice Neighborhoods and Community Development Block Grants

CDBG provides formula funding for a broad range of local community-development activities. Choice Neighborhoods is a competitive, place-based program centred on distressed assisted housing and a defined Transformation Plan.

Choice Neighborhoods and Housing Choice Vouchers

Housing Choice Vouchers subsidize individual households in private rental housing. Choice Neighborhoods finances place-based redevelopment and may use vouchers for relocation or limited replacement purposes.

One-for-One Replacement and On-Site Replacement

One-for-one replacement protects the number of assisted housing opportunities. It does not necessarily require every replacement unit to be constructed on the original site.

Right to Return and Guaranteed Immediate Occupancy

Right to return creates priority and an opportunity for qualifying original residents. It does not guarantee that a suitable unit will be immediately available when a household wishes to move.

Target Housing and Target Neighborhood

Target housing is the distressed assisted development. The target neighborhood is the broader defined area addressed by the Transformation Plan.

Geographic Scope

Choice Neighborhoods operates through competitively selected neighborhoods across the United States. Implementation Grants have supported major cities, midsized cities, and smaller communities in every major region.

HUD’s July 2024 list included projects in Alabama, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Missouri, Nebraska, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, Washington, and Wisconsin.

Target neighborhoods include central-city public housing districts, historic African American communities, downtown-adjacent areas, former industrial districts, transit corridors, rural or small-city centres, and neighborhoods containing privately owned HUD-assisted housing.

Geographic success depends not only on improvement within the boundary but also on connection to regional employment, transportation, schools, housing markets, healthcare, and opportunity.

Legal Context

Annual Appropriations

Choice Neighborhoods was created and continues to operate primarily through annual federal appropriations. Appropriations establish the amount, eligible uses, affordability period, match, applicant categories, and other requirements for each funding year.

Section 24 of the United States Housing Act of 1937

Annual appropriations generally make Choice Neighborhoods grants subject to Section 24 of the United States Housing Act—the former HOPE VI statutory provision—unless the appropriations language specifies otherwise.

Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2010

Fiscal-year 2010 appropriations supplied the first $65 million for Choice Neighborhoods planning and implementation activity.

Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026

Public Law 119-75 appropriated $25 million for Choice Neighborhoods competitive grants. The law authorizes transformation, rehabilitation, and replacement of public and HUD-assisted housing and neighborhood improvement involving services, schools, public assets, transportation, and access to employment.

The 2026 law permits grant use for resident and community services, community development, affordable housing, and conversion of vacant or foreclosed properties. It requires an additional affordability period of at least twenty years and matching resources under HUD requirements.

United States Housing Act of 1937

Public housing redevelopment remains subject to applicable provisions governing public housing agencies, demolition, disposition, development, operation, resident rights, rents, eligibility, mixed finance, and rental assistance.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

The Fair Housing Act governs discrimination, accessibility, site selection, marketing, tenant selection, relocation, and other housing activity.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 requires disability nondiscrimination, reasonable accommodation, effective communication, and program accessibility in covered federally assisted activity.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II applies to public housing agencies and local governments. Other ADA provisions may govern covered services, facilities, leasing offices, and public accommodations.

Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970

The Uniform Relocation Act governs notices, advisory services, moving expenses, replacement-housing assistance, and acquisition procedures for qualifying displaced residents, businesses, and organizations.

National Environmental Policy Act

Choice Neighborhoods activities require environmental review under HUD procedures before grantees take actions that limit alternatives or create adverse effects.

National Historic Preservation Act

Section 106 consultation applies when a federal undertaking can affect historic properties, including older public housing developments, neighborhoods, archaeological resources, and culturally significant sites.

Davis–Bacon Act and Related Labor Standards

Federal prevailing-wage requirements apply to covered construction and rehabilitation financed through Choice Neighborhoods or other federal funding.

Section 3 of the Housing and Urban Development Act of 1968

Section 3 directs covered employment, training, and contracting opportunities toward low-income persons and qualifying businesses connected to HUD-assisted communities.

Low-Income Housing Tax Credit

Many housing phases are subject to Internal Revenue Code Section 42, state Qualified Allocation Plans, income and rent restrictions, investor requirements, compliance monitoring, and extended-use agreements.

Project-Based Voucher and Rental-Assistance Law

Replacement housing using Project-Based Vouchers or Project-Based Rental Assistance must comply with the applicable Section 8 statutory, regulatory, contract, income, rent, inspection, and resident-protection framework.

Grant-Specific Authority

The applicable Notice of Funding Opportunity, grant agreement, approved Transformation Plan, certifications, resident-return requirements, relocation plan, environmental review, development approvals, and affordability documents govern each award.

Summary

The Choice Neighborhoods Initiative is the federal government’s principal competitive program for transforming neighborhoods containing severely distressed public or eligible HUD-assisted housing. Established in fiscal year 2010, it succeeded HOPE VI while expanding federal redevelopment policy beyond an individual housing site.

The program is organized around Housing, People, and Neighborhood goals. Local partners prepare a Transformation Plan coordinating mixed-income housing redevelopment, resident services, schools, health, employment, transportation, public safety, commercial activity, public assets, and neighborhood investment.

Planning Grants support development of the local strategy. Implementation Grants finance housing, relocation, services, critical community improvements, and related administration. Grantees combine federal awards with tax credits, rental assistance, loans, local capital, philanthropy, infrastructure, and private investment.

Choice Neighborhoods strengthened resident protections compared with many HOPE VI projects. Current requirements generally provide one-for-one replacement of covered target housing units and an opportunity for qualifying lease-compliant original residents to return to replacement housing.

Through July 2024, HUD identified 59 Implementation Grants totaling approximately $2.04 billion. Federal evaluation found that early grantees successfully redeveloped distressed housing and maintained one-for-one replacement, while resident and broader neighborhood outcomes remained mixed and difficult to attribute solely to the program.

The initiative’s central operating proposition is that housing redevelopment should be integrated with resident and neighborhood improvement. Its central risk is that a complex, long-term transformation can improve land, buildings, and market conditions without delivering equal benefits to all original residents and nearby low-income households.

Choice Neighborhoods remained active in 2026. Its continuing effectiveness depends on stable federal funding, resident power, strong relocation and return protections, competent development, realistic financing, durable affordability, anti-displacement policy, accountable partnerships, and sustained investment after the federal grant closes.
