National School Lunch Program

The federal school-meal program that reimburses participating public and nonprofit private schools and residential child care institutions for serving nutritionally regulated lunches at free, reduced-price, or paid reimbursement levels.

Overview

The National School Lunch Program is a federally assisted meal program operating in public schools, nonprofit private schools, and qualifying residential child care institutions. Commonly abbreviated NSLP, it provides federal cash reimbursement and USDA Foods assistance for lunches that satisfy federal nutrition, eligibility, accountability, food-safety, procurement, and civil-rights requirements.

Congress established the modern program through the National School Lunch Act of 1946. The statute declared that federal school-lunch assistance would safeguard the health and well-being of the nation’s children and encourage the domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities and other foods. The Act is now officially titled the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act.

The NSLP is administered through a federal–state–local partnership. The United States Department of Agriculture establishes national policy, meal standards, reimbursement rates, income guidelines, program-integrity requirements, and food-assistance rules. State agencies approve and monitor local operators. School food authorities purchase food, prepare or contract for meals, determine student eligibility, claim reimbursement, maintain records, manage nonprofit food-service accounts, and serve meals.

Federal reimbursement is available for every qualifying lunch served to an enrolled child. The reimbursement amount depends on whether the meal is claimed as free, reduced-price, or paid; whether the school food authority serves a high percentage of free and reduced-price meals; whether it is certified for performance-based reimbursement; and whether the school is located in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, the United States Virgin Islands, or the contiguous states.

Children from households with incomes at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty guidelines are generally eligible for free meals. Children from households above 130 percent but at or below 185 percent are generally eligible for reduced-price meals. The maximum student charge for a reduced-price lunch is forty cents. Children not certified for free or reduced-price benefits may purchase paid meals at prices established locally.

Eligibility can be established through household application, direct certification, categorical eligibility, or a school-wide alternative. Children can be directly certified through records from the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other qualifying assistance systems. Foster children and qualifying homeless, migrant, runaway, and Head Start participants can receive free meals without an ordinary income application.

The Community Eligibility Provision allows eligible schools, groups of schools, and local educational agencies in high-poverty areas to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every enrolled student. Rather than collecting household meal applications, the school uses the share of students directly identified through public-benefit and categorical systems to calculate federal reimbursement.

A reimbursable lunch generally includes fruits, vegetables, grains, meats or meat alternates, and fluid milk in quantities established for grade groups. Weekly menus must satisfy calorie ranges and limits concerning saturated fat, trans fat, sodium, and added sugars. Schools must also comply with food-safety, Buy American, professional-standards, local wellness-policy, and competitive-food requirements.

Current rules are in transition. Product-based added-sugar limits for breakfast cereals, yogurt, and flavored milk took effect in school year 2025–2026. A weekly added-sugars limit and a further lunch sodium reduction are scheduled for July 1, 2027. The Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025, enacted in January 2026, restored whole and reduced-fat milk as permissible options for students age two and older.

The NSLP is one of the nation’s largest food and nutrition assistance programs. It operates in nearly 100,000 schools and institutions, reaches roughly thirty million children on a typical school day, and serves approximately five billion lunches annually. USDA reported more than 4.8 billion lunches in fiscal year 2024 and estimated approximately 5.18 billion lunches for fiscal year 2026.

The program has educational, nutritional, agricultural, fiscal, and civil-rights significance. School lunches can reduce food hardship, support concentration and attendance, improve diet quality, stabilize household food budgets, provide a reliable market for food producers, and create a platform for nutrition education and public-health policy.

The program also faces persistent challenges. These include food and labor costs, staffing shortages, kitchen limitations, meal quality, student participation, food waste, unpaid meal charges, stigma, certification error, procurement complexity, domestic-purchasing requirements, local budget pressure, dietary disputes, and unequal access to universal free meals.

Definitions

National School Lunch Program
The federal program reimbursing participating schools and residential child care institutions for qualifying lunches served to enrolled children.
School food authority
The governing body responsible for operating the school meal program in one or more schools. The abbreviation SFA is commonly used.
Local educational agency
A public board of education or other public authority legally responsible for administrative control of public elementary or secondary schools. The abbreviation LEA is commonly used.
State agency
The state education, agriculture, health, or other designated agency administering the NSLP within a state or territory.
Residential child care institution
A qualifying public or nonprofit private residential institution serving children, including certain group homes, schools, shelters, detention facilities, and similar institutions. The abbreviation RCCI is commonly used.
Reimbursable lunch
A lunch offered and selected in accordance with the applicable federal meal pattern, service, recordkeeping, and counting requirements.
Free lunch
A reimbursable lunch served without charge to a child certified for free meal benefits or enrolled in a school-wide free-meal option.
Reduced-price lunch
A reimbursable lunch served to an eligible child at a student charge not exceeding forty cents.
Paid lunch
A reimbursable lunch served to a child who is not claimed in the free or reduced-price category. The lunch still receives a smaller federal reimbursement.
Income Eligibility Guidelines
Annual USDA household-income standards used to determine eligibility for free and reduced-price school meals. The abbreviation IEGs is commonly used.
Categorical eligibility
Eligibility established through participation in another qualifying public-assistance program or through a protected child category rather than an ordinary household-income calculation.
Direct certification
Certification of a child for free meals by matching school enrollment data with records from SNAP, TANF, FDPIR, Medicaid demonstrations, foster care, or another authorized source.
Household application
A paper or electronic form through which a household reports income, household members, assistance-program information, and required certification for meal eligibility.
Verification
The annual process through which an SFA reviews a sample of approved household applications and requests documentation of income or categorical eligibility.
Overt identification
Any practice revealing or signaling a child’s free or reduced-price status to students, staff without a need to know, or the public. Federal law prohibits overt identification.
Identified student percentage
The percentage of enrolled students directly certified or otherwise identified without a household application for purposes of the Community Eligibility Provision. The abbreviation ISP is commonly used.
Community Eligibility Provision
A four-year meal-service option allowing eligible high-poverty schools, groups, or LEAs to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to all students and claim reimbursement through an identified-student formula. The abbreviation CEP is commonly used.
Provision 1
An alternative certification option allowing qualifying schools to reduce application frequency for students certified for free meals while continuing annual applications for reduced-price and paid students.
Provision 2
An option allowing schools to establish free, reduced-price, and paid claiming percentages during a base year and serve meals without charge to all students for later years of a four-year cycle.
Provision 3
An option providing a fixed federal payment based on reimbursement and enrollment data from a base year, adjusted for enrollment and inflation, while meals are served without charge.
Meal component
A required food category within the federal school lunch pattern: fruits, vegetables, grains, meats or meat alternates, and fluid milk.
Food item
A specific food offered within a meal component, such as an apple, brown rice, beans, chicken, or milk.
Offer versus Serve
A meal-service flexibility permitting students to decline specified components or items while still selecting a reimbursable meal. The abbreviation OVS is commonly used.
Meal pattern
The federal requirements concerning components, quantities, grade groups, calories, nutrients, menu planning, and service.
USDA Foods
Domestically produced foods purchased by USDA and made available to participating schools as additional federal assistance.
Entitlement value
The annual per-lunch value used to calculate each state’s minimum USDA Foods assistance.
Performance-based reimbursement
Additional per-lunch cash assistance for school food authorities certified as meeting federal meal-pattern and nutrition standards.
Severe-need reimbursement
An enhanced federal payment associated principally with the School Breakfast Program, not the ordinary NSLP lunch reimbursement, for schools serving high shares of low-income students.
Sixty-percent school food authority
An SFA that served 60 percent or more of its lunches free or at reduced price during the specified prior school year and therefore receives a slightly higher lunch payment.
Nonprofit school food-service account
The restricted account containing revenue and expenditures of the school meal program. Funds must be used for operation or improvement of the nonprofit food service.
Net cash resources
Funds available in the nonprofit school food-service account, subject to federal limits and state-agency oversight.
Paid Lunch Equity
A federal requirement intended to prevent federal free and reduced-price reimbursement from improperly subsidizing paid lunches when local paid prices are below the statutory benchmark.
Unpaid meal charge
A debt created when a student receives a meal but the household or other responsible party does not pay the required charge.
Alternative meal
A meal or food offered under a local unpaid-meal-charge policy instead of the regular reimbursable meal, subject to civil-rights, nutrition, overt-identification, and local requirements.
Smart Snacks in School
Federal nutrition standards for foods and beverages sold to students during the school day outside reimbursable meal programs.
Local school wellness policy
An LEA policy addressing nutrition promotion, nutrition education, physical activity, school foods, stakeholder participation, implementation, and public reporting.
Buy American requirement
The requirement that SFAs purchase domestic agricultural commodities and food products to the maximum extent practicable, subject to documented exceptions.
Administrative review
A state-agency examination of SFA compliance involving meal access, meal patterns, counting and claiming, resource management, civil rights, wellness policy, procurement, food safety, and related requirements.
Meal-counting system
The point-of-service or other approved process identifying each reimbursable meal and its correct free, reduced-price, paid, or school-wide claiming category.

Historical Origins

Local and Charitable Programs

American school lunch programs began as local responses to child hunger, urban poverty, long travel distances, and the educational effects of inadequate nutrition. Charitable organizations, settlement houses, women’s clubs, parent associations, teachers, and city governments established meal services before permanent federal participation.

Agricultural Surplus

During the Great Depression, farmers faced low prices and unsold commodities while unemployed families faced hunger. Federal purchase and distribution of surplus foods connected agricultural stabilization with school feeding.

Works Progress Administration

The WPA supplied workers to cook, transport, and serve school meals. Schools and local sponsors provided facilities, equipment, supervision, and additional food.

Wartime National Security

Military examinations during World War II revealed extensive nutrition-related health problems among recruits. Supporters of a permanent school lunch system framed child nutrition as an issue of education, health, agricultural prosperity, and national defense.

Permanent Federal Law

The 1946 Act replaced unstable annual or emergency arrangements with a continuing federal commitment. It required state plans, nonprofit operation, minimum meal standards, financial accountability, and access for children unable to pay.

Richard B. Russell

Senator Richard B. Russell Jr. of Georgia was a major congressional sponsor of permanent school-lunch legislation. The statute was renamed in his honor in 1999. Russell was also a leading defender of racial segregation, making the law’s current title a subject of continuing public debate.

Program Purpose and Scale

Child Nutrition

The NSLP ensures that children can obtain a meal meeting federal nutritional requirements during the school day. For many students, school lunch provides a substantial share of daily food and nutrient intake.

Educational Support

Reliable meals can reduce hunger-related distraction, fatigue, irritability, absenteeism, and difficulty participating in classroom instruction.

Household Economic Support

Free and reduced-price meals reduce household food expenses. School-wide free-meal models also reduce application burden and the risk that eligible children fail to receive benefits.

Agricultural Policy

The program creates a large institutional market for food. USDA Foods purchases support domestic agriculture while assisting school food-service budgets.

Public-Health Platform

Federal meal standards influence children’s consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, dairy, protein foods, sodium, saturated fat, and added sugars.

Scale

The NSLP operates in nearly 100,000 public and nonprofit private schools and residential institutions. Approximately thirty million children participate on a typical school day.

USDA reported more than 4.8 billion lunches in fiscal year 2024. Since fiscal year 1969, the program has served approximately 246 billion lunches.

Second-Largest Nutrition Program

USDA’s Economic Research Service identifies the NSLP as the nation’s second-largest food and nutrition assistance program after SNAP when measured by federal spending and participation.

Administration and Operating Structure

Federal Administration

The Food and Nutrition Administration:

  • Implements federal statutes and appropriations
  • Issues regulations, policy memoranda, forms, and technical assistance
  • Publishes annual income guidelines and reimbursement rates
  • Allocates funds and USDA Foods assistance to states
  • Establishes nutrition and meal-pattern standards
  • Monitors state agencies and regional operations
  • Collects program data and conducts research
  • Administers civil-rights and program-integrity requirements
  • Provides grants for equipment, technology, training, and farm-to-school initiatives

State Agencies

State agencies approve school food authorities, distribute federal funds, provide training, review applications and agreements, conduct administrative reviews, resolve findings, oversee procurement and resource management, monitor civil rights, and report meal counts to USDA.

School Food Authorities

SFAs operate the local program. Responsibilities include:

  • Preparing annual agreements and policies
  • Determining eligibility or implementing a school-wide option
  • Purchasing food and services under federal procurement rules
  • Planning menus and maintaining production records
  • Training food-service and other responsible staff
  • Counting reimbursable meals at the point of service
  • Submitting accurate reimbursement claims
  • Managing the nonprofit school food-service account
  • Maintaining food safety and sanitation
  • Protecting confidential eligibility information
  • Providing reasonable modifications for children with disabilities
  • Implementing civil-rights and nondiscrimination requirements

Schools

Individual schools coordinate schedules, cafeteria space, student supervision, meal service, point-of-sale procedures, accommodations, unpaid-meal policies, wellness activities, and communication with households.

Private Contractors

An SFA may contract with a food-service management company, vendor, cooperative, or other private provider. The SFA remains responsible for federal compliance and cannot transfer its legal accountability to the contractor.

Federal Regional Offices

USDA regional offices supervise state agencies, provide technical assistance, review state operations, and coordinate policy implementation across multistate regions.

Eligible Schools and Institutions

Public Schools

Public elementary and secondary schools can participate through an approved state agreement. Public charter schools can participate when they satisfy state and federal requirements.

Nonprofit Private Schools

Nonprofit private schools can participate when they possess qualifying nonprofit status, operate an eligible educational program, and comply with federal rules.

Residential Child Care Institutions

Eligible RCCIs can include residential schools, group homes, juvenile facilities, shelters, orphanages, and other qualifying institutions serving children.

For-Profit Institutions

Ordinary for-profit schools are generally not eligible. The NSLP is structured around public and nonprofit educational or residential institutions.

Nonprofit Food Service

Participating institutions must operate a nonprofit school food service. Revenue can cover allowable costs and improve the program but cannot be distributed as profit to the school or private parties.

Program Agreement

The SFA enters into a written agreement with the state agency establishing its responsibilities for meals, eligibility, claims, records, civil rights, procurement, audits, reviews, and corrective action.

Open Access During Meal Service

Participating schools must make meals available to enrolled children without unlawful discrimination and without identifying children according to payment category.

Student Eligibility

Free Meals by Household Income

A child is generally eligible for free meals when household income is at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Reduced-Price Meals by Household Income

A child is generally eligible for reduced-price meals when household income exceeds 130 percent but does not exceed 185 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Gross Income

Household income is generally evaluated before deductions. Wages, self-employment, pensions, unemployment, Social Security, child support, rental income, and other cash income can count, subject to federal exclusions and household rules.

Household

A household generally includes people living as one economic unit and sharing income and expenses. Temporary absence, foster status, institutional placement, joint custody, and military deployment can require special treatment.

Duration

Eligibility generally remains effective for the school year and a carryover period at the beginning of the next school year, unless a change improves the household’s benefit or another authorized action applies.

No Adverse Change Reporting

Households generally are not required to report increases in income or loss of categorical eligibility during the certification period.

Direct and Categorical Certification

Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program

Children identified as members of households receiving SNAP benefits are directly certified for free school meals. States and LEAs match public-benefit and school-enrollment data without requiring a separate household application.

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families

Children in qualifying TANF households can be directly certified for free meals under federal and state data systems.

Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations

Children in households receiving FDPIR benefits are categorically eligible for free meals.

Medicaid Demonstrations

States approved for direct-certification demonstrations can use Medicaid data to identify children eligible for free or reduced-price meals under specified federal procedures.

Foster Children

A foster child formally placed by a state or court is categorically eligible for free meals. The child can be certified independently of other household members.

Homeless Children

Children identified under the McKinney–Vento homeless education system can receive free meals without an ordinary income application.

Migrant and Runaway Children

Children identified through qualifying migrant education and runaway or homeless youth systems are eligible for free meals.

Head Start

Children enrolled in federally funded Head Start or comparable state-funded prekindergarten programs using equivalent eligibility standards can qualify categorically.

Extended Eligibility

When one child is directly certified through SNAP, TANF, or FDPIR, other children in the same household can generally receive the same free-meal status after the household connection is established.

Benefits

Direct certification reduces household paperwork, increases access, lowers certification error, supports CEP eligibility, and links school meals with broader nutrition and income-assistance systems.

Risks

Data-matching errors, name differences, missing records, delayed benefit updates, custody arrangements, and incomplete program participation can leave eligible students unmatched.

Household Applications

Annual Distribution

Schools not relying exclusively on a school-wide alternative distribute applications and public notices before or near the beginning of the school year and accept applications throughout the year.

Required Information

An income application generally includes household members, income amounts and frequency, the last four digits of the signing adult’s Social Security number or an indication that the adult lacks one, and an adult signature certifying accuracy.

Assistance-Program Case Number

A household applying through SNAP, TANF, or FDPIR generally provides the qualifying case number and required household information rather than reporting all income.

Electronic Applications

LEAs may use secure electronic applications that satisfy federal content, signature, privacy, recordkeeping, and language-access requirements.

Incomplete Applications

School officials can contact households to obtain missing information. An application cannot be approved until required information is complete.

Notification

The household receives written notice of approval or denial. Denial notices explain the reason and provide appeal or hearing information.

Confidentiality

Eligibility information is protected. Disclosure is permitted only for authorized purposes and, in some cases, requires written household consent.

Language Access

LEAs must take reasonable steps to ensure meaningful access for households with limited English proficiency and provide assistance to applicants with disabilities.

Community Eligibility Provision

Purpose

CEP permits eligible schools to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every student, eliminate ordinary meal applications, reduce stigma, simplify meal counting, and increase participation.

Eligibility Threshold

A school, group of schools, or LEA generally must have an identified student percentage of at least 25 percent as of the applicable eligibility date.

Identified Students

Identified students generally include children directly certified through qualifying assistance programs and children categorically eligible through foster, homeless, migrant, runaway, or Head Start status. Students approved through ordinary income applications do not count toward the ISP.

Reimbursement Formula

The ISP is multiplied by the federal multiplier, generally 1.6, to calculate the percentage of meals reimbursed at the free rate. The remaining meals receive the paid rate.

Example

A school with a 50-percent ISP claims 80 percent of meals at the free rate and 20 percent at the paid rate. A school with an ISP of 62.5 percent or higher generally reaches 100-percent free-rate reimbursement under the 1.6 multiplier.

Four-Year Cycle

CEP operates through a four-year cycle. The school can use the ISP from the election year or an updated higher ISP from a later year within the cycle. A grace year may be available under specified conditions.

Local Funding Gap

CEP does not guarantee that federal reimbursement covers the full cost of serving meals without charge. The LEA must cover any difference with nonfederal funds unless state or local universal-meal funding applies.

Grouping

An LEA can group schools and calculate a combined ISP. Strategic grouping can allow schools with lower ISPs to participate with higher-poverty schools.

Title I Interaction

Because CEP eliminates individual meal applications, LEAs use approved alternative measures to allocate Title I funds, assess poverty, and satisfy education-program reporting.

Advantages

  • No student meal charges
  • No ordinary household meal applications
  • Reduced stigma and overt identification
  • Higher participation
  • Simplified point-of-service counting
  • Reduced unpaid meal debt
  • Potentially improved school climate and scheduling

Limitations

Schools near the 25-percent threshold can face a substantial nonfederal funding gap. Declines in SNAP or other directly certified enrollment can reduce the ISP even when community poverty remains high.

Provisions 1, 2, and 3

Provision 1

Provision 1 reduces application frequency for children certified for free meals in qualifying schools. Reduced-price and paid eligibility continues to require annual certification.

Provision 2

Provision 2 establishes free, reduced-price, and paid claiming percentages during a base year. The school then serves meals without charge to all students and applies those percentages to later meal counts for a four-year period.

Provision 3

Provision 3 establishes a base-year federal payment rather than meal-category claiming percentages. The payment is adjusted for enrollment and inflation while meals are served without charge.

Extension

A state agency can approve extensions when socioeconomic conditions remain stable and federal requirements are satisfied.

Comparison with CEP

CEP relies on direct-certification data and does not require a base-year collection of meal applications. Provisions 2 and 3 require a base-year eligibility and claiming structure.

Meal Service and Counting

Lunch Period

Lunch must be served during a period recognized as the school’s lunch period. State agencies can approve exceptions when justified by school operations.

Point of Service

The school counts a meal as reimbursable only after determining that the student selected the required components or items. Counting meals before service or relying only on attendance is generally prohibited outside approved alternative systems.

Offer versus Serve

OVS is required in senior high schools and optional in lower grades. A student generally must select at least three of the five meal components and must take at least one-half cup of fruit, vegetable, or a combination.

Unit Pricing

The reimbursable meal is offered at one established price or benefit category. A student can purchase additional foods separately, subject to Smart Snacks and local rules.

Second Meals

Federal reimbursement is ordinarily available for only one lunch per child per meal service. Second meals can be sold but generally are not reimbursable except under limited emergency procedures.

Meals to Adults

Meals served to teachers, staff, visitors, or other adults cannot be claimed as reimbursable student meals. Their price must cover applicable costs.

Field Trips

Reimbursable meals can be served during qualifying school field trips when the SFA maintains required meal-pattern, counting, food-safety, and recordkeeping controls.

Meals Outside the Cafeteria

Breakfast in the classroom, grab-and-go service, mobile carts, outdoor meals, and other models can operate when the SFA preserves accurate counting and nutrition compliance.

At least 80 percent of weekly grains offered in the NSLP and School Breakfast Program must be whole grain-rich under current federal standards, subject to qualifying menu and product rules.

Meat Alternates

Beans, peas, lentils, eggs, cheese, yogurt, tofu, nuts, seeds, nut butters, and other qualifying foods can contribute to the meat or meat-alternate component.

Traditional and Culturally Relevant Foods

Schools can serve culturally relevant meals when foods are credited correctly and the weekly menu satisfies federal requirements.

Nutrition Standards

Calories

Weekly menus must fall within age-appropriate average daily calorie ranges. Individual meals may vary when the weekly average complies.

Saturated Fat

Saturated fat is generally limited to less than 10 percent of total calories across the school week, subject to current statutory treatment of milk fat and implementing regulations.

Trans Fat

Foods and ingredients generally must contain zero grams of trans fat per serving as shown on nutrition labels or manufacturer documentation, excluding naturally occurring trans fat.

Sodium

Lunches must satisfy grade-specific weekly sodium limits. Current limits remain in place through school year 2026–2027. A further 15-percent lunch sodium reduction is scheduled for July 1, 2027 under the 2024 final rule, subject to subsequent federal law.

Added Sugars

Product-based added-sugar limits for breakfast cereals, yogurt, and flavored milk took effect July 1, 2025. Beginning July 1, 2027, school lunch and breakfast menus are scheduled to limit added sugars to less than 10 percent of weekly calories.

Dietary Guidelines

Federal law requires school meal standards to be consistent with the goals of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. USDA updates regulations through notice-and-comment rulemaking rather than automatically changing the meal pattern whenever a new edition is issued.

Menu Flexibility

Schools determine recipes, preparation methods, local foods, cultural menus, vegetarian options, and service models within the federal meal pattern and nutrient limits.

Food Waste

Nutrition standards do not require students to consume every selected item. Schools use taste tests, scheduling, recess placement, share tables, nutrition education, menu choice, and careful portioning to reduce waste.

Fluid Milk Policy

Required Offering

Participating schools generally must offer fluid milk as a meal component. Students are not required to select milk when Offer versus Serve applies and the meal otherwise qualifies.

2026 Milk Options

The Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 expanded permissible milk varieties. Schools may offer conventional or organic whole, reduced-fat, low-fat, fat-free, lactose-free, and qualifying nondairy options under the implementing rules.

Flavored Milk

Flavored milk is subject to product-based added-sugar limits and any applicable age or menu requirements.

Disability Modification

A school must provide an appropriate substitute or modification when a child’s disability restricts ordinary milk consumption and the modification is necessary for equal program access.

Non-Disability Request

Schools may offer qualifying nondairy substitutes for non-disability requests when allowed by federal law and local policy. The substitute must meet USDA nutrient standards, and the school generally bears any additional cost.

Historical Debate

Milk policy has changed repeatedly in response to dietary guidance, dairy policy, student acceptance, food waste, fat consumption, added sugars, and congressional priorities.

Federal Reimbursement

Per-Meal Payments

USDA reimburses states for each properly claimed free, reduced-price, or paid lunch. States reimburse SFAs under approved claim systems.

Two Statutory Payment Components

Lunch payments combine:

  • A general national average payment under Section 4 of the Act; and
  • Additional special assistance for free and reduced-price lunches under Section 11.

Certified SFAs receive an additional nine cents per reimbursable lunch. Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands receive higher rates. USDA Foods assistance is additional.

Performance-Based Assistance

An SFA certified as meeting federal meal-pattern requirements receives the annually adjusted performance-based amount for each reimbursable lunch.

Maximum Rates

USDA also publishes maximum reimbursement amounts to ensure equitable state disbursement. State payment systems must remain within federal requirements.

Annual Inflation Adjustment

Lunch rates are adjusted each July using the Food Away From Home component of the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers.

Claims

SFAs submit monthly claims based on meal counts. Late claims, upward revisions, downward adjustments, and exceptions are governed by federal deadlines.

Entitlement Status

The NSLP is an entitlement to states and participating institutions for meals properly served and claimed. Federal reimbursement does not depend on a fixed annual number of children.

USDA Foods Assistance

Entitlement

In addition to cash reimbursement, states receive an annual USDA Foods entitlement based on the number of reimbursable lunches served.

School Year 2026–2027 Value

The national minimum value is thirty-two cents per reimbursable lunch from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027.

Available Foods

USDA purchases domestic meat, poultry, fish, dairy products, grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, oils, and other foods for state and school use.

Direct Delivery

Schools can receive foods in forms purchased and distributed by USDA.

Processing

States and SFAs can divert USDA Foods to approved processors that convert commodities into finished products such as cheese, poultry items, pasta, or prepared entrees.

Department of Defense Fresh Program

Schools can use part of their entitlement to purchase fresh fruits and vegetables through the USDA Department of Defense Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program.

Cash in Lieu

Specified institutions or jurisdictions can receive cash instead of donated foods where federal law authorizes that arrangement.

Domestic Agriculture

USDA Foods supports American agricultural producers while reducing school food-purchasing costs.

Procurement and the Buy American Requirement

Full and Open Competition

SFAs must conduct procurement in a manner providing full and open competition and must follow federal, state, and local requirements.

Methods

Procurement can involve micro-purchases, small purchases, sealed bids, competitive proposals, noncompetitive procurement under authorized conditions, cooperatives, and contracts with food-service management companies.

Buy American

SFAs must purchase domestic agricultural commodities and food products to the maximum extent practicable.

Domestic Product

A domestic food product is generally processed in the United States using agricultural commodities produced in the United States, subject to current federal percentage and implementation rules.

Exceptions

An SFA can use a documented exception when a domestic product is unavailable in sufficient quantity or quality or when the cost difference satisfies the applicable accommodation process.

School Year 2026–2027

USDA requires SFAs to use the current Buy American accommodation and documentation process and to monitor domestic sourcing in solicitations, contracts, invoices, and food deliveries.

Local Foods

Schools can use geographic preference for qualifying unprocessed locally grown or raised agricultural products and can participate in farm-to-school purchasing and education.

Conflict of Interest

Employees and officials involved in procurement must comply with standards of conduct governing gifts, gratuities, conflicts, and organizational relationships.

Financial Management

Nonprofit Account

Federal reimbursements, student payments, catering revenue, a la carte sales, and other school food-service income are deposited into the nonprofit account.

Allowable Costs

Funds can pay for food, labor, supplies, equipment, utilities, contracts, training, technology, and other costs necessary and reasonable for program operation.

Unallowable Costs

Costs unrelated to the school food service, unsupported charges, excessive compensation, improper transfers, entertainment, and other prohibited expenditures cannot be paid from the account.

Net Cash Resources

State agencies monitor account balances. Excess cash can require an approved spending plan involving equipment, meal quality, staffing, technology, debt repayment, or other program improvements.

Indirect Costs

School districts can charge allowable direct and indirect costs under federal cost principles and approved methodologies. Unclear or excessive charges can weaken food-service finances.

Nonprogram Food Revenue

Revenue from a la carte foods, adult meals, catering, vending, and other nonprogram food must contribute an appropriate share of food-service revenue.

Paid Lunch Equity

Federal law restricts using free and reduced-price meal reimbursement to subsidize paid lunches when local paid prices or nonfederal contributions are insufficient. Annual USDA guidance specifies calculation and exemption procedures.

Financial Pressure

Federal reimbursement must cover food, labor, benefits, equipment, facilities, utilities, waste, administration, and compliance. Rising costs can exceed annual rate adjustments or local revenues.

Program Integrity and Accountability

Eligibility Accuracy

Schools must correctly approve free and reduced-price applications, maintain direct-certification records, extend benefits to qualifying household members, and protect confidential information.

Verification

Each year, SFAs review a federally prescribed sample of approved applications. Households may provide pay records, assistance-program documentation, or other evidence.

Verification for Cause

An SFA can verify an application when it has specific reason to question the information, without discriminating or targeting households arbitrarily.

Meal Counting

Claims must be based on actual reimbursable meals served to eligible enrolled children. Attendance counts, production totals, or pre-service estimates cannot substitute for an approved point-of-service system.

Administrative Reviews

State agencies review each SFA on a federal cycle and can examine eligibility, meal access, meal patterns, counting, claiming, fiscal management, procurement, civil rights, food safety, wellness policy, professional standards, and afterschool snacks.

Corrective Action

Findings can require repayment, revised procedures, retraining, fiscal action, follow-up review, claim adjustment, or termination.

Audit

Governmental and nonprofit entities can be subject to Single Audit requirements and other federal, state, and local audits.

Fraud

Intentional false claims, falsified meal counts, procurement collusion, kickbacks, theft, or misuse of program funds can lead to civil, administrative, or criminal enforcement.

Record Retention

SFAs must retain applications, benefit lists, menus, production records, invoices, contracts, claims, reviews, and other documentation for the federal retention period or longer when an audit remains unresolved.

Professional Standards and Food-Service Workforce

Hiring Standards

Federal regulations establish minimum education and experience requirements for school nutrition program directors based on district size.

Annual Training

Directors, managers, full-time staff, part-time staff, and employees with program responsibilities must complete annual training hours appropriate to their roles.

Training Topics

Training can address nutrition, food safety, menu planning, procurement, eligibility, meal counting, customer service, culinary skills, civil rights, financial management, allergens, and technology.

Workforce Challenges

School nutrition programs compete for cooks, managers, dietitians, drivers, warehouse staff, cashiers, and administrators. Compensation, benefits, limited hours, physical demands, and school calendars affect recruitment and retention.

Culinary Capacity

Scratch cooking and fresh-food preparation require trained staff, adequate kitchens, time, storage, procurement systems, recipes, and equipment.

Institutional Role

School nutrition employees operate one of the country’s largest daily food-service systems and interact directly with students, families, teachers, vendors, and public agencies.

Food Safety, Allergies, and Disability Access

Food-Safety Program

Participating schools must implement a food-safety program based on Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point principles.

Health Inspections

Schools must obtain the required number of food-safety inspections and post or make the most recent inspection available as required.

Temperature and Sanitation

Programs control receiving, storage, thawing, cooking, cooling, reheating, holding, transport, service, and cleaning to prevent foodborne illness.

Food Allergies

Schools must prevent allergen exposure through accurate information, staff training, communication, cleaning, preparation procedures, and emergency response.

Disability Modifications

When a child’s disability restricts diet, the SFA must make reasonable modifications necessary for equal access. A medical statement can be requested when needed to identify the required modification.

Not a Medical Program

The NSLP provides meals under nutrition and civil-rights rules but does not replace individualized medical care or emergency planning.

Afterschool Snacks and Summer Operations

Afterschool Snack Program

Schools can receive NSLP reimbursement for snacks served through qualifying supervised afterschool care programs offering educational or enrichment activities.

Area Eligibility

Programs located in eligible low-income areas can generally claim all qualifying snacks at the free rate. Other programs claim free, reduced-price, and paid snacks according to participant eligibility.

School Year 2026–2027 Contiguous-State Snack Rates

Free snacks receive $1.30, reduced-price snacks receive $0.65, and paid snacks receive $0.12 from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027.

Seamless Summer Option

Participating SFAs can use the Seamless Summer Option to provide free summer meals under NSLP and School Breakfast Program administrative procedures in eligible areas.

Summer EBT

Summer Electronic Benefit Transfer, commonly called SUN Bucks, provides grocery benefits to qualifying children when school is not in session. School meal eligibility and school enrollment data help establish eligibility.

Separate Programs

Summer meals and Summer EBT are related to school lunch but operate under separate statutory, eligibility, reimbursement, and administrative systems.

Unpaid Meal Charges and School Meal Debt

Local Charge Policy

LEAs establish written unpaid-meal-charge policies consistent with federal and state law. Policies should explain whether students can charge meals, what meal is provided, how households are notified, and how debt is collected.

Student Protection

Federal civil-rights and overt-identification rules restrict humiliating or discriminatory practices. States increasingly prohibit meal denial, tray disposal, public identification, alternative meals, or collection directed at children.

Bad Debt

Uncollectible meal charges are bad debt and cannot be paid from the nonprofit school food-service account. The district must use nonfederal funds to restore the account.

Application Outreach

Schools can reduce debt by improving direct certification, assisting households with applications, checking categorical eligibility, offering language access, and communicating before balances grow.

CEP and Universal Meals

School-wide free-meal systems eliminate student meal debt for reimbursable meals but can create a local funding responsibility when federal reimbursement is insufficient.

Policy Debate

Meal debt illustrates the conflict between operating a financially accountable food-service system and ensuring that children are not denied or stigmatized for household inability to pay.

Civil Rights and Nondiscrimination

Federal Nondiscrimination

Participating institutions must operate the NSLP without unlawful discrimination under the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act, Title VI, Section 504, the Age Discrimination Act, Title IX where applicable, disability law, and USDA civil-rights requirements.

Protected Access

Civil-rights compliance includes:

  • Equal access to meal service
  • Reasonable modifications for disability
  • Meaningful access for limited-English-proficient households
  • Nondiscriminatory meal counting and discipline
  • Equal treatment in unpaid-meal policies
  • Public notification and complaint procedures
  • Collection and analysis of racial and ethnic participation data
  • Staff civil-rights training

Overt Identification

Students cannot be separated, publicly listed, given visibly different credentials, required to perform work, or otherwise identified because they receive free or reduced-price meals.

Complaints

Students and households can file civil-rights complaints with USDA and other responsible agencies. Schools must display required nondiscrimination information and inform the public of complaint procedures.

Disability

A school cannot charge a child with a disability more for a required meal modification when the modification is necessary to provide equal access.

Religious Schools

Eligible nonprofit religious schools can participate subject to federal program rules and constitutional limits. Participation does not authorize religious discrimination in federally funded meal service.

African American Communities and Racial Context

Segregated Implementation

The 1946 program entered a public education system that was legally segregated across much of the South and residentially segregated throughout the nation. Federal lunch assistance did not itself desegregate schools.

Black schools frequently operated with inferior buildings, kitchens, cafeterias, equipment, tax bases, and local appropriations. A formally national meal program could therefore produce unequal local service when administered through unequal school systems.

Local Discretion

Early state and local administration gave officials substantial influence over which schools participated, how local matching resources were supplied, what facilities were available, and how children unable to pay were treated.

Civil-Rights Enforcement

Brown v. Board of Education, Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, federal school-desegregation litigation, and USDA civil-rights enforcement increased federal leverage over discriminatory school systems receiving meal funds.

Low-Income Access

African American children have been disproportionately represented among students eligible for free and reduced-price meals because of racial inequalities in employment, wages, wealth, housing, school finance, and neighborhood opportunity.

Stigma

Separate lunch tickets, special lines, public lists, cash-register signals, alternative meals, and debt collection could expose low-income students. Federal overt-identification rules and school-wide free-meal options seek to reduce that stigma.

Community Eligibility

CEP has particular significance in high-poverty urban and rural schools serving substantial Black populations. It can eliminate applications and meal charges while increasing participation.

School Segregation

Residential and school segregation affect program concentration. Schools serving high shares of low-income Black children can receive enhanced reimbursement and CEP access but may also face weaker local tax bases, aging kitchens, staffing shortages, and limited food-service infrastructure.

Black Farmers and Food Procurement

Farm-to-school, local purchasing, cooperatives, and targeted technical assistance can connect school food purchasing with Black farmers and food businesses. Ordinary federal procurement rules do not guarantee that outcome.

Universal Meals and Equity

Universal school meal policies can reduce racialized stigma and administrative exclusion. Their equity effect depends on adequate funding, meal quality, student participation, culturally relevant menus, and equal treatment across schools.

Benefits and Documented Outcomes

Food Security

Free and reduced-price school meals increase reliable access to food for children from households with limited income.

Diet Quality

Research generally finds that reimbursable school lunches provide stronger nutritional quality than many lunches obtained from other sources, particularly after implementation of updated meal standards.

Academic Readiness

Reducing hunger supports attention, behavior, attendance, and readiness to learn, although academic outcomes also depend on many educational and household factors.

Health

Meal standards can increase fruit, vegetable, whole-grain, dairy, and nutrient intake while limiting sodium, saturated fat, trans fat, and added sugars.

Household Budget

A school lunch provided without charge reduces the amount a household must spend or prepare for a child’s midday meal.

School Operations

CEP and universal-meal systems can reduce cashier transactions, applications, debt collection, and benefit-category distinctions.

Agricultural Demand

USDA Foods, local procurement, and ordinary school purchasing create a large market for domestic agriculture and food manufacturing.

Emergency Capacity

School nutrition departments form part of the national emergency food-delivery infrastructure, as demonstrated during natural disasters and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Limits of Evidence

Program effects vary by household need, participation, meal quality, school implementation, student acceptance, and comparison group. Access to a meal does not ensure consumption of every component.

Operational Challenges

Food Costs

Inflation, regional prices, supply disruptions, transportation, dietary modifications, and domestic sourcing affect meal cost.

Labor

Programs face shortages, wage competition, benefit costs, training needs, physical demands, and limited staffing hours.

Kitchen Infrastructure

Schools built around heat-and-serve operations may lack ovens, refrigeration, preparation space, ventilation, storage, dishwashing, and electrical capacity for scratch cooking.

Procurement

Small districts can lack purchasing power and administrative staff. Large districts manage complex bids, distribution systems, contracts, and product specifications.

Student Acceptance

A technically compliant meal produces limited benefit when students do not select or consume it. Taste, appearance, menu choice, culture, time, cafeteria environment, and scheduling matter.

Lunch Period Length

Long lines and short lunch periods can reduce consumption and participation. Recess before lunch can improve eating conditions in some elementary schools.

Food Waste

Waste can result from unwanted foods, rushed service, unfamiliar recipes, required portions, packaging, production error, or variable attendance.

Administrative Burden

Eligibility, verification, menu documentation, procurement, civil rights, claims, USDA Foods, wellness policy, professional standards, and reviews require substantial administrative capacity.

Local Funding

Reimbursement may not cover the full cost of wages, benefits, facilities, debt, and high-quality ingredients. District and state support varies.

Major Policy Debates

Universal or Means-Tested Meals

Supporters of universal meals emphasize access, dignity, participation, administrative simplicity, and recognition of meals as part of education. Critics emphasize federal cost, targeting, local responsibility, and the value of directing larger subsidies toward children with lower incomes.

Nutrition Standards and Local Flexibility

National standards promote consistency and public health. Local operators argue that rigid or rapidly changing standards can increase cost, waste, procurement difficulty, and student rejection.

Whole Milk and Dietary Fat

Supporters of expanded milk options emphasize student consumption, dairy nutrients, preference, and reduced waste. Critics emphasize saturated fat and consistency with dietary guidance.

Sodium Reduction

Public-health organizations support gradual sodium reduction. Schools and manufacturers emphasize product availability, taste, student acceptance, and time needed to reformulate foods.

Added Sugars

Added-sugar limits seek to improve diet quality while preserving school participation. Debate concerns flavored milk, cereal, yogurt, menu design, and food-industry reformulation.

Federal Reimbursement Adequacy

School nutrition organizations argue that reimbursement should reflect the full cost of food, labor, equipment, and compliance. Federal rate formulas rely principally on consumer-price measures rather than each district’s actual cost.

Paid Lunch Equity

The rule protects federal low-income subsidies from supporting paid meals, but districts argue that it can force price increases or administrative calculations when local governments intentionally subsidize all students.

USDA Foods and Agricultural Policy

Commodity assistance lowers costs and supports farmers but can shape menus around available products, processing arrangements, and agricultural-policy priorities.

Meal Debt

Districts must collect revenue while protecting children from hunger and humiliation. State laws vary widely.

Privatization

Food-service management companies can supply expertise, purchasing, and labor systems. Critics raise concerns about profit incentives, food quality, workforce conditions, contract oversight, and public control.

Key Distinctions

National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program

The NSLP reimburses lunches and qualifying afterschool snacks under the School Lunch Act. The School Breakfast Program reimburses breakfasts under the Child Nutrition Act of 1966.

NSLP and SNAP

The NSLP provides prepared meals through schools. SNAP provides household benefits for food purchases through authorized retailers. SNAP participation can directly certify children for free school meals.

NSLP and Summer EBT

The NSLP serves meals through schools during program operations. Summer EBT provides grocery benefits to qualifying households during summer months.

NSLP and Summer Food Service Program

The NSLP is a school-year meal program with a Seamless Summer Option. The Summer Food Service Program is a separate federal program for free summer meals in eligible communities.

Free Meal and Universal Free Meal

An individually free meal is claimed for a certified student. A universal free meal is served without charge to every student through CEP, Provision 2 or 3, or state and local funding.

Community Eligibility and State Universal Meals

CEP is a federal reimbursement option based on directly identified students. State universal-meal laws use state funds to cover federal reimbursement gaps or provide no-charge meals beyond CEP eligibility.

Reimbursable Meal and A La Carte Food

A reimbursable meal contains the required federal components. A la carte items are sold separately and do not receive NSLP meal reimbursement.

Meal Pattern and Dietary Guidelines

The Dietary Guidelines provide national nutrition recommendations. The meal pattern is the legally enforceable USDA rule governing school meals.

USDA Foods and Federal Reimbursement

USDA Foods provide food-value assistance. Cash reimbursement is a separate per-meal payment.

Income Eligibility and Direct Certification

Income eligibility uses household-reported financial information. Direct certification relies on public-benefit or categorical records without a meal application.

Geographic Scope

The NSLP operates in every state, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, and other participating jurisdictions under applicable federal arrangements.

Administration varies by jurisdiction. Most states use an education agency, while some use agriculture or other departments. Tribal schools can participate through state or federal administrative structures depending on school governance.

Federal reimbursement rates are higher in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands because of higher food-service costs. Income guidelines are also adjusted for Alaska and Hawaii.

Rural schools face transportation, workforce, purchasing, enrollment, and distance challenges. Urban districts manage scale, complex procurement, centralized kitchens, high student need, and large labor systems.

State universal-meal policies create geographic differences in student charges. In some states, every public-school student receives meals without charge; in others, access depends on individual certification or CEP participation.

School-level participation varies with menu quality, schedule, stigma, meal price, grade, local income, open-campus policies, cafeteria capacity, and competing food options.

Legal Context

Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act

The Act establishes the federal policy, appropriations, state payments, USDA Foods, nutrition standards, free and reduced-price eligibility, direct certification, summer meals, child-care meals, afterschool snacks, procurement, evaluation, and program-integrity framework.

Child Nutrition Act of 1966

The Child Nutrition Act establishes the School Breakfast Program, Special Milk Program, state administrative expenses, WIC, food-service equipment and training authorities, and related child-nutrition provisions.

Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010

Public Law 111-296 strengthened meal standards, created CEP, expanded direct certification, regulated competitive foods, established professional standards, increased reimbursement, and revised program integrity.

Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025

Public Law 119-69 amended the School Lunch Act to permit whole and reduced-fat milk and directed USDA to revise implementing regulations.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits race, color, and national-origin discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination and requires reasonable modifications and program accessibility.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II applies to public schools and public entities. Other provisions can apply to covered private institutions and services.

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972

Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in federally funded education programs, subject to current statutes, regulations, judicial decisions, and federal policy.

Age Discrimination Act of 1975

The Act prohibits specified age discrimination in federally assisted programs.

McKinney–Vento Homeless Assistance Act

Children identified as homeless under federal education law receive categorical access to free school meals.

Individuals with Disabilities Education Act

IDEA can interact with meal modifications when nutrition or feeding requirements form part of a child’s special education or related services.

Summary

The National School Lunch Program is the permanent federal system supporting nutritionally regulated lunches in public schools, nonprofit private schools, and residential child care institutions. Congress established it in 1946 after decades of local school feeding, Depression-era surplus distribution, WPA labor, and wartime concern about child nutrition.

The program operates through a federal–state–local partnership. USDA establishes national rules and provides reimbursement and food assistance. State agencies approve and monitor local operators. School food authorities purchase food, plan menus, certify students, serve meals, count meals, claim reimbursement, manage finances, and protect civil rights.

Every qualifying lunch receives federal support. Children generally qualify for free meals at or below 130 percent of the poverty guidelines and reduced-price meals at or below 185 percent. Direct certification, categorical eligibility, household applications, and school-wide alternatives establish access.

The Community Eligibility Provision allows eligible high-poverty schools to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every student. It reduces paperwork, stigma, and debt but can require state or local funding when the federal reimbursement formula does not cover total cost.

Reimbursable lunches offer fruits, vegetables, grains, meats or meat alternates, and fluid milk. Weekly menus must satisfy grade-based quantities, calories, and nutrient limits. Current standards include product-level added-sugar limits, sodium controls, whole-grain requirements, and expanded milk options enacted in 2026.

For school year 2026–2027, ordinary contiguous-state free-lunch payments generally total $4.76 or $4.78 before the nine-cent performance supplement and the thirty-two-cent USDA Foods value. Annual rates change with inflation, geography, and federal law.

The NSLP reaches roughly thirty million children on a typical school day and serves approximately five billion lunches annually. It reduces food hardship, supports household budgets and educational readiness, shapes national child nutrition, and provides a major institutional market for American agriculture.

The program’s history also reflects unequal American schools. It operated through segregated and underfunded systems before modern civil-rights enforcement. Current policy must still address racial and economic inequality, stigma, meal debt, school segregation, local funding, and unequal kitchen capacity.

The continuing effectiveness of the NSLP depends on adequate reimbursement, accurate eligibility, strong civil-rights enforcement, trained workers, modern kitchens, responsible procurement, culturally acceptable menus, student participation, food safety, transparent administration, and a balance between national nutrition standards and local operating realities.
