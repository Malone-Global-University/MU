Countering Violent Extremism Program

The United States federal prevention framework that sought to reduce ideologically motivated violence through community engagement, research, training, local intervention, strategic communications, and grants before evolving into the broader Targeted Violence and Terrorism Prevention system.

Terminology Note

Countering Violent Extremism, commonly abbreviated CVE, described a collection of federal strategies, agency initiatives, local pilot frameworks, research projects, training programs, community partnerships, and grants rather than one permanent statutory entitlement.

The phrase Countering Violent Extremism Program can refer specifically to the Department of Homeland Security’s Fiscal Year 2016 Countering Violent Extremism Grant Program.

The named FY2016 grant program awarded funds in 2017 for a performance period generally ending in 2019. It is not the current name of the principal DHS prevention grant.

DHS later adopted the terms Targeted Violence and Terrorism Prevention, or TVTP, and Center for Prevention Programs and Partnerships, or CP3.

CVE prevention was officially distinguished from criminal counterterrorism investigation and prosecution. Prevention sought to act before criminal preparation or violence, while counterterrorism used intelligence, investigation, disruption, arrest, prosecution, and other security authorities.

The distinction was contested because some CVE projects involved law-enforcement partners, information sharing, threat assessment, referrals, or communities already subject to counterterrorism surveillance.

Extremist belief is not itself a federal crime. The First Amendment generally protects political, religious, and ideological expression unless conduct or speech falls within a recognised unprotected category or supports a prosecutable offence.

This fact sheet primarily addresses domestic federal CVE. The Department of State, United States Agency for International Development, Department of Defense, and international partners also used CVE terminology for overseas diplomacy, development, security assistance, messaging, rehabilitation, and counterterrorism.

Overview

Domestic CVE emerged from post‑September 11 concern that individuals in the United States could be inspired, recruited, or mobilised by foreign terrorist organisations or domestic violent movements.

Its principal theory was that government and community partners should intervene before a person begins operational planning or commits a crime.

Federal agencies sought to build local awareness, strengthen protective factors, improve referral systems, support credible community voices, develop research, train practitioners, and reduce access to violent extremist propaganda.

The framework emphasised local design rather than one national intervention model.

Potential partners included police, public-health agencies, educators, mental-health professionals, youth organisations, religious institutions, civil-rights groups, families, technology companies, researchers, and former extremists.

CVE became controversial because it attempted to identify risk before crime, when indicators are uncertain and many behaviours associated with political or religious change are constitutionally protected.

The program’s history is therefore both a history of violence prevention and a history of surveillance, discrimination, community trust, federalism, predictive uncertainty, and constitutional boundaries.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because federal agencies created formal strategies, offices, task forces, grants, performance requirements, technical assistance, and intergovernmental partnerships.

The Department of Homeland Security, Department of Justice, Federal Bureau of Investigation, National Counterterrorism Center, Center for Prevention Programs and Partnerships, and local prevention providers belong under Institutions.

Domestic terrorism, radicalisation, mobilisation to violence, targeted violence, threat assessment, and public-health prevention belong under Concepts and Terminology.

The Boston Marathon bombing, Charleston church massacre, San Bernardino attack, Orlando nightclub attack, Charlottesville violence, El Paso shooting, Pittsburgh synagogue shooting, and January 6 attack are related events that shaped prevention debates.

This page does not classify particular political, religious, racial, or social beliefs as inherently violent.

Post‑September 11 Context

The September 11 attacks produced major expansions of intelligence, law enforcement, military, border, aviation, financial, and information-sharing authorities.

Early federal counterterrorism concentrated on identifying networks, disrupting plots, prosecuting material support, securing borders, and conducting overseas military operations.

Later attacks and disrupted plots involving United States residents increased attention to individual radicalisation, online recruitment, and small autonomous cells.

Policymakers sought a preventive approach that would operate before a criminal case existed.

Community-Engagement Origins

DHS and DOJ conducted outreach with Arab, Muslim, Sikh, South Asian, immigrant, refugee, and other communities before the formal 2011 strategy.

Engagement addressed civil-rights complaints, hate crimes, immigration, travel, religious accommodation, law-enforcement training, and national-security concerns.

Officials argued that trust could help communities identify genuine threats and resist recruitment.

Critics argued that outreach could become a means of intelligence collection or impose collective responsibility on communities defined by religion or ethnicity.

2011 Domestic CVE Strategy

The Obama administration released Empowering Local Partners to Prevent Violent Extremism in the United States on August 3, 2011.

It was described as the first national strategy devoted to preventing violent extremism inside the United States.

The strategy assigned the federal government a supporting role and placed local communities at the centre of prevention.

It sought to provide information, improve coordination, build government expertise, support local programs, and challenge violent extremist propaganda.

Strategic Goal

The strategy sought to prevent violent extremists and their supporters from inspiring, radicalising, financing, or recruiting people in the United States to commit violence.

It rejected broad suppression of beliefs and stated that the Constitution protects freedom of speech, religion, association, and peaceful political activity.

Prevention was intended to focus on mobilisation toward violence rather than unpopular ideology alone.

Guiding Principles

The 2011 strategy emphasised principles including:

  • protecting civil rights and civil liberties;
  • avoiding stigmatisation of communities;
  • building on existing community safety and crime-prevention work;
  • supporting locally appropriate solutions;
  • using a broad range of governmental and nongovernmental partners;
  • separating lawful beliefs from violence; and
  • recognising that no single profile predicts violent mobilisation.

2011 Strategic Implementation Plan

The White House released the initial Strategic Implementation Plan in December 2011.

The plan translated broad strategy into agency responsibilities, milestones, and tasks.

GAO later identified forty-four domestically focused implementation tasks.

The work centred on outreach, research and training, and local prevention capacity.

Community Outreach

Federal agencies convened meetings, roundtables, briefings, town halls, and advisory processes with local leaders.

Outreach sought to explain threats, hear grievances, correct misinformation, connect communities to government services, and identify prevention partners.

Effective outreach required transparency about whether officials attended for civil-rights engagement, community service, intelligence, investigation, or prevention.

Research and Training

Federal research examined radicalisation pathways, online networks, intervention, community resilience, disengagement, recidivism, strategic communications, and program evaluation.

Training targeted law enforcement, educators, social workers, mental-health providers, community leaders, and federal personnel.

Weak or biased training materials created a recurring risk of equating religious practice, political anger, cultural identity, or dissent with terrorism.

Local Capacity Building

Capacity building sought to create durable local systems rather than isolated federal campaigns.

Activities included multidisciplinary teams, referral protocols, practitioner networks, training curricula, online resources, evaluation tools, and community-led programming.

Local adaptation allowed flexibility but produced inconsistent definitions, safeguards, documentation, and quality.

Prevention versus Counterterrorism

Federal strategy described CVE as preventive and distinct from collecting evidence or conducting criminal investigations.

Counterterrorism begins from intelligence, threats, suspected offences, or national-security authorities and can lead to surveillance, arrest, prosecution, sanctions, or military action.

CVE sought to act earlier through education, services, voluntary intervention, and protective community systems.

The practical boundary could become unclear when law enforcement sat on prevention teams or received information from social-service partners.

No New Federal Crime

CVE policy did not create a federal crime of holding extremist beliefs or being considered vulnerable to radicalisation.

Criminal liability continued to depend on existing offences such as violence, threats, conspiracy, weapons violations, material support, civil-rights offences, or other unlawful conduct.

A prevention referral was not itself a criminal conviction or formal terrorist designation.

Violent Extremism

Federal documents used varying definitions, generally connecting ideological, political, religious, racial, or social motivation with violence.

GAO observed that federal stakeholders did not always use consistent terminology.

Ambiguous definitions complicated program scope, data collection, public understanding, and civil-liberties safeguards.

Radicalisation

Radicalisation generally describes movement toward increasingly extreme beliefs or support for political violence.

Belief change is not a linear or uniform process, and most people holding radical ideas never commit violence.

Critics argued that simplified radicalisation models could turn ordinary religious observance, political advocacy, identity exploration, or personal distress into false warning signs.

Mobilisation to Violence

Later prevention frameworks increasingly distinguished radical beliefs from mobilisation toward violent action.

Mobilisation indicators can include concrete planning, weapons acquisition, target research, leakage of violent intent, travel, operational security, or direct facilitation.

Even behavioural indicators require contextual assessment and cannot be treated as automatic proof of criminal intent.

Local Pilot Regions

The federal government supported locally designed prevention frameworks in Greater Boston, Los Angeles, and the Minneapolis–Saint Paul region.

Related prevention planning also occurred in Montgomery County, Maryland, and other jurisdictions.

The pilots were not identical. Each selected different partners, priorities, terminology, governance, and relationships with law enforcement.

Greater Boston Framework

Beginning in 2014, Greater Boston stakeholders developed a multidisciplinary framework with support from DOJ, FBI, DHS, NCTC, and the United States Attorney’s Office.

The framework treated CVE as prevention and intervention rather than law-enforcement suppression.

It drew on public health, mental health, youth violence prevention, social services, education, and community organisations.

The framework stated that it did not focus on one ideology or target one community.

Los Angeles Framework

Los Angeles developed a framework involving local government, law enforcement, community organisations, educators, service providers, and federal partners.

Its stated pillars included prevention, intervention, community engagement, civil rights, and locally appropriate program design.

The framework sought to build resilience while preserving lawful speech and religious practice.

Twin Cities Framework

Minneapolis and Saint Paul received federal attention because young Somali Americans had travelled or attempted to travel to join al‑Shabaab and later the Islamic State.

Local activities included youth opportunity, mentoring, education, community engagement, law-enforcement partnership, and prevention planning.

Critics argued that the focus reinforced suspicion of Somali and Muslim communities and treated social disadvantage as a terrorism indicator.

Montgomery County Model

Montgomery County, Maryland, developed a community-led model associated with the World Organization for Resource Development and Education.

The program emphasised civic engagement, service-provider networks, community awareness, referrals, and relationships with public safety.

A federally supported evaluation reported positive changes across several measured outcomes, while broader questions remained about prediction, replication, and long-term violence reduction.

2015 White House Summit

The White House convened a three-day Summit on Countering Violent Extremism in February 2015.

Domestic sessions addressed community prevention, local pilot frameworks, youth, online propaganda, religious and civic leadership, and law-enforcement partnership.

International sessions brought together representatives from nearly seventy countries, international organisations, civil society, and the private sector.

The summit elevated CVE globally but intensified criticism that Muslim communities were being asked to disprove collective suspicion.

Foreign-Fighter Context

The Syrian civil war and rise of the Islamic State produced concern about people travelling abroad to join terrorist groups and returning with training or contacts.

United Nations Security Council Resolution 2178 identified CVE as part of the international response to foreign terrorist fighters.

Domestic prevention focused on recruitment, travel, online inspiration, peer networks, and family or community intervention.

Online Propaganda and Recruitment

Violent organisations and individual propagandists used social media, video, forums, encrypted applications, and gaming spaces to distribute narratives and identify recruits.

CVE initiatives supported digital literacy, alternative messaging, credible messengers, intervention advertising, referral resources, and research into online mobilisation.

Content moderation and government-platform cooperation created concerns involving protected speech, transparency, algorithmic error, and private censorship.

Strategic Communications

Strategic communications sought to undermine violent propaganda, expose manipulation, elevate victims and defectors, and provide nonviolent channels for identity and political participation.

Federal officials increasingly preferred supporting independent credible voices over direct government messaging.

Government-sponsored messages could lack credibility or place community speakers at personal and reputational risk.

Global Engagement Center

The Department of State established the Global Engagement Center in 2016 to coordinate efforts against foreign terrorist propaganda and later foreign state disinformation.

Its international mission was related to but legally and administratively distinct from domestic DHS community prevention.

Office for Community Partnerships

DHS established the Office for Community Partnerships in 2015 to coordinate department-wide CVE engagement and support local prevention.

The office developed partnerships, technical assistance, grant policy, research priorities, and communications.

Its creation gave CVE an institutional home within DHS but did not create independent investigative authority.

Interagency CVE Task Force

The federal government announced the CVE Task Force on January 8, 2016.

DHS hosted the permanent interagency body, and DHS and DOJ provided overall leadership.

FBI, NCTC, and other agencies assigned personnel.

The Task Force was intended to coordinate policy, communications, research, technical assistance, intervention work, and evaluation across agencies.

Task Force Functions

  • Coordinate federal CVE programs and policy.
  • Support local prevention networks.
  • Develop intervention and referral practices.
  • Improve research and performance measurement.
  • Coordinate online and communications work.
  • Share lessons across federal departments.
  • Promote civil-rights and civil-liberties safeguards.

2016 Strategic Implementation Plan

The Obama administration issued an updated Strategic Implementation Plan in October 2016.

The update reflected local pilot experience, the Islamic State’s online recruitment, intervention development, research needs, and creation of the Task Force.

It organised federal work around engagement and technical assistance, interventions, research and analysis, communications, and digital strategy.

Fiscal Year 2016 Appropriation

Congress provided DHS with $50 million to address emergent threats involving violent extremism and complex coordinated terrorist attacks.

DHS designated $10 million for the CVE Grant Program, $1 million for a joint counterterrorism workshop, and $39 million for the Complex Coordinated Terrorist Attacks Grant Program.

FY2016 CVE Grant Program

DHS announced the competitive grant opportunity in July 2016.

The program sought to help state and local governments and nongovernmental organisations reduce risk factors associated with violent extremism.

FEMA provided grant-administration support, while the DHS community-partnership office supplied program expertise.

Awards were intended to test locally developed approaches and generate evidence for future prevention work.

Eligible Applicants

Eligible applicants included qualifying:

  • state governments;
  • local governments;
  • Tribal governments;
  • territorial governments;
  • nonprofit organisations;
  • institutions of higher education; and
  • public agencies acting within the grant notice.

Applicants could propose partnerships with police, schools, public-health agencies, service providers, faith organisations, researchers, and community groups.

Grant Focus Areas

The funding notice supported projects involving:

  • building resilience;
  • training and engaging communities;
  • managing intervention activities;
  • challenging violent extremist narratives;
  • developing organisational capacity; and
  • supporting research-informed prevention models.

Initial January 2017 Award Announcement

DHS announced its intention to fund thirty-one applications in January 2017.

The selections included governmental, university, nonprofit, law-enforcement, and community-based projects.

The incoming administration suspended the selections for further review.

Revised June 2017 Awards

DHS applied additional selection considerations and issued twenty-six final awards totalling $10 million.

Nineteen of the thirty-one applicants announced in January remained, while seven new applicants received awards.

GAO later concluded that DHS had not adequately documented how the revised award decisions followed the process described in the original announcement.

Award-Selection Controversy

The revised process changed the ideological and organisational composition of funded projects.

Some community organisations withdrew or declined funding because they believed federal participation would damage trust.

Civil-rights groups argued that the grant structure pressured social-service and religious organisations to participate in counterterrorism.

GAO focused on transparency, documentation, and adherence to announced grant criteria rather than deciding which ideological threats should receive priority.

Grant Performance Period

The twenty-six awards generally operated during a two-year period from 2017 through 2019.

Grantees submitted progress and performance reports describing activities, outputs, milestones, and intended outcomes.

Several projects received extensions, and some grantees withdrew before completing the full period.

Types of Grantees

Funded entities included:

  • state and local public-safety agencies;
  • police departments;
  • universities and research centres;
  • youth and community organisations;
  • faith-linked organisations;
  • nonprofit violence-prevention groups;
  • online communications organisations; and
  • institutions supporting intervention and reintegration.

Community Resilience

Resilience projects sought to strengthen relationships, civic participation, youth opportunity, conflict resolution, media literacy, social connection, and access to services.

The theory was that individuals embedded in supportive communities would be less vulnerable to violent recruitment.

Critics warned that broad social problems such as unemployment, alienation, discrimination, or family conflict are not reliable terrorism predictors.

Individual Intervention Programs

Intervention sought to support a person believed to be moving toward violence but not yet appropriate for criminal prosecution.

Services could include counselling, mental-health treatment, mentoring, education, employment, family support, religious consultation, substance-use treatment, and safety planning.

Effective intervention required voluntary participation where possible, informed consent, confidentiality rules, qualified professionals, risk management, and clear thresholds for emergency reporting.

Referral Systems

Community members, families, schools, clinicians, or police could identify a concern and refer a person to a multidisciplinary team.

Referral alone did not establish guilt or prove violent intent.

Systems needed criteria for acceptance, consent, information sharing, law-enforcement escalation, record retention, reassessment, and case closure.

Behavioural Threat Assessment and Management

Later prevention programs increasingly used behavioural threat assessment and management rather than ideology-specific radicalisation profiles.

Multidisciplinary teams assess concerning behaviour, context, capability, stressors, protective factors, targets, and pathways to violence.

Management can include services, monitoring, security measures, communication, and law-enforcement action when legally justified.

Threat assessment does not predict violence with certainty.

Public-Health Prevention Model

Some CVE practitioners used a public-health framework involving universal prevention, targeted prevention, and individual intervention.

Universal prevention addresses broad protective factors without labelling particular people as threats.

Targeted and indicated interventions focus on escalating risk or concerning behaviour.

Critics objected when public-health language obscured law-enforcement purposes or treated ideology as disease.

Mental Health

Mental illness alone does not cause terrorism or targeted violence, and most people with mental-health conditions are not violent.

Clinicians can address crisis, trauma, depression, psychosis, substance use, social isolation, and other needs relevant to an individual case.

Prevention programs must avoid stigmatising disability and must preserve professional ethics, confidentiality, and clinical independence.

Schools and Youth Programs

Schools can support belonging, conflict resolution, digital literacy, bullying prevention, counselling, and threat assessment.

CVE involvement raised concerns that teachers and counsellors could become national-security informants or interpret political and religious development as risk.

Youth programs require age-appropriate safeguards, parental rights, nondiscrimination, educational privacy, and clear limits on law-enforcement referrals.

Faith Communities

Religious leaders can provide counselling, theological knowledge, community legitimacy, family support, and alternatives to violent interpretations.

Partnership can also place congregations under collective suspicion or create pressure to police protected belief.

Participation should not condition ordinary government services on cooperation with counterterrorism.

Law-Enforcement Role

Police and federal agents contributed threat information, emergency response, legal authority, training, and public-safety experience.

Law-enforcement involvement could help manage credible danger but also deter community members from seeking assistance.

Prevention and investigation roles require separate records, authorities, disclosure rules, and accountability.

Community Policing

Federal agencies promoted community-policing practices as a basis for prevention partnerships.

Community policing emphasises problem solving, trust, local relationships, and shared responsibility for safety.

Critics argued that adding terrorism-prevention objectives could convert routine engagement into intelligence gathering.

Former Extremists and Credible Messengers

Former members of violent movements can explain recruitment, group dynamics, disengagement, and reintegration.

Their testimony can challenge propaganda and assist interventions.

Programs must verify backgrounds, avoid glorification, manage safety, compensate transparently, and recognise that one person’s experience is not universally representative.

Prisons, Probation, and Reentry

CVE planning considered radicalisation, recruitment, rehabilitation, supervision, and reintegration in correctional settings.

GAO found in 2017 that planned federal work on prisons and learning from former extremists remained incomplete.

Correctional programs must protect religious exercise, attorney access, due process, and equal treatment while responding to actual threats.

First Amendment

The First Amendment protects religious exercise, speech, press, assembly, petition, and association.

Advocacy of radical, revolutionary, racist, religious, nationalist, anti-government, anarchist, or other controversial ideas is generally protected unless it crosses a recognised legal boundary.

Government cannot lawfully treat ideology, religious observance, criticism of foreign policy, or peaceful protest as proof of violent intent.

CVE programs therefore required behaviour-based thresholds and viewpoint-neutral administration.

Unprotected Conduct and Criminal Liability

The Constitution does not protect true threats, criminal solicitation, conspiracy, material support, assault, weapons offences, or intentional incitement satisfying the governing legal standard.

Whether speech is criminal depends on statutory elements, intent, context, imminence, coordination, and evidence.

A prevention practitioner should not independently convert ambiguous expression into a criminal conclusion.

Domestic Terrorism Law

Federal law defines domestic terrorism in 18 U.S.C. § 2331(5), but the definition is not a general standalone charging offence.

Domestic violent acts are prosecuted through underlying crimes such as homicide, assault, arson, explosives, weapons, civil-rights offences, conspiracy, threats, or destruction of property.

Federal investigative labels do not by themselves establish guilt.

Material Support Statutes

Federal law criminalises specified material support for terrorism and for designated foreign terrorist organisations.

The foreign-organisation statute does not create an equivalent designation regime for domestic political organisations.

Independent advocacy and coordinated support are legally distinct under Supreme Court precedent.

Religious Freedom

CVE initiatives operated within constitutional free-exercise protections, the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, and federal nondiscrimination requirements.

Religious dress, dietary practice, mosque attendance, conversion, increased devotion, theological debate, and religious travel cannot be treated as automatic violence indicators.

Government partnership with selected religious leaders also raises establishment, favouritism, and representativeness concerns.

Equal Protection and Nondiscrimination

Federal action is constrained by the Fifth Amendment’s equal-protection principles, while state and local partners are subject to the Fourteenth Amendment.

Federally funded programs must also comply with applicable civil-rights statutes and grant conditions.

Programs cannot lawfully select participants or communities solely because of race, ethnicity, national origin, or religion.

Privacy and Information Governance

Prevention programs could collect identity, family, education, health, religion, online activity, associations, grievances, and threat-related information.

Collection required a defined purpose, lawful authority, data minimisation, access controls, retention limits, correction procedures, and disclosure rules.

DHS issued a Privacy Impact Assessment for the FY2016 grant program.

Local grantees also had to follow state privacy, education, health, juvenile, and public-records law.

Health and Counselling Confidentiality

Mental-health, substance-use, school, and social-service records can be protected by federal and state confidentiality laws.

Confidentiality is not absolute where an imminent threat, abuse, court order, or other legal exception applies.

Programs needed to tell participants when information could be shared with police or federal agencies.

Criminal-Intelligence Systems

State and local criminal-intelligence systems receiving certain federal assistance are subject to 28 C.F.R. Part 23.

Criminal intelligence generally requires reasonable suspicion of criminal activity and cannot be maintained solely because of lawful political, religious, or social views.

A broad community referral database should not silently become a criminal-intelligence file.

Civil-Rights and Civil-Liberties Training

DHS civil-rights personnel reviewed training, provided guidance, and later required TVTP grantees to receive privacy and civil-liberties instruction.

Training addressed protected activity, bias, religious practice, information sharing, disability, language access, and lawful referral standards.

Formal training did not eliminate concerns about local implementation or implicit bias.

Anti-Muslim-Discrimination Controversy

Muslim civil-rights organisations and other critics argued that domestic CVE focused disproportionately on American Muslims despite official claims of ideology neutrality.

Pilot locations, federal messaging, Islamic State recruitment concerns, mosque outreach, and Muslim-led grants reinforced that perception.

Critics contended that the program cast ordinary Muslims as a population requiring national-security monitoring.

Supporters responded that Muslim organisations were partners and that later projects addressed white-supremacist, anti-government, and other violence.

Racial, Ethnic, and Religious Profiling

Profiling occurs when identity substitutes for reliable evidence of threat.

Arab, South Asian, Somali, Black Muslim, refugee, immigrant, and convert communities faced overlapping risks of national-origin, racial, and religious suspicion.

A lawful prevention model must evaluate individual behaviour and context rather than demographic proxies.

Black Muslim Communities

Black Muslims experienced both anti-Black policing and post‑September 11 Muslim surveillance.

CVE programs could overlook the distinct histories of Black religious movements, prison conversion, civil-rights activism, police surveillance, and community self-protection.

Treating Muslim communities as a single immigrant population obscured these differences.

Somali American Communities

Federal concern about al‑Shabaab and Islamic State recruitment brought intensive attention to Somali communities in Minnesota.

Youth opportunity and community services received new resources, but CVE branding could associate an entire refugee population with terrorism.

Community trust was affected by prosecutions, informants, travel cases, media narratives, and disagreement among local leaders.

Securitisation of Social Services

Securitisation occurs when education, mental health, youth work, religious counselling, or public health becomes organised around national-security objectives.

Federal funding could expand valuable services while changing how providers viewed clients and shared information.

Critics argued that people might avoid assistance if seeking help could trigger a terrorism referral.

The Prediction Problem

Violent attacks are rare, pathways are diverse, and many potential indicators are common among nonviolent people.

A model can therefore generate large numbers of false positives even when it appears statistically accurate.

No checklist can reliably determine that a person will commit ideologically motivated violence.

Prevention should combine evidence, professional judgement, contextual behaviour, safeguards, and continuing reassessment.

Risk and Protective Factors

Research examines grievance, trauma, social isolation, identity conflict, violent networks, previous violence, weapons access, propaganda exposure, and personal crisis.

Protective factors can include family connection, trusted relationships, stable housing, employment, education, coping skills, nonviolent political participation, and access to care.

Risk factors are not causes, and protective factors do not guarantee safety.

Community Trust

Prevention depends on people being willing to seek help, report credible danger, and cooperate during emergencies.

Trust can be damaged by secret surveillance, discriminatory enforcement, informant recruitment, immigration consequences, broken confidentiality, or misleading outreach.

Transparent authority, independent community leadership, service access unrelated to intelligence, and complaint procedures can reduce harm.

Voluntary Participation

Programs described as voluntary can still involve pressure when the alternative is police investigation, school discipline, immigration consequences, employment loss, or prosecution.

Informed consent should identify the team, program purpose, records, information-sharing rules, possible law-enforcement involvement, and right to withdraw.

Emergency and court-ordered interventions require separate legal authority.

Due Process

A person labelled at risk may face consequences without being charged with a crime.

Fair procedures can include notice where safe, factual correction, periodic review, defined exit criteria, nondiscriminatory standards, and independent oversight.

Secret watchlisting or intelligence processes should not be conflated with voluntary community intervention.

Performance Measurement

Prevention success is difficult to measure because the desired outcome is an event that does not occur.

Simple counts of meetings, trainings, referrals, videos, or participants measure activity rather than reduced violence.

Strong evaluation requires theories of change, baseline data, consistent definitions, comparison, follow-up, participant outcomes, adverse-effect monitoring, and privacy protection.

GAO Review of Federal Strategy in 2017

GAO assessed forty-four tasks in the 2011 implementation plan.

By December 2016, nineteen were implemented, twenty-three were in progress, and two had not begun.

GAO concluded that the federal government lacked a cohesive strategy with measurable outcomes and a process for evaluating the collective effort.

DHS and DOJ agreed with recommendations to strengthen strategy and assessment.

GAO Review of the CVE Grant Program in 2021

GAO found that DHS followed general grant-announcement and application-review guidance but did not adequately document the basis for its final revised award decisions.

GAO also found that grantees did not provide the consistent performance information needed to evaluate the overall grant program.

DHS had information about activities and individual projects but could not determine the grant program’s overall effectiveness.

GAO recommended documented award rationales and stronger performance-data requirements for future programs.

Outputs versus Outcomes

Outputs include people trained, events held, materials produced, cases referred, and partnerships established.

Outcomes include improved practitioner knowledge, reduced violent intent, successful disengagement, safer behaviour, stronger trust, and fewer attacks.

Programs can produce substantial outputs without demonstrating causal impact on violence.

Adverse-Effect Evaluation

Evaluation should measure not only intended benefits but also stigma, false referrals, discrimination, privacy breaches, reduced service use, damaged trust, and chilling of protected activity.

A project that reduces one risk while creating broad community harm cannot be judged by training counts alone.

Research Limitations

CVE studies often used small samples, self-reported attitudes, short follow-up periods, inconsistent definitions, and programs too different for direct comparison.

Randomised evaluation can be difficult or unethical in high-risk individual cases.

Evidence supporting general violence prevention does not automatically validate ideology-specific intervention.

2017 Administrative Transition

The first Trump administration reviewed the CVE grant selections and changed award priorities.

Federal offices and public communications reduced use of the CVE label.

Prevention activities continued through grants, research, community engagement, threat assessment, and counterterrorism programs, but institutional support and terminology shifted.

Office Renaming and Reorganisation

The DHS Office for Community Partnerships was renamed and reorganised during the late 2010s.

DHS increasingly used “terrorism prevention,” “targeted violence,” and behaviour-based language rather than “countering violent extremism.”

The changes sought to cover violence beyond one ideology and respond to criticism of the CVE framework.

2019 DHS Strategic Framework

DHS released its Strategic Framework for Countering Terrorism and Targeted Violence in September 2019.

The framework addressed foreign terrorist organisations, domestic violent extremists, targeted attackers without a clear ideological motive, online mobilisation, infrastructure, and local prevention.

It replaced ideology-specific framing with a broader prevention mission while preserving many CVE tools.

FY2020 TVTP Grant Program

Congress funded a $10 million Targeted Violence and Terrorism Prevention Grant Program for Fiscal Year 2020.

The program supported local prevention frameworks, domestic-terrorism prevention, threat assessment and management, innovation, resilience, intervention, and reintegration.

It explicitly built on lessons from the FY2016 CVE grants.

Center for Prevention Programs and Partnerships

DHS established CP3 in 2021 as the principal federal centre for targeted-violence and terrorism prevention partnerships.

CP3 provides grants, training, technical assistance, research translation, public awareness, regional coordination, and practitioner resources.

Its approach emphasises locally led, multidisciplinary, evidence-informed, behaviour-based prevention.

Regional Prevention Coordinators

CP3 regional coordinators connect state, local, Tribal, territorial, university, nonprofit, healthcare, education, and public-safety partners.

They provide training, technical assistance, resource navigation, and support for prevention networks.

Coordinators do not replace local emergency services or federal criminal investigators.

Current TVTP Grant Architecture

TVTP grants support governments, nonprofits, and institutions of higher education developing sustainable prevention capabilities.

Projects include behavioural threat assessment, multidisciplinary prevention, online intervention, youth safety, community awareness, practitioner training, recidivism prevention, and evaluation.

Beginning with later award cycles, grantees received explicit civil-rights, civil-liberties, privacy, and performance requirements.

Published Successor-Program Scale

DHS reported in March 2025 that TVTP had provided nearly $90 million through 178 awards across five funding cycles beginning in 2020.

DHS also reported that grant-supported activity had trained more than 38,000 people.

These figures describe the successor TVTP program, not the closed FY2016 CVE grant.

Status in 2026

The federal government does not currently administer the 2016 grant under the title “Countering Violent Extremism Grant Program.”

DHS webpages updated in 2026 continue to describe CP3 as providing partnerships, grant funding, training, technical assistance, and resources for terrorism and targeted-violence prevention.

The policy environment changed under 2025–2026 presidential counterterrorism directives, which place greater emphasis on investigation, disruption, domestic political violence, transnational gangs, and identified ideological threat categories.

Current prevention practice should therefore be distinguished from both the 2011 Obama strategy and the 2016 grant program.

2026 United States Counterterrorism Strategy

The May 2026 national strategy presents counterterrorism as a core national-security mission and identifies narcoterrorists and transnational gangs, legacy Islamist terrorist organisations, and violent left-wing extremists among principal threat categories.

The strategy states that counterterrorism should be apolitical and should not target Americans merely for disagreement.

Its enforcement-centred language differs from the locally empowering and public-health language associated with 2011 CVE.

The strategy does not make protected ideology criminal and must be implemented within constitutional and statutory limits.

Continuity and Discontinuity

TVTP preserved several CVE ideas: early prevention, local partnerships, multidisciplinary teams, online intervention, community resilience, and federal grants.

It broadened the mission beyond terrorism inspired by a foreign organisation to include domestic violent extremism and targeted violence without a clear ideology.

It also placed greater emphasis on behavioural threat assessment, measurable capabilities, evaluation, and formal privacy and civil-rights safeguards.

Critics continue to regard TVTP as a rebranding of CVE when it uses similar referral, prediction, and community-partnership structures.

Ideological Neutrality

Federal prevention policy has repeatedly stated that violence should be addressed regardless of ideology.

In practice, threat priorities, political leadership, appropriations, grant selections, public messaging, and recent attacks influence which movements receive attention.

Neutrality requires comparable behavioural standards, transparent threat evidence, and protection for lawful advocacy across political and religious viewpoints.

White-Supremacist and Racially Motivated Violence

White-supremacist violence, antisemitic attacks, anti-Black terrorism, anti-immigrant violence, and other racially motivated attacks challenged early CVE’s perceived concentration on Muslim communities.

Later DHS and federal frameworks explicitly included racially or ethnically motivated violent extremists.

Prevention must distinguish protected racist expression from threats, conspiracies, weapons preparation, and violent action while recognising the historical role of organised racial terror.

Anti-Government and Anti-Authority Violence

Militia violent extremists, sovereign-citizen actors, anarchist violence, and other anti-authority movements have appeared within federal threat assessments.

Opposition to government, firearm ownership, protest, tax resistance, or constitutional criticism is not by itself terrorism.

Prevention should focus on mobilisation to violence, credible threats, criminal planning, and capability.

Single-Issue Violence

Federal threat frameworks have included violence connected to abortion, environmental, animal-rights, racial, religious, gender, and other single-issue causes.

The overwhelming majority of people advocating strongly on controversial issues do not commit violence.

Programs should avoid designating an entire movement as violent because of isolated offenders.

Targeted Violence without a Clear Ideology

TVTP broadened prevention to planned violence against schools, workplaces, houses of worship, public events, or individuals even when investigators could not identify a coherent ideology.

This change connected terrorism prevention with school, workplace, and behavioural threat-assessment practice.

Broader scope can improve safety but also greatly expand the population subject to preventive scrutiny.

Changing Online Ecosystem

Contemporary mobilisation can cross mainstream platforms, encrypted channels, niche forums, livestreaming, gaming networks, payment systems, and artificial-intelligence tools.

Online content may mix ideology, humour, grievance, conspiracy, personal crisis, and tactical information.

Effective prevention requires context and cannot rely solely on keywords, memes, religious terms, political slogans, or automated classification.

Technology Companies

Platforms can enforce terms of service, remove content, preserve evidence, refer emergencies, promote counter-content, and support research.

Private moderation is not identical to government censorship, but governmental pressure, coordination, and information requests can raise constitutional and transparency questions.

Errors can suppress journalism, documentation, satire, religious discussion, political advocacy, or research.

Prevention Best Practices

  • Define violence-related objectives rather than policing ideology.
  • Use behaviour and context rather than demographic or religious profiles.
  • Separate voluntary services from intelligence collection.
  • State confidentiality and reporting limits clearly.
  • Use multidisciplinary teams with qualified professionals.
  • Provide civil-rights, privacy, disability, and bias training.
  • Establish appeal, complaint, correction, and case-closure processes.
  • Measure both beneficial and adverse outcomes.
  • Engage affected communities in governance rather than symbolic consultation.
  • Escalate to law enforcement when behaviour and law justify it.

Program Achievements

  • Established prevention as a distinct component of domestic counterterrorism policy.
  • Created formal federal support for locally led prevention.
  • Encouraged multidisciplinary collaboration beyond police agencies.
  • Developed local frameworks in Boston, Los Angeles, and the Twin Cities.
  • Expanded research into mobilisation, disengagement, and intervention.
  • Supported community, youth, educational, and online projects.
  • Created the first dedicated DHS competitive prevention grant.
  • Advanced behavioural threat-assessment and management practice.
  • Produced privacy impact assessments and civil-liberties training.
  • Generated lessons used in the successor TVTP grant program.
  • Broadened later prevention efforts to multiple ideologies and nonideological targeted violence.
  • Created a continuing federal prevention centre through CP3.

Limitations and Failures

  • The federal government lacked a consistent definition of violent extremism.
  • The 2011 effort lacked a cohesive measurable strategy.
  • DHS could not establish the overall effectiveness of the FY2016 grants.
  • Grant award revisions were inadequately documented.
  • Outputs were often easier to count than actual prevention outcomes.
  • Programs relied on uncertain theories of radicalisation.
  • Rare-event prediction created a high false-positive risk.
  • Early implementation was perceived as disproportionately focused on Muslims.
  • Community engagement and intelligence functions were not always clearly separated.
  • Social-service involvement could discourage people from seeking help.
  • Local variation produced inconsistent safeguards and quality.
  • Online interventions faced free-speech, privacy, and attribution problems.
  • Program terminology changed faster than underlying prevention dilemmas.
  • Political transitions changed priorities and grantee selection.
  • Evidence from individual projects did not establish national causal effectiveness.

Major Controversies

Prevention or Surveillance

Supporters described voluntary services; critics saw an intelligence pipeline operating before reasonable suspicion.

Community Partnership or Collective Suspicion

Federal outreach brought resources and access but also implied that Muslim communities were responsible for detecting terrorism.

Behavioural Risk or Ideological Profiling

Programs claimed to focus on violence while training and referrals sometimes relied on belief, identity, grievance, or religious change.

Public Health or Securitised Care

Multidisciplinary support could prevent harm, but national-security objectives could compromise therapeutic trust.

Local Flexibility or Inconsistent Rights

Local design permitted adaptation but produced different standards for consent, records, referrals, and law-enforcement involvement.

Innovation or Unproven Experimentation

Grants tested new models on communities before the federal government had strong evidence of predictive validity or effectiveness.

All Ideologies or Political Selection

Administrations differed over whether Islamist, white-supremacist, anti-government, left-wing, or other threats deserved priority.

Documented Outcomes

The FY2016 grantees produced trainings, partnerships, intervention structures, digital projects, curricula, research, and community programming.

DHS evaluated selected projects and later used their experience in the FY2020 successor program.

GAO concluded that available data were not sufficient to evaluate the overall CVE Grant Program’s effectiveness.

DHS’s later TVTP program reported substantial grantmaking and practitioner training, but those successor figures should not be attributed to the FY2016 CVE grants.

Historical Significance

CVE marked a shift from reacting to terrorist plots toward attempting prevention before criminal conduct.

It brought teachers, clinicians, religious leaders, social workers, researchers, and technology companies into a policy area formerly dominated by intelligence and law enforcement.

It also demonstrated the constitutional danger of preventive security when protected beliefs and uncertain behavioural indicators become government risk categories.

The successor TVTP system cannot be understood without CVE’s innovations, failures, and anti-Muslim-discrimination controversies.

Research Cautions

  • CVE was not one permanent program. Identify the strategy, office, grant, pilot, agency, and year.
  • The FY2016 grant is closed. Do not describe it as the current TVTP grant.
  • Prevention is not prosecution. A referral is not proof of a crime.
  • Extremist belief is not automatically unlawful. The First Amendment protects broad ideological activity.
  • Radicalisation is not a uniform pathway. Most people with radical beliefs never commit violence.
  • Outputs are not outcomes. Training counts do not prove attacks were prevented.
  • Community participation may not be fully voluntary. Power, funding, immigration, and policing can create pressure.
  • Official ideology neutrality did not guarantee neutral implementation.
  • Muslim-community criticism is central to the history. It should not be reduced to a minor communications problem.
  • TVTP broadened the framework. Broader scope can reduce religious profiling but increase preventive surveillance.
  • Current policy changed after 2025. Verify present grant, office, and strategy documents before describing active priorities.

Definitions

Behavioural threat assessment and management
A multidisciplinary process for evaluating concerning behaviour and managing risk of targeted violence.
Community resilience
Community relationships, resources, skills, and institutions that help resist, recover from, or prevent violence and recruitment.
Countering violent extremism
A prevention framework intended to reduce ideological mobilisation to violence before criminal action.
Counterterrorism
Government activity using intelligence, law enforcement, diplomacy, military, financial, border, and security authorities against terrorism.
Credible messenger
A person considered trustworthy by the intended audience because of lived experience, community standing, or specialised knowledge.
Disengagement
Reduction or ending of participation in violent activity or violent groups, which may occur without complete ideological change.
Domestic terrorism
Activities meeting the definition in 18 U.S.C. § 2331(5); the definition is not itself a general standalone federal offence.
Mobilisation to violence
Movement from belief, grievance, or intent toward preparation, facilitation, or violent action.
Protective factor
A circumstance associated with reduced risk or increased resilience, without guaranteeing an outcome.
Radicalisation
A contested term describing movement toward more extreme beliefs or support for violence.
Referral
Communication of a concern to a prevention, service, threat-assessment, or law-enforcement entity.
Risk factor
A circumstance statistically or clinically associated with concern but not proof that an individual will commit violence.
Securitisation
Transformation of a social, religious, educational, or health issue into a national-security concern.
Targeted violence
Planned violence directed at an identified person, group, institution, or location, with or without a clear ideology.
TVTP
Targeted Violence and Terrorism Prevention, the successor terminology used by DHS.

Summary

Countering Violent Extremism was a federal prevention framework rather than one permanent criminal or benefit program.

The Obama administration formalised domestic CVE through a 2011 strategy, Strategic Implementation Plans, local pilot frameworks, a White House summit, the DHS Office for Community Partnerships, and an interagency Task Force.

CVE sought to prevent ideological mobilisation to violence through community engagement, research, training, local capacity, online communications, and voluntary intervention before criminal activity.

Congress funded a $10 million FY2016 CVE Grant Program. DHS initially announced thirty-one intended awards and, after a 2017 review, made twenty-six final awards for projects generally operating through 2019.

GAO found that the wider federal effort lacked a cohesive measurable strategy and that DHS lacked sufficient award documentation and performance data to establish overall grant effectiveness.

The program became deeply controversial because Muslim, Arab, Somali, South Asian, immigrant, refugee, and Black Muslim communities were frequently treated as the principal terrain of prevention.

Critics argued that CVE relied on unreliable radicalisation indicators, chilled protected speech and religion, securitised social services, and blurred voluntary assistance with surveillance.

Supporters argued that locally led services, credible messengers, intervention teams, public-health tools, and online prevention could provide alternatives to arrest and prosecution.

CVE created no new federal offence, and extremist ideology alone remains protected by the First Amendment.

DHS replaced the terminology and grant structure with a broader Targeted Violence and Terrorism Prevention framework, launched a successor grant in 2020, and established CP3 in 2021.

DHS reported nearly $90 million through 178 TVTP awards across five funding cycles by its March 2025 summary.

As of August 3, 2026, the named FY2016 CVE Grant Program is closed, while CP3 continues prevention partnerships, grants, training, and technical assistance within a substantially changed national counterterrorism strategy.

The lasting significance of CVE lies in its attempt to move counterterrorism upstream—and in the unresolved question of how a constitutional democracy can prevent rare acts of political violence without criminalising identity, belief, association, dissent, or communities.
