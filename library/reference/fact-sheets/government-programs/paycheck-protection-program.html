Paycheck Protection Program

The emergency federal loan-guarantee and forgiveness programme that distributed nearly $800 billion through private lenders to help small businesses and other eligible organisations retain workers and meet operating costs during the COVID-19 pandemic.

What Was the Paycheck Protection Program?

PPP was a temporary SBA-guaranteed lending programme designed for an economy in which public-health orders, voluntary distancing, supply disruptions, and collapsing demand threatened millions of small businesses.

Participating lenders advanced money to eligible borrowers. SBA guaranteed the loans in full. Borrowers could obtain forgiveness if they spent the proceeds on authorised costs and complied with applicable certifications and forgiveness rules.

Economically, a fully forgiven PPP loan functioned much like a grant. Legally and administratively, it began as debt and required a lender loan file, federal guarantee, borrower certifications, forgiveness application, and possible SBA review.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the programme’s creation, funding rounds, eligible borrowers, payroll formulas, terms, lender network, forgiveness, tax treatment, access disparities, economic effects, fraud schemes, audits, criminal and civil enforcement, appeals, collections, programme closure, and continuing legacy through August 3, 2026.

It does not determine whether a specific borrower was eligible, whether a particular expense qualified, or whether a loan should be forgiven, recovered, or prosecuted. The controlling law, regulations, forms, guidance, loan documents, tax law, and final agency or court decisions govern individual cases.

COVID-19 Economic Emergency

In March 2020, governments restricted travel, dining, entertainment, personal services, schools, offices, and public gatherings to slow a new infectious disease.

Small businesses experienced:

  • abrupt revenue loss;
  • mandatory or voluntary closure;
  • supply-chain interruption;
  • employee illness and caregiving absence;
  • rent and debt obligations without normal income;
  • mass layoffs and furloughs;
  • uncertain reopening dates;
  • limited cash reserves;
  • tightened credit; and
  • risk of permanent closure.

Policy Objective

Congress sought to preserve the employer-worker relationship rather than allow temporary shutdowns to produce permanent business failures and unemployment.

The programme attempted to:

  • keep employees attached to employers;
  • preserve health and retirement benefits;
  • support rent, mortgage interest, and utilities;
  • stabilise small-business cash flow;
  • reduce pressure on unemployment insurance;
  • speed reopening;
  • protect productive capacity; and
  • deliver aid through the existing banking system.

CARES Act Creation

President Donald J. Trump signed the CARES Act on March 27, 2020.

Section 1102 temporarily expanded SBA’s 7(a) loan programme, while section 1106 created loan forgiveness.

Congress initially authorised and appropriated $349 billion for guarantees and related costs.

SBA and Treasury converted the statute into an operational programme in approximately one week, issuing interim rules while lenders were already preparing applications.

April 3, 2020 Launch

Banks began accepting applications on April 3, 2020.

Demand was immediate and enormous. Websites failed, lender portals became congested, borrowers struggled to obtain guidance, and SBA’s E-Tran system faced unprecedented volume.

The first appropriation was exhausted in less than two weeks.

April 2020 Replenishment

The Paycheck Protection Program and Health Care Enhancement Act added approximately $321 billion in PPP authority.

Congress reserved portions for smaller lenders, including community financial institutions, insured depository institutions, and credit unions within specified asset ranges.

The second tranche reopened on April 27 and continued until the 2020 application period ended.

First Draw PPP Loans

A First Draw loan was available to an eligible recipient that had not previously received PPP funds.

The standard maximum was the lesser of:

  • 2.5 times average monthly eligible payroll costs; or
  • $10 million.

Special calculation rules applied to seasonal employers, new businesses, farmers and ranchers, partnerships, sole proprietors, independent contractors, and self-employed individuals.

Second Draw PPP Loans

The Economic Aid to Hard-Hit Small Businesses, Nonprofits, and Venues Act created Second Draw loans in December 2020.

A typical applicant had to:

  • have received a First Draw loan;
  • use or plan to use the full First Draw amount for authorised purposes;
  • have no more than 300 employees; and
  • demonstrate at least a twenty-five-percent reduction in gross receipts.

The maximum was $2 million.

Gross-Receipts Reduction Test

Second Draw borrowers generally compared gross receipts for a quarter in 2020 with the corresponding quarter in 2019.

Annual comparisons were permitted in specified circumstances.

Gross receipts included total revenue under SBA rules, with adjustments for affiliates, tax accounting, and amounts such as net capital gains.

Loans of $150,000 or less could initially certify the reduction and provide documentation when seeking forgiveness.

Eligible Businesses and Organisations

Eligibility evolved over several laws and rules.

  • small business concerns;
  • businesses meeting the 500-employee rule or SBA industry size standard;
  • sole proprietors;
  • independent contractors;
  • self-employed individuals;
  • 501(c)(3) nonprofit organisations;
  • 501(c)(19) veterans organisations;
  • tribal business concerns;
  • certain 501(c)(6) organisations and destination marketing organisations;
  • housing cooperatives;
  • certain nonprofit and internet-only news organisations; and
  • other specifically authorised entities.

Size Standards

The ordinary First Draw rule permitted many organisations with 500 or fewer employees to qualify even when they might not meet the traditional 7(a) programme’s financial tests.

Businesses could also qualify under SBA’s employee- or revenue-based industry size standard or the alternative size standard available under programme rules.

Second Draw generally reduced the employee ceiling to 300.

Affiliation Rules

SBA affiliation rules combine related businesses when ownership, management, contractual control, or identity of interest creates common control.

Affiliation affected employee counts, loan limits, and eligibility.

Congress waived or modified affiliation rules for selected borrowers, including:

  • certain accommodation and food-service businesses with 500 or fewer employees per physical location;
  • qualifying franchises listed in SBA’s franchise directory;
  • certain businesses receiving financial assistance from Small Business Investment Companies; and
  • selected nonprofit and news entities under later laws.

Restaurants and Hotels

Congress gave accommodation and food-service businesses special treatment because they were heavily affected by closure orders.

First Draw eligibility could be determined per physical location under specified conditions.

Eligible Second Draw borrowers in NAICS sector 72 could receive 3.5 times average monthly payroll rather than 2.5 times, subject to the $2 million maximum.

Nonprofit Organisations

PPP departed from ordinary commercial loan programmes by including many nonprofit employers.

Eligible nonprofits used funds for:

  • staff salaries and benefits;
  • rent and mortgage interest;
  • utilities;
  • personal protective equipment;
  • remote-work systems;
  • supplier costs; and
  • continuity of charitable, religious, educational, cultural, and human services.

Religious Organisations

Churches and other religious organisations could qualify based on their eligible employment and operational expenses.

SBA issued guidance addressing faith-based eligibility, affiliation, religious autonomy, and the application of programme nondiscrimination requirements.

The inclusion of religious organisations raised constitutional and policy debate, but the programme was structured as neutral emergency aid based on secular payroll and operating costs.

Self-Employed Individuals and Sole Proprietors

Sole proprietors, independent contractors, and self-employed individuals could treat owner compensation as payroll replacement.

Early rules relied principally on Schedule C net profit, disadvantaging businesses with low taxable profit despite substantial gross receipts and fixed expenses.

In 2021, SBA allowed eligible Schedule C filers to use gross income for loans not yet approved, improving potential access for very small and minority-owned businesses.

Farmers and Ranchers

Agricultural producers could qualify when they met PPP eligibility standards.

Later legislation and guidance allowed eligible farmers and ranchers filing Schedule F to use gross income rather than only net farm profit in the loan calculation.

PPP could cover employee payroll and authorised owner compensation but was separate from USDA commodity, disaster, and farm-loan programmes.

News Organisations

Congress created special eligibility routes for certain newspapers, broadcasters, nonprofit news organisations, and internet-only news publishers.

Rules generally emphasised employee limits per location and certification that funds would support locally focused news or emergency information.

The policy reflected concern that pandemic revenue loss was accelerating closure of local journalism.

Ineligible Businesses and Persons

Ineligibility included categories barred under PPP statutes, SBA rules, or ordinary 7(a) restrictions not waived by Congress.

  • businesses engaged in illegal activity under federal law;
  • household employers;
  • passive businesses not meeting an exception;
  • certain government-owned entities;
  • businesses permanently closed before the qualifying date;
  • entities that exceeded applicable size or affiliation limits;
  • publicly traded companies for Second Draw loans;
  • entities receiving specified venue grants during prohibited periods; and
  • applicants subject to disqualifying criminal, delinquency, or debarment rules as those rules existed at the application date.

Criminal-History Restrictions

Early rules excluded applicants based on incarceration, pending criminal proceedings, probation, parole, and certain recent convictions involving fraud or financial misconduct.

SBA and Treasury narrowed some exclusions after criticism that the rules disproportionately affected Black and Hispanic entrepreneurs and people rebuilding their lives after conviction.

False answers to criminal-history questions could support denial, recovery, or prosecution.

Economic-Necessity Certification

Applicants certified in good faith that current economic uncertainty made the loan necessary to support ongoing operations.

The certification did not require proof that the business would otherwise fail, and Congress waived the ordinary credit-elsewhere requirement.

Public controversy arose when large restaurant chains, public companies, universities, and well-capitalised organisations received loans.

Necessity Safe Harbours

SBA announced that borrowers with loans below $2 million would be deemed to have made the necessity certification in good faith.

Larger borrowers could face review of liquidity, access to capital, business activity, and the basis for the certification.

SBA also offered a temporary repayment safe harbour for borrowers who reconsidered eligibility after the agency issued new guidance.

Payroll-Based Loan Calculation

The standard formula began with eligible payroll costs during a selected reference period.

Payroll costs included:

  • salary, wages, commissions, and tips;
  • cash compensation subject to the annual compensation cap;
  • vacation, parental, family, medical, and sick leave;
  • allowances for dismissal or separation;
  • employer payments for health benefits;
  • retirement contributions; and
  • state and local taxes assessed on employee compensation.

Payroll Exclusions

Excluded amounts generally included:

  • individual cash compensation above the prorated $100,000 annual cap;
  • federal employment taxes withheld or imposed during specified periods under programme definitions;
  • compensation of employees whose principal residence was outside the United States;
  • qualified sick or family leave wages receiving specified federal tax credits; and
  • independent-contractor payments counted by the contractor in a separate PPP application.

Partnerships

Partnerships applied at the entity level rather than having individual partners submit separate applications for partner compensation.

Eligible partner self-employment income was incorporated into the partnership’s payroll calculation, subject to caps and guidance.

Early uncertainty led SBA to allow some partnerships to increase existing loans to include partner compensation that had initially been omitted.

Loan Terms

PPP loans carried:

  • a one-percent fixed interest rate;
  • a one-hundred-percent SBA guarantee;
  • no collateral requirement;
  • no personal guarantee;
  • no ordinary borrower guarantee fee;
  • no prepayment penalty;
  • payment deferral while forgiveness was pending; and
  • two- or five-year maturity depending on origination date and later agreement.

Authorised Uses

Loan proceeds could be used for authorised payroll and operating costs.

  • payroll costs;
  • employee benefits;
  • owner compensation within applicable limits;
  • covered mortgage interest;
  • covered rent;
  • covered utilities;
  • interest on other pre-existing debt, although not necessarily forgivable;
  • covered operations expenditures;
  • covered property-damage costs;
  • covered supplier costs; and
  • covered worker-protection expenditures.

Operations Expenditures

Later law made specified business software and cloud-computing costs eligible.

Covered functions included:

  • business operations;
  • product or service delivery;
  • payroll processing;
  • human resources;
  • sales and billing;
  • inventory;
  • records; and
  • expense tracking.

Worker-Protection Expenditures

Borrowers could receive forgiveness for qualifying adaptations required or recommended by public-health authorities.

  • personal protective equipment;
  • ventilation and filtration;
  • physical barriers;
  • drive-through or outdoor service areas;
  • health screening;
  • expansion of indoor or outdoor space;
  • onsite or offsite health-related improvements; and
  • other qualifying measures made during the national emergency.

Prohibited or Unforgivable Uses

PPP funds could not lawfully be used as unrestricted personal money.

Misuse included:

  • personal luxury purchases;
  • unrelated investments;
  • unauthorised owner distributions;
  • purchase of personal real estate;
  • gambling;
  • fabricated payroll;
  • payments unsupported by business records;
  • duplicate claims for the same costs; and
  • any use outside statutory and regulatory purposes.

Loan Forgiveness

Forgiveness converted eligible principal and accrued interest into an SBA payment to the lender.

The borrower submitted an application or certification documenting:

  • eligible costs;
  • the covered period;
  • employee levels;
  • wage levels;
  • payroll tax records;
  • bank statements;
  • rent, mortgage, utility, supplier, and protection expenses; and
  • required certifications.

Covered Period

The original CARES Act used an eight-week period.

The PPP Flexibility Act allowed most borrowers to use a period of up to twenty-four weeks.

Later rules allowed a borrower to choose a covered period between eight and twenty-four weeks, beginning on the disbursement date.

The selected period affected payroll caps, employee measurements, and expense timing.

Sixty-Percent Payroll Rule

The Flexibility Act reduced the payroll threshold from seventy-five percent to sixty percent.

At least sixty percent of the forgiveness amount generally had to consist of payroll costs.

Borrowers spending less than sixty percent of the loan on payroll could still receive partial forgiveness, subject to the proportional limit.

Full-Time-Equivalent Reductions

Forgiveness could be reduced when average full-time-equivalent staffing fell during the covered period compared with an authorised reference period.

The calculation was intended to reward employee retention.

Exemptions and safe harbours addressed rehire efforts, employee refusal, voluntary resignation, reduced hours requested by employees, for-cause termination, and inability to return to the same level of business activity because of public-health requirements.

Salary and Wage Reductions

Forgiveness could be reduced if salary or hourly wages for certain employees fell by more than twenty-five percent during the relevant period.

The wage-reduction test generally excluded employees whose annualised compensation exceeded $100,000 during the comparison year.

Restoration by the statutory safe-harbour date could prevent or reduce the penalty.

Owner Compensation

Forgiveness of owner compensation was subject to special limits based on the covered period and prior-year compensation or business income.

Rules differed for:

  • owner-employees of corporations;
  • S corporation owners;
  • self-employed Schedule C filers;
  • general partners;
  • farmers filing Schedule F; and
  • individuals owning multiple PPP borrowers.

Forgiveness Forms

SBA Form 3508
Standard forgiveness application with schedules and detailed calculations.
SBA Form 3508EZ
Simplified application for borrowers meeting specified employee, wage, or business-activity conditions.
SBA Form 3508S
Simplified certification for loans of $150,000 or less.
SBA direct forgiveness portal
Electronic process corresponding to the applicable forgiveness form.

Lender Forgiveness Review

The lender reviewed the application, supporting documents, calculations, and certifications before issuing a forgiveness decision to SBA.

Lender decisions could recommend:

  • full forgiveness;
  • partial forgiveness;
  • denial without prejudice while SBA review continued; or
  • denial.

SBA retained authority to review the loan independently.

SBA Loan Review

SBA could review any PPP loan for:

  • borrower eligibility;
  • loan amount;
  • necessity certification;
  • affiliate treatment;
  • use of proceeds;
  • forgiveness eligibility;
  • duplicate benefits;
  • fraud indicators;
  • lender compliance; and
  • accuracy of certifications and documents.

Appeals

A borrower receiving a final SBA loan-review decision could appeal to SBA’s Office of Hearings and Appeals within the applicable deadline.

Appealable issues could include:

  • ineligibility;
  • excess loan amount;
  • misuse of proceeds;
  • denial of forgiveness;
  • partial forgiveness; and
  • SBA demands for repayment.

Lender-only forgiveness decisions and SBA final review decisions followed different procedural paths.

Direct Forgiveness Portal

SBA opened a direct portal in 2021 for borrowers whose lenders opted into the process.

Effective March 13, 2024, SBA expanded direct portal access to all borrowers regardless of loan size.

The portal can reduce paperwork and allow borrowers to submit information directly, but SBA or the lender can still request documents and conduct review.

Unforgiven Loans

Any principal not forgiven remains an enforceable loan.

The borrower owes:

  • unforgiven principal;
  • accrued interest;
  • scheduled payments under the note;
  • amounts arising from an SBA review or forgiveness reversal; and
  • collection costs or other charges where legally authorised.

Failure to apply for forgiveness within ten months after the covered period ended caused payment deferral to expire.

Loan Servicing

The originating lender ordinarily serviced the PPP note.

Servicing functions included:

  • payment collection;
  • forgiveness processing;
  • changes of ownership;
  • loan sales and participations;
  • default administration;
  • guarantee purchase requests;
  • charge-off;
  • record retention;
  • SBA reporting; and
  • cooperation with audits and investigations.

Collections and Treasury Referral

Delinquent unforgiven loans can move from lender servicing to SBA guarantee purchase, charge-off, federal debt collection, or Treasury cross-servicing.

Collection tools can include:

  • payment demands;
  • administrative offset;
  • tax-refund offset;
  • Treasury collection contractors;
  • credit reporting;
  • civil litigation;
  • False Claims Act recovery; and
  • criminal restitution after conviction.

Federal Tax Treatment

Forgiven PPP principal is excluded from federal gross income.

Treasury and IRS initially concluded that expenses producing tax-exempt forgiveness were not deductible.

Congress reversed that result in December 2020:

  • eligible expenses remained deductible;
  • tax attributes were not reduced merely because of forgiveness;
  • basis increases were allowed under applicable entity rules; and
  • forgiveness generated tax-exempt income.

State and Local Tax Treatment

State tax treatment varied because states conform to federal income-tax law in different ways.

A state could:

  • exclude forgiveness and allow deductions;
  • exclude forgiveness but disallow some deductions;
  • tax forgiveness under nonconforming law;
  • apply different rules to individuals and corporations; or
  • adopt special PPP legislation.

Borrowers had to review the law for each relevant tax year and jurisdiction.

Employee Retention Credit

The original CARES Act generally prevented a PPP borrower from claiming the Employee Retention Credit.

Congress later permitted eligible employers to benefit from both programmes retroactively.

Double counting was prohibited: wages used to support PPP forgiveness could not also generate the credit.

Allocation of wages between the programmes became a major payroll and tax-compliance issue.

Relationship to COVID-19 EIDL

A business could receive both a PPP loan and a COVID-19 Economic Injury Disaster Loan if the funds did not pay the same costs.

Certain EIDL loans made during the early pandemic could be refinanced into PPP.

EIDL advances were initially deducted from PPP forgiveness payments. Congress later repealed that deduction and directed SBA to remit the difference.

PPP was potentially forgivable; COVID-EIDL was generally long-term repayable debt.

Relationship to Other Relief Programmes

PPP interacted with:

  • Shuttered Venue Operators Grants;
  • Restaurant Revitalization Fund grants;
  • Provider Relief Fund payments;
  • state and local business grants;
  • paid-leave tax credits;
  • Employee Retention Credit;
  • unemployment insurance;
  • industry-specific assistance; and
  • ordinary SBA loans.

Congress frequently prohibited the same expense from being reimbursed twice.

Lender Network

SBA delivered PPP through more than five thousand lenders.

Participants included:

  • national and regional banks;
  • community banks;
  • credit unions;
  • Farm Credit System institutions;
  • Community Development Financial Institutions;
  • Minority Depository Institutions;
  • certified development companies in limited roles;
  • nonbank small-business lenders;
  • financial-technology platforms; and
  • new lenders approved specifically for PPP.

Lender Incentives

The programme eliminated ordinary credit risk for compliant lenders by providing a one-hundred-percent federal guarantee.

Lenders received processing fees based on loan size, creating strong incentive to originate loans rapidly.

Early fee tiers generally paid:

  • five percent for loans up to $350,000;
  • three percent for loans above $350,000 and below $2 million; and
  • one percent for loans of $2 million or more.

Later law increased compensation for selected very small loans.

Bank Relationships

The speed of the first round made an existing lender relationship unusually valuable.

Many banks initially prioritised:

  • existing commercial customers;
  • borrowers with deposit accounts;
  • firms with established payroll records;
  • larger loans that generated higher absolute fees;
  • applications that fit automated systems; and
  • businesses able to obtain professional assistance.

These practices disadvantaged businesses historically underserved by banks.

Community Financial Institutions

CDFIs, Minority Depository Institutions, Certified Development Companies, microlenders, and small banks played a larger role as the programme evolved.

Congress and SBA reserved funding, created priority access, and opened the 2021 programme first to community financial institutions.

These lenders often served smaller businesses, rural communities, minority-owned firms, sole proprietors, and borrowers lacking conventional bank relationships.

Financial-Technology and Nonbank Lenders

Fintech lenders used online applications, automated document review, identity tools, and national reach.

They improved access for many businesses rejected or ignored by banks.

At the same time, some nonbank channels produced high fraud risk because of:

  • remote onboarding;
  • limited customer relationships;
  • automated underwriting;
  • third-party agents;
  • high application volume;
  • incentive-based fees; and
  • weak or inconsistent identity and payroll verification.

Paycheck Protection Program Liquidity Facility

The Federal Reserve created the PPPLF to ensure that lenders had cash to fund PPP loans.

The facility extended credit to eligible financial institutions and accepted PPP loans as collateral at face value.

This arrangement:

  • removed PPP loans from practical liquidity constraints;
  • supported banks and community lenders;
  • encouraged continued origination;
  • reduced balance-sheet pressure; and
  • connected fiscal small-business relief with central-bank liquidity policy.

Transparency and Public Loan Data

SBA and Treasury initially released aggregate data and loan ranges for larger loans.

Journalists, watchdogs, and litigants sought complete loan-level disclosure.

SBA eventually released data including:

  • borrower name;
  • address;
  • loan amount;
  • lender;
  • industry code;
  • business type;
  • jobs reported;
  • approval date;
  • forgiveness status; and
  • voluntarily supplied demographic information.

Demographic Data Limitations

Race, ethnicity, sex, and veteran-status fields were voluntary and frequently left blank.

This limited the government’s ability to determine:

  • who received early loans;
  • whether minority-owned firms were proportionately served;
  • which lenders produced equitable outcomes;
  • whether forgiveness differed by group;
  • how denial or delay affected survival; and
  • where outreach and capital gaps remained.

African American-Owned Businesses

Black-owned businesses entered the pandemic with lower average wealth, thinner cash reserves, weaker bank relationships, smaller payrolls, and greater concentration in heavily affected industries and neighbourhoods.

Early programme features created barriers:

  • bank-customer prioritisation;
  • payroll-based formulas;
  • net-profit calculations for sole proprietors;
  • limited lender outreach;
  • digital application failures;
  • lack of accountants and lawyers;
  • voluntary demographic reporting; and
  • rapid exhaustion of the first appropriation.

Racial Disparities in Access

Census and other research found that Black-owned businesses were more likely to operate in COVID-19 hotspots and less likely to be covered by PPP during the early period.

Researchers linked disparities to banking relationships, lender geography, business size, industry, documentation, and programme design.

Because demographic fields were incomplete, estimates vary and cannot be reduced to a single definitive national percentage.

Hispanic-, Asian-, and Immigrant-Owned Businesses

Immigrant and minority entrepreneurs faced language barriers, limited conventional credit, cash-based bookkeeping, informal employment, uncertainty about federal rules, and fear concerning immigration or public disclosure.

Asian-owned businesses in restaurants, lodging, personal services, and retail were affected early by pandemic-related consumer avoidance.

Hispanic-owned firms often operated with few employees and limited payroll infrastructure, making community-lender and sole-proprietor reforms important.

Women-Owned Businesses

Women-owned firms were heavily represented among very small businesses, personal services, childcare, hospitality, retail, and sole proprietorships.

They could face:

  • lower business capitalisation;
  • caregiving burdens;
  • school and childcare closures;
  • smaller payroll-based loan amounts;
  • weaker bank relationships;
  • revenue interruption; and
  • difficulty documenting owner compensation.

Rural Businesses

Community banks, Farm Credit institutions, credit unions, and rural development organisations helped deliver PPP outside metropolitan areas.

Rural challenges included:

  • limited lender competition;
  • broadband barriers;
  • seasonal tourism and agriculture;
  • small payrolls;
  • remote professional services;
  • hospital and nonprofit dependence;
  • older business owners; and
  • local bank consolidation.

Very Small and Nonemployer Businesses

Businesses with fewer than ten employees and firms without employees often had the least capacity to navigate the first round.

The 2021 programme improved their access through:

  • community-lender priority;
  • a fourteen-day exclusivity period for businesses with fewer than twenty employees;
  • gross-income calculations;
  • simplified forgiveness;
  • lower average loan size;
  • more fintech participation;
  • targeted outreach; and
  • longer programme availability.

2021 Equity Reforms

The Biden administration implemented changes intended to reach businesses left behind in 2020.

  • Fourteen days of exclusive applications for businesses and nonprofits with fewer than twenty employees.
  • Gross-income loan calculations for eligible Schedule C filers.
  • Expanded access for lawful noncitizen business owners using an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number.
  • Narrower criminal-history exclusions.
  • Revised treatment of delinquent federal student loans and prior nonfraud felonies.
  • Community outreach and technical assistance.

GAO Findings on Access

GAO found that programme changes increased lending to smaller businesses and traditionally underserved counties.

By the programme’s close, lending in underserved counties was approximately proportional to their share of the small-business population.

This county-level finding did not prove equal access for every racial or demographic group because complete owner-demographic data were unavailable.

Employment Effects

PPP preserved employment by subsidising payroll and giving businesses liquidity during an unprecedented shock.

Research using lender timing, eligibility thresholds, payroll data, and administrative records generally found positive employment effects.

Federal Reserve research found that delays caused by exhaustion of the first appropriation increased job loss and reduced recalls, especially among financially constrained firms.

Economic Efficiency Debate

Studies disagree on how efficiently PPP converted public spending into employment.

One major study estimated that the programme preserved two to three million job-years over fourteen months at a cost of $170,000 to $257,000 per job-year.

The estimated cost exceeded typical worker compensation because much of the benefit supported:

  • business owners;
  • creditors;
  • landlords;
  • suppliers;
  • business continuity; and
  • workers who might have remained employed without PPP.

Business Survival and Liquidity

Employment was not the programme’s only possible benefit.

PPP could:

  • prevent bankruptcy;
  • preserve firm-specific knowledge;
  • maintain supplier relationships;
  • cover fixed expenses;
  • support reopening;
  • reduce permanent closure;
  • stabilise local commercial districts; and
  • prevent cascading defaults.

These benefits are difficult to measure and can persist beyond the covered payroll period.

Targeting

PPP was not tightly targeted to firms that would otherwise fail or lay off workers.

Congress prioritised speed and broad eligibility because real-time proof of future failure would have delayed assistance.

The trade-off produced:

  • aid to firms with mild revenue loss;
  • aid to businesses with access to other capital;
  • large benefits to owners and creditors;
  • unequal early access; and
  • less verification before disbursement.

Fraud Risk in Programme Design

PPP combined multiple high-risk characteristics:

  • large appropriations;
  • rapid launch;
  • self-certification;
  • limited initial documentation;
  • no collateral;
  • no personal guarantee;
  • one-hundred-percent federal lender protection;
  • processing fees;
  • forgiveness;
  • remote applications;
  • new and nontraditional lenders; and
  • pressure to approve before funds ran out.

Common Fraud Schemes

  • inventing businesses or employees;
  • inflating payroll and revenue;
  • submitting altered tax forms and bank statements;
  • using stolen identities;
  • applying through multiple lenders;
  • concealing affiliated companies;
  • misrepresenting criminal history or federal debt;
  • claiming a business operated before February 15, 2020 when it did not;
  • using funds for luxury goods, homes, vehicles, investments, or gambling;
  • paying kickbacks to preparers or lender insiders;
  • laundering proceeds through cryptocurrency or shell companies; and
  • submitting false forgiveness applications.

Identity Theft

Fraudsters used stolen names, Social Security numbers, employer identification numbers, and business records to obtain loans.

Victims could discover PPP debt or public loan records connected to businesses they did not own.

Identity-theft cases demonstrate why suspicious data are not conclusive proof against the named borrower: the person’s identity may have been misused.

Lender and Agent Misconduct

Most lenders delivered emergency aid lawfully, but some institutions, employees, brokers, accountants, and application agents were accused of:

  • approving false applications;
  • manufacturing payroll records;
  • soliciting kickbacks;
  • splitting proceeds;
  • ignoring obvious red flags;
  • submitting duplicate loans;
  • charging prohibited fees;
  • misusing borrower identities; and
  • seeking guarantee payment on defective loans.

Programme Controls

SBA added controls over time, including:

  • duplicate-loan checks;
  • Do Not Pay database screening;
  • taxpayer-identification validation;
  • identity verification;
  • business-formation review;
  • public-record checks;
  • hold codes;
  • automated compliance rules;
  • manual review;
  • loan-review questionnaires;
  • lender referrals; and
  • data analytics.

Many safeguards arrived after hundreds of billions of dollars had already been approved.

“Pay and Chase” Criticism

SBA’s Inspector General described the early pandemic approach as one in which preventive controls were weakened or removed to disburse money quickly.

The government then had to identify, investigate, and recover improper payments after funds were gone.

Post-payment enforcement is expensive because proceeds can be spent, transferred, laundered, or hidden before investigators act.

Inspector General Fraud Estimate

SBA’s Inspector General estimated that more than $200 billion in PPP and COVID-EIDL assistance combined was potentially fraudulent.

“Potentially fraudulent” means that loans matched analytical indicators or suspicious patterns; it does not mean every flagged borrower committed a crime.

The estimate combined programmes and should not be described as a PPP-only proven fraud loss.

Improper Payments Versus Fraud

An improper payment is a payment that should not have been made, was made in the wrong amount, lacked sufficient documentation, or could not be verified.

Fraud requires intentional deception to obtain money or property.

A loan can be improper without criminal fraud, and a suspicious loan can later be validated as eligible.

Forgiven Loans Later Flagged

SBA OIG reported that, as of May 24, 2024, 37,938 forgiven PPP loans totalling approximately $4.6 billion had an open hold code indicating potential clawback.

The loans had been flagged after forgiveness for possible ineligibility.

OIG found SBA had not completed the full review process for those loans and recommended final review and formal recovery criteria.

Criminal Enforcement

Federal prosecutors have charged PPP schemes under:

  • wire fraud;
  • bank fraud;
  • false statements to financial institutions;
  • false claims;
  • money laundering;
  • aggravated identity theft;
  • conspiracy;
  • access-device fraud;
  • tax offenses;
  • obstruction; and
  • related federal crimes.

Civil and Administrative Enforcement

The government can pursue recovery without a criminal conviction.

Tools include:

  • False Claims Act litigation;
  • Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act penalties;
  • common-law unjust enrichment;
  • payment demands;
  • forgiveness reversal;
  • loan acceleration;
  • administrative offset;
  • debarment and suspension;
  • exclusion from SBA programmes;
  • professional discipline; and
  • settlement.

Ten-Year Limitations Period

In August 2022, Congress enacted bipartisan laws extending the statute of limitations for civil and criminal PPP and COVID-EIDL fraud enforcement to ten years.

The extension allows investigations and prosecutions to continue well beyond the programme’s closure.

It also reflects the scale and complexity of analysing millions of applications and tracing funds.

Pandemic Response Accountability Committee

PRAC coordinates federal inspectors general overseeing pandemic spending.

Its Pandemic Analytics Center of Excellence supports:

  • cross-programme data matching;
  • identity analysis;
  • network analysis;
  • investigative referrals;
  • Do Not Pay comparisons;
  • death-record matching;
  • duplicate-payment detection;
  • fraud alerts; and
  • support for law-enforcement investigations.

Government Accountability Office Oversight

GAO repeatedly reviewed PPP implementation, lender participation, forgiveness, fraud controls, equity, and recovery.

GAO found that SBA:

  • moved rapidly under extreme conditions;
  • initially relied on limited safeguards;
  • added controls over time;
  • did not initially have a comprehensive fraud-risk strategy;
  • lacked complete demographic data;
  • improved access to smaller firms in later rounds;
  • needed stronger lender and forgiveness oversight; and
  • needed better tracking of overpayments and recoveries.

SBA Office of Inspector General

SBA OIG audited programme controls, lender conduct, loan eligibility, forgiveness, hold codes, guarantee purchases, data quality, and fraud referrals.

OIG also:

  • received fraud complaints;
  • supported criminal investigations;
  • issued management alerts;
  • identified suspicious loans;
  • recommended recovery actions;
  • tracked corrective action; and
  • reported to Congress.

Public Companies and Large Organisations

Public disclosure that large restaurant groups, public companies, private-equity-backed firms, universities, and national organisations received PPP loans produced public backlash.

Critics argued that these recipients had better access to capital than neighbourhood businesses.

SBA and Treasury issued additional necessity guidance, established the repayment safe harbour, and required enhanced review of large loans.

Franchises

Selected franchises qualified without ordinary affiliation treatment when they carried an SBA franchise identifier.

This allowed many separately owned restaurant, hotel, retail, and service locations to qualify.

Critics argued that national brands and private-equity structures could obtain substantial aggregate aid through location-based or franchise exceptions.

Scale of Forgiveness

By May 24, 2024, SBA had forgiven more than 10.5 million loans totalling more than $750 billion.

The overwhelming majority of programme dollars therefore became public expenditure rather than long-term small-business debt.

Forgiveness prevented an enormous repayment burden but transferred eligibility and fraud risk to taxpayers.

2026 Collection and Enforcement Actions

SBA announced in April 2026 that it was referring approximately 562,000 suspected fraudulent PPP and COVID-EIDL borrowers tied to $22.2 billion in delinquent debt to Treasury for collection.

The agency also announced state-focused suspensions and investigations during 2026.

These announcements concern suspected or potentially fraudulent loans and administrative action. They do not establish guilt for every borrower or prove that every referred debt arose from PPP rather than EIDL.

Relief for Identity-Theft Victims

A person whose identity was used to obtain a PPP loan may need to:

  • report identity theft to SBA OIG;
  • contact the lender;
  • file an identity-theft report;
  • preserve tax and business records;
  • dispute credit reporting;
  • respond to collection notices;
  • notify law enforcement; and
  • seek legal assistance.

Data matching must distinguish actual borrowers from victims whose identifiers were stolen.

Major Achievements

  • Launched within days of enactment during an unprecedented shutdown.
  • Delivered more than 11.7 million loans through thousands of lenders.
  • Provided nearly $800 billion in emergency business liquidity.
  • Helped many employers retain or recall workers.
  • Preserved employee benefits and employer-worker relationships.
  • Supported rent, utilities, suppliers, software, and safety adaptations.
  • Reached sole proprietors, contractors, nonprofits, tribes, and farms.
  • Expanded the role of CDFIs, MDIs, community banks, credit unions, and fintech lenders.
  • Reduced average loan size and increased participation by the smallest firms in 2021.
  • Provided simplified forgiveness for small loans.
  • Enabled tax-free forgiveness with deductible expenses.
  • Demonstrated rapid coordination among Congress, SBA, Treasury, lenders, and the Federal Reserve.
  • Generated extensive public data for programme evaluation and oversight.
  • Preserved business organisations and community institutions during severe uncertainty.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • The first appropriation ran out before many businesses could apply.
  • Existing bank customers received an early advantage.
  • Minority-owned and very small firms faced disproportionate barriers.
  • The payroll formula undercompensated many nonemployer businesses.
  • Rules changed frequently and were difficult to interpret.
  • Lenders applied inconsistent documentation and eligibility standards.
  • The programme was not tightly targeted to firms that would otherwise fail.
  • Forgiveness delivered substantial benefits to owners, creditors, landlords, and suppliers as well as workers.
  • Economic studies estimate a high cost per job preserved.
  • Initial fraud controls were inadequate.
  • The full federal guarantee weakened lender credit-risk incentives.
  • Processing fees encouraged volume and larger loans.
  • Public demographic data were incomplete.
  • Millions of loans created a huge forgiveness and audit workload.
  • Identity theft and fictitious businesses produced extensive losses.
  • Public reporting focused on approvals rather than final business survival.
  • Tax and relief-programme interactions were complex.
  • Ongoing collections and clawbacks continue years after programme closure.

Policy Lessons

Build Verification Before Crisis

Government should establish interoperable business, payroll, tax, identity, death-record, and debarment verification before the next emergency.

Preserve Speed Without Abandoning Controls

Automated checks can occur before payment without imposing full conventional underwriting.

Reach the Smallest Firms Directly

Community lenders, simplified forms, multilingual assistance, gross-income formulas, and reserved application windows should be included at launch rather than added after disparities appear.

Collect Demographic Data

Voluntary and incomplete information prevents timely measurement of equitable access.

Separate Employment Aid from General Business Support

A programme designed to preserve jobs should distinguish worker support from owner, creditor, landlord, and business-survival benefits.

Plan Forgiveness and Recovery at Creation

Application, forgiveness, appeal, audit, collection, and identity-theft procedures should be operational before mass disbursement.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

PPP loan
A temporary, one-hundred-percent SBA-guaranteed loan made under pandemic section 7(a) authority.
First Draw
A borrower’s initial PPP loan.
Second Draw
A later PPP loan for an eligible prior borrower demonstrating required revenue loss.
Payroll costs
Eligible employee and owner compensation, benefits, leave, retirement, and specified taxes, subject to exclusions and caps.
Covered period
The selected eight- to twenty-four-week period during which eligible forgiveness costs were paid or incurred.
Forgiveness
SBA payment of eligible loan principal and interest to the lender, releasing the borrower from that amount.
Full-time equivalent
A measure of employee hours used in forgiveness calculations.
Gross receipts
Total revenue used to determine Second Draw revenue reduction under SBA rules.
Necessity certification
The borrower’s good-faith statement that economic uncertainty made the loan necessary to support operations.
Affiliation
SBA rules combining businesses under common ownership or control for size and eligibility purposes.
Hold code
An SBA system flag indicating a review, eligibility, fraud, or other issue requiring resolution.
Potentially fraudulent
Displaying indicators associated with fraud; not equivalent to a criminal finding.
Improper payment
A payment that should not have been made, was made in the wrong amount, lacked support, or could not be verified.
Guaranty purchase
SBA payment to a lender under the federal guarantee after qualifying default or forgiveness treatment.
Charge-off
Accounting recognition that a debt is unlikely to be collected, without necessarily extinguishing the legal obligation.
Special Assistant United States Attorney
An attorney from another agency or jurisdiction authorised to handle designated federal cases.

Summary

The Paycheck Protection Program was created on March 27, 2020, as an emergency response to the COVID-19 collapse in small-business revenue and employment.

Private lenders made one-percent, fully SBA-guaranteed loans without collateral, personal guarantees, or a credit-elsewhere test.

First Draw loans were generally based on 2.5 months of payroll and capped at $10 million. Second Draw loans were capped at $2 million and generally required 300 or fewer employees and a twenty-five-percent gross-receipts reduction.

Borrowers could obtain forgiveness for payroll and authorised operating costs during an eight- to twenty-four-week period, generally using at least sixty percent of the forgiven amount for payroll.

More than 11.7 million loans totalling nearly $800 billion were originated. By May 2024, more than 10.5 million loans and more than $750 billion had been forgiven.

The programme delivered extraordinary liquidity and preserved employment and business continuity, but research finds substantial variation in targeting and high estimated cost per job preserved.

Early access favoured firms with strong banking relationships. Later reforms increased participation by smaller businesses, sole proprietors, community lenders, and underserved counties.

PPP’s speed-first design created severe fraud and improper-payment risk. Initial controls were limited, millions of applications relied on self-certification, and the government continues audits, clawbacks, collections, civil suits, and criminal cases.

The programme closed on May 31, 2021. Its remaining administration now concerns forgiveness, servicing, appeals, records, recovery, identity theft, and enforcement.

PPP demonstrated both the power and danger of using the private financial system to distribute mass emergency aid: it could move money quickly, but access, targeting, documentation, and accountability depended heavily on lender incentives and government data systems.
