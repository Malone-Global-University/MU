Temporary Assistance for Needy Families

The federal block grant created by the welfare reform law of 1996 to replace Aid to Families with Dependent Children and finance state, territorial, and tribal cash assistance, employment, child-care, family-support, and related programs for families with children.

What Is Temporary Assistance for Needy Families?

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families is a federal block grant that finances state, territorial, and tribal programs intended to promote economic security, employment, family stability, and care of children in needy families.

TANF is not one nationally uniform cash-welfare program. Each jurisdiction designs its own assistance program within broad federal purposes, fiscal rules, work-accountability standards, time limits, reporting requirements, and prohibited uses.

Monthly cash assistance is only one use of TANF. Jurisdictions also spend TANF and state maintenance-of-effort funds on child care, employment and training, refundable tax credits, prekindergarten, child-welfare services, emergency benefits, transportation, pregnancy-prevention programs, fatherhood initiatives, and other services.

The federal government does not ordinarily accept public applications or issue TANF cash benefits directly. Families apply through the responsible state, territorial, or tribal agency.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains TANF’s origins, statutory purposes, financing, jurisdictional structure, eligibility, cash assistance, time limits, work rules, child-support provisions, sanctions, services, expenditure categories, performance system, outcomes, controversies, and current status.

It does not replace a state or tribal eligibility determination. Benefit levels, income tests, resource limits, family caps, diversion policies, work exemptions, sanctions, time-limit rules, and application procedures vary significantly.

Tribal TANF, Native Employment Works, Healthy Marriage and Responsible Fatherhood grants, the TANF Contingency Fund, and outcomes-based pilots are included as related parts of the statutory structure but may merit separate fact sheets.

Geographic Scope

TANF basic block grants are available to the fifty states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the United States Virgin Islands.

Federally recognized tribes, Alaska Native organizations, and approved intertribal consortia may operate Tribal TANF programs for defined service populations and geographic areas.

American Samoa is treated as a territory for selected regulatory purposes but does not receive the ordinary TANF state-family-assistance grant in the same manner as the principal participating territories.

Local administration varies. Some states operate centralized programs, while others delegate eligibility and employment functions to counties, regions, contractors, workforce boards, or nonprofit organizations.

Historical Context

TANF emerged from decades of political conflict over Aid to Families with Dependent Children, work, family structure, federalism, race, gender, cash-assistance adequacy, and state authority.

By the early 1990s, reform proposals increasingly favored time limits, mandatory work, block grants, family caps, stronger child-support enforcement, and broad state experimentation.

Several states obtained Section 1115 waivers to test policies that later appeared in federal TANF law.

President Bill Clinton campaigned to “end welfare as we know it.” After vetoing two earlier congressional bills, he signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act on August 22, 1996.

Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996

The 1996 welfare law repealed AFDC, the Job Opportunities and Basic Skills Training program, and the federal Emergency Assistance entitlement.

It consolidated historical federal funding into TANF, a fixed block grant with broad state flexibility.

The law imposed work-participation targets, a federal time limit, state maintenance-of-effort requirements, child-support cooperation, noncitizen restrictions, data reporting, fiscal penalties, and other conditions.

It also stated that TANF creates no individual entitlement to assistance or services.

Transition from AFDC to TANF

States began TANF at different dates after submitting plans and receiving federal block-grant authority.

Existing AFDC families were converted into state TANF programs and became subject to new eligibility, work, sanction, time-limit, and reporting rules.

All states completed the transition no later than July 1, 1997.

Fiscal year 1997 statistics can include both AFDC and TANF months and must be interpreted carefully.

The Four Statutory Purposes

  1. Provide assistance to needy families so children may be cared for in their own homes or in the homes of relatives.
  2. End dependence on government benefits by promoting job preparation, work, and marriage.
  3. Prevent and reduce out-of-wedlock pregnancies and establish annual numerical goals for prevention and reduction.
  4. Encourage the formation and maintenance of two-parent families.

The four purposes are broader than monthly cash assistance and allow TANF funds to support a wide range of activities.

Federal Administration

The Office of Family Assistance administers TANF within the Administration for Children and Families, Department of Health and Human Services.

Federal responsibilities include:

  • awarding state, territorial, and tribal grants;
  • reviewing state and tribal plans;
  • interpreting federal law;
  • collecting caseload, work, expenditure, and outcome data;
  • calculating work-participation rates and caseload-reduction credits;
  • monitoring maintenance-of-effort spending;
  • administering penalties and corrective compliance;
  • operating contingency and competitive grants;
  • providing training and technical assistance;
  • supporting research and evaluation; and
  • coordinating with child care, child support, child welfare, workforce, and other systems.

Limitation on Federal Regulatory Authority

TANF law limits the federal government’s authority to regulate state conduct except where the statute expressly authorizes regulation or enforcement.

This limitation reflects Congress’s decision to replace detailed AFDC federal rules with state flexibility.

Most federal rules focus on grants, work accountability, time limits, penalties, maintenance of effort, data, and prohibited uses rather than imposing one national eligibility system.

Funding Authorization

The original 1996 law authorized TANF funding through fiscal year 2002.

Congress has repeatedly continued the program through temporary extensions, with one longer extension under the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005.

Public Law 119-75, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, continues TANF and related activities through December 31, 2026.

The recurring use of short-term extensions creates administrative uncertainty even though the basic block-grant formula has remained largely unchanged.

State Family Assistance Grants

The State Family Assistance Grant is the basic TANF block grant.

The national total is approximately $16.5 billion annually before specified reservations, sequestration, tribal adjustments, or penalties.

State allocations are based largely on federal AFDC, Emergency Assistance, and JOBS expenditures from the early and mid-1990s.

The formula does not automatically adjust for inflation, population, poverty, unemployment, or current caseload.

Declining Real Value of the Block Grant

The nominal basic block grant has remained nearly flat since 1996.

Inflation has substantially reduced its purchasing power.

Population growth, housing costs, child-care costs, and changing labor markets have further reduced the value of a fixed historical allocation.

States can carry forward unused federal funds without fiscal-year limitation, but reserves do not restore the block grant’s national real value.

Maintenance of Effort

Maintenance of effort requires states to continue spending their own funds on eligible families and allowable TANF-related activities.

The basic requirement is generally seventy-five percent of the state’s historical fiscal year 1994 spending on AFDC and related programs.

The requirement rises to eighty percent if the state fails to meet federal work-participation requirements.

Qualifying state spending may occur within the TANF program, in segregated state-funded programs, or in separate state programs when statutory conditions are satisfied.

Maintenance-of-Effort Eligible Families

Most maintenance-of-effort expenditures must benefit “eligible families,” generally families containing a child or pregnant individual that meet state-defined financial need.

Healthy-marriage and responsible-fatherhood activities are a major exception and may serve people without a financial eligibility test.

States must avoid counting expenditures already used to satisfy another federal matching requirement, except where TANF law expressly permits overlap.

TANF Contingency Fund

The Contingency Fund supplies additional federal money to qualifying states during periods of economic need.

A state generally must satisfy an unemployment or food-stamp caseload trigger and meet an increased maintenance-of-effort requirement.

Payments are provisional and reconciled according to federal formulas and state spending.

Only the fifty states and District of Columbia qualify for ordinary Contingency Fund payments.

Territorial TANF

Puerto Rico, Guam, and the United States Virgin Islands operate TANF programs under federal block-grant law.

Territorial grants are affected by statutory funding caps under Section 1108 of the Social Security Act.

Territories establish their own cash-assistance and service systems within federal requirements.

Territorial economic conditions, disasters, migration, benefit adequacy, and federal funding limitations create distinct policy issues.

Tribal TANF

Eligible tribes, Alaska Native organizations, and intertribal consortia may operate their own TANF programs.

A tribal grantee submits a three-year Tribal Family Assistance Plan defining its service area, eligible population, benefits, work activities, time limits, sanctions, and administration.

The tribal grant generally represents the state block-grant amount attributable to Native families transferred into the tribal program.

Tribal TANF permits greater cultural, governmental, and labor-market adaptation than ordinary state administration.

Tribal Sovereignty and Program Design

Tribal TANF operates within the federal government’s relationship with sovereign tribal nations.

Tribal programs can incorporate local customs, language, kinship, subsistence activity, community employment, and economic conditions.

Federal approval is required, but tribal work-participation standards and time-limit policies can differ from state rules through the approved plan.

Coordination is required when families move between state and tribal service areas.

Native Employment Works

Native Employment Works is a separate formula-grant program created by the 1996 law for eligible tribes and Alaska Native organizations that operated qualifying employment programs before TANF.

Funds support work activities and services designed to help needy Native adults obtain employment.

A grantee may operate both Tribal TANF and Native Employment Works when it satisfies each program’s requirements.

State TANF Plans

Each state submits a plan describing how it intends to conduct TANF.

State plans include certifications and descriptions concerning:

  • assistance and services;
  • work requirements;
  • child care;
  • support for teen parents;
  • child-support cooperation;
  • time limits;
  • domestic-violence procedures;
  • fraud and abuse controls;
  • consultation with local governments and private organizations;
  • appeals and administrative systems; and
  • treatment of families moving into the state.

Federal approval of a state plan does not establish that every state policy is federally endorsed or legally immune from challenge.

State and Local Administration

States administer TANF through human-services departments, workforce agencies, county welfare offices, contractors, or combinations of institutions.

Local offices accept applications, determine eligibility, issue cash assistance, assign work activities, impose sanctions, authorize supportive services, and conduct recertification.

Administrative structure affects access, consistency, data quality, staff training, appeals, and coordination with SNAP, Medicaid, child care, and child support.

No Individual Entitlement

Federal TANF law expressly states that no individual entitlement to assistance or services is created.

A jurisdiction may restrict eligibility, create waiting periods, use diversion, close categories, impose family sanctions, or limit benefits subject to state law, federal restrictions, and constitutional requirements.

State statutes or constitutions may create enforceable rights beyond federal TANF law.

The absence of a federal entitlement is one of the most important differences from AFDC.

Definition of a Needy Family

Federal law generally leaves each state, territory, or tribe to define financial need.

A family receiving “assistance” with federal TANF or maintenance-of-effort funds must ordinarily contain a dependent child or pregnant individual and meet the jurisdiction’s financial test.

TANF does not impose a national income ceiling, asset limit, standard of need, or minimum benefit.

Dependent Child

For federal TANF purposes, a dependent child is generally younger than eighteen or age eighteen and a full-time student in secondary, vocational, or technical school.

The child must live with a parent or specified adult relative for federally funded assistance.

States may define broader service populations for nonassistance activities when the expenditure remains reasonably calculated to accomplish a statutory purpose.

Financial Eligibility

States determine income tests, resource limits, disregards, family composition, budgeting methods, and payment standards.

Relevant factors may include:

  • earned and unearned income;
  • assets and vehicles;
  • family size;
  • residence;
  • citizenship or eligible immigration status;
  • child relationship;
  • earnings disregards;
  • child support;
  • housing and child-care expenses;
  • work compliance; and
  • lifetime receipt.

Cash-Benefit Levels

States set maximum monthly grants and adjustment rules.

Benefit levels vary dramatically and are not indexed uniformly to inflation or poverty.

In most states, maximum cash assistance for a family of three remains far below the federal poverty line.

A family can meet the state’s definition of need yet receive no benefit because of income thresholds, sanctions, time limits, or diversion policies.

Federal Definition of Assistance

“Assistance” is a technical federal term covering cash, payments, vouchers, and other benefits designed to meet ongoing basic needs.

It also includes supportive services such as transportation or child care when provided to families not employed and as part of ongoing assistance.

Assistance generally triggers federal time limits, work rules, child-support assignment, data reporting, and other requirements.

The classification of a benefit as assistance or nonassistance is therefore legally significant.

Benefits and Services That Are Not Assistance

Federal regulations exclude several benefits and services from the definition of assistance.

Examples include:

  • nonrecurrent short-term benefits addressing a specific crisis;
  • work subsidies paid to employers;
  • supportive services for employed families;
  • refundable tax credits;
  • education and training for people not receiving assistance;
  • information and referral;
  • case-management services that do not provide ongoing basic-needs support;
  • child-care assistance for employed families; and
  • other services meeting regulatory exclusions.

Nonassistance benefits generally do not count toward the federal time limit and may avoid some work and reporting rules.

Monthly Cash Assistance

State TANF programs provide monthly cash benefits under names such as Temporary Assistance, Family Assistance, WorkFirst, CalWORKs, or Families First.

Cash may be issued by electronic benefit card, electronic transfer, check, or another approved method.

Families use cash for rent, utilities, clothing, transportation, personal care, food, household supplies, and other needs.

Cash assistance represents a minority of total TANF and maintenance-of-effort spending.

Child-Only Cases

A child-only case provides cash assistance for children while excluding the adult caretaker from the grant.

Common reasons include:

  • the caretaker is a nonparent relative;
  • the adult receives Supplemental Security Income;
  • the adult is ineligible because of immigration status;
  • the adult is sanctioned;
  • the adult has reached a time limit;
  • the adult declines assistance for personal needs; or
  • the child receives aid under a kinship-care policy.

Child-only cases generally do not accumulate federal time-limit months when no adult or minor head of household receives assistance.

Kinship Care

TANF can support children living with grandparents or other relatives outside formal foster care.

Relative caregivers may receive a child-only grant without including their own income under state rules, or they may apply as a needy family.

TANF grants are usually much smaller than licensed foster-care maintenance payments.

States may also use TANF for kinship navigator programs, legal assistance, emergency support, or other services.

Two-Parent Families

TANF does not require a parent to be absent, deceased, incapacitated, or unemployed in the manner required under AFDC.

States can assist eligible two-parent families under their own financial and program rules.

Two-parent cases are subject to a federal ninety-percent work-participation target unless excluded or served through arrangements outside the federal rate.

Some states use Separate State Programs for two-parent families because the federal target is difficult to satisfy.

Pregnant Individuals

TANF permits assistance to a needy pregnant individual without another child, subject to jurisdictional policy.

States determine the point in pregnancy at which eligibility begins and the services available.

Pregnancy-only cases may be subject to different work, benefit, and time-limit rules.

Teen Parents

Unmarried minor parents generally must live with a parent, legal guardian, adult relative, or in an approved supervised setting to receive federally funded assistance.

They must generally participate in secondary education or an equivalent activity unless they already possess a diploma or equivalent.

Exceptions can apply when the home is unsafe, unavailable, or otherwise inappropriate.

States must provide or assist in locating an approved supervised living arrangement when necessary.

Citizenship and Noncitizen Eligibility

United States citizens and qualified noncitizens may qualify subject to state financial and program rules.

The 1996 welfare law generally bars many qualified noncitizens entering on or after August 22, 1996, from federally funded TANF assistance during their first five years in qualified status.

Exceptions apply to refugees, asylees, veterans, active-duty service members, certain trafficking survivors, and other statutory groups.

States may use their own maintenance-of-effort funds to assist lawfully present noncitizens whom federal TANF funds cannot support, subject to state policy.

Ineligible parents may apply for eligible citizen children without receiving assistance for themselves.

Application Process

Families apply through the responsible state, territorial, county, or tribal TANF agency.

The process commonly includes:

  • application and interview;
  • identity and residency verification;
  • household and child-relationship documentation;
  • income and asset review;
  • citizenship or immigration verification for applicants;
  • child-support cooperation screening;
  • time-limit review;
  • work assessment;
  • orientation or employability planning;
  • domestic-violence screening; and
  • written eligibility notice.

Diversion Programs

States use diversion to address an immediate need without opening an ongoing cash-assistance case.

Diversion may involve a lump-sum payment, job referral, transportation, child care, housing assistance, or referral to another program.

A family may be required to agree not to apply for monthly assistance for a specified period or may have the payment counted against future benefits.

Diversion can prevent prolonged assistance but can also discourage eligible families from applying or obscure unmet need.

Nonrecurrent Short-Term Benefits

A nonrecurrent short-term benefit addresses a specific crisis or episode of need, is not intended to meet recurrent needs, and does not extend beyond four months.

Examples include:

  • rent arrears;
  • utility shutoff prevention;
  • emergency food or clothing;
  • transportation repair;
  • relocation;
  • disaster assistance;
  • short-term domestic-violence support; and
  • employment-related expenses.

Properly classified nonrecurrent benefits do not count as federal assistance for time-limit and work-rule purposes.

Federal Five-Year Time Limit

A state generally may not use federal TANF funds to provide assistance to a family containing an adult who has received federally funded TANF assistance for sixty cumulative months.

The federal limit is measured by months, not consecutive years.

States may impose shorter limits, intermediate limits, periodic eligibility closures, or benefit reductions under state law.

The federal limit applies to adult-headed assistance units, not automatically to every child-only case.

Months Excluded from the Federal Time Limit

Months generally do not count when:

  • no adult or minor head of household receives assistance;
  • benefits are funded solely with state maintenance-of-effort money in a qualifying Separate State Program or segregated arrangement;
  • the benefit is nonassistance;
  • the adult lives in qualifying Indian country or an Alaska Native village with unemployment of at least fifty percent; or
  • another statutory exclusion applies.

Hardship and Domestic-Violence Extensions

States may provide federally funded assistance beyond sixty months to as many as twenty percent of the average monthly caseload.

Extensions must be based on hardship, as defined by the state, or domestic violence and related circumstances recognized by federal law.

States may also use state-only funds to serve families beyond the federal time limit.

The twenty-percent provision is a ceiling on federally funded extensions, not a requirement that every state extend assistance to that share of families.

State Time Limits

State policies vary in length, exemptions, extensions, and consequences.

Some states terminate the entire family grant at the limit; others remove adults, reduce benefits, or continue child-only assistance.

States may stop the clock during employment, disability, caregiving, domestic violence, or other conditions.

A state limit may expire before the federal sixty-month limit.

Individual Work Requirements

States establish work requirements for adult recipients within federal law and state plans.

Requirements may involve:

  • job search;
  • employment;
  • work experience;
  • community service;
  • vocational education;
  • school attendance;
  • subsidized employment;
  • job-readiness activity;
  • rehabilitation or barrier-removal activity; and
  • cooperation with case-management plans.

State individual rules should be distinguished from the federal state-level work-participation-rate calculation.

Federal Work-Participation Rates

The federal government measures whether a state has a required percentage of work-eligible families participating for the required hours in countable activities.

The statutory targets are:

  • fifty percent for all families; and
  • ninety percent for two-parent families.

Actual required rates can be reduced by the caseload-reduction credit and qualified state spending above the maintenance-of-effort floor.

Failure can reduce a state’s block grant unless reasonable-cause or corrective-compliance provisions apply.

Caseload-Reduction Credit

The caseload-reduction credit lowers a state’s work-participation target when the state’s assistance caseload falls relative to a statutory base year for reasons other than eligibility changes.

The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 reset the base year from fiscal year 1995 to fiscal year 2005.

The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 reset the base year to fiscal year 2015 beginning in fiscal year 2026.

Because caseloads declined substantially, credits have often reduced effective state targets far below fifty or ninety percent.

Excess Maintenance-of-Effort Credit

State spending above the required maintenance-of-effort level can increase the caseload-reduction credit.

The calculation treats the state as though the excess spending supported additional cases, increasing the measured caseload decline.

This mechanism can lower the effective work-participation target without increasing the share of current assistance recipients engaged in work.

The Small-Checks Practice

Some states provided small maintenance-of-effort-funded payments to employed families outside the TANF program and included those working families in work-participation calculations.

Because the recipients were already employed, the practice could increase the calculated participation rate with little relationship to ordinary cash-assistance services.

The Fiscal Responsibility Act requires HHS to exclude families receiving less than thirty-five dollars per month in Separate State Program assistance from the rate calculation beginning in fiscal year 2026.

HHS proposed implementing regulations in April 2026.

Countable Work Activities

Federal law identifies twelve work activities:

  • unsubsidized employment;
  • subsidized private-sector employment;
  • subsidized public-sector employment;
  • work experience;
  • on-the-job training;
  • job-search and job-readiness assistance;
  • community-service programs;
  • vocational educational training;
  • job-skills training directly related to employment;
  • education directly related to employment for people without a diploma;
  • satisfactory secondary-school attendance; and
  • child-care services for another recipient participating in community service.

Core and Supplemental Activities

To count toward the federal rate, most adults must complete a minimum number of hours in designated core activities before supplemental education or training hours can count.

Core activities emphasize employment, subsidized work, work experience, community service, on-the-job training, job search within limits, and vocational education within limits.

This structure can discourage longer education programs even when postsecondary credentials may improve long-term earnings.

Required Hours

A single parent with a child younger than six generally counts when participating at least twenty hours per week.

Other single-parent or caretaker families generally require thirty hours.

Two-parent families generally require thirty-five hours, rising to fifty-five hours when receiving federally funded child care in connection with participation.

Special rules apply to teen parents and single parents younger than twenty who maintain satisfactory school attendance or education directly related to employment.

Education and Training Limits

Vocational educational training can generally count as a core activity for no more than twelve months per individual.

No more than thirty percent of the families counted toward a state’s rate may ordinarily rely on vocational education or teen-parent school participation, subject to statutory calculation.

Postsecondary degree programs may not fit federal countability rules even when a state supports them with its own policies.

States can offer broader education using state funds or accept that participation will not count toward the federal rate.

Job Search and Job Readiness

Job-search and job-readiness assistance counts for a limited number of weeks each fiscal year.

Additional weeks can count in states meeting federal unemployment or SNAP-related criteria.

Job readiness may include substance-use treatment, mental-health treatment, or rehabilitation when necessary for employment and supervised as required.

Verification of Work Hours

States must document and verify actual hours of participation using federal definitions and approved procedures.

Required evidence may include payroll, employer statements, attendance records, electronic timekeeping, school records, case notes, or approved projections of employment hours.

The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 strengthened federal definitions and verification after concerns about inconsistent state reporting.

Work-Eligible Individual

Federal regulations define which adults and minor heads of household are included in the work-rate denominator.

Certain individuals are excluded, including some recipients with disabilities, nonrecipient parents, sanctioned individuals for limited months, and other specified categories.

A person can be exempt from a state’s individual work requirement yet still affect federal rate calculations unless excluded under federal rules.

Sanctions

States must reduce or terminate assistance when an individual refuses without good cause to engage in required work, subject to state policy and federal protections.

Sanctions can remove the adult’s needs, reduce the full grant, or terminate the entire family benefit.

States determine good cause, cure periods, conciliation, duration, and whether sanctions escalate.

Full-family sanctions can reduce resources available to children who did not commit the violation.

Child-Care Protection Against Sanctions

A state may not penalize a single parent with a child younger than six for refusing work when the parent demonstrates an inability to obtain needed child care.

Federal law identifies lack of appropriate care, unreasonable distance, unsuitable informal care, or unavailable affordable care as relevant conditions.

The protection depends on effective notice, documentation, and state implementation.

Nondisplacement

TANF work participants may not displace existing employees, replace workers laid off from the same or substantially equivalent job, or infringe collective-bargaining agreements.

Workers and participants must have access to grievance procedures concerning displacement.

Nondisplacement protections are especially relevant to unpaid work experience and community service.

Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023

The Fiscal Responsibility Act changed TANF work accountability and authorized performance pilots.

Major provisions include:

  • employment-outcome reporting beginning in fiscal year 2025;
  • resetting the caseload-reduction-credit base year to fiscal year 2015 beginning in fiscal year 2026;
  • excluding certain Separate State Program payments below thirty-five dollars from work-rate calculations beginning in fiscal year 2026;
  • authorizing HHS to select up to five states for outcomes-based pilot programs; and
  • rescinding remaining Emergency Pandemic Fund balances.

Outcomes-Based TANF Pilot Programs

The Fiscal Responsibility Act authorizes HHS to test alternative performance systems in as many as five states.

Pilot measures can emphasize employment, earnings, retention, skill gains, or other outcomes rather than relying solely on participation hours.

Selected states require negotiated benchmarks, data systems, evaluation, and protections against reducing services to people with greater barriers.

Federal evaluation activity remained underway in 2026.

Child-Support Cooperation

TANF applicants and recipients generally must assign support rights to the state and cooperate in establishing paternity and obtaining child support, unless good cause or another exception applies.

States can impose sanctions for noncooperation.

Domestic-violence screening is essential because enforcement can expose survivors to contact, retaliation, or danger.

Child-Support Distribution

States collect support through the Title IV-D child-support system.

Federal law permits states to pass through specified amounts to TANF families and disregard those payments in benefit calculations, with partial federal cost sharing under statutory conditions.

Policies vary concerning whether families receive current support while receiving cash assistance and how arrears are distributed.

Family Violence Option

States may adopt the Family Violence Option and certify that they will identify victims, refer them to services, and waive requirements that would make it more difficult to escape violence or unfairly penalize them.

Waivers may involve time limits, work requirements, child-support cooperation, residence, or other program rules.

A waiver should be individualized and accompanied by confidentiality and safety protections.

Sexual Harassment, Domestic Violence, Sexual Assault, and Stalking

Federal extensions enacted in 2022 required state-plan certifications concerning procedures to help applicants and potential applicants who experience sexual harassment, domestic violence, sexual assault, or stalking.

States must provide information, referrals, confidentiality, and appropriate program responses consistent with federal law.

Family Caps

A family-cap policy denies or reduces additional cash assistance for a child conceived or born while the family receives assistance.

Several states carried AFDC waiver family caps into TANF or enacted similar policies.

Supporters argued that caps reduced incentives for additional births. Critics argued that they punished children, increased hardship, and intruded into reproductive decisions.

Family caps are state policies rather than a federal TANF requirement.

Drug-Felony Restrictions

The 1996 welfare law imposed a lifetime ban on TANF and SNAP for people convicted of specified drug felonies after August 22, 1996.

States may opt out of or modify the TANF ban.

State policies range from full repeal to testing, treatment, probation compliance, waiting periods, or continued disqualification.

Fugitive-Felon and Probation Rules

Federal law restricts TANF assistance for people fleeing to avoid prosecution, custody, or confinement for a felony and for people violating probation or parole.

Administration requires reliable evidence and due process rather than unverified database matches.

Strikers

Federal TANF funds cannot provide assistance to a family for a month in which the parent or other caretaker relative is participating in a strike.

A striker’s income and resources can still affect the eligibility of other family members.

Electronic Benefit Restrictions

States must prevent TANF cash benefits from being accessed through electronic benefit transactions at:

  • liquor stores;
  • casinos, gambling establishments, and gaming facilities; and
  • adult-entertainment establishments.

These restrictions apply to access locations and do not transform TANF into a food-only benefit.

Permitted Uses of Federal TANF Funds

Federal TANF funds may support:

  • activities reasonably calculated to accomplish one of the four TANF purposes;
  • activities authorized under the former AFDC, Emergency Assistance, or JOBS programs as of September 30, 1995;
  • transfers to the Child Care and Development Fund;
  • transfers to the Social Services Block Grant within statutory limits;
  • Individual Development Accounts;
  • administration within the federal cap;
  • information technology and program systems;
  • employment and training;
  • cash and emergency assistance;
  • child care;
  • pregnancy-prevention activities;
  • fatherhood and marriage programs;
  • child-welfare and family-support services; and
  • other qualifying benefits and services.

Services for Needy and Nonneedy Families

Federal TANF funds used for assistance under the first and second purposes generally must serve needy families.

Activities under the third and fourth purposes—pregnancy prevention and two-parent-family formation—may serve people without a financial-need test.

Some nonassistance services can reach broader populations when reasonably calculated to accomplish a statutory purpose.

Transfers to Child Care and Social Services

A state may transfer up to thirty percent of its TANF grant to the Child Care and Development Fund and the Social Services Block Grant combined.

The Social Services Block Grant transfer is subject to a separate statutory percentage limit.

Transferred funds become subject primarily to the rules of the receiving program.

Transfers reduce the amount remaining for direct TANF expenditures but may support long-term service infrastructure.

Child Care

TANF is a major source of child-care funding.

States can:

  • transfer federal funds to the Child Care and Development Fund;
  • spend TANF directly on child care;
  • count qualifying state child-care spending toward maintenance of effort; and
  • provide child care as a work support, diversion service, or short-term benefit.

Eligibility and provider rules depend on whether funds are transferred, directly spent, or counted as state maintenance of effort.

Employment, Education, and Training

TANF funds support job search, vocational education, basic education, subsidized employment, work experience, apprenticeships, transportation, case management, uniforms, tools, and other work supports.

States may provide activities broader than those countable in the federal work-participation rate.

Fiscal year 2024 financial data reported approximately 7.7 percent of combined TANF and maintenance-of-effort spending and transfers for work, education, and training activities.

Subsidized Employment

TANF can reimburse or subsidize wages for private, public, or nonprofit employment.

Effective programs combine real wages, employer supervision, training, worker protections, and transition to unsubsidized employment.

The TANF Emergency Fund during the Great Recession demonstrated the program’s capacity to expand subsidized employment when additional federal reimbursement was available.

Refundable Tax Credits

States may use TANF or maintenance-of-effort funds for the refundable portion of state earned-income, child, or related tax credits when statutory conditions are satisfied.

Refundable credits support working families and may count as nonassistance.

Spending through tax systems can reach families outside the cash-assistance caseload.

Prekindergarten and Education

States use TANF and maintenance-of-effort funds for prekindergarten, school readiness, after-school programs, youth development, and other education services.

An expenditure must be reasonably calculated to accomplish a TANF purpose and satisfy applicable eligibility and maintenance-of-effort requirements.

Broad education spending illustrates how TANF evolved from a cash-welfare replacement into a general family-services funding source.

Child Welfare and Family Preservation

TANF can fund services intended to prevent child removal, preserve families, support reunification, assist kinship caregivers, or address family crises.

Uses include home-based services, emergency assistance, case management, transportation, housing support, and prevention programs.

TANF cannot substitute automatically for every Title IV-B or Title IV-E requirement, and states must assign costs correctly.

Housing and Homelessness

TANF funds may support rent, utility assistance, shelter, relocation, eviction prevention, short-term housing, and services for eligible families.

Ongoing housing payments designed to meet basic needs may constitute assistance and trigger time-limit and work rules.

Short-term crisis payments can qualify as nonrecurrent benefits.

Transportation

TANF can fund bus passes, vehicle repair, car purchase programs, mileage, rides, driver licensing, and transportation coordination.

Transportation is often essential for work, child care, training, health care, and rural participation.

States determine eligibility, limits, ownership rules, and whether the support is assistance or nonassistance.

Individual Development Accounts

TANF permits eligible individuals to save in Individual Development Accounts for specified purposes.

Authorized uses can include postsecondary education, first-home purchase, and business capitalization.

State policy governs matching, financial education, withdrawal, and participation.

Healthy Marriage and Responsible Fatherhood

The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 established federal competitive grants for healthy-marriage and responsible-fatherhood activities associated with TANF’s family-formation purposes.

Activities can include relationship education, marriage skills, parenting, employment services for fathers, co-parenting, and economic stability.

Participation must comply with domestic-violence safeguards, civil rights, and restrictions on religious activity.

These grants are distinct from each state’s basic TANF block grant.

Administrative Costs

States generally may use no more than fifteen percent of the federal block grant for administrative costs.

Information technology and systems costs can receive special treatment under federal definitions.

Case management and program services are not necessarily administrative merely because staff perform them.

Accurate classification matters because excessive administrative spending can produce fiscal penalties.

Unspent and Reserved Funds

States may reserve federal TANF funds without fiscal-year limitation.

Reserved funds can support future allowable benefits, services, or activities.

Large balances may provide recession or disaster reserves but can also indicate that funds are not reaching families with current needs.

Fiscal Year 2024 TANF and Maintenance-of-Effort Spending

Combined federal TANF and state maintenance-of-effort expenditures and transfers totaled approximately $37.516 billion in fiscal year 2024.

National spending shares included:

  • 21.8 percent for basic assistance;
  • approximately 7.7 percent for work, education, and training;
  • approximately thirteen percent for child care, including transfers to the Child Care and Development Fund;
  • additional spending for refundable tax credits, prekindergarten, child welfare, pregnancy prevention, fatherhood, administration, and other services; and
  • transfers to the Social Services Block Grant and other authorized categories.

National percentages conceal major state differences.

Declining Share Used for Basic Assistance

In the early TANF years, a much larger share of funding supported cash assistance.

By fiscal year 2024, only 21.8 percent of combined federal and state spending and transfers supported basic assistance.

The shift reflects caseload decline, broad spending authority, use of TANF for other state programs, and the fixed value of the grant.

Whether the shift represents beneficial service diversification or diversion away from the poorest families is a central policy dispute.

Fiscal Substitution

TANF flexibility allows states to use federal funds for programs previously supported by state money.

Maintenance of effort limits but does not eliminate substitution because it is tied to historical spending and permits broad qualifying expenditures.

Substitution can free state revenue for other priorities without increasing total assistance to low-income families.

Evaluating TANF therefore requires examining both federal and state spending and identifying whether services expanded or financing merely shifted.

Fiscal Year 2024 Caseload

Office of Family Assistance data show that in an average month in fiscal year 2024:

  • approximately 569,600 adults received TANF cash assistance; and
  • approximately 1.5 million children received TANF cash assistance.

These figures include cash-assistance recipients and do not measure every person benefiting from TANF-funded child care, tax credits, education, child welfare, or other services.

Caseload Decline Since 1996

The cash-assistance caseload declined sharply after 1994 and especially after TANF implementation.

Contributing factors include:

  • strong late-1990s labor markets;
  • expanded earned-income tax credits;
  • state waiver policies;
  • work requirements;
  • sanctions;
  • time limits;
  • diversion;
  • administrative barriers;
  • changes in eligibility; and
  • changes in family structure and participation choices.

Cash Assistance and the Number of Families in Poverty

A common safety-net measure compares the number of families receiving TANF cash assistance with the number of families with children living in poverty.

Nationally, the ratio fell substantially from AFDC levels and varies widely by state.

A low ratio can reflect restrictive eligibility, low benefits, sanctions, time limits, administrative barriers, or families choosing not to participate.

The ratio does not measure noncash TANF services.

Benefit Adequacy

Maximum TANF grants remain below the federal poverty line in every state.

In many jurisdictions, inflation has reduced real benefit value since 1996.

Families often combine TANF with SNAP, housing aid, Medicaid, child support, earnings, tax credits, or informal support.

Low grants can reduce state spending and work-disincentive concerns but leave families unable to meet rent, utilities, transportation, and personal needs.

Responsiveness to Recession

The fixed block grant does not automatically increase when unemployment or applications rise.

States must use reserves, contingency funds, supplemental appropriations, state revenue, or service reductions.

During the Great Recession, TANF cash caseloads increased much less than unemployment and SNAP participation.

The limited response compared with AFDC is a major criticism of block-grant design.

TANF Emergency Contingency Fund, 2009–2010

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act created a five-billion-dollar TANF Emergency Contingency Fund.

It reimbursed states, territories, and tribes for eighty percent of increased expenditures on basic assistance, nonrecurrent short-term benefits, and subsidized employment.

The fund supported large subsidized-employment initiatives and demonstrated that TANF could expand during recession when Congress supplied additional financing.

COVID-19 Pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic produced unemployment, school closures, child-care disruption, housing instability, and administrative challenges.

States used TANF for emergency payments, remote services, work-rule adjustments, housing support, technology, and other responses.

Cash caseload response varied and was smaller than increases in unemployment insurance and SNAP.

The American Rescue Plan Act created a one-billion-dollar Pandemic Emergency Assistance Fund for nonrecurrent short-term benefits.

Pandemic Emergency Assistance Fund

The Pandemic Emergency Assistance Fund distributed temporary federal money to states, territories, and tribes.

Funds supported nonrecurrent short-term benefits for low-income families with children.

The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 rescinded remaining unobligated balances.

The fund did not permanently increase the TANF basic block grant.

Employment and Earnings Outcomes

TANF recipients who leave assistance for work often enter low-wage service, care, retail, hospitality, logistics, or temporary employment.

Employment can increase income, but instability, limited hours, child care, transportation, health, and housing costs affect sustainability.

Caseload exit does not establish that a family obtained work or escaped poverty.

The Fiscal Responsibility Act requires new employment-outcome reporting and authorizes pilots intended to measure results more directly.

Criticism of the Work-Participation Rate

The work-participation rate measures whether states place a required share of counted families in federally countable activities for specified hours.

It does not directly measure:

  • employment after leaving TANF;
  • earnings growth;
  • job quality;
  • poverty reduction;
  • credential attainment;
  • family stability;
  • reduction of barriers; or
  • well-being of children.

States can satisfy the rate through caseload reduction, excess maintenance-of-effort credits, or separate-state-program design rather than engaging a majority of recipients.

New Employment-Outcome Reporting

Beginning in fiscal year 2025, states must report employment and earnings outcomes for work-eligible individuals leaving assistance.

Measures include employment rates in specified quarters after exit, earnings, and other information required by the Fiscal Responsibility Act.

Outcome reporting supplements rather than automatically replaces the work-participation rate outside selected pilot states.

TANF Data Reporting

States and tribes submit detailed information on assistance families and closed cases.

Data include:

  • family composition;
  • age and relationship;
  • race and ethnicity;
  • citizenship status;
  • income;
  • benefit amount;
  • work activity;
  • hours;
  • sanctions;
  • time-limit months;
  • child support;
  • education;
  • case closure; and
  • expenditures.

Aggregate public data do not identify individual recipients.

Financial Reporting

States report federal TANF and state maintenance-of-effort expenditures by category.

Financial reports distinguish current-year federal funds, prior-year funds, transfers, state TANF spending, Separate State Programs, and other maintenance-of-effort expenditures.

Correct categorization is necessary to determine allowability, administrative caps, transfer limits, and maintenance of effort.

Audits and Oversight

TANF is subject to federal review, state audits, Single Audit requirements, Office of Inspector General investigations, congressional oversight, and Government Accountability Office studies.

Oversight concerns include:

  • allowable use of funds;
  • maintenance-of-effort claims;
  • contract management;
  • eligibility;
  • work data;
  • improper payments;
  • reserve balances;
  • fraud;
  • subrecipient monitoring;
  • information systems; and
  • performance measurement.

Improper Payments and Program Integrity

TANF does not operate one national payment-error-rate system comparable to SNAP or Medicaid.

States use different eligibility rules and data systems, complicating national measurement.

Improper payments can include overpayments, underpayments, ineligible payments, unsupported expenditures, duplicate assistance, or incorrectly classified benefits.

Program integrity also requires protecting eligible families from wrongful denial and sanctions.

Government Accountability Concerns

Federal oversight reports have identified weaknesses in TANF financial guardrails, performance measures, audit findings, and the connection between expenditures and measurable outcomes.

Broad flexibility permits innovation but also makes it difficult to determine whether funds consistently reach families with the greatest need.

Reform proposals in 2026 sought stronger targeting, expenditure reporting, improper-payment measurement, and performance accountability, but pending legislation should not be described as enacted law.

Federal Penalties Against States

HHS can reduce a state’s block grant for failure to meet federal requirements.

Penalties can involve:

  • maintenance of effort;
  • work-participation rates;
  • timely data reporting;
  • five-year-limit compliance;
  • child-support enforcement;
  • misuse of funds;
  • failure to replace penalized funds;
  • contingency-fund requirements;
  • EBT access restrictions; and
  • other statutory duties.

Reasonable-cause and corrective-compliance provisions can avoid or reduce some penalties.

Notices, Appeals, and Due Process

TANF eliminated the federal entitlement but did not eliminate constitutional due process or state administrative law.

Applicants and recipients generally receive written notice and an opportunity for a fair hearing concerning eligibility, benefits, sanctions, time limits, overpayments, and termination.

The exact hearing right and continuation of benefits depend on federal regulations, state law, and the nature of the benefit.

Language access and disability accommodation remain required under applicable civil-rights law.

Civil Rights and Equal Opportunity

TANF agencies and contractors are subject to federal civil-rights laws.

Administration must avoid unlawful discrimination based on race, color, national origin, disability, sex, age, religion where applicable, and other protected status.

Equal access includes language assistance, accessible systems, reasonable accommodation, fair sanction procedures, and nondiscriminatory assignment to work activities.

Race and Unequal Access

TANF developed from an AFDC system shaped by racial discrimination and racially charged political narratives.

State variation means families in similar economic circumstances receive different benefits and face different rules depending on residence.

Research has examined associations between racial composition and restrictive benefit, sanction, family-cap, and time-limit policies.

Administrative fairness requires examining not only formal rules but also application approval, sanction, closure, and service-assignment patterns.

Gender and Caregiving

Adult TANF recipients are disproportionately women caring for children.

Work rules often treat unpaid caregiving as a barrier to employment rather than economically necessary labor.

Child care, paid leave, domestic violence, disability, pregnancy, transportation, and job quality determine whether employment requirements promote security or deepen hardship.

Disability and Health Barriers

TANF families include adults and children with physical, mental-health, developmental, and substance-use conditions.

States define work exemptions and accommodations but remain subject to disability nondiscrimination law.

Screening must not use disability merely as a basis for sanction or referral without meaningful support.

Some adults transition from TANF to Supplemental Security Income after establishing disability eligibility.

Rural Families

Rural TANF recipients can face long distances to offices, employers, child care, training, health care, and public transportation.

Work-participation assignments may be impractical without vehicles, fuel, broadband, or local employers.

Tribal and regional programs may provide more locally appropriate services than centralized state systems.

Immigrant and Mixed-Status Families

Mixed-status families may include eligible citizen children and ineligible parents.

Agencies should request citizenship or immigration information only for people seeking benefits and must calculate assistance according to state and federal rules.

Fear, misinformation, language barriers, and public-charge concerns can deter eligible children from receiving aid.

Child Well-Being

TANF’s statutory purposes center on families with children, yet children can lose resources through full-family sanctions, time limits, low benefits, or administrative closure.

Cash assistance can reduce material hardship, housing instability, and unmet basic needs.

Employment services can improve family income when jobs are stable and supported by child care, health care, transportation, and adequate wages.

Caseload reduction alone is not a measure of child well-being.

Poverty and Material Hardship

TANF cash benefits reduce hardship for families receiving them but are usually too low to lift a family above poverty.

The program reaches a smaller share of poor families than AFDC did.

Noncash TANF spending may benefit broader groups, but national data do not always reveal who receives each service or the resulting poverty effect.

Major Achievements

  • Created broad state and tribal authority to design employment and family-support programs.
  • Supported substantial investment in child care, work services, refundable tax credits, education, and emergency aid.
  • Allowed tribal nations to operate culturally and economically tailored cash-assistance programs.
  • Supported subsidized employment during the Great Recession.
  • Financed nonrecurrent emergency benefits during disasters and the COVID-19 pandemic.
  • Encouraged integration of cash assistance with work, child support, child care, and supportive services.
  • Allowed jurisdictions to reserve funds and respond to local priorities.
  • Reduced reliance on one federally prescribed eligibility model.

Limitations and Failures

  • The basic block grant has lost substantial real value since 1996.
  • TANF cash assistance reaches a smaller share of poor families than AFDC did.
  • Benefit levels are inadequate in every state.
  • State flexibility creates extreme geographic inequality.
  • Only about one-fifth of combined spending supports basic assistance.
  • The work-participation rate rewards process and caseload reduction rather than employment quality or poverty reduction.
  • Time limits and full-family sanctions can harm children.
  • The program responds weakly and unevenly to recessions.
  • Broad spending authority permits fiscal substitution and weakens transparency.
  • Some families face administrative barriers, diversion pressure, or wrongful sanctions.
  • Education and training rules can favor short-term activity over durable credentials.
  • Short-term federal reauthorization creates uncertainty.

Major Controversies

Flexibility versus accountability
States can innovate, but broad discretion makes national oversight and outcome measurement difficult.
Cash assistance versus broad services
TANF supports child care and other services but directs a declining share toward families needing cash.
Work requirements
Supporters emphasize employment; critics argue that hour-counting and sanctions do not ensure good jobs or reduced poverty.
Time limits
Limits reinforce temporary assistance but can terminate aid despite continuing hardship.
State inequality
Residence determines benefit amount, eligibility, sanction severity, and access to extensions.
Family formation
Federal purposes concerning marriage and nonmarital pregnancy remain politically and ethically contested.
Block-grant erosion
Fixed nominal funding reduces real resources but also limits federal spending.
Performance measurement
Caseload decline and participation hours can obscure poverty, employment stability, and child outcomes.

Current Regulatory Developments in 2026

HHS proposed regulations in April 2026 to implement the Fiscal Responsibility Act’s reset of the caseload-reduction-credit base year and exclusion of certain Separate State Program payments below thirty-five dollars.

On July 31, 2026, HHS published a separate final rule removing obsolete, duplicative, or purely statutory restatements from TANF, Tribal TANF, Native Employment Works, and former AFDC regulations.

The regulatory-cleanup rule states that the removals do not change statutory program operations and becomes effective September 29, 2026.

As of August 2, 2026, that final rule had been published but was not yet effective.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

TANF remained active under Part A of Title IV of the Social Security Act.

Public Law 119-75 funded the program through December 31, 2026.

Jurisdictions continued operating cash-assistance, work, child-care, emergency, tax-credit, family-support, and other TANF-funded programs.

HHS was implementing Fiscal Responsibility Act reporting and work-accountability changes and evaluating outcomes-based state pilots.

Congress was considering additional program-integrity and reauthorization proposals, but pending bills were not current law.

How TANF Should Be Evaluated

Evaluation should identify the jurisdiction, year, family type, benefit or service, funding source, and policy regime.

Important questions include:

  • Was the program cash assistance, nonassistance, child care, tax credit, or another service?
  • Were federal TANF, state TANF, Separate State Program, or maintenance-of-effort funds used?
  • How many eligible families received help?
  • What happened to applicants denied or diverted?
  • Did recipients obtain stable employment and higher earnings?
  • Were child-care, transportation, health, and disability barriers addressed?
  • Did sanctions or time limits increase hardship?
  • Did funded services continue after TANF financing changed?
  • Were outcomes independently verified?
  • Did policy effects differ by race, gender, disability, family structure, or geography?

Why Temporary Assistance for Needy Families Matters

TANF represents one of the most consequential changes in modern American social policy.

It ended the federal individual entitlement to cash assistance for needy families with children and replaced open-ended matching with a fixed block grant.

The program gave states and tribes extensive authority to define need, set benefits, impose sanctions, establish time limits, create work programs, and direct funds toward services beyond cash welfare.

This flexibility generated innovation in child care, subsidized employment, tribal administration, tax credits, and emergency assistance. It also produced unequal benefits, weak recession response, declining cash access, and difficulty connecting spending with measurable outcomes.

TANF cannot be evaluated solely by caseload decline. Its central questions are whether families obtain adequate resources, stable work, safe housing, child care, and long-term economic security—and whether federal and state funds remain accountable to families with the greatest need.

Summary

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families is the federal block grant created by the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 to replace Aid to Families with Dependent Children.

TANF supplies approximately $16.5 billion annually in basic federal grants to states, territories, and participating tribes. States must maintain a specified level of their own historical welfare spending.

Jurisdictions have broad authority to define need, set cash-benefit levels, establish work rules, impose sanctions and time limits, operate diversion programs, and spend funds on child care, employment, education, tax credits, child welfare, emergency assistance, and other activities connected to four statutory purposes.

Federal law creates no individual entitlement. Federally funded adult assistance is generally limited to sixty cumulative months, and states face work-participation targets of fifty percent for all families and ninety percent for two-parent families, reduced by statutory credits.

In fiscal year 2024, approximately 569,600 adults and 1.5 million children received cash assistance in an average month. Combined federal TANF and state maintenance-of-effort expenditures and transfers totaled approximately $37.5 billion, with 21.8 percent used for basic assistance.

TANF has supported child care, employment services, tribal self-administration, subsidized jobs, and emergency aid. It has also produced declining cash-assistance access, low benefits, geographic inequality, weak recession response, and limited accountability for broad noncash spending.

As of August 2, 2026, TANF remained active and funded through December 31, 2026. HHS was implementing Fiscal Responsibility Act work-accountability and outcome-reporting reforms while preparing a regulatory cleanup scheduled to become effective September 29, 2026.
