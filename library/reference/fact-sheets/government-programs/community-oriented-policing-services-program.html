What Is the Community Oriented Policing Services Program?

The Community Oriented Policing Services Program is the federal grant and assistance system administered by the COPS Office.

It provides money, training, technical assistance, research translation, publications, and organizational support intended to improve public safety through community policing.

The program is broader than officer hiring. Modern COPS funding supports school security, tribal policing, technology, drug enforcement, crisis response, de-escalation, officer wellness, accreditation, active-shooter preparation, and agency reform.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the COPS Office’s creation, governing law, community-policing model, hiring grants, cost sharing, retention, nonsupplanting, application and award management, current and historical grant programs, training, technical assistance, civil-rights requirements, evaluations, controversies, and current 2026 status.

It distinguishes the COPS Office from the television program Cops, local community-policing units, the Office of Justice Programs, and other federal law-enforcement grants.

Geographic Scope

COPS programs serve eligible jurisdictions throughout the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, and tribal lands.

Eligibility depends on the specific funding opportunity. Some programs are limited to primary law-enforcement agencies, while others permit governments, school districts, academies, nonprofit organizations, universities, or specialized state agencies.

Historical Context

The program arose during national concern about violent crime, drug markets, gang activity, declining confidence in public institutions, and strained police-community relations.

Community policing offered an alternative to a purely reactive model by emphasizing partnerships, prevention, problem solving, decentralization, and sustained engagement with residents.

The 1994 crime law joined this strategic concept to a large federal commitment to subsidize local police hiring.

The Early-1990s Crime Debate

Violent-crime rates were high when Congress debated the 1994 legislation.

Supporters argued that more officers using community-policing methods could prevent crime, improve information sharing, and restore neighbourhood trust.

Critics questioned whether federal subsidies would produce lasting local staffing, whether quantity would substitute for reform, and whether the broader crime law would accelerate punitive policy.

Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994

President Bill Clinton signed Public Law 103-322 on September 13, 1994.

Title I included the Public Safety Partnership and Community Policing Act, authorizing billions of dollars over six years for community-policing grants.

The law created the COPS Office to distribute, monitor, and evaluate the new assistance.

Public Safety Partnership and Community Policing Act

The founding act amended the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968.

It authorized a single grant program for hiring and redeployment, equipment and technology, officer training, support systems, and community-policing implementation.

The act embedded requirements for applications, matching funds, nonsupplanting, progress review, and continuation of funded staffing.

Creation of the COPS Office

The COPS Office became the first Justice Department component dedicated solely to advancing community policing among state, local, territorial, and tribal agencies.

Unlike a general criminal-justice grant bureau, its institutional identity centred on a policing philosophy as well as award administration.

Codification in Title 34

The original COPS provisions were codified in Title 42.

In 2017, the Office of the Law Revision Counsel transferred federal crime-control provisions into Title 34, Crime Control and Law Enforcement.

Historical citations beginning with 42 U.S.C. 3796dd correspond to current sections beginning with 34 U.S.C. 10381.

Federal Administration

The COPS Office is a component of the Department of Justice headed by a director appointed by the Attorney General.

Its responsibilities include:

  • designing funding opportunities;
  • reviewing applications;
  • making and managing awards;
  • monitoring financial and programmatic compliance;
  • approving modifications and waivers;
  • collecting performance data;
  • providing training and technical assistance;
  • publishing community-policing resources;
  • supporting organizational assessments;
  • coordinating tribal funding;
  • responding to critical incidents; and
  • suspending, terminating, or recovering funds when necessary.

Mission

The enduring mission is to advance public safety through the practice of community policing.

Fiscal year 2026 materials also describe community policing as “common sense policing” that seeks to prevent crime and disorder by addressing underlying causes and building mutually beneficial police-community relationships.

The current terminology should be distinguished from the program’s original statutory and professional history.

Community Policing

Community policing is a philosophy promoting systematic partnerships and problem-solving techniques to address conditions producing crime, disorder, fear, and reduced quality of life.

It is not limited to foot patrols, public-relations events, or assignment of a single community officer.

Community Partnerships

Partnerships can include residents, neighbourhood groups, schools, businesses, faith organizations, service providers, public-health agencies, victim advocates, youth organizations, housing authorities, and other government institutions.

Effective partnerships involve shared information, consultation, problem definition, action, and evaluation rather than one-directional police messaging.

Organizational Transformation

Community policing requires alignment of agency management, supervision, recruitment, training, evaluation, assignments, information systems, accountability, and incentives.

An agency cannot sustain problem solving if promotion and performance systems reward only arrests, citations, and rapid call clearance.

Problem Solving

Problem solving examines recurring patterns rather than responding to each incident in isolation.

Agencies identify problems, analyse causes and stakeholders, develop tailored responses, and assess outcomes.

The SARA model—Scanning, Analysis, Response, and Assessment—is a widely used framework.

Prevention and Proactive Policing

Community policing seeks to reduce the conditions creating repeated calls, victimization, disorder, and fear.

Prevention can include environmental design, youth services, nuisance abatement, traffic safety, procedural reform, targeted patrol, community mediation, public-health partnerships, and focused investigations.

Community Engagement

Engagement is meaningful when residents can influence priorities, identify harms, review results, and challenge agency assumptions.

Attendance at meetings alone does not establish representative or effective engagement.

Trust and Legitimacy

Police legitimacy concerns public belief that authorities act lawfully, fairly, respectfully, and for appropriate purposes.

Community-policing grants can support trust-building, but additional officers or technology do not automatically improve legitimacy.

Procedural Justice

Procedural justice emphasizes voice, neutrality, respectful treatment, and trustworthy motives.

It became a prominent COPS training and reform theme, particularly after the President’s Task Force on 21st Century Policing.

Eligible Applicants

Eligibility differs by program.

Common eligible applicants include:

  • municipal police departments;
  • sheriff’s offices;
  • state police and highway patrols;
  • territorial law-enforcement agencies;
  • federally recognized tribes and tribal agencies;
  • units of state or local government;
  • school districts;
  • law-enforcement academies;
  • state standards and training commissions;
  • universities;
  • nonprofit organizations;
  • commercial training providers; and
  • community or faith-based organizations under selected opportunities.

Primary Law-Enforcement Authority

The COPS Hiring Program generally requires an established, operational agency with primary law-enforcement authority for the jurisdiction served.

Specialized investigative agencies or correctional institutions can be ineligible under funding opportunities limited to general primary law enforcement.

Tribal Eligibility

Federally recognized tribes and their public agencies can seek COPS assistance through the Coordinated Tribal Assistance Solicitation and other eligible programs.

Federal law permits special treatment of tribal matching funds, award duration, and Bureau of Indian Affairs resources.

Application Process

Most competitive applications use a two-step federal process.

  1. The applicant submits a Standard Form 424 through Grants.gov.
  2. The applicant completes programmatic, budget, compliance, and attachment requirements through JustGrants.

Active registration in SAM.gov and a valid Unique Entity Identifier are required.

Required Application Content

Statutory and program-specific applications can require:

  • a description of the public-safety problem;
  • community-policing strategy;
  • project goals and activities;
  • budget and cost assumptions;
  • existing staffing and vacancies;
  • financial need;
  • community partnerships;
  • organizational changes;
  • outcome measures;
  • nonsupplanting assurances;
  • retention plans;
  • civil-rights compliance;
  • environmental review information;
  • certifications and disclosures; and
  • plans to recruit women and members of racial and ethnic minority groups to sworn positions where practicable.

Competitive Review

Competitive awards can consider need, crime, staffing, fiscal condition, project quality, community-policing commitment, prior performance, statutory preference, rural or tribal status, and administration priorities.

Application scores do not always determine awards mechanically because statutory distribution, risk, and available funds also matter.

Award Document and Conditions

A successful applicant receives an award package through JustGrants.

Accepting the award binds the recipient to federal statutes, regulations, DOJ financial guidance, program terms, civil-rights requirements, approved budget, performance reporting, and special conditions.

Discretionary Nature of Awards

No eligible agency has an entitlement to a COPS grant.

Demand frequently exceeds available funding, particularly during recessions or periods of local staffing shortages.

Federal Share

The founding statute generally limited the federal share to 75 percent, subject to waiver and program-specific law.

Some modern programs have no match, while hiring and school-safety grants ordinarily require at least 25 percent nonfederal cost sharing unless a waiver applies.

Local Cost Share

Matching funds demonstrate local commitment and extend federal dollars.

The recipient must budget and document the required nonfederal share from allowable sources.

Match obligations can strain small, rural, tribal, or financially distressed jurisdictions.

Matching-Fund Waivers

The Attorney General or delegated COPS official can waive part or all of a match when fiscal circumstances make a waiver equitable.

A waiver is not automatic and ordinarily requires detailed financial documentation.

Equitable-Sharing Funds as Match

Federal law permits assets received through the federal equitable-sharing program to satisfy the nonfederal share of COPS-funded activities.

Use must comply with both COPS and asset-forfeiture rules.

Nonsupplanting Requirement

COPS funds must supplement rather than replace state, local, or Bureau of Indian Affairs resources that otherwise would have supported the funded purpose.

Nonsupplanting is intended to produce additional capacity rather than reimburse planned local spending.

Understaffed Agencies and the Recruit and Retain Act

Agencies can operate below budgeted strength because of vacancies, recruitment delay, attrition, or hiring freezes.

The Recruit and Retain Act of 2024 directed DOJ to clarify that covered understaffed agencies remain eligible and can attest that federal funds are not supplanting local money.

The change addressed uncertainty that vacant but budgeted positions could automatically disqualify an applicant.

Retention Requirement

Hiring applicants must plan to assume a progressively larger share of cost and continue the increased staffing level with state or local resources after federal support ends.

Current award conditions generally require retention of each funded position for at least twelve months after the three years of federal funding, unless a waiver or approved exception applies.

Retention Risk

Local recessions, revenue decline, bargaining agreements, pension cost, recruitment difficulty, and changing priorities can make continued staffing difficult.

A grant can create a future fiscal obligation greater than the original local match.

Progressively Larger Local Share

The statute contemplates a declining federal contribution over a multiyear hiring award.

Current CHP implementation funds up to 75 percent of entry-level salary and fringe benefits over three years within a five-year performance period, while the recipient pays the remaining eligible amount and all costs above the federal cap.

COPS Hiring Program

The COPS Hiring Program provides direct competitive funding to hire or rehire full-time career law-enforcement officers or deputies.

Funded positions must increase community-policing and crime-prevention capacity rather than merely restore local budgets without additional activity.

Fiscal Year 2026 COPS Hiring Program

Up to $157.5 million was available for fiscal year 2026.

The application period closed July 29, 2026.

Each award can support up to 75 percent of entry-level salary and fringe benefits for three years within a five-year period.

The ordinary maximum federal share is $125,000 per funded position over the three funded years, unless a matching-fund waiver is approved.

Hiring New Officers

A grant can fund newly created positions that increase the agency’s budgeted and actual sworn strength.

Agencies remain responsible for recruitment, background investigation, academy training, certification, equipment, supervision, and costs beyond allowable entry-level salary and fringe benefits.

Rehiring Laid-Off Officers

CHP can support rehiring officers who were laid off because of local fiscal distress.

Applicants must document the layoff and show that the federal award produces additional staffing relative to the lawful local baseline.

Preventing Scheduled Layoffs

Under specified conditions, hiring grants can retain officers scheduled for layoff on a future date.

The recipient must provide credible budget and personnel documentation demonstrating that the layoff would occur without the award.

Entry-Level Salary and Fringe Benefits

CHP ordinarily calculates the federal share using entry-level salary and fringe benefits for the funded position.

The local agency pays differences caused by seniority, promotion, overtime, special pay, equipment, academy expense, and other unallowable costs.

Direct Award-Administration Costs

Fiscal year 2026 CHP applicants could request up to 2 percent of the federal award for direct costs of administering the grant.

Administrative charges must be necessary, reasonable, allocable, documented, and consistent with the approved budget.

School Resource Officers

Hiring grants can fund school resource officers when the funding opportunity permits and the agency proposes a community-policing school-safety strategy.

Current requirements can include a memorandum of understanding with the school partner and specialized training.

An SRO is a sworn law-enforcement officer, not a substitute teacher, counsellor, or routine school disciplinarian.

School Resource Officer Memorandum of Understanding

A written agreement should define roles, supervision, information sharing, student privacy, arrest and citation policy, school discipline, training, complaint handling, and coordination with administrators and mental-health professionals.

Clear role boundaries reduce unnecessary criminalization of ordinary student behaviour.

School Resource Officer Training

COPS-funded SRO positions are subject to mandatory training requirements identified in the funding opportunity.

Relevant topics include youth development, disability, de-escalation, school law, trauma, cultural competence, emergency response, and alternatives to arrest.

Accelerated Hiring, Education, and Deployment

AHEAD was one of the first COPS hiring programs launched in 1994.

It provided rapid funding to larger jurisdictions while the permanent grant infrastructure was being established.

Funding Accelerated for Smaller Towns

FAST was an early streamlined program designed for smaller communities.

It recognized that small departments had fewer grant-writing and administrative resources.

Making Officer Redeployment Effective

The MORE program funded technology, equipment, and civilian personnel intended to free sworn officers from administrative tasks and redeploy officer time to community policing.

Redeployment grants required credible measurement of time saved and community-policing use.

Universal Hiring Program

The Universal Hiring Program combined early hiring mechanisms into a national competitive program beginning in the mid-1990s.

It became the historical predecessor of the current COPS Hiring Program.

The 100,000-Officer Goal

The Clinton administration promoted the goal of placing 100,000 additional community-policing officers on the street.

The COPS Office reported funding its 100,000th community-policing professional in May 1999.

“Funded” did not necessarily mean that every position was simultaneously hired, retained, and deployed at that moment.

Recovery Act Hiring Grants

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 provided $1 billion for COPS hiring during the Great Recession.

The office reported that awards supported nearly 5,000 positions.

Applications greatly exceeded available money, demonstrating the effect of local fiscal crisis on police staffing.

Technology and Equipment Program

The Technology and Equipment Program funds technology, equipment, interoperable communications, information sharing, and related community-policing capacity.

Modern awards are principally congressionally designated projects rather than an open national technology competition.

Fiscal Year 2026 Technology and Equipment Program

Up to $401,406,387 was available in fiscal year 2026.

Awards have a twenty-four-month performance period and no local matching requirement.

Projects must correspond to the entities and purposes identified through the appropriations process.

Technology Uses

Allowable projects can involve:

  • interoperable communications;
  • records and case-management systems;
  • dispatch and emergency communications;
  • crime analysis;
  • digital evidence;
  • cameras and sensor systems;
  • information sharing;
  • cybersecurity;
  • forensic capability;
  • equipment supporting officer and public safety; and
  • implementation and training.

Technology Risks

Technology grants can create privacy, surveillance, civil-rights, cybersecurity, procurement, interoperability, maintenance, and vendor-lock-in risks.

Federal approval does not replace state law, local procurement rules, impact assessment, public oversight, or constitutional limits.

School Violence Prevention Program

The School Violence Prevention Program supports evidence-based security improvements at kindergarten-through-twelfth-grade schools.

It is administered under the STOP School Violence Act framework.

Fiscal Year 2026 School Violence Prevention Program

Up to $73 million was available.

Awards last thirty-six months, with a maximum federal share of $500,000 and a minimum 25 percent local match.

Approximately $1 million was reserved for microgrants of up to $100,000 for school districts, including rural, tribal, and low-resourced schools.

School-Safety Uses

COPS-administered statutory purposes include:

  • coordination with law enforcement;
  • training officers to prevent student violence against others and self;
  • metal detectors, locks, lighting, and deterrent measures;
  • technology for expedited law-enforcement notification during emergencies; and
  • other measures determined to provide significant security improvement.

School-Safety Consultation

School-security applications should incorporate perspectives beyond law enforcement, including educators, students, parents, researchers, mental-health professionals, disability advocates, and emergency managers.

Security technology is most effective when integrated with prevention, climate, behavioural-health, and emergency-planning strategies.

Secure Our Schools Program

The historical Secure Our Schools program funded school security equipment and planning during the 2000s.

The modern School Violence Prevention Program succeeded and expanded this federal role under newer statutory authority.

COPS in Schools

The COPS in Schools program began in 1999 after high-profile school shootings.

It funded school resource officers and school-community partnerships.

Its legacy remains controversial because SRO programs can improve emergency coordination while also increasing student contact with the justice system.

Tribal Resources Grant Program

The Tribal Resources Grant Program supports tribal law-enforcement staffing, equipment, vehicles, training, technology, and community-policing capacity.

It is commonly administered as Purpose Area 1 of the Coordinated Tribal Assistance Solicitation.

Coordinated Tribal Assistance Solicitation

CTAS combines multiple Justice Department tribal funding programs into one coordinated application.

Tribes select purpose areas based on policing, courts, victim services, youth, corrections, and other justice needs.

COPS manages the tribal community-policing purpose area.

Tribal Federal Share

Tribal awards can receive favourable cost-share treatment because of federal responsibility in Indian country.

Bureau of Indian Affairs resources can be used as the nonfederal share where permitted.

COPS Anti-Methamphetamine Program

The Anti-Methamphetamine Program provides competitive funding to eligible state law-enforcement agencies addressing illicit manufacture and distribution of methamphetamine.

Activities can include investigations, task-force staffing, equipment, intelligence, training, and prosecution support within the approved scope.

COPS Anti-Heroin Task Force Program

The Anti-Heroin Task Force Program supports state agencies combating heroin, fentanyl, opioids, and related trafficking.

Up to $34.5 million was available for fiscal year 2026, with awards of as much as $4 million over thirty-six months and no local match.

Drug-Task-Force Issues

Specialized drug grants can strengthen interstate investigations and overdose-related enforcement.

Critics question whether enforcement-focused grants adequately address treatment, prevention, harm reduction, racial disparities, and civil asset forfeiture.

Community Policing Development

Community Policing Development funds demonstration projects, training, technical assistance, national resources, accreditation, and innovative practice.

Unlike hiring grants, CPD emphasizes development, testing, dissemination, and organizational change.

Fiscal Year 2026 CPD Microgrants

Approximately $6.7 million was available for multiple fiscal year 2026 microgrants.

Awards are capped at $200,000, last twenty-four months, and require no local match.

The application period was scheduled to close August 24, 2026.

Microgrant Projects

Microgrants support pilot or demonstration projects involving crime reduction, community engagement, problem solving, domestic-terrorism investigation, organizational change, and other current priorities.

Projects should generate transferable lessons rather than only purchase isolated equipment.

Safer Outcomes Program

Safer Outcomes supports de-escalation, crisis response, co-responder teams, academy curriculum integration, state training standards, and regional training centres.

The program implements federal legislation emphasizing alternatives, communication, and effective response to people in crisis.

Fiscal Year 2026 Safer Outcomes Funding

Fiscal year 2026 included approximately:

  • $8 million for law-enforcement agency crisis-response training and embedded co-responder teams;
  • $3 million for academy curriculum integration and state training commissions; and
  • $2 million for continuation and coordination of regional de-escalation training centres.

These opportunities required no local match and generally used twenty-four-month performance periods.

Co-Responder Crisis Intervention Teams

Co-responder models pair law-enforcement officers with mental-health, behavioural-health, social-service, or emergency professionals.

Grant funds can support training, equipment, supplies, personnel costs, and contracts within the approved project.

Co-response differs from civilian-only alternative crisis response, although local systems can include both.

De-Escalation Training

De-escalation training develops communication, time, distance, cover, tactical repositioning, crisis recognition, and decision-making practices intended to reduce unnecessary force.

Effective training requires policy, supervision, scenario practice, evaluation, and organizational reinforcement.

Regional De-Escalation Training Centres

Regional centres deliver multistate training and promote consistency, instructor development, and curriculum dissemination.

Fiscal year 2026 continuation funding was limited to organizations with prior Safer Outcomes or regional-centre awards.

Law Enforcement Mental Health and Wellness Program

LEMHWA supports officer and family wellness through peer support, counselling access, suicide prevention, stress reduction, family resources, education, and clinical support.

The program recognizes that officer wellness can affect judgement, retention, safety, absenteeism, and community interaction.

Fiscal Year 2026 LEMHWA Funding

Approximately $9 million was available for start-up and enhanced implementation projects, with $250,000 awards lasting twenty-four months and no local match.

An additional $250,000 was available for a Community of Practice initiative supporting grantee learning and technical assistance.

Peer-Support Programs

Peer-support programs train agency members to provide confidential initial assistance, referral, and follow-up.

They should operate with clinical oversight, privacy safeguards, clear limits, and access to licensed care.

Suicide Prevention

COPS wellness funding supports education, crisis pathways, family resources, leadership training, and reduction of barriers to seeking care.

Confidentiality, employment consequences, firearm policy, and trust in providers strongly affect use.

Law-Enforcement Accreditation

COPS programs support accreditation readiness, standards implementation, policy development, training, and technical assistance.

Accreditation can improve consistency and documentation but does not independently prove lawful or equitable outcomes.

Preparing for Active Shooter Situations

The Preparing for Active Shooter Situations program supports multidisciplinary, evidence-based training for law enforcement and other first responders.

It emphasizes integrated response, command, rescue, emergency medicine, decision making, and survivability during hostile events.

Collaborative Reform Initiative

Collaborative Reform provides voluntary technical assistance, critical-incident review, and organizational assessment.

It is distinct from a court-enforced consent decree or statutory pattern-or-practice investigation.

Collaborative Reform Initiative Technical Assistance Center

CRI-TAC provides customized technical assistance requested by law-enforcement agencies.

Assistance can address policy, training, recruitment, investigations, use of force, community engagement, crime analysis, wellness, leadership, and organizational systems.

Critical Response

Critical Response supports agencies after mass violence, civil unrest, officer-involved incidents, or other major events.

Reviews can identify operational lessons and produce public reports intended to improve national practice.

Organizational Assessments

Voluntary assessments examine agency policies, training, supervision, accountability, operations, data, and community relationships.

Participating agencies agree to provide information and engage in reform planning.

Las Vegas Collaborative Reform Review

The COPS Office’s review of the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department became an early model for voluntary assessment of officer-involved shootings and use-of-force systems.

The initiative produced findings, recommendations, and progress reports beginning in 2012.

Ferguson Collaborative Reform Work

The COPS Office provided technical assistance to the Ferguson Police Department after the 2014 protests.

The work occurred alongside a separate Justice Department civil-rights investigation and later consent decree.

Robb Elementary Critical-Incident Review

The COPS Office led the Justice Department’s critical-incident review of the law-enforcement response to the 2022 shooting at Robb Elementary School in Uvalde, Texas.

The 2024 report examined leadership, command, tactics, communications, training, trauma, and post-incident response.

Regional Community Policing Institutes

COPS funding established a national network of Regional Community Policing Institutes during the 1990s.

The institutes delivered training to executives, supervisors, officers, residents, and local partners.

Publications and Resource Center

The COPS Office publishes guidebooks, reports, case studies, problem-oriented policing guides, training materials, and critical-incident reviews.

Publications are intended for law enforcement and community stakeholders and are generally available through the COPS Resource Center.

Problem-Oriented Policing Guides

The Problem-Specific Guides series summarizes research and practical responses to recurring crime and disorder problems.

Guides are tools for analysis rather than mandatory federal policy.

Training and Technical Assistance

COPS training can be delivered through classrooms, online systems, academies, technical-assistance providers, peer exchanges, publications, and direct consulting.

Federal assistance seeks to convert grant-funded demonstrations and research into operational practice.

Award Management

Recipients manage awards through federal systems and assigned grant managers.

Management includes budgets, drawdowns, performance reports, financial reports, modifications, monitoring, records, procurement, property, subawards, closeout, and audit.

JustGrants

JustGrants is the Justice Department’s grants-management platform.

Applicants and recipients use it for application materials, award acceptance, performance management, grant adjustment modifications, monitoring, and closeout.

Payment Management

Federal award payments are drawn through the Department of the Treasury’s Automated Standard Application for Payments or another designated system.

Drawdowns must correspond to immediate allowable cash needs and cannot be used as an unrestricted local reserve.

Financial Management

Recipients must maintain internal controls, separate accounting, source documentation, time and effort records, procurement files, property records, and audit trails.

Costs must be necessary, reasonable, allocable, and permitted under federal cost principles and award conditions.

Procurement

Grant-funded procurement must follow federal standards and applicable state or local law.

Competition, conflicts of interest, sole-source justification, contract oversight, cybersecurity, accessibility, and domestic-preference requirements can apply.

Environmental Review

Construction, renovation, equipment installation, communications towers, firing ranges, and other physical projects can require environmental review.

Recipients should not begin restricted activity before receiving required approval.

Performance Reporting

Recipients report activities, staffing, partnerships, problem-solving work, organizational change, outputs, and outcomes.

Hiring programs can require information on officer deployment, vacancies, retention, and the public-safety problem identified in the application.

Outcome Measures

Statutory measures can include:

  • crime and victimization;
  • quality of life;
  • community perceptions;
  • police perceptions;
  • response and problem-resolution measures;
  • organizational change;
  • partnership activity;
  • staffing and retention;
  • training completion; and
  • project-specific safety or wellness outcomes.

Monitoring

The COPS Office uses desk reviews, site visits, risk assessments, financial analysis, data validation, special-condition review, and audit findings.

Monitoring seeks to confirm that federal funds produce the approved additional capacity and comply with grant law.

Noncompliance

Noncompliance can include supplanting, unsupported costs, failure to hire or retain positions, inaccurate reports, prohibited spending, civil-rights violations, missed match, procurement failures, or refusal to cooperate with monitoring.

Corrective action can include conditions, repayment, withholding, suspension, termination, or future award restrictions.

Suspension or Revocation

Under 34 U.S.C. 10386, the Attorney General may suspend or revoke all or part of an award when a recipient is not in substantial compliance with its approved application and grant requirements.

Award Closeout

Closeout occurs after the performance period when the recipient submits final financial, programmatic, property, and invention reports and resolves remaining issues.

Closeout does not eliminate record-retention, audit, civil-rights, or recovery obligations.

Audit and Inspector-General Oversight

Recipients can be reviewed through Single Audits, DOJ Office of the Inspector General audits, congressional investigations, and program monitoring.

Historical audits identified weaknesses involving supplanting, unsupported expenditures, officer retention, performance measurement, and grant management.

Civil-Rights Compliance

COPS recipients must comply with applicable federal civil-rights statutes, DOJ regulations, and award conditions.

Requirements can prohibit discrimination based on race, colour, national origin, sex, religion, disability, age, and other protected status.

Law-enforcement activity funded by a grant remains subject to the Constitution and federal civil-rights enforcement.

Title VI

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

Language access is part of meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency.

Disability Nondiscrimination

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act and the Americans with Disabilities Act require accessible programs, effective communication, and nondiscriminatory policing and employment practices.

Equal Employment Opportunity

Recipients can be required to maintain equal-employment opportunity plans or certifications.

The founding statute also directs applicants, to the extent practicable, to seek, recruit, and hire women and members of racial and ethnic minority groups for sworn positions.

Fiscal Year 2026 Immigration-Information Condition

Fiscal year 2026 COPS funding pages state that state and local governmental entities must comply with 8 U.S.C. 1373 concerning restrictions on exchanging immigration-status information.

Federal grant conditions involving immigration have generated extensive litigation and can change with statutes, judicial decisions, and administration policy.

Constitutional Policing

Community policing must operate within constitutional rules governing searches, seizures, force, speech, assembly, equal protection, and due process.

Community trust cannot substitute for legal accountability, and legal compliance alone does not guarantee community legitimacy.

Use of Force

COPS grants can support de-escalation, policy revision, training, data, supervision, accreditation, and review.

The COPS Office does not replace courts, state regulators, prosecutors, civilian oversight, or DOJ pattern-or-practice enforcement.

Racial Profiling

Community policing is undermined when enforcement decisions are based on race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, or other improper classifications.

Grant-funded partnerships and data systems should be evaluated for discriminatory impact as well as operational output.

Data and Transparency

Public transparency can include award records, staffing, project goals, technology purchases, policies, performance measures, and final results.

Federal award databases show obligations but may not reveal actual deployment, local match, continuing cost, or community impact.

Evaluation History

Congress and DOJ funded evaluations of whether COPS grants increased police staffing, advanced community policing, reduced crime, and produced lasting organizational change.

The evidence is mixed because the program operated during a national crime decline and funded diverse agencies with different baseline trends.

Effect on Police Staffing

Research generally finds that COPS hiring grants increased the number of officers relative to what localities would otherwise have employed.

The increase was smaller than the number of federally funded positions because of vacancies, attrition, delayed hiring, local budget changes, and incomplete retention.

Effect on Crime

Several national studies found modest reductions in crime associated with COPS-funded officer increases and community-policing activity.

Other research questions causal methods, variation among agencies, and whether staffing or strategy produced the observed effect.

The program should not be credited with the entire 1990s crime decline.

Effect on Police Organizations

Grants encouraged agencies to adopt community-policing language, partnerships, problem solving, civilian support, technology, and training.

Deep organizational transformation was uneven and could weaken after grant funding or executive leadership changed.

Effect on Communities

Community effects depend on how officers are deployed and how residents participate.

More officers can improve responsiveness and prevention, but can also increase stops, arrests, surveillance, and enforcement exposure.

Evaluation should measure fairness, trust, victimization, complaints, and quality of life—not staffing alone.

Small and Rural Agencies

Federal grants can provide staffing, technology, and training unavailable in small tax bases.

Small agencies can also struggle with matching funds, recruitment, grant administration, technology maintenance, and post-grant retention.

Tribal Communities

Tribal law enforcement faces extensive geography, limited infrastructure, jurisdictional complexity, recruitment difficulty, and federal trust responsibilities.

COPS funding supports locally determined tribal policing capacity but does not resolve chronic underfunding or overlapping federal, state, and tribal jurisdiction.

Urban Agencies

Large agencies can use COPS resources for significant hiring, technology, training, and reform projects.

Urban grants can affect many residents and therefore require strong community engagement, privacy safeguards, and outcome evaluation.

Recruitment and Retention

Modern agencies report difficulty recruiting and retaining qualified officers.

COPS work addresses hiring standards, applicant pipelines, background processes, wellness, organizational culture, compensation, leadership, and community confidence.

Background Investigations

Hiring-grant recipients remain responsible for lawful and thorough background investigations.

Pressure to fill grant-funded vacancies should not weaken suitability, integrity, certification, or nondiscrimination standards.

Workforce Diversity

A workforce reflecting the community can improve language capacity, perspectives, trust, and recruitment.

Diversity does not automatically change organizational policy, accountability, or outcomes without broader institutional reform.

Women in Policing

Women remain underrepresented in sworn law enforcement.

Recruitment, physical testing, harassment, assignments, family policy, promotion, and workplace culture affect representation and retention.

COPS grants and technical assistance can support equitable recruitment and organizational change.

Police Unions and Grant Implementation

Collective-bargaining agreements can affect hiring, assignments, promotion, discipline, schedules, layoffs, and retention.

Federal awards do not override lawful labour agreements but recipients remain responsible for meeting grant conditions.

Federal Funding and Local Control

Policing is primarily a state and local function, while COPS funding allows federal priorities to shape staffing and strategy.

Supporters view this as productive partnership; critics view it as federal influence without full federal responsibility for local outcomes.

Congressionally Designated Projects

A large share of recent COPS appropriations supports congressionally designated technology and equipment projects.

These awards differ from nationally competed grants and reflect project lists enacted through appropriations.

Critics question whether designated projects reduce strategic competition and evaluation.

Funding History

COPS appropriations rose rapidly during the 1990s, exceeded $1 billion in several years, declined sharply during parts of the 2000s and early 2010s, and later increased through school-safety, technology, wellness, and directed-project funding.

The office reports more than $14 billion invested since 1994.

Fiscal Year 1994

The first-year appropriation was approximately $148.4 million.

The office awarded approximately $200 million to 392 agencies for about 2,700 community-policing professionals using available startup and related funds.

Peak 1990s Funding

Annual appropriations exceeded $1.3 billion in fiscal years 1995–1999, reaching approximately $1.633 billion in fiscal year 1998.

This period financed the initial officer goal and rapid expansion of the office.

Funding Decline in the Early 2010s

Annual appropriations fell below $250 million during several years in the early 2010s.

Hiring grants became smaller and more targeted while technical assistance and reform initiatives gained prominence.

Fiscal Year 2026 Enacted Funding

DOJ’s fiscal year 2027 budget documents identify the fiscal year 2026 enacted COPS level as $800 million with eighty-one positions and six attorneys.

The total includes programmatic grants, congressionally designated projects, transfers, and administration.

Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Proposal

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 request proposed $303.2 million for COPS, a reduction of $496.8 million from the fiscal year 2026 enacted level.

It also proposed consolidating COPS, the Office of Justice Programs, and the Office on Violence Against Women into a Bureau of Justice Grants.

As of August 2, 2026, these were budget and reorganization proposals rather than enacted changes to the COPS Office.

Current Fiscal Year 2026 Funding Portfolio

COPS and DOJ released notices representing nearly $700 million in June 2026 and additional opportunities in July.

The portfolio included officer hiring, school safety, technology and equipment, officer wellness, de-escalation, tribal resources, drug task forces, community-policing development, accreditation, active-shooter training, and technical assistance.

Current Application Status on August 2, 2026

The fiscal year 2026 COPS Hiring Program closed on July 29, 2026.

The LEMHWA JustGrants deadline was scheduled for August 5, and the CPD Microgrants deadlines were scheduled for August 17 and August 24.

Funding status is time sensitive and should be verified through the COPS grants page.

Major Achievements

  • Created a permanent federal institution devoted to community policing.
  • Supported deployment of more than 100,000 community-policing professionals during the original initiative.
  • Invested more than $14 billion in policing grants and assistance.
  • Helped agencies add officers during fiscal crises.
  • Expanded tribal law-enforcement resources.
  • Built national training and technical-assistance networks.
  • Developed widely used problem-oriented policing guides and publications.
  • Supported school safety, officer wellness, de-escalation, and crisis response.
  • Conducted major critical-incident and organizational reviews.
  • Made community partnerships and problem solving central concepts in national police policy.

Limitations and Failures

  • Grant demand greatly exceeds available funding.
  • Federal hiring subsidies create future local staffing obligations.
  • Some agencies have struggled to retain funded positions.
  • Nonsupplanting is difficult to measure when local budgets and vacancies change.
  • Community-policing language can be adopted without deep organizational change.
  • Additional officers can increase enforcement exposure as well as public service.
  • Performance measures often emphasize outputs rather than legitimacy or equity.
  • Technology grants can create privacy and maintenance burdens.
  • School-policing grants can contribute to criminalization of student behaviour.
  • Directed projects can reduce competitive review.
  • Voluntary reform lacks the enforceability of a consent decree.
  • Federal grant compliance does not guarantee constitutional or effective policing.

How the COPS Program Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the specific funding opportunity, fiscal year, recipient, award amount, federal share, local match, project period, funded positions, retention period, community-policing strategy, and actual outcome.

Important distinctions include:

  • the COPS Office versus the COPS Hiring Program;
  • funded positions versus filled positions;
  • filled positions versus retained positions;
  • federal obligations versus local expenditures;
  • competitive awards versus congressionally designated projects;
  • community engagement versus public relations;
  • community policing versus broken-windows enforcement;
  • technical assistance versus civil-rights enforcement;
  • school security grants versus SRO hiring;
  • outputs versus outcomes;
  • correlation with crime decline versus causal effect; and
  • budget proposals versus enacted appropriations.

Why the Community Oriented Policing Services Program Matters

The COPS program changed the relationship between the federal government and local policing.

Before 1994, federal law-enforcement assistance funded equipment, block grants, drug enforcement, and research, but no Justice Department component existed solely to advance one comprehensive policing philosophy.

COPS grants influenced staffing, school policing, technology, tribal law enforcement, de-escalation, wellness, organizational assessment, and national professional standards.

Its central achievement was making partnerships, prevention, problem solving, and organizational change part of the national policing vocabulary.

Its central limitation is that federal funding can encourage but cannot guarantee authentic community participation, constitutional policing, durable local staffing, or improved outcomes.

Summary

The Community Oriented Policing Services Program is the federal community-policing grant and assistance system administered by the Department of Justice COPS Office.

Congress created the office through the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994. The original initiative sought to support 100,000 additional community-policing officers while changing police organizations toward partnerships, prevention, and problem solving.

Current statutory authority appears principally in 34 U.S.C. 10381–10389. The law addresses eligible uses, applications, matching, retention, nonsupplanting, review, and enforcement.

The modern portfolio extends beyond hiring. It includes school safety, technology and equipment, tribal resources, anti-heroin and anti-methamphetamine task forces, officer wellness, de-escalation, crisis response, accreditation, active-shooter preparation, community-policing microgrants, publications, and collaborative reform.

Fiscal year 2026 enacted COPS funding totalled $800 million. Up to $157.5 million supported hiring, $401.4 million supported Technology and Equipment Program projects, and $73 million supported school violence prevention.

Hiring grants ordinarily support up to 75 percent of entry-level salary and fringe benefits for three years, require at least a 25 percent match unless waived, cap the ordinary federal share at $125,000 per position, prohibit supplanting, and require post-grant retention.

The COPS Office reports investing more than $14 billion since 1994 and funding its 100,000th community-policing professional in 1999.

The program’s record is mixed. It expanded staffing, training, technical assistance, tribal policing, and national community-policing practice, but additional funding does not by itself guarantee sustainable staffing, equitable enforcement, authentic community partnership, or constitutional policing.

As of August 2, 2026, the COPS Office remained active. A proposed fiscal year 2027 consolidation and funding reduction had not been enacted.
