Medicaid

The joint federal-state health-coverage program created in 1965 for eligible people with limited income or resources, including children, parents, pregnant people, older adults, people with disabilities, and—where states adopted the Affordable Care Act expansion—many other low-income adults.

What Is Medicaid?

Medicaid is a means-tested health-coverage program jointly financed by the federal government and participating states. It was created in 1965 as Title XIX of the Social Security Act and originally linked medical assistance to categories of people receiving or qualifying for public cash assistance.

Medicaid is not a single nationally uniform insurance policy. Federal law establishes mandatory populations, minimum benefits, financing rules, beneficiary protections, administrative standards, and oversight requirements. States then make numerous choices about optional eligibility groups, income and resource standards, covered services, payment rates, managed-care systems, waiver programs, delivery networks, and application procedures.

A person may therefore qualify in one state and not another, or receive a different service package after moving. Variation is a defining feature of the program rather than an administrative exception.

Medicaid also functions as more than ordinary health insurance. It finances nursing-facility services, home- and community-based supports, school-based health services, transportation, case management, services for people with intellectual or developmental disabilities, assistance with Medicare premiums and cost sharing, and other functions that commercial insurance commonly excludes or limits.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines Medicaid as a federal-state institution: its origins, statutory framework, eligibility categories, benefits, financing, managed care, provider payment, long-term services and supports, waiver authorities, civil-rights significance, racial and regional effects, legislative development, administrative limitations, and current policy questions through August 2, 2026.

It is not an individual eligibility determination, application guide, legal opinion, or guarantee that a particular service will be covered. Medicaid rules change through federal legislation, regulations, court decisions, state statutes, budgets, state plan amendments, waiver approvals, and administrative guidance.

Applicants and beneficiaries must consult their state Medicaid agency, managed-care plan, provider, legal-assistance program, or other current official source for personal eligibility and coverage decisions.

Geographic Scope

Medicaid operates in every state, the District of Columbia, and participating United States territories, but the federal-state financing structure differs between states and territories. State programs generally receive federal matching funds under the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage formula. Territorial programs historically have operated under statutory funding caps and separate matching arrangements, although Congress has enacted temporary and permanent modifications.

States designate Medicaid agencies and may delegate eligibility, enrolment, behavioural-health, developmental-disability, long-term-care, child-welfare, and provider functions among different departments. Counties may perform administrative duties in some states.

Geographic variation affects eligibility thresholds, managed-care availability, provider networks, reimbursement, hospital access, home-care capacity, nursing-facility supply, transportation, language access, and the practical value of coverage.

Medicaid ordinarily covers eligible residents through the program of the state in which they reside. Emergency and limited out-of-state services may be covered under federal and state rules, but beneficiaries do not possess the same nationwide provider access associated with Original Medicare.

Historical Context

Before Medicaid, federal and state governments financed medical care through fragmented public-assistance programs, public hospitals, charity institutions, veterans’ systems, maternal and child-health grants, disability programs, and state or local indigent-care arrangements.

The Social Security Act of 1935 created federal-state cash-assistance programs for certain older people, dependent children, and blind people. Congress later authorized federal matching payments for medical care furnished to public-assistance recipients.

The Kerr-Mills Act of 1960 established Medical Assistance for the Aged, allowing states to receive federal funding for medical care furnished to certain older people who were not receiving cash assistance but could not afford necessary care.

These arrangements produced uneven coverage. State participation, eligibility standards, provider availability, racial discrimination, local fiscal capacity, and political priorities determined whether poor residents could obtain care.

National health-insurance proposals repeatedly failed during the Truman and Eisenhower administrations. By the early 1960s, reformers increasingly pursued a combined strategy: social insurance for older Americans through Medicare and federal-state medical assistance for low-income populations through Medicaid.

Creation of Medicaid

Congress created Medicaid through the Social Security Amendments of 1965. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the legislation on July 30, 1965, at the Harry S. Truman Presidential Library in Independence, Missouri.

The same law established Medicare under Title XVIII and Medicaid under Title XIX. The programs were related but institutionally different. Medicare was designed primarily as federal social insurance for older people, while Medicaid offered federal matching funds to states that operated medical-assistance programs meeting federal requirements.

Medicaid consolidated and expanded earlier medical-vendor-payment provisions. States were not compelled to participate, but every state eventually did. Arizona became the last state to establish a Medicaid program, implementing the Arizona Health Care Cost Containment System in 1982.

The initial program remained closely connected to public-assistance categories. Eligibility did not arise merely because a person was poor. Applicants generally had to fit a recognized category associated with dependent children, old age, blindness, disability, or another statutory class.

Federal-State Structure

Medicaid is a cooperative federalism program. The federal government authorizes funding, establishes legal conditions, approves state plans and waivers, reviews expenditures, and enforces compliance. States administer eligibility, contract with plans, enrol providers, set many payment rates, process claims, investigate program integrity, and determine numerous optional policies.

A state Medicaid plan is an agreement describing how the state will administer its program. It identifies covered populations, services, reimbursement methods, administrative structures, and assurances of compliance with federal law.

When a state changes a matter governed by its approved plan, it generally submits a state plan amendment to CMS. Certain experimental or specialized programs require waiver or demonstration authority.

Federal matching funds give Congress and HHS substantial leverage, while state administration produces policy diversity. The resulting balance creates continuing disputes over national standards, state flexibility, fiscal responsibility, civil rights, beneficiary protections, and the proper scope of federal supervision.

Eligibility

Medicaid eligibility depends on financial criteria and a legally recognized eligibility group. Federal law requires coverage of certain populations and permits states to cover additional groups.

Children
States must cover specified groups of low-income children and may establish higher income limits through Medicaid or CHIP.
Pregnant people
Federal law requires pregnancy-related coverage for defined income groups, and states may use higher thresholds or extended postpartum options.
Parents and caretaker relatives
States cover qualifying adults responsible for dependent children under income standards that vary substantially.
Older adults
People age 65 or older may qualify through pathways connected to Supplemental Security Income, poverty-related groups, medically needy programs, institutional care, or home- and community-based services.
People with disabilities
Eligibility may arise through Supplemental Security Income, state disability determinations, working-disabled programs, medically needy pathways, institutional eligibility, or waiver services.
Affordable Care Act adult group
Expansion states cover most adults ages 19 through 64 with income at or below the effective 138-percent-of-poverty threshold, subject to citizenship, immigration, residency, and other requirements.
Medically needy populations
Participating states may permit people with income above an ordinary threshold to qualify after incurring medical expenses sufficient to reduce countable income through a spend-down process.
Limited-benefit groups
Some people qualify only for family-planning services, tuberculosis services, emergency Medicaid, Medicare cost-sharing assistance, or another restricted benefit package.

MAGI and Non-MAGI Eligibility

The Affordable Care Act established Modified Adjusted Gross Income methodologies for most children, pregnant people, parents, and nonelderly adults. MAGI eligibility generally relies on federal tax concepts and household relationships and ordinarily does not impose an asset test.

Non-MAGI methodologies continue for populations whose eligibility is based on age, blindness, disability, long-term-care need, medically needy status, or other statutory pathways. These programs may apply resource limits, income disregards, spousal-impoverishment protections, disability standards, transfer-of-assets rules, and estate-recovery requirements.

Financial eligibility is only one component. Applicants may also need to satisfy state residency, citizenship or qualified immigration status, Social Security number, age, pregnancy, disability, household, institutional, or functional-need requirements.

States must consider whether an applicant qualifies under another Medicaid category before issuing a final denial when federal rules require such review.

Affordable Care Act Medicaid Expansion

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010 sought to establish Medicaid eligibility for most adults under age 65 with income at or below 133 percent of the federal poverty level. A five-percentage-point income disregard makes the practical threshold 138 percent for the adult group.

The law initially conditioned existing federal Medicaid funding on state participation. In National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), the Supreme Court held that Congress could not withdraw a state’s existing Medicaid funding for refusing the expansion. The ruling converted expansion into a practical state option.

Expansion took effect in participating states beginning January 1, 2014. The federal government paid 100 percent of qualifying newly eligible costs from 2014 through 2016, with the enhanced matching rate declining to 90 percent in 2020 and remaining at that level under current law.

As of the CMS March 2026 reporting map, 41 state-level jurisdictions had expansion status and 10 did not. Because eligibility for traditional parent categories can remain far below the federal poverty level in non-expansion states, some adults have income too high for Medicaid but too low for Marketplace premium tax credits under the original ACA design. This condition is commonly called the coverage gap.

Application, Enrollment, and Renewal

People may apply through a state Medicaid agency, an integrated benefits portal, the federal or state Health Insurance Marketplace, telephone, mail, or in person, depending on state systems.

States verify income and other eligibility information using applications, electronic data sources, federal hubs, wage records, Social Security information, immigration records, tax data, and documents supplied by applicants.

At renewal, states must first attempt an ex parte determination using reliable information already available. When eligibility cannot be renewed automatically, the state must send a renewal form and provide a reasonable opportunity to submit information.

Effective January 1, 2024, federal law requires twelve months of continuous Medicaid and CHIP eligibility for children under age 19, subject to limited exceptions. This policy reduces coverage losses caused by temporary income fluctuations or administrative interruptions.

Federal legislation enacted in 2025 requires six-month renewals beginning January 1, 2027, for most people in the ACA adult expansion group and certain comparable demonstration populations, with statutory exceptions. States were developing systems and procedures for this change during 2026.

Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment

Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment, commonly abbreviated EPSDT, is Medicaid’s comprehensive child-health benefit for most enrolled people under age 21.

EPSDT requires periodic screening, vision, dental, hearing, and diagnostic services. When screening or evaluation identifies an illness or condition, the state must cover medically necessary treatment that fits within a Medicaid-coverable service category under section 1905(a) of the Social Security Act, even when the state does not ordinarily provide that service to adults.

The statutory standard is to correct or ameliorate physical and mental conditions. EPSDT therefore creates a broader entitlement for children than the ordinary adult benefit package.

Implementation remains uneven. Families may encounter limited provider participation, waiting lists, insufficient notices, transportation barriers, narrow medical-necessity interpretations, or delays in obtaining therapy, behavioural-health treatment, dental care, equipment, and home nursing.

Pregnancy and Maternal Health

Medicaid finances a substantial share of births in the United States and is central to prenatal, labour and delivery, postpartum, family-planning, and infant-care systems.

Pregnancy-related eligibility generally uses income thresholds higher than those for parents. A person found eligible during pregnancy receives protection through the pregnancy and a statutory postpartum period.

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 created a state option to extend full Medicaid and CHIP postpartum coverage from 60 days to 12 months after the pregnancy ends. Congress later made the option permanent. State adoption and implementation remain important to maternal-health access.

States have expanded coverage for doulas, midwives, birth centres, substance-use treatment, home visiting, remote monitoring, and maternal-health care coordination through state plans and waivers. Coverage alone does not resolve hospital closures, obstetric workforce shortages, racial discrimination, transportation barriers, or maternal-mortality disparities.

Long-Term Services and Supports

Medicaid is the principal public payer for long-term services and supports. These services assist people who need help with activities such as bathing, dressing, eating, mobility, medication, communication, supervision, employment, or community participation.

Mandatory nursing-facility coverage gave the program an early institutional orientation. Over time, Congress and states created pathways for home- and community-based services through sections 1915(c), 1915(i), 1915(j), 1915(k), state-plan personal care, managed long-term services and supports, and section 1115 demonstrations.

Home- and community-based programs can fund personal attendants, habilitation, respite, supported employment, adult day services, home modifications, transportation, and other supports. Eligibility may depend on functional need, institutional level-of-care standards, financial criteria, waiver capacity, and available providers.

Many programs maintain waiting lists or service limits. The balance between institutions and community services remains a major disability-rights, fiscal, workforce, and quality-of-life issue.

Medicaid Managed Care

States increasingly deliver Medicaid through managed-care organizations. Under risk-based managed care, the state pays a plan a fixed per-member monthly amount to arrange covered services for enrolled beneficiaries.

Managed care can coordinate services, create predictable state expenditures, establish provider networks, and support quality measurement. It can also create risks involving inadequate networks, delayed authorization, service denial, weak encounter data, insufficient payment, plan withdrawal, administrative complexity, and reduced transparency.

Federal law requires actuarially sound capitation rates, enrollee protections, access standards, quality programs, grievance and appeal systems, and oversight of plan operations.

Some states exclude selected populations or services from managed care. Others use specialized plans for children in foster care, people with serious mental illness, people with disabilities, or beneficiaries receiving long-term services and supports.

Fee-for-Service Medicaid

In fee-for-service Medicaid, the state pays enrolled providers for covered services furnished to eligible beneficiaries. The state establishes payment methods and rates within federal requirements.

Fee-for-service systems may preserve broader provider choice than closed networks, but access still depends on provider participation, reimbursement levels, billing capacity, transportation, appointment availability, and local supply.

States commonly use fee schedules, prospective payment systems, supplemental payments, value-based arrangements, cost-based reimbursement, and special rates for safety-net providers.

Many state programs operate mixed systems in which some populations or benefits remain fee-for-service while others are assigned to managed care.

Financing

Medicaid is an open-ended matching program. The federal government reimburses participating states for a defined share of qualifying expenditures rather than providing one fixed annual national appropriation.

Federal Medical Assistance Percentage

The regular Federal Medical Assistance Percentage is calculated through a statutory formula related to state per-capita income and is subject to a federal floor. States with lower per-capita income generally receive higher federal matching percentages.

Enhanced Matching Rates

Federal law provides enhanced matching for the ACA expansion population, CHIP, certain family-planning services, specified health-information systems, health homes, Money Follows the Person activities, and other authorized programs.

State Share

States finance their share through general revenues and other lawful mechanisms, including provider taxes, intergovernmental transfers, and certified public expenditures. Federal law regulates the permissibility, uniformity, hold-harmless effects, and documentation of these arrangements.

Federal Review

States report expenditures to CMS and claim federal financial participation. CMS may defer or disallow claims that do not comply with federal requirements. Audits, actuarial reviews, expenditure systems, and data submissions support oversight.

Provider Payment and Safety-Net Institutions

States possess broad authority to set Medicaid payment rates, but payments must comply with federal statutes, regulations, approved state plans, managed-care requirements, and access standards.

Medicaid reimbursement is frequently lower than Medicare or commercial-insurance payment. Low rates can reduce provider participation, while supplemental payments and directed payments can support hospitals, nursing facilities, teaching institutions, public providers, and other safety-net systems.

Disproportionate Share Hospital payments assist hospitals serving large numbers of Medicaid and uninsured patients. Federal law establishes state allotments, hospital qualifications, reporting duties, and limits.

Federally qualified health centres and rural health clinics receive payment under federally defined prospective systems. These institutions play an important role in primary care, behavioural health, dental services, and care for medically underserved communities.

Payment design can produce unintended incentives. States and plans must guard against underpayment, excessive supplemental arrangements, patient selection, service reduction, unsupported coding, institutional dependence, and weak accountability for quality.

Waivers and Demonstrations

Federal waiver authorities permit states to depart from selected Medicaid requirements or obtain federal matching for otherwise uncovered services and populations.

Section 1115 demonstrations
Permit experimental, pilot, or demonstration projects that the Secretary of Health and Human Services determines are likely to promote Medicaid objectives. Demonstrations are generally required to be budget neutral to the federal government.
Section 1915(b) waivers
Allow states to implement managed-care systems, limit provider choice, or use savings for additional services under specified conditions.
Section 1915(c) waivers
Permit home- and community-based services for people who would otherwise require institutional care.
Combined authorities
States may coordinate multiple waiver and state-plan authorities to operate managed long-term-services programs or specialized benefit systems.

Waivers can promote innovation, but they can also increase variation, complexity, administrative burden, and political conflict. Approval terms, evaluation methods, budget-neutrality calculations, public notice, beneficiary protections, and federal enforcement are central oversight concerns.

Medicaid and Medicare

Medicaid and Medicare were created by the same 1965 legislation but serve different institutional functions.

Medicaid
A joint federal-state, means-tested program with eligibility and benefits that vary by state and category.
Medicare
A federal social-insurance program based principally on age, disability, end-stage renal disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, insured status, and enrolment.

A person can be enrolled in both programs. These beneficiaries are called dually eligible. Medicare generally pays first for Medicare-covered acute medical services, while Medicaid may pay Medicare premiums and cost sharing and cover services Medicare excludes, particularly long-term services and supports.

State Medicare Savings Programs include the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary, Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary, Qualifying Individual, and Qualified Disabled and Working Individuals categories. Eligibility thresholds and administrative procedures differ.

Medicaid and the Children’s Health Insurance Program

Congress created the Children’s Health Insurance Program in 1997 under Title XXI of the Social Security Act to cover uninsured children in families with income above Medicaid thresholds but insufficient access to affordable private insurance.

States may operate CHIP as a Medicaid expansion, a separate program, or a combination. Medicaid-expansion CHIP beneficiaries receive Medicaid rules and benefits, including EPSDT, while separate CHIP programs operate under Title XXI requirements.

CHIP uses an enhanced federal matching rate and a capped federal allotment structure. Cost sharing may be higher than in Medicaid but remains limited by federal law.

Medicaid and CHIP share application systems, eligibility data, outreach, renewal rules, and quality measures. Federal reporting commonly presents the programs together, but they remain legally distinct.

Premiums and Cost Sharing

States may impose premiums, enrolment fees, copayments, coinsurance, or deductibles on certain beneficiaries and services within federal limits.

Federal law protects specified populations and services from cost sharing, including many services for children, pregnancy-related services, family planning, emergency services, and preventive services under applicable conditions.

Aggregate out-of-pocket obligations are limited as a share of household income. Even small charges, however, can deter care among people with very limited income.

Providers generally may not balance bill beneficiaries for the difference between their ordinary charge and Medicaid payment. Rules governing refusal of service for nonpayment depend on the beneficiary, service, plan, and cost-sharing authority.

Beneficiary Rights and Due Process

Medicaid applicants and beneficiaries possess rights to written notice, reasonable application processing, nondiscrimination, privacy, accessible communication, fair hearings, continuation of benefits in certain disputes, managed-care grievances, appeals, and external review.

Before terminating, suspending, or reducing eligibility or services, an agency generally must provide advance notice explaining the factual and legal basis, effective date, hearing rights, and method of appeal.

Managed-care enrollees may challenge adverse benefit determinations through plan appeals and state fair-hearing systems. Expedited review applies when delay could seriously jeopardize life, health, or function.

Rights are meaningful only when beneficiaries receive timely notices, language assistance, disability accommodations, legal help, functional telephone systems, accurate records, and continued access during review.

Civil-Rights and Disability-Rights Context

Medicaid agencies, plans, providers, and contractors may be subject to Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, the Americans with Disabilities Act, section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, the Age Discrimination Act, and other federal and state protections.

These laws address discrimination, language access, effective communication, physical accessibility, program modification, and equal participation. Applicability and remedies depend on the entity, funding source, conduct, and statutory framework.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Olmstead v. L.C. (1999) held that unjustified institutional isolation of people with disabilities can violate Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Medicaid home- and community-based services became a central vehicle for implementing community integration.

Civil-rights enforcement remains necessary because common coverage does not prevent discriminatory treatment, inaccessible facilities, inadequate interpretation, biased clinical decisions, segregated service systems, or unequal provider availability.

Significance for African American Communities

Medicaid expanded access to medical care for Black families affected by poverty, occupational exclusion, segregated employment, discriminatory insurance markets, unequal hospital access, residential segregation, and underinvestment in community health institutions.

The program became particularly important for children, pregnant people, low-wage workers, people with disabilities, nursing-home residents, and Medicare beneficiaries with limited income. It financed care through public hospitals, community health centres, children’s hospitals, pharmacies, home-care agencies, behavioural-health providers, and other institutions serving Black communities.

Medicaid’s federal-state design also preserved geographic inequality. Southern states historically maintained lower public-assistance standards and weaker medical systems, and several later declined the ACA expansion. Because Black residents are disproportionately concentrated in parts of the South, state decisions can produce racially unequal access even when statutory classifications are facially neutral.

Provider shortages, hospital closures, low reimbursement, maternal-health disparities, administrative disenrolment, transportation barriers, nursing-home quality, and limited home-care capacity continue to affect Black beneficiaries.

Medicaid therefore operates as both an instrument of inclusion and a system through which broader racial, fiscal, geographic, and institutional inequalities are reproduced.

American Indian and Alaska Native Significance

Medicaid is a major payer for eligible American Indian and Alaska Native beneficiaries receiving services through the Indian Health Service, tribal health programs, urban Indian organizations, and other enrolled providers.

The federal government generally provides a 100-percent matching rate for qualifying services furnished through eligible Indian Health Service and tribal facilities. State policies, billing systems, managed-care arrangements, and referral networks affect whether federal resources reach tribal health systems effectively.

Federal law provides certain cost-sharing protections and special enrolment rules. The 2025 Medicaid legislation also created exceptions for specified American Indian and Alaska Native beneficiaries from certain six-month renewal and community-engagement provisions scheduled for 2027.

Medicaid does not replace the federal trust responsibility or the Indian Health Service. It supplements health-system financing and can expand access beyond facilities directly operated or funded through Indian health programs.

Citizenship and Immigration Rules

Full Medicaid coverage generally requires United States citizenship or a qualifying immigration status, subject to verification and statutory exceptions.

Many qualified noncitizens historically faced a five-year waiting period after obtaining a qualifying status. States may elect federal options to cover lawfully residing children and pregnant people without imposing that waiting period.

Emergency Medicaid can reimburse treatment of an emergency medical condition for people who otherwise satisfy the state’s Medicaid eligibility requirements but do not qualify for full coverage because of immigration status.

Some states finance additional coverage with state-only funds. These programs are not necessarily Medicaid even when administered by the same agency.

Public Law 119-21, enacted in 2025, changed federal eligibility and financing rules for several immigrant categories. CMS issued implementation guidance in 2026, and states were modifying systems and policy accordingly.

Estate Recovery and Long-Term-Care Financial Rules

Federal law requires states to seek recovery from the estates of certain deceased Medicaid beneficiaries for nursing-facility services, home- and community-based services, and related hospital and prescription-drug services received after age 55. States may recover additional correctly paid benefits under broader state policies.

Recovery is delayed or prohibited while specified surviving spouses, children, or other protected relatives remain. States must establish hardship-waiver procedures.

Medicaid long-term-care eligibility also includes transfer-of-assets rules, home-equity limits, annuity requirements, liens, and spousal-impoverishment protections.

Estate recovery is controversial because it can preserve Medicaid as a payer of last resort while also reducing intergenerational property transfer among families with modest assets. The policy can be especially consequential in communities where homeownership is the principal form of family wealth.

Program Integrity

Medicaid program integrity addresses fraud, waste, abuse, improper payments, false claims, kickbacks, identity misuse, provider screening, plan misconduct, inaccurate eligibility, unsupported billing, and misuse of public funds.

State Medicaid Fraud Control Units investigate and prosecute provider fraud and patient abuse or neglect under federal and state authority. CMS, the HHS Office of Inspector General, the Department of Justice, state attorneys general, auditors, and contractors perform additional oversight.

Program integrity must distinguish deliberate fraud from administrative error, eligibility-system failure, ambiguous policy, documentation problems, and provider disputes.

Aggressive controls can protect public funds but may also delay legitimate care, burden providers, or terminate eligible beneficiaries when verification systems are inaccurate.

Quality Measurement and Accountability

CMS and states measure quality through child and adult core sets, home- and community-based-service measures, managed-care reporting, encounter data, surveys, audits, state scorecards, and program-specific evaluations.

Quality measurement addresses preventive services, maternal care, chronic disease, behavioural health, dental care, hospital use, readmissions, long-term-care outcomes, access, and beneficiary experience.

Measures can identify disparities and support performance improvement, but they depend on accurate data, consistent definitions, sufficient sample sizes, reliable race and ethnicity information, and meaningful consequences for poor performance.

Payment tied to quality requires careful risk adjustment so that providers are not rewarded for avoiding medically or socially complex patients.

COVID-19 Continuous Enrollment and Unwinding

The Families First Coronavirus Response Act increased federal Medicaid matching funds during the COVID-19 emergency on the condition that states maintain continuous enrolment for most beneficiaries.

Continuous enrolment reduced coverage losses and increased Medicaid rolls during the emergency. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 ended the condition independently of the public-health-emergency declaration and permitted states to resume terminations beginning April 1, 2023.

The return to ordinary renewal operations became known as the Medicaid unwinding. States reviewed millions of cases, renewed eligible people, transferred some applicants to Marketplace coverage, and terminated coverage for people found ineligible or who did not complete procedural requirements.

CMS required corrective action when state systems failed to comply with federal renewal rules. States were directed to complete unwinding-related renewals by the end of 2025, while certain compliance remediation continued into 2026.

The unwinding demonstrated how administrative process can determine coverage independently of underlying medical need.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Means-tested program
A program using income, resources, or other financial standards to determine eligibility.
Federal Medical Assistance Percentage
The federal matching percentage applied to qualifying state Medicaid expenditures.
State plan
The federally approved document describing how a state administers Medicaid.
State plan amendment
A formal state request to revise an approved Medicaid state plan.
MAGI
Modified Adjusted Gross Income, the financial methodology used for most children, pregnant people, parents, and nonelderly adults.
Non-MAGI
Eligibility methodologies used primarily for age, blindness, disability, long-term care, medically needy, and related pathways.
Spend-down
A process allowing a medically needy applicant to qualify after medical expenses reduce countable income to an applicable standard.
EPSDT
The comprehensive Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment benefit for qualifying Medicaid enrollees under age 21.
Long-term services and supports
Institutional or community services assisting people with continuing functional needs.
Capitation
A fixed periodic payment to a managed-care organization for each enrolled member.
Dual eligibility
Concurrent enrolment in Medicare and Medicaid.
Section 1115 demonstration
An experimental project approved by HHS to test approaches likely to promote Medicaid objectives.
Section 1915(c) waiver
An authority permitting home- and community-based services for people who would otherwise require institutional care.
Disproportionate Share Hospital payment
A supplemental payment to qualifying hospitals serving substantial Medicaid and uninsured populations.
Ex parte renewal
A renewal completed using reliable information already available without requiring the beneficiary to submit a form.
Procedural disenrolment
Termination because required information or process was not completed, rather than a substantive finding that the person exceeded eligibility standards.

Major Achievements

  • Created health coverage for tens of millions of low-income Americans.
  • Established a federal financing guarantee responsive to enrolment and qualifying service costs.
  • Expanded coverage for children and pregnant people beyond traditional cash-assistance recipients.
  • Financed comprehensive preventive and treatment services for children through EPSDT.
  • Became the principal public payer for nursing-facility and home- and community-based services.
  • Paid Medicare premiums and cost sharing for millions of low-income Medicare beneficiaries.
  • Supported safety-net hospitals, community health centres, rural providers, pharmacies, and public health systems.
  • Expanded access for nonelderly adults through the Affordable Care Act.
  • Supported movement from institutional care to community living for people with disabilities.
  • Reduced the uninsured rate among children through coordination with CHIP.
  • Financed maternity care, family planning, behavioural health, substance-use treatment, and chronic-disease services.
  • Created extensive data, quality, oversight, and beneficiary-appeal systems.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Eligibility and benefits vary substantially by state.
  • Some low-income adults remain in the coverage gap in non-expansion states.
  • Administrative renewals and documentation requirements can terminate eligible people.
  • Low provider payment can reduce access to physicians, dentists, specialists, home-care workers, and behavioural-health services.
  • Managed-care prior authorization and narrow networks can delay or restrict necessary care.
  • Home- and community-based programs may maintain waiting lists while institutional services remain mandatory for qualifying beneficiaries.
  • Beneficiaries frequently face fragmented agencies, plans, contractors, notices, and appeal systems.
  • Racial, disability, maternal-health, rural, and territorial disparities persist.
  • Estate recovery can reduce intergenerational transfer of modest family assets.
  • Provider taxes, supplemental payments, and intergovernmental financing arrangements can be difficult to evaluate for transparency and equity.
  • Waiver programs can create innovation but also instability and unequal rights across states.
  • Federal and state budget pressures can lead to payment reductions, benefit limits, or eligibility restrictions.
  • Program integrity controls can generate burdens and erroneous denials when data or processes are unreliable.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Implementation of Public Law 119-21

CMS and states were implementing a wide set of changes enacted in July 2025. Major subjects included eligibility renewals, community engagement, immigrant eligibility, retroactive coverage, provider taxes, state-directed payments, demonstration budget neutrality, and rural-health funding.

Community-Engagement Requirements

Beginning January 1, 2027, states must generally condition eligibility for specified expansion-group adults and comparable demonstration populations on work, education, community service, or other qualifying activities, subject to exemptions and verification procedures. States may elect earlier implementation where the statute permits.

Six-Month Renewals

Most expansion-group adults will move from annual to six-month eligibility renewals for renewals scheduled on or after January 1, 2027. States were modifying notices, data systems, staffing, ex parte processes, and renewal calendars during 2026.

Financing Restrictions

New statutory limits affect provider taxes, state-directed managed-care payments, and other financing arrangements. States and providers are assessing the implications for hospital systems, nursing facilities, rural institutions, and state budgets.

Rural Health Transformation

The 2025 law established a Rural Health Transformation Program. Federal and state implementation raises questions about allocation, provider stability, infrastructure, workforce, and the relationship between temporary transformation funding and recurring Medicaid revenue.

Coverage Retention

States continue to improve ex parte renewals, address system defects identified during the unwinding, and reduce procedural terminations while maintaining eligibility accuracy.

Long-Term-Care Workforce

Demand for home-care workers, personal attendants, nurses, direct-support professionals, and nursing-facility staff exceeds supply in many regions. Payment rates, wages, training, immigration policy, and working conditions affect access.

Managed-Care Accountability

Oversight priorities include network adequacy, prior authorization, encounter data, medical-loss ratios, quality, state-directed payments, plan profits, behavioural-health access, and the accuracy of capitation rates.

Maternal and Child Health

States continue expanding postpartum coverage and child continuous eligibility while confronting maternal mortality, maternity-care deserts, paediatric behavioural-health shortages, and uneven EPSDT implementation.

Expansion and the Coverage Gap

Non-expansion states continue to debate whether to adopt the ACA adult group, pursue limited demonstrations, or maintain existing categorical eligibility.

Summary

Medicaid is the joint federal-state health-coverage program established under Title XIX of the Social Security Act in 1965. It covers eligible children, parents, pregnant people, older adults, people with disabilities, and—where states adopted the Affordable Care Act expansion—many other low-income adults.

Federal law requires minimum populations, benefits, financing rules, due-process protections, and administrative standards. States operate distinct programs and make major decisions about eligibility, optional services, payment, managed care, waivers, and provider networks.

Medicaid is especially important because it finances services that other insurance often excludes, including nursing-facility care, home- and community-based supports, comprehensive child treatment under EPSDT, and Medicare cost-sharing assistance.

The program has expanded health access and strengthened safety-net institutions, but state variation, low payment, administrative burden, provider shortages, coverage gaps, estate recovery, and racial and geographic inequality remain significant limitations.

As of August 2026, states were implementing a major 2025 federal reconciliation law and preparing for 2027 changes involving six-month renewals and community-engagement requirements for many expansion-group adults. These changes will affect coverage retention, state administration, provider financing, and the future balance of federal and state authority.
