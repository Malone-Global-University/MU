FBI Racial Matters Program

The historical FBI framework of racial-matters captions, Classification 157 files, field-office squads, civil-rights and bombing investigations, civil-unrest intelligence, informants, and political surveillance that placed legitimate investigation of racist violence alongside broad monitoring of Black freedom organizations and racial politics.

What Was the FBI Racial Matters Program?

“Racial Matters” was an FBI administrative and investigative label used to organize information concerning racial conflict, civil-rights activity, racist violence, demonstrations, Black political organizations, bombings, urban unrest, and alleged subversive influence.

Historians and research institutions sometimes call this body of activity the Racial Matters Program. The surviving record shows a mixture of file captions, classification rules, field-office squads, intelligence responsibilities, civil-rights investigations, informant systems, and later counterintelligence operations rather than one uniform program.

Terminology and Historical Language

FBI documents used language now recognized as outdated, racialized, or offensive. Terms such as “Negro,” “racial agitator,” “Black extremist,” “Black nationalist hate group,” and “ghetto informant” should appear in quotation marks when necessary to identify an official file title or historical policy.

Modern description should distinguish the government’s terminology from the identity, conduct, or beliefs of the people it classified.

Not One Neatly Bounded Program

Unlike a grant program created by statute, Racial Matters did not have a single public enabling law, annual appropriation line, nationwide application process, or formal termination date.

Its practical boundaries changed as the FBI reorganized files, created squads, renamed classifications, opened special programs, and shifted between criminal, civil-rights, security, and civil-unrest missions.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the meaning of Racial Matters, Classification 157, early civil-rights surveillance, Klan and bombing investigations, alleged Communist influence, Martin Luther King Jr., Black organizations, urban-unrest intelligence, field-office squads, informants, indexes, COINTELPRO, the Ghetto Informant Program, civil-rights enforcement, constitutional concerns, Church Committee findings, reforms, records access, and present-day distinctions.

Geographic Scope

Racial Matters files were produced by FBI headquarters and field offices throughout the United States and Puerto Rico.

Records cover nationally significant leaders and organizations as well as local demonstrations, school desegregation, voting campaigns, church bombings, neighborhood conflict, public speeches, student groups, and civil disturbances.

Historical Context

The framework developed during segregation, the Cold War, the Second Red Scare, the Civil Rights Movement, white supremacist violence, school desegregation, urban rebellions, the rise of Black Power, and national concern about political disorder.

Government officials confronted actual violence and foreign-intelligence concerns while also treating lawful racial protest and political organization as objects of security intelligence.

Use of Racial-Matters Captions Before 1959

The FBI used “Racial Matters,” “Racial Situation,” and comparable language before establishing Classification 157.

Martin Luther King Jr. was monitored beginning in December 1955 during the Montgomery bus boycott under a racial-matters framework.

This chronology shows that the historical program cannot be reduced to Classification 157’s 1959 creation date.

Classification 157

FBI central records used numeric classifications to organize cases by subject or legal responsibility.

Classification 157 became the principal archival classification associated with Racial Matters, bombings, civil unrest, and later extremist matters.

January 23, 1959 Creation

The National Archives states that the FBI established Classification 157 on January 23, 1959.

Headquarters and field offices thereafter opened files using classification-and-case numbers such as 157-HQ or a field-office prefix followed by a local case number.

Racial Matters/Bombing Matters

Classification 157’s original title joined two subjects that were often connected during the civil-rights era: racial conflict and bombing activity.

White supremacist attacks against Black homes, churches, schools, synagogues, and integration efforts made bombings a major federal concern even when federal jurisdiction was limited.

Initial Investigative Focus

Early Classification 157 files focused on:

  • Ku Klux Klan organizations;
  • racially motivated bombings and bomb threats;
  • attacks on religious and educational institutions;
  • American Nazi Party activity;
  • anti-Semitic and other hate groups;
  • attacks on civil-rights workers; and
  • civil disorders and demonstrations with a potential federal interest.

Federal Criminal Jurisdiction

The FBI could investigate only when a federal statute, presidential responsibility, Secret Service request, federal-property interest, interstate element, civil-rights law, or other authorized basis existed.

Many murders, assaults, and bombings were primarily state crimes during the early period, limiting federal prosecution even when FBI agents gathered evidence.

Civil-Disorder Responsibilities

Classification 157 included violations of criminal law connected to civil disturbances, information requested for Secret Service protection, intelligence concerning disorders that might require federal troops, and information needed for federal health-and-safety planning during demonstrations.

1971 Renaming

In 1971, the FBI changed Classification 157’s title to “Extremist Matters/Civil Unrest.”

The change reflected expansion beyond the original racial-matters and bombing focus, but it did not erase the earlier records or the racial assumptions embedded in many investigations.

Post-1971 Files

After renaming, Classification 157 included investigations concerning the American Indian Movement, Symbionese Liberation Army, civil disturbances, and other subjects classified as extremist.

Researchers should identify the date and caption rather than assuming every 157 file was created under the original Racial Matters title.

Classification 44: Civil Rights

Classification 44 was a separate FBI category for civil-rights cases.

National Archives holdings include files concerning the Ku Klux Klan, racial disturbances, police brutality, and later federal civil-rights enforcement.

A single event or person could appear in Classification 44, Classification 157, domestic-security files, assassination files, or other classifications for different investigative purposes.

Domestic-Security and Subversive Files

FBI Classification 100 and related domestic-security categories concerned subversive matters, Communist organizations, and internal security.

Civil-rights leaders sometimes appeared in those files because the Bureau investigated alleged Communist influence, not because the racial-matters classification alone governed every aspect of the inquiry.

Informant Classifications

The FBI used separate classifications for security and criminal informants.

National Archives guidance states that informants reporting exclusively on racist hate groups were redesignated as criminal informants in 1959, illustrating how source administration could be separated from the subject file receiving the information.

How FBI File Numbers Work

A file number typically identifies the classification, office, case, subfile, volume, and serial.

For example, “157” identifies the classification, while headquarters or field-office prefixes identify the office maintaining the file.

File numbers are archival keys, not findings of guilt or proof that the subject committed a crime.

Headquarters Files

Headquarters files collected reports from field offices, established policy, maintained national indexes, coordinated multi-office cases, and preserved information concerning organizations or leaders with national significance.

Field-Office Files

Field offices opened local files, contacted sources, attended public events, interviewed witnesses, investigated violence, and reported to headquarters.

Surviving National Archives listings demonstrate extensive state-by-state variation in subjects, file size, and preservation.

Racial Matters Squads

Some major field offices organized personnel into units described as Racial Matters squads.

Their responsibilities could include civil-rights cases, urban unrest, Black organizations, demonstrations, informants, and coordination with local police.

Squad structure varied by field office and period.

FBI Headquarters Direction

Headquarters issued instructions to special agents in charge concerning racial matters, reporting standards, important organizations, demonstrations, urban unrest, and alleged Communist influence.

Central direction encouraged nationally comparable files while allowing local offices substantial discretion in source development and reporting.

J. Edgar Hoover

Hoover directed the FBI throughout the principal Racial Matters period.

He viewed Communist influence, civil disorder, Black nationalism, criticism of the Bureau, and certain civil-rights leaders as interrelated threats.

His personal hostility toward Martin Luther King Jr. profoundly shaped the Bureau’s treatment of King.

Domestic Intelligence Division

The Domestic Intelligence Division handled internal-security, Communist-infiltration, extremist, and related intelligence responsibilities.

Its work overlapped criminal civil-rights and civil-unrest functions, producing bureaucratic tension between prosecuting racist violence and monitoring civil-rights activism.

Criminal and Civil-Rights Functions

Criminal investigative components handled bombings, civil-rights violations, violence, and other federal offenses.

The same event could generate intelligence and criminal files, making it essential to distinguish the purpose and legal predicate of each inquiry.

Department of Justice Civil Rights Division

Congress created the Civil Rights Division in 1957.

The Division relied on FBI agents to investigate voting discrimination, school desegregation resistance, racial violence, police misconduct, and other federal civil-rights violations.

Church Committee records show that the FBI also supplied the Division with political and intelligence information, sometimes blurring investigative and policy roles.

The FBI’s Dual Role

Civil-rights organizations often needed FBI protection and investigation while knowing that the Bureau monitored their leaders, meetings, allies, and political activity.

This dual role damaged trust and allowed intelligence gathered through legitimate cases to contribute to political surveillance.

Ku Klux Klan Violence

Classification 157 initially focused heavily on Klan groups responsible for bombings, murder, intimidation, cross burnings, and attacks on desegregation.

FBI infiltration and investigation sometimes supplied crucial evidence when local law enforcement was unwilling or complicit.

Church and Institution Bombings

Bombings and bomb threats against Black churches, synagogues, schools, homes, and integration supporters were central Racial Matters/Bombing Matters subjects.

Investigations involved explosives analysis, witnesses, informants, interstate evidence, extremist organizations, and local-police cooperation.

Sixteenth Street Baptist Church Bombing

The FBI investigated the September 15, 1963 bombing that killed four girls in Birmingham, Alabama.

The case illustrates both federal investigative capacity and the long delay between evidence gathering and successful prosecution of responsible Klansmen.

Mississippi Burning

The 1964 murders of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner led to one of the FBI’s largest civil-rights investigations.

The Bureau identified conspirators, including local law-enforcement participants, but federal jurisdiction and hostile state institutions limited immediate murder prosecutions.

Medgar Evers

The FBI investigated the 1963 murder of NAACP field secretary Medgar Evers.

The case was later reopened, and Byron De La Beckwith was convicted in state court in 1994.

Evers’s case demonstrates the contrast between legitimate civil-rights investigation and intelligence directed at civil-rights organizations.

Freedom Riders

Freedom Riders challenged segregated interstate transportation in 1961 and faced mob violence.

FBI files documented attacks, local officials, demonstrators, and racial conditions. Critics faulted federal officials for failing to prevent violence despite available intelligence.

Voting-Rights Investigations

FBI agents investigated intimidation, discriminatory registration practices, conspiracies, and violence connected to Black voting.

Voting-rights enforcement expanded after the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Police Brutality and Color of Law

Federal law permitted investigation of officials who willfully deprived people of constitutional rights under color of law.

Police brutality could appear in civil-rights files while protest against brutality appeared in racial-matters or civil-unrest intelligence files.

American Nazi Party and Anti-Semitic Groups

Classification 157 included investigations of the American Nazi Party, anti-Semitic organizations, and other hate groups.

The classification therefore cannot accurately be described as a system concerned only with Black activism.

Montgomery Bus Boycott

The FBI began monitoring Martin Luther King Jr. in December 1955 during the Montgomery bus boycott.

Early reporting concerned racial tension, speeches, threats, organizations, local officials, violence, and alleged Communist associations.

Martin Luther King Jr.

King became the most prominent individual associated with the Racial Matters framework.

His file history moved from racial-matters monitoring to Communist-infiltration investigation, electronic surveillance, personal intelligence collection, and covert attempts to discredit him.

Early Monitoring of King

The FBI’s first interest in King arose from his leadership in a major racial protest rather than evidence that he had committed a federal crime.

Bureau officials raised Communist-association concerns in 1956, but the major Communist-infiltration investigation developed later.

Communist Influence in Racial Matters

FBI files used captions such as “Communist Influence in Racial Matters” and “Communist Party, USA—Negro Question.”

The Bureau investigated whether Communists were influencing Black leaders and civil-rights organizations, often treating interracial political cooperation as possible subversion.

Stanley Levison

The FBI considered Stanley Levison, a close adviser to King, to have significant Communist Party associations.

Levison’s relationship with King became the principal justification for intensified electronic surveillance and investigation during the Kennedy administration.

Robert F. Kennedy

Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy authorized wiretaps on King and Southern Christian Leadership Conference telephones in 1963 after FBI representations concerning Communist influence.

The authorization did not approve every later FBI use of the information or every covert effort to damage King.

Electronic Surveillance of King

The FBI used telephone wiretaps and microphones against King and associates.

Surveillance moved beyond alleged Communist influence and collected intensely personal information later used in efforts to discredit and pressure him.

King’s Conflict with the FBI

King publicly criticized the FBI’s failure to resolve racist violence in the Deep South.

Hoover responded publicly in 1964 by calling King a notorious liar, revealing how institutional criticism had become personal conflict.

Anonymous Package Sent to King

The FBI anonymously sent King a recording and hostile letter in 1964.

King’s associates interpreted the message as urging him to take his own life before personal information was exposed.

The action was not a legitimate method of investigating crime or foreign influence.

King’s Multiple File Labels

King appeared in Racial Matters, Communist-infiltration, security, assassination, and COINTELPRO-related records.

Researchers should not assume that all anti-King activity occurred under one classification or that the Racial Matters caption alone explains the entire campaign.

Southern Christian Leadership Conference

SCLC was monitored because of its national leadership in nonviolent civil-rights protest, relationship with King, advisers accused of Communist connections, antiwar activity, fundraising, and political influence.

Its lawful nonviolent mission did not prevent inclusion in intelligence and later counterintelligence systems.

Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee

SNCC organized sit-ins, Freedom Rides, voter registration, Freedom Summer, and later Black Power campaigns.

FBI files tracked leaders, field projects, demonstrations, speeches, travel, alliances, internal debates, and alleged subversive influence.

Congress of Racial Equality

CORE organized nonviolent direct action and interstate desegregation campaigns.

National Archives guidance states that preliminary investigations of major civil-rights groups such as CORE were frequently opened to determine the degree of subversive influence even when the organization’s general purpose was not questioned.

National Association for the Advancement of Colored People

NAACP leaders, branches, meetings, voter-registration work, legal campaigns, and public activity appeared in FBI files.

The organization also relied on federal investigators in racial-violence and civil-rights cases, illustrating the Bureau’s contradictory relationship with established civil-rights institutions.

Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party

The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenged the state’s all-white regular Democratic delegation and demanded political representation.

FBI reporting on its organizers and conventions demonstrates how electoral activity and racial protest entered intelligence files.

Fannie Lou Hamer

Fannie Lou Hamer’s voting-rights and Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party work drew FBI attention.

Her case illustrates how a person suffering racial violence and seeking federal protection could also become the subject of political and racial-matters reporting.

Nation of Islam

The FBI monitored Nation of Islam temples, leaders, publications, membership, speeches, finances, prison activity, and internal disputes.

Records appeared under racial, security, Black nationalist, and counterintelligence classifications.

Malcolm X

Malcolm X was monitored during his Nation of Islam leadership and after forming new organizations.

FBI files documented speeches, travel, foreign contacts, threats, organizational conflict, and political development.

Evidence of surveillance should be distinguished from disputed claims concerning government responsibility for his assassination.

Black Power Movement

The rise of Black Power expanded FBI attention from traditional civil-rights organizations to racial autonomy, armed self-defense, socialism, anti-imperialism, cultural nationalism, and community control.

The Bureau often treated these distinct ideas as components of one extremist threat.

Black Panther Party

Classification 157 records include extensive Black Panther Party files from headquarters and field offices.

The FBI monitored chapters, leaders, weapons, arrests, community programs, publications, finances, international contacts, membership, and internal disputes.

Huey P. Newton

Black Panther cofounder Huey P. Newton appeared in Classification 157, indexes, criminal files, and counterintelligence records.

His legal cases and leadership status made him a national subject of FBI reporting.

Bobby Seale

Black Panther cofounder Bobby Seale was monitored in connection with organization leadership, speeches, travel, prosecutions, and Party activity.

Files concerning Seale span criminal cases, civil unrest, political intelligence, and counterintelligence.

Fred Hampton

Fred Hampton organized the Illinois Black Panther Party and a multiracial Rainbow Coalition in Chicago.

The Chicago FBI’s racial-matters personnel recruited William O’Neal as an informant inside the Panthers. Information supplied by O’Neal was shared before the December 4, 1969 raid in which Hampton and Mark Clark were killed.

Federal and local liability was later contested in civil litigation and resolved through settlement.

William O’Neal

O’Neal became an FBI informant and the Chicago Black Panther Party’s security chief.

His position allowed access to leadership, facilities, plans, and security arrangements, demonstrating the depth an informant could reach within a targeted organization.

Angela Davis

Angela Davis appeared in Classification 157 headquarters and field-office files concerning political activity, Communist associations, Black liberation, public support campaigns, and her criminal prosecution.

She was acquitted of the principal charges in 1972.

Stokely Carmichael

Stokely Carmichael, later known as Kwame Ture, appeared in Classification 157, the Rabble Rouser Index, SNCC files, Black nationalist records, and international-travel reporting.

His advocacy of Black Power increased the Bureau’s efforts to isolate and track him.

H. Rap Brown

H. Rap Brown was monitored as a SNCC leader, public speaker, Black Power advocate, and alleged instigator of civil disorder.

His files illustrate how rhetoric, travel, protest, criminal allegations, and political leadership became intertwined in FBI intelligence.

Revolutionary Action Movement

The Revolutionary Action Movement was monitored as a Black nationalist and revolutionary organization.

FBI reporting examined ideology, leaders, chapters, alliances, publications, and alleged plans for violence.

Deacons for Defense and Justice

The Deacons for Defense and Justice organized armed self-defense against racist violence in the South.

Their activity challenged federal and local assumptions that civil-rights protection should remain exclusively nonviolent and state controlled.

Artists and Public Figures

FBI racial-matters files also mentioned artists, athletes, clergy, writers, and public figures because they performed at civil-rights events, supported defendants, associated with leaders, or might influence public gatherings.

Appearance in a file did not necessarily mean the person was a formal investigative target.

Demonstrations

Field offices reported marches, sit-ins, boycotts, rallies, conventions, memorials, and mass meetings.

Reports could serve legitimate safety or federal-protection needs while also recording political views, speakers, organizations, and attendance.

Public Speeches

Agents and informants recorded or summarized public speeches by civil-rights and Black political leaders.

Public availability reduced privacy expectations but did not eliminate First Amendment concerns when speech alone became the basis for continuing intelligence files or adverse action.

Churches and Religious Institutions

Black churches were central to civil-rights organization, fundraising, shelter, leadership, and public meetings.

They were also targets of racist bombing and objects of FBI intelligence collection, placing religious exercise and political association in the same institutional files.

Universities and Student Organizations

Student groups, campus speakers, Black student unions, faculty, demonstrations, and university responses appeared in racial-matters and civil-unrest files.

Intelligence collection could affect academic freedom, discipline, employment, and student participation.

Racial-Situation Reports

Field offices prepared reports describing local racial tension, political activity, violence, community sentiment, government response, and possible disorder.

Such reports combined factual incident information with subjective assessments from agents, police, officials, informants, business owners, and community sources.

Urban Unrest and Riots

Rebellions and riots in Watts, Newark, Detroit, and other cities expanded FBI demand for intelligence concerning urban Black communities.

The Bureau investigated interstate incitement, organized violence, weapons, leaders, and groups, while often collecting broad information from people with no connection to criminal activity.

Federal Riot and Civil-Disorder Laws

Federal law concerning interstate travel or facilities used to incite riot and later civil-disorder statutes supplied possible criminal predicates.

Political speech and travel remained protected unless statutory elements and constitutional standards were met.

Information for Possible Federal Troop Use

Classification 157 responsibilities included information concerning disorders that might require federal troops.

This mission connected local racial intelligence with presidential emergency authority and military planning.

Secret Service Assistance

The FBI supplied information concerning demonstrations and racial tensions that could affect presidential or other Secret Service-protected visits.

Protective intelligence can be legitimate, but reporting should remain connected to concrete safety needs rather than general political opposition.

Informants

Informants supplied information concerning organizations, leaders, meetings, demonstrations, violence, community sentiment, and local conditions.

Sources included organization members, police contacts, business owners, workers, clergy, political opponents, and residents.

Source Reliability

Informant reports varied in access, motive, accuracy, bias, and corroboration.

Political opponents, paid sources, frightened residents, and local police could provide distorted accounts, especially when racial assumptions influenced interpretation.

Ghetto Informant Program

The FBI established the Ghetto Informant Program during the urban-unrest crisis of 1967.

Sources were positioned as listening posts in Black neighborhoods and asked to report information concerning possible disorder, organizations, and local activity.

The Church Committee reported rapid growth from 4,067 ghetto informants in 1969 to 7,402 in 1972.

Distinguishing the Ghetto Informant Program

The Ghetto Informant Program was not identical to Racial Matters or COINTELPRO.

It was primarily an intelligence-source system. Information it produced could enter racial-matters, civil-unrest, extremist, criminal, or counterintelligence files.

Termination of the Ghetto Informant Program

The Ghetto Informant Program ended in 1973 after internal concern about its breadth and collection of information unrelated to riots.

Some sources were discontinued, while others were reclassified under criminal, security, extremist, or revolutionary-activity programs.

Poor People’s Campaign

The FBI developed extensive intelligence concerning the 1968 Poor People’s Campaign, its participants, camps, routes, leadership, logistics, speeches, and potential disorder.

The campaign sought economic justice through mass protest and was not itself a criminal enterprise.

Operation POCAM

FBI records used the caption POCAM for intelligence concerning the Poor People’s Campaign.

The operation drew on field offices and informants and demonstrates how a national protest could become a coordinated intelligence project.

Indexes and Lists

The FBI maintained indexes identifying people considered important agitators, security risks, extremists, or organizational leaders.

Inclusion could increase reporting and dissemination even when no criminal charge existed.

Rabble Rouser Index

Classification 157 records include a Rabble Rouser Index containing prominent activists and speakers.

The title illustrates the Bureau’s political and pejorative framing of dissenting leadership.

Agitator Index

The FBI used an Agitator Index for individuals believed capable of influencing civil disorder or protest.

Labels could be based on leadership and rhetoric rather than proven criminal conduct.

Key Black Extremist Program

The Bureau created special reporting concerning people it designated “Key Black Extremists.”

This system overlapped Racial Matters, civil-unrest intelligence, informant programs, and COINTELPRO but had its own reporting and administrative functions.

Recording Public Appearances

Classification 157 files include instructions and reports concerning concealed recording devices used at public appearances by people classified as Black or New Left extremists.

Recording public speech can preserve evidence, but systematic collection based on viewpoint creates constitutional and policy concerns.

Photography and Identification

Agents photographed demonstrations, meetings, leaders, vehicles, and participants.

Images supported identification, indexes, investigative leads, and intelligence reports, but could chill lawful participation and create long-term records on people never suspected of crime.

Local Police Cooperation

FBI field offices exchanged information with state police, municipal departments, sheriffs, intelligence units, and prosecutors.

Local cooperation could help solve racist violence or transmit political intelligence into agencies with histories of segregation and misconduct.

Local Red Squads and Intelligence Units

Many cities maintained political-intelligence or “red squad” units that monitored civil-rights and protest groups.

Their records and actions overlapped FBI reporting, but local surveillance should not automatically be attributed to the federal Racial Matters framework.

Military Intelligence

Military intelligence collected information concerning civil disturbances, demonstrations, and political organizations during the same period.

Interagency exchange widened the audience for racial and political intelligence and complicated later efforts to determine the source of particular records.

White House and Executive-Branch Requests

Presidents and Attorneys General requested information concerning civil-rights leaders, demonstrations, Communist influence, violence, and political conditions.

Executive requests helped legitimize broad intelligence collection even when officials did not know every field technique or file detail.

Congressional Requests and Political Demand

Members of Congress requested information on subversion, racial unrest, organizations, and leaders.

Congress supplied political pressure for aggressive intelligence while failing for decades to establish systematic oversight.

Media Relationships

FBI officials supplied background information to journalists and monitored reporting on racial conflict and civil-rights leaders.

Information could support public understanding, but selective or unattributed dissemination could damage reputations and manipulate political debate.

Dissemination of Intelligence

Racial-matters information was shared with the White House, Department of Justice, Secret Service, military, state and local police, other agencies, and selected public or private recipients.

Dissemination multiplied harm when information was unverified, irrelevant, racialized, or collected through intrusive means.

Employment and Institutional Harm

Intelligence shared with employers, schools, licensing bodies, or public officials could affect jobs, education, travel, appointments, and public standing.

People often received no notice or opportunity to challenge the information.

Relationship to COINTELPRO

COINTELPRO was designed to disrupt and neutralize selected organizations and individuals.

Racial Matters was a broader information and case-management framework that included legitimate criminal investigations, intelligence collection, and political surveillance.

Information gathered under Racial Matters could support COINTELPRO proposals and actions.

Black Nationalist–Hate Groups COINTELPRO

In August 1967, the FBI opened a COINTELPRO against organizations it labelled Black nationalist hate groups.

Targets included revolutionary groups, religious organizations, civil-rights groups, student organizations, and leaders committed to nonviolence.

Covert-Action Methods

COINTELPRO methods included anonymous letters, forged communications, disinformation, false accusations, media manipulation, employer pressure, and exploitation of factional conflict.

Those actions went beyond the collection and reporting characteristic of many Racial Matters files.

King and the COINTELPRO Label

Some of the FBI’s worst actions against King occurred under security and Communist-infiltration files rather than a formal COINTELPRO caption.

The archival distinction matters because abusive political action cannot be understood solely by searching for the word COINTELPRO.

Civil-Rights Enforcement Versus Counterintelligence

Investigating a church bombing seeks evidence for lawful prosecution.

Secretly attempting to break a civil-rights coalition seeks to alter political activity.

The Racial Matters framework sometimes placed these fundamentally different functions within overlapping personnel, sources, and files.

First Amendment

Speech, press, religion, assembly, petition, and association protect civil-rights organizing and political advocacy.

Government can investigate violence and federal crimes but cannot lawfully create intelligence files solely because people advocate racial change or hold unpopular political views.

Fourth Amendment

Wiretaps, microphones, searches, mail-related surveillance, tracking, and seizures implicate the Fourth Amendment.

Domestic-security surveillance during the period often occurred without the judicial procedures expected in ordinary criminal cases.

Due Process

Secret files and undisclosed dissemination could damage employment, education, custody, reputation, and liberty without notice or a hearing.

The inability to see or correct government information was a central weakness of the historical intelligence system.

Equal Protection and Racialized Targeting

The Racial Matters label made race itself an organizing feature of federal intelligence.

Although the files included white supremacist violence, Black organizations and communities became subjects of broad political and civil-unrest collection in ways that reflected racial stereotypes about disorder and subversion.

Freedom of Association

Informants, attendance lists, photographs, indexes, and organization files could discourage people from joining lawful groups.

Association with a monitored leader or event could generate additional records even without evidence of personal wrongdoing.

Religious Freedom

Black churches and the Nation of Islam were both religious institutions and political communities.

Surveillance based on religious association or political theology raises First Amendment concerns distinct from investigation of particular criminal acts.

Chilling Effect

Knowledge or suspicion of FBI surveillance could deter attendance, donations, speech, travel, organizing, and cooperation with civil-rights groups.

The chilling effect extended beyond formal targets to entire communities.

Racial Profiling

A racial-matters system risks treating racial identity, neighborhood, organization membership, or political advocacy as a proxy for dangerousness.

Modern investigation requires individualized, lawful purpose and cannot rely solely on race or protected activity.

Accuracy and Verification

Intelligence reports often recorded allegations, rumors, source opinions, or preliminary information.

Repetition across files could make uncertain information appear confirmed when several reports traced to the same source.

Retention of Information

FBI files preserved personal and political information for decades.

Long retention increased historical value while also extending privacy harm and the possibility of later use outside the original purpose.

Privacy Act of 1974

The Privacy Act established rules for federal systems of records, disclosure, access, correction, and relevance.

Intelligence and Watergate scandals, including COINTELPRO and related domestic surveillance, formed part of the law’s historical context.

Freedom of Information Act

FOIA allowed activists, journalists, lawyers, and historians to seek Racial Matters and related FBI files.

Records can still be withheld or redacted to protect privacy, sources, law-enforcement methods, grand-jury secrecy, and national security.

Church Committee

The Senate created the Church Committee in 1975 to investigate intelligence activities and abuses.

Its reports examined FBI racial intelligence, informants, COINTELPRO, King, civil-rights groups, demonstrations, and the absence of effective oversight.

Church Committee Findings on Racial Matters

The committee found that the FBI investigated racial matters, the New Left, student agitation, and protest under broad domestic-intelligence authorities.

It concluded that the Bureau collected information about lawful political activity and used intelligence systems without adequate standards, review, or congressional control.

Church Committee Findings on Informants

The committee documented extensive use of informants in domestic-intelligence investigations and examined the Ghetto Informant Program’s size and breadth.

It warned that informants could influence organizations, collect irrelevant personal information, and become instruments of disruption.

Church Committee Findings on King

The committee found that the FBI’s campaign against King extended far beyond investigation of alleged Communist influence.

The Bureau sought to discredit King among government officials, churches, donors, media, and the public and used private surveillance information for coercive purposes.

Lack of Effective Oversight

Attorneys General, presidents, and Congress received incomplete information and did not impose clear, enforceable limits on domestic intelligence.

FBI secrecy and Hoover’s institutional authority made external review unusually difficult.

Levi Guidelines

Attorney General Edward Levi issued domestic-security guidelines in 1976 after congressional investigations and public criticism.

The guidelines required factual predicates and approvals and rejected investigation based merely on unpopular political views.

Decline After the 1976 Guidelines

National Archives guidance states that activity in Classification 157 decreased dramatically after the Levi Guidelines.

Attorney General Levi reported that open domestic-security cases fell from more than 21,000 in July 1973 to 626 by September 1976.

The nationwide case total was broader than Classification 157 alone.

Successor Attorney General Guidelines

Later Attorneys General repeatedly revised FBI investigative guidelines.

Modern rules balance crime prevention, national security, intelligence collection, privacy, civil liberties, and oversight but remain subjects of public controversy.

Attorney General’s Guidelines for Domestic FBI Operations

The consolidated domestic guidelines took effect in 2008 and continue to govern FBI domestic operations.

They prohibit investigation, collection, or maintenance of information solely for monitoring First Amendment-protected activity or other lawful constitutional rights.

Domestic Investigations and Operations Guide

The FBI’s Domestic Investigations and Operations Guide implements Attorney General guidelines through internal standards, approval rules, investigative levels, techniques, records, and oversight.

Public versions are partly redacted and revised over time.

Modern FBI Civil-Rights Mission

The modern FBI investigates hate crimes, color-of-law violations, human trafficking, freedom-of-access offenses, and other federal civil-rights crimes.

This mission is legally and institutionally distinct from the historical Racial Matters classification.

Hate-Crime Investigations

Modern hate-crime investigations focus on criminal acts motivated by protected characteristics rather than monitoring racial politics as a general subject.

Federal jurisdiction depends on applicable statutes and facts.

Color-of-Law Investigations

The FBI investigates officials accused of willfully depriving people of constitutional rights through excessive force, unlawful arrest, sexual abuse, fabricated evidence, or other misconduct.

These cases continue the legitimate civil-rights-enforcement side of the Bureau’s historical role.

No Current Racial Matters Program

Public FBI classifications and current mission descriptions do not identify an active “Racial Matters Program.”

Modern investigations should be described by their actual legal authority and subject—such as hate crime, civil disorder, domestic terrorism, or civil rights—rather than by the obsolete historical title.

Modern Comparisons

Activists sometimes compare later surveillance of racial-justice movements with the Racial Matters system or COINTELPRO.

Historical analogy can identify recurring risks but does not prove that a current investigation has the same authority, purpose, scale, or methods.

How to Evaluate a Modern Surveillance Claim

Researchers should identify:

  • the agency and office;
  • the legal and policy authority;
  • the investigative predicate;
  • the specific conduct under investigation;
  • whether race or protected activity influenced selection;
  • the techniques used;
  • the approving officials;
  • information retention and dissemination;
  • oversight findings; and
  • documentary evidence rather than analogy alone.

National Archives Holdings

The National Archives preserves Classification 157 headquarters files, field-office files, and indexes.

Its Black Power records guide lists files by state, field office, person, and organization.

Access can require screening under FOIA exemptions and classification rules.

Classification 157 Research Guide

The National Archives online guide provides National Archives Identifiers for headquarters and numerous field-office series.

It lists Black Panther Party, Nation of Islam, Angela Davis, Stokely Carmichael, Huey Newton, and other subjects as archival access points.

Martin Luther King Jr. Records

FBI records concerning King exist across multiple collections and classifications.

A 1977 court order transferred extensive surveillance materials to the National Archives under a fifty-year seal scheduled to expire in 2027, subject to court administration and archival processing.

The 2025 King Records Release

The National Archives released more than 243,000 pages of assassination-related King records in July 2025 under Executive Order 14176.

The release includes Classification 157 material but is not identical to the sealed surveillance collection or a complete Racial Matters archive.

FBI Vault

The FBI Vault publishes FOIA releases concerning King, the Black Panther Party, the Ghetto Informant Program, COINTELPRO, civil unrest, and related subjects.

Scans can be fragmented, duplicated, redacted, or difficult to search.

Dispersed Records

Racial Matters records can appear in FBI headquarters files, field files, individual subject files, assassination files, civil-rights files, court records, presidential libraries, congressional records, and local police archives.

No one repository contains every relevant document.

Redactions and Restrictions

Records can be redacted to protect personal privacy, confidential sources, national-security information, grand-jury secrecy, investigative methods, and living third parties.

Redactions can be necessary while limiting historical accountability.

Missing or Destroyed Records

Some files were destroyed under records schedules, lost, never centralized, or created under captions researchers do not recognize.

Absence of a file cannot establish that no surveillance occurred, but allegations should not be treated as proven without evidence.

Bias Within the Archive

FBI records reflect the priorities, language, assumptions, and source networks of the agency that created them.

They document government perception and activity but do not automatically provide an accurate account of the targeted person or movement.

Proposal, Approval, and Action

A memorandum proposing investigation or counterintelligence does not prove that headquarters approved it.

Approval does not prove implementation, and an implementation report does not necessarily prove the claimed effect.

Legitimate Investigative Achievements

  • Investigated Ku Klux Klan terrorism and racist violence.
  • Gathered evidence concerning church bombings and attacks on civil-rights workers.
  • Supported federal civil-rights prosecutions when local authorities failed.
  • Documented bomb threats and public-safety risks.
  • Assisted protection planning for demonstrations and federal officials.
  • Investigated interstate riot and civil-disorder offenses when federal elements existed.
  • Provided evidence in major cases such as Mississippi Burning.

Documented Abuses and Institutional Failures

  • Monitored lawful civil-rights leadership because of racial politics and alleged subversion.
  • Collected broad information concerning speeches, meetings, churches, students, and organizations.
  • Used racialized and ideological categories as intelligence shortcuts.
  • Expanded community informant networks far beyond specific criminal suspects.
  • Shared derogatory or unverified information without notice or correction.
  • Allowed political intelligence to support COINTELPRO disruption.
  • Used intimate surveillance information against Martin Luther King Jr.
  • Blurred civil-rights enforcement with surveillance of civil-rights complainants.
  • Maintained indexes and files on people not charged with crime.
  • Operated without clear congressional or judicial oversight.

Limits of the Historical Record

  • “Racial Matters Program” was not consistently used as one official title.
  • Classification 157 began after some Racial Matters monitoring was already occurring.
  • File classifications changed over time.
  • Many subjects appear in several classifications.
  • Criminal, intelligence, and covert-action purposes overlap.
  • Records contain hearsay, source bias, and administrative shorthand.
  • Some material remains redacted, sealed, dispersed, or missing.
  • Later summaries sometimes treat all Black surveillance as COINTELPRO.
  • Legitimate Klan investigations can be conflated with political repression.
  • Modern analogies can exceed the available evidence.

Major Controversies

Program identity
Historians use “Racial Matters Program,” while official records show changing captions, classifications, and units rather than one uniform program.
Civil-rights protection
The FBI solved or advanced important racial-violence cases while surveilling the movements demanding protection.
Communist influence
Foreign-influence concerns existed, but allegations were used to justify broad monitoring of Black politics and civil-rights leadership.
Martin Luther King Jr.
Monitoring that began as racial and security intelligence evolved into a personal campaign of coercion and discrediting.
Informants
Sources could provide warning of violence while also creating blanket surveillance of Black communities.
Urban unrest
Officials required emergency information, but riot preparation encouraged assumptions that Black neighborhoods were security threats.
Classification 157
The same classification included Klan terrorism, Black activism, bombings, civil disorders, and extremist organizations, obscuring major legal and moral distinctions.
Modern legacy
Current FBI rules reject viewpoint-only monitoring, but debate continues over race, protest surveillance, informants, assessments, and domestic terrorism labels.

How the Racial Matters Program Should Be Researched

Research should identify the date, office, classification, caption, subject, legal predicate, source, investigative technique, dissemination, and relationship to other programs.

Important distinctions include:

  • Racial Matters as a caption versus Classification 157;
  • pre-1959 monitoring versus post-1959 classification files;
  • Classification 44 Civil Rights versus Classification 157;
  • criminal civil-rights enforcement versus domestic intelligence;
  • Racial Matters versus Communist-infiltration files;
  • Racial Matters versus COINTELPRO;
  • Racial Matters versus the Ghetto Informant Program;
  • public-event reporting versus covert disruption;
  • legitimate investigation of racist violence versus monitoring of lawful protest;
  • file inclusion versus criminal suspicion;
  • source allegation versus verified fact;
  • proposal versus approved or implemented action;
  • historical FBI terminology versus modern neutral description;
  • federal activity versus state or local police intelligence; and
  • historical programs versus modern analogies.

Why the FBI Racial Matters Program Matters

The Racial Matters framework reveals that federal responses to the Black freedom struggle cannot be divided neatly into protection on one side and repression on the other.

The same institution investigated Klan murder, church bombing, voter intimidation, and police violence while monitoring the organizations and leaders demanding federal action.

Classification 157’s movement from Klan and bombing cases into broad Black political and civil-unrest intelligence demonstrates how emergency and criminal authorities can expand into surveillance of lawful dissent.

Its history explains later reforms requiring factual predicates, political neutrality, privacy controls, congressional oversight, and restrictions on investigations based solely on First Amendment activity.

Summary

The FBI Racial Matters Program was not one statute-created program with a single formal charter. It was a historical network of captions, classifications, squads, reports, informants, civil-rights cases, civil-unrest intelligence, and political surveillance.

The FBI used racial-matters and racial-situation captions before 1959, including when it began monitoring Martin Luther King Jr. during the Montgomery bus boycott.

On January 23, 1959, the Bureau created Classification 157 under the title Racial Matters/Bombing Matters.

The original classification focused on Ku Klux Klan organizations, racist bombings, bomb threats, hate groups, attacks on civil-rights workers, and civil disturbances.

During the 1960s, Classification 157 expanded to urban unrest, Black nationalist organizations, the Black Panther Party, demonstrations, and individuals classified as agitators or extremists.

Classification 44 Civil Rights remained a distinct category for criminal civil-rights enforcement. Communist-infiltration, informant, assassination, and COINTELPRO files also overlapped the same people and events.

The FBI performed valuable work against Klan terrorism, church bombings, voting intimidation, police misconduct, and attacks on civil-rights workers.

At the same time, it collected extensive intelligence concerning lawful Black political activity, churches, organizations, students, speeches, demonstrations, and civil-rights leaders.

King’s monitoring moved from Racial Matters into Communist-infiltration and electronic-surveillance files and eventually into a covert campaign intended to discredit him.

The Ghetto Informant Program expanded community surveillance from 1967 to 1973, while the Black Extremist COINTELPRO used racial and organizational intelligence for covert disruption.

In 1971, Classification 157 was renamed Extremist Matters/Civil Unrest. Activity decreased dramatically after the 1976 Levi Guidelines.

The historical Racial Matters title is inactive. Modern FBI civil-rights investigations operate under different statutes, Attorney General guidelines, and policies that prohibit monitoring people solely because of First Amendment-protected activity.
