Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program

The federal block grant established in 1981 to help households with low incomes meet heating and cooling costs, prevent or resolve home-energy crises, and reduce long-term energy vulnerability through weatherization and related services.

What Is the Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program?

LIHEAP is a federal block grant helping households with low incomes meet immediate home-energy needs.

The federal government distributes funds to state, territorial, and tribal grantees. Each grantee designs its own program within federal assurances, income limits, targeting requirements, crisis deadlines, spending restrictions, reporting rules, and civil-rights obligations.

LIHEAP is not one uniform national benefit. Program names, application periods, income limits, benefit amounts, energy types, crisis definitions, local offices, and covered services vary considerably.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains LIHEAP’s origins, statutory structure, funding formula, grantee administration, eligibility, benefit calculation, heating and cooling assistance, crisis intervention, weatherization, utility coordination, tribal programs, application procedures, program integrity, performance, and current 2026 status.

It does not replace a determination by a state, tribal, territorial, or local LIHEAP agency. Applicants should use the rules and dates for their own jurisdiction and program year.

Geographic Scope

LIHEAP serves eligible households throughout the fifty states and District of Columbia.

Territorial grantees include Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Federally recognized tribes and tribal organizations may receive direct grants and operate programs for eligible households within approved service areas.

Historical Context

Federal energy assistance developed during the energy-price shocks of the 1970s.

The Arab oil embargo, rising fuel-oil and natural-gas prices, inflation, unemployment, and severe winters exposed the inability of households with low incomes to absorb rapidly increasing heating costs.

Congress initially responded with emergency appropriations and specialized energy-assistance initiatives before creating a permanent program.

The Energy Crises of the 1970s

Oil-price shocks increased heating and transportation costs, particularly in cold-weather states dependent on fuel oil.

Utilities also faced higher generation and fuel costs, which were reflected in residential rates.

Energy assistance became linked to public health because loss of heating or cooling can produce hypothermia, heat illness, fire risk, carbon-monoxide poisoning, and displacement.

Early Federal Energy Assistance

Before LIHEAP, federal energy aid was delivered through emergency programs, Community Services Administration grants, welfare systems, and special appropriations.

Program design varied from one year to another, creating uncertainty for states, local agencies, utilities, and households.

Low-Income Energy Assistance Program of 1980

Congress created the Low-Income Energy Assistance Program, commonly abbreviated LIEAP, through the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980.

LIEAP was the immediate statutory predecessor of LIHEAP and included federal energy-assistance payments administered through states.

The 1981 block-grant legislation replaced the short-lived program before a long independent administrative history developed.

Creation of LIHEAP in 1981

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 consolidated several federal grant programs and expanded state discretion.

Title XXVI established the Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Act and authorized grants beginning in fiscal year 1982.

LIHEAP represented the Reagan administration and Congress’s broader shift from categorical federal programs toward block grants.

Statutory Purpose

LIHEAP assists households with low incomes—particularly households with the lowest incomes that pay a high proportion of income for home energy—in meeting immediate home-energy needs.

The statute emphasizes safety and health rather than general energy consumption or unrestricted income replacement.

Block-Grant Structure

Congress appropriates a national amount, and HHS distributes funds according to statutory formulas and tribal allocations.

Grantees determine benefit structures and administrative systems within federal law.

The federal grant is capped. Funding does not automatically expand when every eligible household applies or when energy prices rise.

No Individual Federal Entitlement

Meeting a grantee’s eligibility conditions does not create a federal right to receive a benefit after available funds are exhausted.

States can operate limited seasons, close applications, establish priorities, and maintain waiting lists subject to their approved plans and federal assurances.

Applicants retain hearing and complaint rights concerning administration, but those protections do not convert LIHEAP into an open-ended entitlement.

Federal Administration

The Office of Community Services administers LIHEAP within the Administration for Children and Families, Department of Health and Human Services.

Federal responsibilities include:

  • awarding formula grants;
  • reviewing model plans and certifications;
  • allocating tribal shares;
  • issuing policy guidance;
  • monitoring statutory assurances and internal controls;
  • collecting household and performance data;
  • approving weatherization waivers;
  • resolving complaints;
  • providing training and technical assistance;
  • reallotting returned funds; and
  • administering emergency appropriations when enacted.

State and Territorial Administration

A governor or territorial chief executive designates the agency responsible for LIHEAP.

Administrators can include health and human-services departments, housing and community-development departments, energy offices, ageing agencies, or other institutions.

State agencies establish benefit matrices, seasons, crisis definitions, vendor procedures, local contracts, and application standards.

Local Administration

Many states delegate application and service delivery to counties, community action agencies, nonprofit organizations, municipalities, tribal agencies, or regional contractors.

Local agencies verify eligibility, calculate benefits, communicate with vendors, prevent disconnections, arrange fuel delivery, perform outreach, and maintain case records.

Community Action Agencies

Community Action Agencies administer LIHEAP in many jurisdictions.

Their local networks connect energy assistance with Community Services Block Grant services, weatherization, housing counselling, emergency aid, and poverty-reduction programs.

LIHEAP and the Community Services Block Grant remain legally and financially distinct.

Tribal LIHEAP

Federally or state-recognized tribes and tribal organizations satisfying federal requirements can receive direct LIHEAP grants.

Tribal programs define service populations and geographic areas in coordination with states to prevent gaps and duplication.

Direct funding supports tribal sovereignty and adaptation to climate, housing, fuel availability, remoteness, and cultural conditions.

Tribal Funding Shares

Tribal grant amounts are generally derived from the portion of a state allocation attributable to eligible tribal households in the approved service population.

HHS can resolve disagreements when a state and tribe cannot agree on the allocation.

Small tribal allocations can make administration and year-round service difficult.

Territorial Programs

Territories operate LIHEAP under the same federal statute but face distinct climates, utility systems, fuel imports, electricity costs, disaster exposure, and funding allocations.

Cooling assistance and disaster recovery can be especially important in tropical jurisdictions.

LIHEAP Model Plans

Each grantee submits an annual plan describing intended program operation.

Plans address:

  • income eligibility;
  • categorical eligibility;
  • heating and cooling benefits;
  • crisis assistance;
  • weatherization;
  • benefit determination;
  • outreach;
  • vendor agreements;
  • administration;
  • fair hearings;
  • program integrity;
  • public participation;
  • tribal coordination; and
  • household and performance reporting.

Public Participation

States must provide timely and meaningful public participation in development of the annual plan.

Public hearings, draft plans, written comments, advisory committees, and consultations allow households, utilities, local agencies, tribes, and advocates to identify problems.

Federal Assurances

A grantee must certify compliance with statutory assurances governing eligibility, targeting, outreach, crisis response, vendor payments, coordination, administration, hearings, fiscal control, and public participation.

HHS oversight focuses on whether the grantee operates consistently with those assurances rather than imposing one national benefit design.

Regular Block-Grant Funding

Regular block-grant funds are the principal annual LIHEAP funding source.

Congress ordinarily provides money through Labor-HHS-Education appropriations legislation.

Regular funds can support heating, cooling, crisis, weatherization, administration, outreach, and related authorized activities.

Fiscal Year 2026 Funding

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 provided $4.045 billion in regular LIHEAP budget authority.

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act provided an additional $100 million advance appropriation becoming available in fiscal year 2026.

After statutory reservations, sequestration effects where applicable, tribal allocations, and other adjustments, the LIHEAP Clearinghouse reported a total fiscal year 2026 program budget of approximately $4.131 billion.

Funding Releases During the Fiscal Year

HHS can release regular appropriations in several installments.

Initial releases allow grantees to begin winter programs, while later releases reflect final appropriations, formula calculations, and remaining balances.

In April 2026, HHS announced approximately $421.5 million in additional regular block-grant allocations.

Emergency Contingency Funds

The statute authorizes additional emergency funds for extreme weather, disasters, sharp energy-price increases, supply shortages, and other emergency conditions.

Emergency funding depends on congressional appropriations and federal release decisions; it is not guaranteed every year.

Allocation can consider weather, unemployment, energy prices, disasters, and other relevant conditions.

Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act Funding

The 2021 infrastructure law supplied $500 million in advance LIHEAP appropriations over five fiscal years.

One hundred million dollars became available in each fiscal year from 2022 through 2026.

The temporary advance appropriation did not permanently change the regular LIHEAP formula or authorization.

Federal Allocation Formulas

LIHEAP uses two principal formula systems depending on the national regular appropriation.

When appropriations are below the statutory trigger, funds are distributed under the historical formula based largely on predecessor-program shares.

When appropriations reach or exceed the trigger, the newer formula considers state residential heating and cooling expenditures, low-income households, and climate-related energy needs, subject to statutory hold-harmless protections.

The $1.975 Billion Formula Trigger

The newer formula generally applies to regular appropriations above $1.975 billion.

Statutory provisions preserve minimum shares and limit sudden losses, producing a complex distribution rather than a simple temperature or poverty formula.

Hold-Harmless Provisions

Hold-harmless rules protect states from losing too much of their historical share when the newer formula operates.

These protections promote funding stability but can preserve allocations that do not fully reflect current population, poverty, energy prices, or climate.

Funding-Formula Controversy

Cold-weather states emphasize heating demand and historical energy costs.

Warm-weather states emphasize population growth, extreme heat, cooling need, and electricity burden.

Formula reform therefore creates regional distributional conflict even when total federal funding remains unchanged.

Carryover and Reallotment

A grantee can carry forward up to 10 percent of its annual allocation into the following fiscal year.

Excess funds must be returned for federal reallotment under statutory procedures.

Carryover provides operational continuity but can also indicate delayed spending or lower-than-expected participation.

Administrative Costs

A grantee generally may use no more than 10 percent of funds payable to it for planning and administration.

Administrative costs include eligibility systems, staff, contracts, fiscal controls, hearings, data, monitoring, and management.

Direct service, outreach, intake, assurance activities, and energy-needs reduction can receive different treatment depending on their function.

Leveraging Incentive Program

Federal law authorizes incentive awards for grantees that obtain nonfederal resources benefiting households eligible for LIHEAP.

Leveraged resources can include utility contributions, fuel funds, discounts, charitable assistance, and other energy aid.

HHS stated in a 2026 regulatory action that the leveraging incentive had not been used by the Secretary in approximately a decade, although the statutory authority remained available.

Residential Energy Assistance Challenge Option

The Residential Energy Assistance Challenge Option, commonly called REACH, authorized competitive projects intended to reduce energy vulnerability and improve self-sufficiency.

Projects historically tested energy education, budgeting, arrearage reduction, conservation, and service coordination.

REACH has not been continuously funded.

Eligible Household

LIHEAP eligibility is determined for a household rather than an individual alone.

Household definitions vary by grantee but generally include people living together and sharing home-energy responsibility.

A household must meet the grantee’s financial, residence, energy-responsibility, documentation, and program-year conditions.

Federal Income Ceiling

A state ordinarily cannot set the maximum income above the greater of:

  • 150 percent of the federal poverty guidelines; or
  • 60 percent of state median income.

Grantees can choose a lower ceiling, generally not below 110 percent of poverty.

State-Selected Income Limits

States and territories select income limits within the federal range and publish household-size tables.

Some use federal poverty guidelines, some use state median income, and some apply different thresholds for heating, crisis, cooling, or weatherization assistance.

Applicants should not assume that a national percentage automatically represents their local limit.

Categorical Eligibility

A grantee may treat a household as income eligible when at least one member receives specified means-tested benefits.

Common qualifying programs include:

  • Temporary Assistance for Needy Families;
  • Supplemental Security Income;
  • Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program; and
  • specified means-tested veterans benefits.

Categorical treatment varies by grantee and does not guarantee that funds or a particular benefit remain available.

Income Definition

Each grantee defines countable household income within federal law and its approved plan.

Countable sources can include wages, self-employment, Social Security, pensions, unemployment compensation, cash assistance, child support, and regular contributions.

SNAP and WIC benefits are not ordinarily counted as household cash income.

Income-Measurement Period

Grantees can measure income over the preceding month, several months, calendar year, or another representative period.

Short measurement periods respond to recent job loss but can produce fluctuating eligibility.

Seasonal workers, self-employed households, and households with irregular income may require special calculation.

Asset Tests

Federal LIHEAP law does not require one national asset limit.

Some grantees impose tests involving cash, bank accounts, or other property, while many do not.

Exemptions and limits vary and should be verified in the current state or tribal plan.

Citizenship and Noncitizen Eligibility

LIHEAP is treated as a federal public benefit for purposes of the 1996 welfare law.

Grantees must apply federal and state rules concerning citizens, qualified noncitizens, humanitarian categories, verification, and mixed-status households.

An ineligible household member does not necessarily prevent assistance attributable to eligible members, but calculation and documentation vary.

Residency and Service Area

A household must reside in the state, territory, tribe, or local area administering the benefit.

Programs generally do not require a lengthy prior-residence period, but applicants must document current residence and energy responsibility.

Home-Energy Responsibility

The household generally must be responsible for home-energy costs directly or indirectly.

Responsibility can arise through a utility account, fuel-delivery bill, rent that includes energy, shared meter, landlord arrangement, or other approved circumstance.

Renters

Renters can qualify whether they pay utilities directly or energy costs are included in rent, subject to state methodology.

Federal law prevents categorical exclusion of renters solely because heat is included in rent.

Benefits may differ when the household does not receive an individual energy bill.

Subsidized-Housing Residents

Residents of public or subsidized housing can qualify when they bear an identifiable home-energy burden.

Utility allowances, master metering, rent calculations, and housing subsidies affect benefit design.

LIHEAP cannot duplicate assistance already fully covering the same cost.

Homeowners

Homeowners can receive bill assistance, delivered-fuel support, crisis intervention, weatherization, or minor repair under grantee rules.

Ownership does not establish eligibility without meeting income and energy-responsibility conditions.

Households Without Conventional Utility Accounts

Households using wood, propane, kerosene, fuel oil, coal, or other delivered fuels can qualify.

Programs may issue vouchers, direct vendor payments, purchase orders, or reimbursements rather than utility-account credits.

Benefit Targeting

LIHEAP does not require equal benefits for all eligible households.

Federal law requires the highest assistance to households with the lowest incomes and highest energy costs or needs relative to income, taking household size into account.

Grantees use benefit matrices, energy burden, fuel type, climate zone, household size, poverty level, vulnerability, arrearage, and crisis status.

Home-Energy Burden

Home-energy burden is the percentage of household income spent on residential energy.

Households with low incomes often face high burdens even when their absolute bills are lower than those of higher-income households.

Severe burden can force tradeoffs among energy, rent, food, medicine, transportation, and child care.

Vulnerable Households

Federal outreach assurances identify households containing:

  • people age sixty or older;
  • people with disabilities; and
  • young children.

Grantees may establish priority periods or benefit additions for these groups.

Older Adults

Older adults can face fixed incomes, medical vulnerability, inefficient homes, social isolation, and difficulty applying online or travelling to agencies.

Outreach can operate through ageing networks, home-delivered meals, senior housing, utilities, and health providers.

People with Disabilities

Disability can increase energy need through medical equipment, temperature sensitivity, home presence, mobility limitations, and reliance on electrically powered supports.

Reasonable accommodations, accessible applications, communication support, and crisis coordination are essential.

Households with Young Children

Infants and young children are particularly vulnerable to extreme cold, extreme heat, unsafe alternative heating, and displacement after utility shutoff.

LIHEAP served approximately 968,000 households containing a young child in fiscal year 2024.

Heating Assistance

Heating assistance is the most common LIHEAP benefit.

It can address electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, propane, kerosene, wood, coal, district heating, or another approved primary heating source.

Programs can operate before or during winter and may issue one-time or multiple payments.

Cooling Assistance

Cooling assistance helps households meet electricity and equipment needs during hot weather.

Benefits can include bill payments, crisis payments, fans, air conditioners, cooling equipment repair, or other measures allowed by the state plan.

Cooling programs have become increasingly important with extreme heat, population shifts, and electricity-cost growth.

Seasonal and Year-Round Programs

Some grantees operate year round, while others open separate heating and cooling seasons.

Application periods can end when appropriated funds are exhausted even if the weather season continues.

Crisis assistance may remain available after regular benefit enrollment closes.

Benefit-Calculation Methods

A grantee can calculate benefits using:

  • income percentage;
  • household size;
  • actual or estimated energy cost;
  • fuel type;
  • climate zone;
  • dwelling type;
  • energy burden;
  • vulnerability factors;
  • arrearage;
  • minimum and maximum grants; and
  • available funding.

Benefit Amounts

LIHEAP has no national minimum or maximum household benefit.

State and tribal benefit ranges vary from modest credits to several thousand dollars in exceptional crisis or delivered-fuel cases.

Benefit tables can change during the year in response to appropriations, weather, fuel prices, and participation.

Vendor Payments

Most benefits are paid directly to utilities, fuel dealers, landlords, or other energy vendors.

Direct payment protects the energy purpose of the grant and supports rapid crisis resolution.

Some grantees use two-party checks, reimbursement, vouchers, or household payments under controlled circumstances.

Vendor Agreements

Vendors receiving LIHEAP payments must comply with state agreements and statutory protections.

Agreements commonly address:

  • timely account credit;
  • equal price and service treatment;
  • restoration of service;
  • refund of account credits;
  • customer confidentiality;
  • disconnection status;
  • payment posting;
  • records and audits; and
  • return of unused funds.

Energy-Crisis Assistance

Crisis assistance responds to immediate or imminent loss of home energy or another dangerous energy condition.

Each grantee defines crisis within federal law.

Common examples include disconnection, shutoff notice, empty fuel tank, inoperable heating or cooling equipment, unsafe heating, eviction for energy nonpayment, or a medical emergency involving temperature or electricity.

Crisis Response Deadlines

A grantee must provide an intervention resolving an eligible crisis within:

  • forty-eight hours after a completed application for an ordinary crisis; and
  • eighteen hours for a life-threatening crisis.

The requirement concerns effective intervention, which may be a payment commitment, fuel delivery, reconnection, repair, or another action—not necessarily completion of every administrative step.

Life-Threatening Energy Crisis

A life-threatening crisis involves conditions posing a serious danger to health or life.

Examples can involve extreme temperature, medically necessary electricity, an infant or medically fragile person, carbon-monoxide risk, or complete absence of safe heat.

State definitions and documentation requirements apply.

Utility Disconnection

LIHEAP can prevent a scheduled disconnection or restore service after shutoff.

Assistance may require a utility pledge, customer payment, payment arrangement, arrearage plan, or coordination with charitable funds.

LIHEAP does not create a universal federal prohibition on utility disconnection.

Delivered-Fuel Emergencies

Fuel-oil, propane, kerosene, wood, and coal households can face minimum-delivery amounts and lack of service when they cannot prepay.

Crisis agencies can authorize delivery, negotiate minimums, arrange emergency fuel, or issue vendor guarantees.

Heating and Cooling Equipment Repair

LIHEAP can support minor energy-related home repair when necessary to resolve an energy crisis or make weatherization effective.

Common services include furnace repair, boiler repair, thermostat replacement, cooling-system repair, electrical correction, venting, and safety testing.

Major rehabilitation generally falls outside ordinary LIHEAP authority.

Temporary Shelter and Relocation

Some grantees use crisis funds for temporary shelter when a home is unsafe because of energy loss and no immediate repair or reconnection is possible.

Such use must be authorized by the state plan and connected to the home-energy emergency.

LIHEAP Weatherization

Grantees may use LIHEAP funds for low-cost residential weatherization or other energy-related home repair.

Measures reduce energy consumption, improve safety, lower future bills, and extend the value of bill assistance.

LIHEAP weatherization can be delivered through the same agencies operating the Department of Energy Weatherization Assistance Program.

Weatherization Spending Limit

A grantee ordinarily may use no more than 15 percent of LIHEAP funds for weatherization.

HHS may approve a waiver increasing the share to as much as 25 percent when the grantee demonstrates that the higher expenditure is reasonable and will not impair crisis and bill assistance.

Common Weatherization Measures

Measures can include:

  • air sealing;
  • insulation;
  • duct sealing;
  • heating-system tune-up or repair;
  • cooling-efficiency measures;
  • water-heating conservation;
  • thermostats;
  • health and safety corrections;
  • minor roof or moisture repair necessary to protect measures;
  • energy education; and
  • replacement of unsafe or inefficient equipment where authorized.

LIHEAP and the Weatherization Assistance Program

The Department of Energy Weatherization Assistance Program is a separate federal program with separate funding, regulations, technical standards, audits, and eligibility rules.

States can coordinate or braid LIHEAP and Weatherization Assistance Program resources but must account for each funding source correctly.

Energy-Needs Reduction Activities

LIHEAP funds can support services encouraging households to reduce energy need and become less vulnerable.

Activities can include budget counselling, energy education, utility-rate assistance, arrearage management, referrals, conservation kits, and coordination with efficiency programs.

Energy Education

Energy education provides information about thermostat use, ventilation, appliance safety, utility rates, billing, conservation, payment arrangements, and available assistance.

Education should not imply that behaviour alone can overcome unaffordable rates, inefficient housing, or inadequate income.

Arrearage Management

Utilities and grantees may combine LIHEAP with arrearage-management programs that forgive part of past debt when the customer makes current payments.

LIHEAP funds must be used consistently with the state plan and cannot automatically finance unrelated utility debt.

Percentage-of-Income Payment Plans

Some states or utilities operate plans capping customer energy payments at a percentage of income.

LIHEAP can coordinate with these plans, but they are generally established by state law, utility regulation, or separate funding rather than federal LIHEAP law alone.

Utility Shutoff Moratoria

States and utility commissions may restrict disconnection during extreme cold, extreme heat, medical emergencies, or winter periods.

Moratoria vary by utility type and jurisdiction.

A moratorium delays shutoff but usually does not erase the bill, and debt can continue to accumulate.

Public Utility Commissions

State utility commissions regulate many investor-owned utilities and can establish disconnection, payment-plan, arrearage, discount, and consumer-protection rules.

Municipal utilities, cooperatives, propane dealers, fuel-oil companies, and landlords may be governed differently.

LIHEAP agencies coordinate with regulators but do not replace them.

Application Process

Households apply through the state, tribal, territorial, county, or local agency responsible for their area.

Applications may be accepted online, by mail, by telephone, in person, through outreach workers, or through authorized community organizations.

Common Application Documents

A grantee may request:

  • identity documents;
  • Social Security numbers or other identifiers where authorized;
  • proof of residence;
  • household-member information;
  • income evidence;
  • proof of categorical benefits;
  • utility bills or fuel statements;
  • shutoff or crisis notices;
  • landlord statements;
  • medical or vulnerability documentation;
  • citizenship or immigration documentation where required; and
  • authorization to contact vendors.

Application Seasons and Priority Periods

Some programs open first to older adults, people with disabilities, or households with young children.

General enrollment may open later and continue until a stated date or exhaustion of funds.

Crisis applications can operate on a different calendar.

Completed Application

Crisis deadlines generally begin after an application is complete under the grantee’s rules.

Missing income evidence, vendor information, signatures, or required verification can delay the deadline.

Agencies should identify missing items promptly and provide reasonable assistance to applicants.

Eligibility Verification

Agencies can verify information through documents, wage databases, benefit systems, utility records, landlords, employers, and other lawful sources.

Data matching should be accurate, timely, and accompanied by notice and an opportunity to correct conflicting information.

Priority and Waiting Lists

Grantees can prioritize vulnerable or high-burden households when funds are limited.

An eligible applicant can receive a waiting-list placement, reduced benefit, or no regular benefit when funds are exhausted.

Crisis cases may remain eligible for separate intervention.

Eligibility and Benefit Notices

Applicants should receive written notice of approval, denial, benefit amount, vendor payment, reason, and appeal rights.

Notice quality is essential because a utility credit may appear weeks after approval and may not equal the full outstanding bill.

Fair Hearings

Grantees must provide an opportunity for a fair administrative hearing when assistance is denied, delayed, reduced, or not acted upon with reasonable promptness.

Procedures should include notice, evidence review, representation, an impartial decision-maker, and a written determination.

Federal Complaints

Individuals and organizations may submit complaints to HHS alleging a grantee’s substantial or serious failure to comply with statutory assurances.

Federal complaint review does not replace ordinary state fair-hearing procedures for an individual benefit dispute.

Outreach

Grantees must conduct outreach designed to ensure that eligible households—especially vulnerable households—are aware of assistance.

Outreach can involve utilities, health providers, ageing agencies, disability organizations, schools, housing agencies, tribal programs, faith organizations, shelters, and community action networks.

Language Access

Programs receiving federal funds must provide meaningful access for applicants with limited English proficiency.

Translated applications, interpreters, multilingual notices, accessible call centres, and community partnerships support accurate and safe service.

Disability Access

Agencies must provide reasonable modifications, accessible facilities, effective communication, and application assistance under federal disability law.

Home visits, telephone applications, authorised representatives, and alternative document formats can reduce barriers.

Civil Rights and Nondiscrimination

LIHEAP administration is subject to federal civil-rights laws and program-specific nondiscrimination requirements.

Grantees and local agencies must avoid unlawful discrimination based on race, colour, national origin, disability, age, sex, religion where applicable, and other protected status.

Treatment of LIHEAP Benefits by Other Programs

Federal law generally excludes LIHEAP payments from income and resources when determining eligibility or benefit amounts under federal and federally assisted means-tested programs.

This protection prevents energy assistance from reducing SNAP, SSI, housing, or other aid merely because a household received LIHEAP.

Relationship to SNAP

SNAP receipt can establish categorical LIHEAP eligibility in participating jurisdictions.

LIHEAP benefits are excluded from SNAP income.

A verified LIHEAP payment can affect the SNAP heating-and-cooling standard utility allowance under separate SNAP rules.

Relationship to Supplemental Security Income

SSI receipt can establish categorical LIHEAP eligibility under a grantee’s plan.

LIHEAP payments are excluded from SSI income and resources.

Households containing SSI recipients are often prioritised because of disability or age.

Relationship to Temporary Assistance for Needy Families

TANF receipt can establish categorical LIHEAP eligibility under state policy.

TANF agencies can refer families to LIHEAP and can sometimes use TANF or state emergency funds to supplement energy assistance.

The programs have separate federal financing and eligibility rules.

Relationship to Housing Programs

Public housing and rental-assistance programs use utility allowances and reimbursements to account for tenant-paid energy.

LIHEAP can supplement but should not duplicate fully reimbursed costs.

Housing authorities, landlords, and LIHEAP agencies can coordinate on master-metered buildings and emergency shutoffs.

Relationship to Federal Weatherization

Department of Energy weatherization funds support comprehensive efficiency improvements.

LIHEAP weatherization funds can extend services, resolve health and safety barriers, or support additional households.

Separate accounting and compliance remain required.

Relationship to Water Assistance

LIHEAP is principally an energy program and does not establish a permanent national water-assistance benefit.

Congress temporarily funded the Low Income Household Water Assistance Program during the COVID-19 period through a separate HHS grant.

LIHEAP funds can address water only when directly related to an authorized home-energy purpose, such as heating fuel or an approved crisis repair.

Charitable Fuel Funds

Utilities, nonprofit organizations, churches, and community foundations operate private fuel funds.

These funds can supplement LIHEAP, address gaps, or help households above public-program income limits.

Charitable assistance is not a substitute for federal block-grant funding and can be geographically uneven.

Energy Poverty

Energy poverty describes inability to secure sufficient, safe, reliable, and affordable household energy.

It can involve unaffordable bills, shutoff, underheating, undercooling, reliance on unsafe devices, debt, or displacement.

LIHEAP addresses immediate symptoms but cannot alone correct low wages, high rents, inefficient housing, utility-rate design, or energy-market volatility.

Household Tradeoffs

Households with high energy burdens may reduce food, medicine, medical care, transportation, or rent payments to maintain utility service.

The phrase “heat or eat” describes this forced tradeoff but can also involve cooling, electricity, water heating, and medical equipment.

Health Effects of Energy Insecurity

Energy insecurity is associated with respiratory illness, cardiovascular stress, heat illness, hypothermia, unsafe heating, carbon-monoxide exposure, medication disruption, food spoilage, and mental distress.

Children, older adults, and people with chronic conditions face elevated risks.

Housing Stability

Utility arrears and shutoff can contribute to eviction, homelessness, unsafe doubling up, or child-welfare involvement.

Crisis assistance can preserve housing when utility service is required by a lease or local habitability law.

Extreme Cold

Severe cold increases heating demand, fuel consumption, system failures, and crisis applications.

Emergency appropriations and state reserves can support response, but the capped grant does not automatically increase with heating degree days.

Extreme Heat

Heat waves increase electricity costs and threaten people without effective cooling.

Cooling assistance, air-conditioner repair, crisis benefits, and outreach can prevent heat illness and death.

Not all states operate regular cooling programs, and funding formulas historically emphasised heating need.

Climate Change and Energy Assistance

More frequent extreme heat, severe storms, wildfires, and weather variability can increase energy burden and service disruptions.

LIHEAP can respond through cooling benefits, crisis intervention, emergency releases, and weatherization, but its annual appropriations and historical formula limit automatic adaptation.

Natural Disasters

Hurricanes, floods, wildfires, winter storms, earthquakes, and other disasters can destroy equipment, interrupt power, displace households, and raise fuel prices.

HHS can release emergency funds when appropriated, and grantees can modify plans or use crisis authority.

Federal Emergency Management Agency assistance and LIHEAP must be coordinated to avoid duplication.

COVID-19 Pandemic

Pandemic unemployment, arrears, illness, utility shutoff moratoria, and remote-service needs increased energy-assistance pressure.

Congress provided supplemental LIHEAP funding through pandemic relief legislation, and agencies expanded remote applications and vendor coordination.

Moratoria delayed disconnections but left substantial arrears when protections expired.

American Rescue Plan Funding

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 appropriated approximately $4.5 billion in supplemental LIHEAP funding.

The funds helped address pandemic-related arrears, household hardship, and energy emergencies.

Supplemental pandemic funding was temporary and did not permanently increase the annual block grant.

Performance Management

HHS and grantees collect data intended to connect LIHEAP benefits with energy burden, service restoration, crisis prevention, and household characteristics.

Performance measures include benefit targeting, energy burden reduction, restoration of service, prevention of loss, and vulnerable-household reach.

Household Reports

Grantees report households receiving heating, cooling, crisis, weatherization, and other assistance.

Reports distinguish households from individuals and attempt to prevent double counting among benefit types.

National totals should be interpreted carefully because a household can receive more than one service.

Fiscal Year 2024 Vulnerable-Household Data

HHS reported that households served in fiscal year 2024 included approximately:

  • 2.1 million households containing a person with a disability;
  • 2.5 million households containing an older adult; and
  • 968,000 households containing a young child.

Categories overlap because one household can contain members of more than one vulnerable group.

Benefit-Targeting Measures

Federal performance analysis compares benefits received by high-burden households with benefits received by all assisted households.

The purpose is to determine whether grantees direct larger benefits toward households facing the greatest energy need relative to income.

Energy-Burden Reduction

Energy-burden reduction measures the extent to which LIHEAP benefits lower the share of household income devoted to home energy.

A one-time payment can reduce immediate burden without making future energy costs affordable.

Weatherization and rate assistance can produce longer-lasting reductions.

Restoration and Loss Prevention

Crisis-performance measures track restoration of home-energy service and prevention of imminent loss.

Effective intervention can include reconnection, fuel delivery, repair, payment pledge, or negotiated suspension of shutoff.

Program Integrity

Program integrity includes accurate eligibility, proper benefit calculation, vendor controls, fiscal accounting, data security, prevention of duplicate assistance, fair hearings, and correction of underpayments and improper denials.

It is broader than fraud enforcement.

Improper Payments

Improper payments can result from ineligible households, inaccurate income, duplicate benefits, incorrect vendor accounts, payment after household relocation, or unsupported crisis claims.

Underpayments and delayed crisis action can also represent administrative failure.

Fraud and Abuse

Intentional violations can include false household composition, concealed income, falsified shutoff notices, duplicate applications, vendor collusion, diversion of delivered fuel, or misuse of agency funds.

Remedies include recovery, disqualification, contract termination, civil action, and criminal prosecution.

Error, misunderstanding, and fraud should not be treated as interchangeable.

Vendor Integrity

Vendor risks include failure to credit accounts, inflated prices, false delivery, duplicate billing, misuse of customer data, and retention of unused balances.

Grantees use contracts, transaction review, customer confirmation, audits, and sanctions.

Audits and Monitoring

LIHEAP grantees and subrecipients are subject to federal grant requirements, Single Audits, HHS monitoring, state audits, internal controls, and records retention.

Federal monitoring reviews state-plan compliance, crisis timeliness, eligibility, weatherization, vendors, fiscal management, and data quality.

The 2026 Regulatory-Cleanup Rule

HHS published a final rule on June 9, 2026, removing several obsolete or unnecessary block-grant regulations.

The rule removes 45 C.F.R. 96.80, which merely stated the scope of the LIHEAP subpart, and 45 C.F.R. 96.87, which contained procedures for the leveraging incentive.

HHS stated that the underlying statutory authority remains and program operations are not changed.

The rule is scheduled to take effect August 10, 2026, and therefore was published but not yet effective on August 2, 2026.

Federalism and State Variation

LIHEAP reflects cooperative federalism with unusually broad grantee discretion.

Flexibility permits adaptation to heating fuels, climate, utility systems, rural geography, and local administration.

It also produces unequal income limits, benefit amounts, seasons, crisis definitions, and service availability among jurisdictions.

Regional Equity

Heating-dominated and cooling-dominated regions face different energy needs.

Rural regions can depend on expensive delivered fuels, while urban households may face high electricity rates and old multifamily housing.

Territorial and tribal communities can face isolated grids, high transport costs, and limited vendor competition.

Race and Energy Inequality

Historical housing segregation, disinvestment, inefficient housing, income inequality, and utility burden disproportionately affect many Black, Latino, Native, and other underserved communities.

Equal administration requires language access, nondiscriminatory utility coordination, geographic outreach, transparent benefit formulas, and analysis of approval and denial patterns.

Rural Households

Rural households may rely on propane, fuel oil, wood, or electricity and can face long delivery distances, minimum fuel orders, limited contractors, and scarce application sites.

Local community action and tribal networks are central to effective service.

Manufactured Housing

Manufactured homes can have high heating and cooling needs because of insulation, air leakage, duct conditions, skirting, equipment, and exposure.

Residents can face restrictions on structural weatherization when they do not own the land or home.

Multifamily Housing

Multifamily residents can have individual utility accounts, shared systems, master metering, or heat included in rent.

Benefit and weatherization design must address landlord ownership, tenant contribution, building-wide savings, and protection against rent increases.

Energy Efficiency and Equity

Households with low incomes often rent inefficient homes and lack capital to purchase insulation, appliances, or heating systems.

Weatherization can reduce bills, but limited funding, health and safety deferrals, landlord permission, and workforce shortages restrict access.

Weatherization Deferrals

Weatherization work can be deferred when a home has major roof failure, structural damage, mould, asbestos, electrical hazards, pests, or other conditions preventing safe installation.

LIHEAP crisis or minor-repair funds can sometimes resolve barriers, but not every home can be repaired within program limits.

Major Achievements

  • Created a permanent federal framework for household energy assistance.
  • Supports heating and cooling across diverse climates and fuel systems.
  • Requires rapid response to life-threatening energy crises.
  • Targets high benefits toward households with low income and high energy need.
  • Prioritises older adults, people with disabilities, and young children.
  • Supports tribal and territorial administration.
  • Coordinates public benefits with utilities and fuel vendors.
  • Funds weatherization and minor energy-related repair.
  • Expands during major emergencies when Congress provides supplemental funding.
  • Excludes benefits from income in other federal means-tested programs.

Limitations and Failures

  • LIHEAP is not an individual entitlement.
  • Annual funding reaches only a portion of income-eligible households.
  • The block grant does not automatically rise with prices, weather, or applications.
  • Eligibility and benefits vary widely by residence.
  • Programs can close before the heating or cooling season ends.
  • One-time payments may not resolve chronic unaffordability.
  • Weatherization funding is limited relative to housing need.
  • Formula rules reflect historical allocations and regional compromises.
  • Applicants face documentation, scheduling, technology, and transportation barriers.
  • Utility moratoria can allow arrears to accumulate.
  • Delivered-fuel households can face minimum-order and vendor shortages.
  • LIHEAP cannot correct inadequate income, unsafe housing, or high energy prices by itself.

Major Controversies

Block grant versus entitlement
A capped grant gives states flexibility but leaves many eligible households unserved.
Heating versus cooling
Historical funding patterns favour heating need while extreme heat creates growing demand.
Formula reform
Changes benefiting fast-growing warm states can reduce shares for cold states.
Bill assistance versus weatherization
Immediate payments prevent shutoff, while efficiency investment can reduce future need.
Universal utility protections
LIHEAP can prevent individual shutoffs but does not establish a national service guarantee.
Benefit adequacy
A one-time grant can be small relative to arrears or annual energy burden.
State discretion
Local adaptation creates unequal treatment of similar households.
Program elimination proposals
Supporters of elimination emphasise state utility protections and energy-price policy; opponents emphasise health, safety, and the absence of comparable national aid.

How LIHEAP Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the fiscal year, grantee, benefit type, energy source, season, crisis status, and funding source.

Important distinctions include:

  • regular assistance versus crisis assistance;
  • heating versus cooling;
  • bill payment versus weatherization;
  • federal eligibility ceilings versus state-selected limits;
  • households served versus benefit transactions;
  • regular block grants versus emergency appropriations;
  • LIHEAP weatherization versus Department of Energy weatherization;
  • utility moratoria versus payment assistance;
  • state programs versus direct tribal programs; and
  • enacted appropriations versus presidential or congressional proposals.

Why the Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program Matters

Residential energy is essential to health, safety, food storage, sanitation, communication, medical equipment, education, and housing stability.

LIHEAP is the principal federal program devoted specifically to helping households with low incomes afford heating and cooling and survive energy emergencies.

Its block-grant design allows states, territories, and tribes to adapt to climate and fuel markets. The same flexibility creates large geographic differences and leaves many eligible households without assistance.

LIHEAP works most effectively when combined with utility protections, arrearage management, housing assistance, income support, weatherization, and safe affordable housing.

The program’s central policy question is whether annual capped appropriations can keep pace with energy prices, extreme weather, ageing housing, and the number of households facing severe energy burden.

Summary

The Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program is the principal federal program helping households with low incomes pay heating and cooling costs and resolve energy emergencies.

Congress created LIHEAP through the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981. The program replaced the 1980 Low-Income Energy Assistance Program and consolidated earlier emergency energy aid into a flexible block grant.

HHS distributes capped annual grants to states, territories, tribes, and tribal organizations. Grantees determine income limits, benefit levels, seasons, crisis rules, and delivery systems within federal assurances.

LIHEAP can fund regular heating and cooling assistance, crisis intervention, weatherization, minor energy-related repair, outreach, and energy-needs reduction. Crisis intervention must occur within forty-eight hours, or eighteen hours in life-threatening cases.

Federal law requires benefits to target households with the lowest income and highest energy cost relative to income and emphasises households containing older adults, people with disabilities, and young children.

LIHEAP is not an entitlement. Funds can be exhausted before all eligible households receive assistance.

Fiscal year 2026 included a $4.045 billion regular appropriation and $100 million in temporary infrastructure-law funding, producing an approximately $4.131 billion program budget after federal adjustments.

As of August 2, 2026, LIHEAP remained active. A federal regulatory-cleanup rule had been published but was not scheduled to take effect until August 10, and a proposal to eliminate fiscal year 2027 funding had not been enacted.
