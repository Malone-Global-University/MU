The federal community-development tax incentive that attracts private investment into economically distressed communities through certified Community Development Entities.

What Is the New Markets Tax Credit Program?

The New Markets Tax Credit Program is a federal tax incentive intended to channel private capital into low-income communities.

Treasury does not ordinarily award the tax credit directly to a business seeking a loan. The CDFI Fund first allocates tax-credit authority to certified Community Development Entities.

A CDE raises private equity from an investor and designates that equity as a Qualified Equity Investment. The investor claims the federal credit over seven years.

The CDE then uses the capital to make qualified loans or equity investments in businesses, real-estate projects, and community facilities serving eligible low-income communities.

The credit compensates the investor for providing long-term capital under conditions that often produce lower interest rates, subordinate debt, longer amortisation, reduced collateral requirements, interest-only periods, equity-like financing, or other flexible terms.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the programme’s origins, statutory structure, permanent authorization, CDE certification, allocation competition, Qualified Equity Investments, credit calculation, low-income community rules, targeted populations, project eligibility, Qualified Low-Income Community Investments, leveraged financing, compliance, recapture, fees, outcomes, racial and geographic significance, criticisms, and current policy conditions through August 2, 2026.

It does not provide legal, tax, accounting, underwriting, or investment advice. NMTC transactions require specialised review of Section 45D, Treasury regulations, IRS guidance, allocation agreements, project documents, state law, accounting treatment, and current CDFI Fund policy.

A business seeking financing does not apply to the CDFI Fund for an allocation. It must approach an allocatee or another financing intermediary with available allocation authority.

Historical Context

Many low-income urban and rural communities experienced sustained capital flight during the twentieth century.

Redlining, racial segregation, deindustrialisation, suburbanisation, bank consolidation, discriminatory lending, public disinvestment, population loss, environmental hazards, and declining property values reduced conventional investment.

Businesses in distressed communities often faced higher financing costs, weaker collateral, lower appraisals, limited local bank competition, and difficulty attracting equity.

Federal policy responded through urban renewal, enterprise zones, Community Development Block Grants, Community Reinvestment Act obligations, Small Business Administration programmes, Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, Empowerment Zones, CDFIs, and other place-based tools.

NMTC emerged as a national tax-based mechanism designed to attract private capital without creating a direct federal loan programme for every project.

Policy Origins

The programme developed during the Clinton administration from proposals for “new markets” investment in communities and populations excluded from ordinary capital markets.

The policy reflected the idea that distressed communities contained unmet demand, underused buildings, workers, entrepreneurs, and public assets but lacked risk-tolerant investment.

Supporters sought to connect Wall Street and institutional investors to community-based financial intermediaries that understood local needs.

The original proposal combined market incentives with local accountability rather than relying solely on federal project selection.

Creation in 2000

Congress enacted the New Markets Tax Credit as part of the Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000, included in Public Law 106-554.

The law added Section 45D to the Internal Revenue Code and authorised a limited amount of investment authority for early calendar years.

Treasury assigned the CDFI Fund responsibility for certifying CDEs, conducting allocation competitions, executing allocation agreements, collecting transaction data, and monitoring award compliance.

The IRS issued regulations defining the tax credit, qualified investments, qualifying businesses, low-income communities, credit allowance dates, substantially-all tests, and recapture.

Temporary Extensions Before Permanence

NMTC was originally temporary and repeatedly required congressional extension.

Congress renewed the programme through tax legislation enacted under both Republican and Democratic administrations.

Temporary authorization created uncertainty for CDEs, investors, developers, businesses, and communities because allocation competitions depend on multiyear planning.

Congress also authorised special increases after Hurricane Katrina and during the Great Recession.

The Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021 authorised $5 billion annually through calendar year 2025 before permanent legislation was enacted.

Permanent Authorization in 2025

Public Law 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025, permanently extended the New Markets Tax Credit.

Section 70423 amended Section 45D so the $5 billion annual allocation applies to each calendar year after 2019 rather than ending after 2025.

The law also permits unused annual allocation authority to be carried forward for up to five calendar years.

Unused authority associated with years before 2026 is treated as arising in 2025 for the new carryover limitation.

Permanent authorization provides long-term certainty but does not guarantee that a particular CDE or project will receive allocation or investment.

Role of the CDFI Fund

The Community Development Financial Institutions Fund is a Treasury bureau created in 1994.

For NMTC, the Fund:

  • certifies Community Development Entities;
  • publishes Notices of Allocation Availability;
  • receives and evaluates competitive applications;
  • selects allocatees;
  • executes allocation agreements;
  • tracks Qualified Equity Investments;
  • collects project and transaction data;
  • reviews annual compliance reports;
  • monitors service-area and deployment commitments;
  • enforces allocation-agreement remedies; and
  • publishes award and investment information.

The Fund does not underwrite every project directly and does not determine the investor’s final federal tax liability.

Role of the Internal Revenue Service

The Internal Revenue Service administers Section 45D as part of the federal income-tax system.

The IRS:

  • issues regulations and tax guidance;
  • defines credit calculation and recapture;
  • reviews investor tax returns;
  • audits CDE and investor compliance;
  • receives Form 8874 claims;
  • uses Forms 8874-A and 8874-B for investment and recapture notices;
  • addresses basis adjustment and general-business-credit treatment; and
  • can impose tax, interest, or other consequences after noncompliance.

CDFI Fund contractual remedies and IRS tax recapture are related but distinct enforcement systems.

Community Development Entity

A Community Development Entity is a domestic corporation or partnership certified by the CDFI Fund.

The entity must:

  • have a primary mission of serving or providing investment capital for low-income communities or low-income persons;
  • maintain accountability to residents of low-income communities through representation on a governing or advisory board; and
  • be a legal entity at the time of certification.

Certified CDFIs and certain subsidiaries can qualify automatically or through streamlined treatment, but CDE and CDFI are not identical classifications.

Banks, nonprofit loan funds, municipal entities, development corporations, national organisations, tribal organisations, and specialised affiliates can establish CDEs.

For-Profit and Nonprofit CDEs

Both for-profit and nonprofit entities can become certified CDEs and compete for an allocation.

Only a for-profit entity can issue the equity interest that carries the tax credit to an investor.

A nonprofit allocatee therefore commonly suballocates authority to one or more controlled or affiliated for-profit subsidiary CDEs.

The nonprofit sponsor can retain mission control while the for-profit subsidiary executes tax-credit transactions.

Community Accountability

CDE certification requires accountability to residents of low-income communities.

Accountability is usually demonstrated through low-income community representatives serving on a governing board or advisory board.

Representatives can include residents, business owners, nonprofit leaders, service providers, tribal members, workers, borrowers, or other people connected to the service area.

The requirement is intended to give affected communities a voice in strategy and project selection.

Critics note that advisory representation can become formal rather than controlling when investors, national institutions, or professional managers retain decisive authority.

Competitive Allocation Application

CDE certification does not itself provide tax-credit authority.

A certified CDE competes in an allocation round by submitting a detailed application.

Applications commonly address:

  • business strategy;
  • community outcomes;
  • market need;
  • products and better-than-market terms;
  • management capacity;
  • investor commitments;
  • project pipeline;
  • service area;
  • deployment schedule;
  • fees;
  • track record;
  • rural, tribal, or deeply distressed investment;
  • community accountability; and
  • risk management and compliance.

Allocation Review and Selection

The CDFI Fund uses trained external reviewers and federal staff to score and evaluate applications under the published Notice of Allocation Availability.

Applicants must reach minimum quality thresholds before receiving an award.

Treasury can consider geographic diversity, rural commitments, Native investment, prior performance, deployment, investor readiness, and other programme objectives.

The requested allocation amount often substantially exceeds the amount available, making the programme competitive even among experienced CDEs.

An award announcement does not mean the allocatee has received cash or completed a project. The CDE must close its allocation agreement, raise equity, designate QEIs, and deploy capital.

Allocation Agreement

The allocation agreement is the contract between the CDFI Fund and the allocatee.

It establishes:

  • the amount of allocation authority;
  • service area;
  • investment products;
  • business and real-estate limits;
  • rural and deeply distressed commitments;
  • Native investment commitments;
  • better-than-market terms;
  • deployment deadlines;
  • fees;
  • reporting;
  • community outcomes;
  • compliance covenants;
  • events of default; and
  • CDFI Fund remedies.

The agreement can impose requirements more demanding than the minimum tax regulations.

Allocation Authority Is Not the Tax Credit Amount

A $100 million allocation permits a CDE to designate up to $100 million in eligible Qualified Equity Investments.

It does not mean Treasury gives the CDE $100 million or that investors receive $100 million in credits.

Investors receive credits totalling 39 percent of the actual eligible equity invested.

If a CDE designates $100 million in QEIs, investors can receive up to $39 million in federal credits over seven years, assuming continuing compliance.

The investment principal remains part of the financing structure and is separate from the credit.

Qualified Equity Investment

A Qualified Equity Investment is generally an equity investment in a CDE that:

  • is acquired at original issue directly or through an underwriter;
  • is made solely in exchange for cash;
  • is formally designated as a QEI by the CDE;
  • does not exceed the CDE’s available allocation authority; and
  • supports the CDE’s qualifying low-income community investments.

Corporate stock and partnership capital interests can qualify.

The CDE provides the original investor notice on Form 8874-A, generally within sixty days after the investment.

The first allowance date is the investment date, followed by each of the six anniversaries.

An investor uses Form 8874 and the general-business-credit rules to claim the credit.

Investor Basis Adjustment

Section 45D requires the investor to reduce the tax basis in the Qualified Equity Investment by the amount of NMTC claimed.

Over the seven-year period, the basis can be reduced by the full 39-percent credit amount.

Basis affects gain, loss, and tax treatment when the investment is sold, redeemed, or otherwise disposed of.

Transaction documents must account for credit claims, basis, partnership allocations, exit rights, and possible recapture.

Basic Flow of Funds

  1. The CDFI Fund awards allocation authority to a CDE.
  2. The CDE closes an allocation agreement.
  3. An investor contributes cash equity to the CDE or a subsidiary investment fund.
  4. The CDE designates the investment as a QEI.
  5. The investor begins claiming the seven-year tax credit.
  6. The CDE deploys the proceeds into one or more QLICIs.
  7. A qualifying business receives loans or equity with flexible terms.
  8. The CDE monitors the project and programme compliance.
  9. After the seven-year period, the transaction exits or restructures according to negotiated documents and tax law.

Leveraged Financing Structure

Many NMTC transactions use a leveraged investment fund.

A typical structure combines:

  • tax-credit investor equity;
  • a leverage loan from a project sponsor, lender, government programme, foundation, or other source;
  • an investment fund;
  • one or more subsidiary CDEs; and
  • parallel loans to a qualified project business.

The investment fund combines investor equity and borrowed funds to make the QEI. The CDE then makes QLICI loans, often structured as senior and subordinate debt.

This structure increases the QEI base and the total project capital but creates legal, accounting, tax, intercreditor, and unwinding complexity.

How the Project Receives the Economic Benefit

The project does not ordinarily claim the tax credit.

The investor pays for expected tax credits and other investment benefits. That equity helps the CDE provide favourable financing to the project.

Project benefits can include:

  • below-market interest rates;
  • longer maturity;
  • interest-only periods;
  • subordinate debt;
  • reduced collateral requirements;
  • higher loan-to-value ratios;
  • patient equity;
  • lower origination cost;
  • forgivable or deeply discounted exit debt; and
  • financing unavailable from conventional lenders.

Fees, reserves, investor pricing, debt service, and transaction expenses reduce the gross economic subsidy.

Qualified Low-Income Community Investment

Section 45D recognises four principal types of QLICI:

  1. a capital or equity investment in, or loan to, a Qualified Active Low-Income Community Business;
  2. purchase from another CDE of a qualifying loan;
  3. financial counselling and other services to qualifying businesses or low-income community residents; and
  4. an equity investment in, or loan to, another CDE under applicable rules.

Most programme dollars finance loans to project businesses.

A CDE generally must deploy the investor’s cash into QLICIs within twelve months.

Substantially-All Requirement

In general, at least 85 percent of QEI proceeds must remain invested in QLICIs.

Compliance is tested under direct-tracing or safe-harbour methods prescribed by regulation.

The threshold generally falls to 75 percent in the seventh year.

A limited reserve for loan loss or future investments can count under applicable rules.

Failure can trigger recapture unless the CDE corrects the deficiency within the authorised cure period. Only one substantially-all cure is generally permitted per QEI during the credit period.

Reinvestment of Loan Repayments

Principal repaid to a CDE during the compliance period can create a need to reinvest the funds.

Under applicable rules, principal received during a calendar year can remain treated as invested when the CDE reinvests it in a QLICI by the end of the following calendar year.

Interest, fees, and principal receive different treatment.

CDEs must track cash, loan balances, repayments, reserves, reinvestments, and testing dates carefully to protect investors from recapture.

Low-Income Community

The ordinary geographic rule uses population census tracts.

A tract generally qualifies when:

  • at least 20 percent of residents live in poverty;
  • the tract is nonmetropolitan and median family income does not exceed 80 percent of statewide median family income; or
  • the tract is metropolitan and median family income does not exceed 80 percent of the greater of statewide or metropolitan-area median family income.

Special rules address possessions, low-population tracts, non-tract areas, targeted populations, and statutory disaster zones.

The CDFI Fund publishes eligibility data and mapping tools using applicable census datasets.

Minimum Eligibility and Deeper Distress

A census tract can satisfy the tax-code definition while differing greatly from another eligible tract in unemployment, poverty, income, population loss, vacancy, food access, health outcomes, and credit availability.

Allocation agreements commonly require a large share of investments to occur in areas with greater distress than the minimum legal threshold.

Measures can include:

  • poverty significantly above 20 percent;
  • median family income substantially below 80 percent;
  • high unemployment;
  • federal disaster designation;
  • food desert status;
  • medically underserved status;
  • brownfields;
  • Native areas;
  • rural counties;
  • population loss; and
  • other indicia of severe disinvestment.

Targeted Populations

Congress authorised selected targeted populations to be treated as low-income communities.

A qualifying business can satisfy standards based on transactions with low-income persons, low-income employees, or low-income ownership.

Common safe-harbour standards include:

  • at least 50 percent of gross income from transactions with low-income persons;
  • at least 40 percent of employees being low-income persons at hiring; or
  • at least 50 percent ownership by low-income persons.

Special rules apply to targeted-population businesses located in higher-income tracts and to Native populations.

Qualified Active Low-Income Community Business

A Qualified Active Low-Income Community Business, or QALICB, is the entity that receives most NMTC project financing.

It can be a corporation, partnership, nonprofit corporation, or qualifying sole proprietorship.

The business must conduct qualifying activity in an eligible community or satisfy targeted-population rules.

A project sponsor frequently creates a special-purpose QALICB to own and operate a facility financed by the CDE.

The CDE must reasonably expect the business to satisfy applicable requirements during the investment period.

QALICB Location and Activity Tests

Under the ordinary geographic tests:

  • at least 50 percent of total gross income must derive from active conduct of a qualified business within low-income communities;
  • at least 40 percent of tangible property use must occur within low-income communities;
  • at least 40 percent of employee services must be performed within low-income communities;
  • less than five percent of property can consist of collectibles; and
  • less than five percent can consist of nonqualified financial property.

Regulations provide alternative safe harbours. For example, satisfying either the property or services test at 50 percent can establish the gross-income requirement.

Employee-free businesses and targeted-population businesses use specialised rules.

Excluded and Restricted Businesses

The tax code excludes selected activities from qualified-business treatment.

Examples include:

  • golf courses;
  • country clubs;
  • massage parlours;
  • hot-tub facilities;
  • suntan facilities;
  • racetracks and other gambling facilities;
  • stores whose principal business is sale of alcoholic beverages for off-premises consumption;
  • certain farming businesses exceeding statutory asset limits;
  • businesses principally developing or holding intangibles for sale or licence; and
  • ordinary residential rental property.

Allocation agreements can impose additional restrictions reflecting programme policy or an allocatee’s commitments.

Commercial and Mixed-Use Real Estate

Rental of real property in a low-income community can qualify when the property is not treated as residential rental property under the applicable tax definition and other requirements are satisfied.

NMTC commonly finances:

  • industrial buildings;
  • office buildings;
  • retail centres;
  • grocery stores;
  • health facilities;
  • schools;
  • community centres;
  • hotels supporting local development;
  • cultural facilities;
  • nonprofit campuses;
  • food-production facilities; and
  • mixed-use buildings with a sufficient qualifying nonresidential component.

Housing and NMTC

NMTC is not principally an affordable-rental-housing credit; that role belongs mainly to the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit.

Ordinary residential rental buildings generally do not qualify as QALICBs.

NMTC can support housing-related activity through:

  • mixed-use projects with qualifying commercial space;
  • homeownership development and sales;
  • manufactured-housing businesses;
  • supportive-service facilities;
  • homeless-service centres;
  • housing counselling;
  • community facilities connected to housing; and
  • certain structures combining NMTC with separate housing finance.

Tax rules limit use of the same financing basis for both NMTC and Low-Income Housing Tax Credits.

Operating Businesses

NMTC can finance businesses that produce goods or services rather than merely owning real estate.

Examples include:

  • manufacturers;
  • food processors;
  • healthcare providers;
  • grocery operators;
  • childcare businesses;
  • technology firms;
  • logistics and distribution companies;
  • renewable-energy businesses;
  • social enterprises;
  • tribal enterprises;
  • community-owned businesses; and
  • small-business loan pools.

The latest 2024–2025 allocatees projected that approximately 85 percent of investment proceeds would support operating-business loans or investments, with approximately 15 percent supporting real-estate projects.

Community Facilities

Nonprofit and public-purpose facilities represent a major NMTC use.

Projects include:

  • community health centres;
  • hospitals and rural clinics;
  • charter and independent schools;
  • early-childhood centres;
  • workforce-development facilities;
  • food banks;
  • arts and cultural institutions;
  • social-service campuses;
  • homeless shelters;
  • libraries;
  • community kitchens;
  • recreation centres; and
  • tribal community facilities.

Such projects can generate social benefits without sufficient cash flow to support conventional debt, making subsidy especially important.

Small-Business Financing

CDEs can make direct loans, equity investments, or pooled small-business financing.

NMTC can help businesses facing collateral gaps, limited equity, startup risk, unconventional ownership, rural markets, or expansion needs.

Allocation competitions increasingly evaluate whether CDEs provide genuinely flexible products rather than ordinary bank loans merely labelled as community development.

Transaction cost can make individual NMTC financing difficult for very small businesses. Loan funds and pooled structures can spread the cost across multiple borrowers.

Rural Communities

Rural communities can face branch closures, limited equity investors, low appraisals, declining population, small projects, infrastructure gaps, and long distances to services.

NMTC has financed rural hospitals, manufacturing plants, food systems, broadband-related facilities, agricultural processing, tribal projects, and small businesses.

The 2024–2025 award round projected approximately $2.417 billion in rural investment and required sixty-eight allocatees to deploy approximately $1.937 billion in nonmetropolitan counties.

Rural projects can remain disadvantaged when national CDEs prefer larger urban transactions with lower administrative cost.

Native Communities

Native communities face distinct barriers involving trust land, collateral, jurisdiction, infrastructure, distance, limited banking, and federal approval systems.

CDEs can use NMTC for tribal businesses, healthcare, schools, cultural facilities, energy, food systems, housing-related enterprises, and community infrastructure.

The CDFI Fund identifies Native areas and can reward allocation commitments to them.

Nine allocatees in the 2024–2025 round committed an estimated $236.4 million for NMTC Native-area investment.

Tribal consent, sovereignty, ownership, and long-term community benefit should guide project development.

Significance for African American Communities

NMTC addresses communities affected by redlining, urban renewal, deindustrialisation, business-credit discrimination, racial wealth inequality, public disinvestment, and bank withdrawal.

It has financed Black-community health centres, grocery stores, schools, manufacturing facilities, cultural institutions, small businesses, mixed-use redevelopment, and nonprofit organisations.

The programme can lower capital cost in neighbourhoods where appraisals and conventional underwriting undervalue community demand.

Benefits are not automatic. A project can be located in a Black neighbourhood while ownership, contracts, jobs, governance, and financial return flow primarily to outside institutions.

Equitable use therefore requires attention to Black ownership, local hiring, procurement, affordable services, community control, displacement, and whether residents share in appreciation and long-term economic benefit.

Minority-Led CDE Participation

GAO reported in 2009 that minority CDEs were less successful than non-minority entities in obtaining allocation awards.

Challenges included:

  • limited prior allocation experience;
  • smaller balance sheets;
  • fewer investor relationships;
  • less application-writing capacity;
  • difficulty documenting a large project pipeline;
  • limited operating capital; and
  • competition against national banks and established intermediaries.

Technical assistance and partnerships can improve participation but can also leave minority organisations as subordinate partners rather than allocation decision-makers.

Latino and Immigrant Communities

NMTC can finance businesses and facilities serving Latino and immigrant communities, including healthcare, food systems, childcare, workforce centres, commercial corridors, and minority-owned enterprises.

Language, immigration concerns, informal business structures, limited collateral, and exclusion from conventional finance can affect access.

Community representation and culturally competent intermediaries help identify projects that serve local demand rather than impose outside redevelopment priorities.

Disaster Recovery

Congress increased NMTC authority after Hurricane Katrina through the Gulf Opportunity Zone Act.

Special authority supported businesses, facilities, and redevelopment in disaster-affected communities.

NMTC can complement FEMA grants, CDBG-DR, insurance, philanthropic funding, SBA loans, tax-exempt bonds, and state incentives.

Combining sources can fill large recovery gaps but raises duplication, leverage, fee, and transparency concerns.

Disaster projects must still satisfy Section 45D and allocation-agreement requirements.

American Recovery and Reinvestment Act

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 authorised $3 billion in additional allocation authority divided between the 2008 and 2009 rounds.

The increase responded to recession, credit-market disruption, unemployment, and reduced private investment.

NMTC’s ability to attract institutional capital made it part of the federal economic-recovery strategy.

The 2024–2025 Allocation Round

Treasury announced a combined double-round allocation on December 23, 2025.

The CDFI Fund awarded $10 billion to 142 CDEs selected from 216 applicants that requested approximately $19.2 billion.

The allocatees were headquartered in forty-one states, Puerto Rico, and the District of Columbia and had local, statewide, multistate, or national service areas.

Certified CDFIs or their subsidiaries received $4.625 billion, representing approximately 45 percent of the allocation.

All 142 allocatees committed to invest at least 95 percent of QEI proceeds in QLICIs, exceeding the general 85-percent minimum.

Current Allocation Priorities

Treasury’s December 2025 announcement emphasised:

  • lasting job creation;
  • small-business growth;
  • domestic manufacturing;
  • rural investment;
  • rural hospitals and healthcare infrastructure;
  • affordable-housing-related development where legally eligible;
  • measurable community revitalisation; and
  • compliance with federal anti-discrimination law and current executive policy.

Treasury stated that it would strengthen monitoring, allocation-agreement restrictions, and enforcement remedies.

Administrative priorities can change while the statutory credit remains permanent.

Fees and Transaction Costs

NMTC transactions can include:

  • CDE allocation and closing fees;
  • annual asset-management fees;
  • legal fees;
  • tax opinions;
  • accounting and modelling fees;
  • investor underwriting;
  • loan fees;
  • community-impact reporting;
  • audit and compliance cost;
  • special-purpose entity cost;
  • trustee or fund administration; and
  • exit and unwinding expense.

Fees can be justified by complex compliance and underwriting but reduce capital reaching the project.

CDFI Fund applications and allocation agreements require disclosure and limits on selected fees.

Exit After Seven Years

Investor exit is negotiated before closing but ordinarily occurs after the recapture period ends.

A common leveraged structure gives the project sponsor or leverage lender an option to acquire the investor’s interest at fair market value under applicable tax guidance.

Parallel QLICI loans can be repaid, refinanced, forgiven, assigned, or restructured according to their terms.

Some transactions leave a deeply subordinate loan or equity contribution with the project, creating the lasting subsidy.

Exit must avoid a prearranged redemption during the compliance period and other tax-abuse concerns.

Tax-Credit Recapture

Recapture protects the federal government against early withdrawal or failure to maintain qualifying investment.

A recapture event occurs when:

  1. the CDE ceases to qualify as a CDE;
  2. the substantially-all requirement is not met and not cured; or
  3. the CDE redeems or otherwise cashes out the Qualified Equity Investment during the seven-year period.

Recapture can apply to original and subsequent holders of the QEI.

The tax increase generally includes previously used credits plus interest calculated under federal rules.

CDEs provide notice of a recapture event through Form 8874-B.

Cure and Reasonable Expectations

Regulations provide limited protections against recapture when noncompliance is corrected.

A CDE can receive a six-month cure period for a substantially-all failure after it knows or reasonably should know of the problem.

The reasonable-expectations rule can protect a CDE when a financed business later fails, provided the CDE reasonably expected at closing that the business would satisfy QALICB requirements.

Business failure alone does not necessarily trigger investor recapture, which is important for genuinely risky community-development investment.

CDFI Fund Remedies

Allocation-agreement violations do not always produce automatic IRS recapture.

The CDFI Fund can use contractual remedies including:

  • compliance demands;
  • corrective-action plans;
  • reporting conditions;
  • suspension of QEI issuance;
  • reduction or termination of unused allocation authority;
  • default findings;
  • restrictions on future applications;
  • CDE decertification; and
  • referral to the IRS, inspector general, or other authorities.

Treasury announced stronger compliance and anti-discrimination enforcement expectations for the 2024–2025 awards.

Reporting and Data

Allocatees submit reports concerning:

  • Qualified Equity Investments;
  • investors;
  • QLICIs;
  • borrowers and projects;
  • census tracts;
  • loan and investment terms;
  • fees;
  • jobs;
  • business ownership;
  • square footage;
  • community facilities;
  • rural and Native commitments;
  • better-than-market features; and
  • compliance with allocation agreements.

Data quality depends on CDE and borrower reporting, definitions, projections, follow-up, and validation.

Programme Outcomes

CDFI Fund reporting states that NMTC generated approximately eight dollars of private investment for each dollar of federal investment.

Through fiscal year 2024, approximately $71 billion in NMTC investments had entered low-income communities.

The programme supported tens of millions of square feet of manufacturing, office, retail, healthcare, education, and community space.

Reported jobs include temporary construction employment, permanent jobs created, and jobs retained.

Output totals demonstrate scale but do not by themselves establish that all investment was caused by the credit or that benefits reached low-income residents.

Additionality

Additionality asks whether a project or investment would have occurred in substantially the same form without NMTC.

Strong additionality exists when the credit:

  • closes a documented financing gap;
  • allows a project to proceed earlier;
  • supports a distressed location rejected by conventional lenders;
  • reduces debt to a sustainable level;
  • adds community services;
  • protects affordability;
  • supports local or minority ownership; or
  • improves financing terms materially.

Weak additionality exists when the credit mainly increases investor return or replaces financing that was already available.

GAO Findings on Complexity and Transparency

GAO reported that NMTC financial structures became increasingly complex.

From 2010 to 2012, an estimated 62 percent of surveyed projects combined NMTC with other federal, state, or local government assistance.

Combining programmes can make socially valuable projects feasible but can also obscure:

  • total public subsidy;
  • investor return;
  • fees;
  • risk actually borne by the investor;
  • project benefit;
  • duplication; and
  • whether less costly financing could have achieved the same result.

GAO recommended stronger controls and better data to assess effectiveness.

Combining NMTC with Other Subsidies

NMTC projects can combine:

  • federal grants;
  • state and local grants;
  • tax-exempt bonds;
  • historic rehabilitation tax credits;
  • state NMTCs;
  • CDBG;
  • CDBG-DR;
  • USDA financing;
  • SBA loans;
  • philanthropic grants;
  • foundation programme-related investments;
  • local tax increment financing;
  • energy incentives; and
  • conventional debt.

Tax rules permit many combinations but limit selected overlap, including financing of Low-Income Housing Tax Credit eligible basis.

Community Benefits

A well-designed NMTC project can produce:

  • accessible healthcare;
  • fresh food;
  • childcare;
  • education;
  • living-wage employment;
  • small-business opportunities;
  • reused vacant buildings;
  • environmental remediation;
  • local procurement;
  • community ownership;
  • cultural preservation;
  • public services;
  • tax-base growth; and
  • neighbourhood confidence.

Benefits depend on project selection, enforceable commitments, pricing, governance, hiring, and long-term operation.

Gentrification and Displacement Risk

Investment can improve services and property conditions while increasing rents, land values, taxes, and development pressure.

NMTC does not automatically require residential anti-displacement protections.

CDEs and public partners can reduce harm through:

  • community ownership;
  • affordable commercial leases;
  • local hiring;
  • small-business protections;
  • community benefit agreements;
  • land trusts;
  • home-repair and tax-relief programmes;
  • affordable housing funded separately; and
  • resident participation in project governance.

Environmental and Brownfield Redevelopment

NMTC can help finance reuse of abandoned factories, warehouses, contaminated industrial sites, and obsolete commercial buildings.

Brownfield projects can face cleanup liability, uncertain cost, low collateral value, and long development periods.

Combining NMTC with environmental grants, state cleanup credits, and local redevelopment tools can restore land to productive use.

Projects must avoid shifting pollution, demolition, or displacement burdens onto nearby residents.

What the Programme Does Not Provide

NMTC does not ordinarily provide:

  • a direct federal grant to a small business;
  • automatic financing because a project lies in an eligible tract;
  • a tax credit claimed by the project borrower;
  • permanent operating subsidy;
  • guaranteed loan forgiveness;
  • ordinary residential rental assistance;
  • unrestricted money to a CDE;
  • a guarantee that local residents will be hired;
  • protection against business failure; or
  • exemption from zoning, environmental, licensing, tax, or securities law.

How a Project Seeks NMTC Financing

  1. Confirm that the business, property, population, or activity can qualify.
  2. Develop a credible business plan, sources-and-uses budget, operating projections, site control, and community-impact case.
  3. Identify CDEs with the correct service area, product type, project size, and available allocation.
  4. Submit project information to one or more CDEs.
  5. Demonstrate financing gap and community need.
  6. Secure leverage debt, grants, sponsor equity, and other sources.
  7. Complete CDE, investor, lender, legal, tax, environmental, and community underwriting.
  8. Negotiate fees, QLICI terms, reporting, covenants, and exit structure.
  9. Close before allocation and project deadlines.
  10. Maintain compliance and community reporting for the full term.

Project Readiness

CDEs often prefer projects that can close within months because allocation deployment deadlines and investor commitments are time sensitive.

A competitive project usually has:

  • site control;
  • advanced design;
  • credible construction budget;
  • zoning and permitting path;
  • experienced management;
  • committed leverage sources;
  • operating projections;
  • community support;
  • measurable outcomes;
  • a documented financing gap; and
  • a plan for seven-year compliance and exit.

Early-stage projects can be selected conditionally but face greater risk of losing allocation to a more ready transaction.

Major Achievements

  • Created a national channel for private investment in low-income communities.
  • Made $81 billion in allocation awards through the 2024–2025 round.
  • Produced approximately $71 billion in completed NMTC investment through fiscal year 2024.
  • Financed manufacturing, healthcare, education, food access, childcare, cultural, and commercial facilities.
  • Supported urban, rural, Native, and disaster-affected communities.
  • Provided flexible capital beyond ordinary bank underwriting.
  • Encouraged banks and institutional investors to enter underserved markets.
  • Supported community-development organisations and CDFIs.
  • Enabled adaptive reuse of vacant and historic buildings.
  • Financed nonprofit facilities with limited conventional cash flow.
  • Supported jobs, business retention, and new economic activity.
  • Provided special investment authority after Hurricane Katrina and during the Great Recession.
  • Developed a nationwide intermediary network with community-accountability requirements.
  • Received permanent statutory authorization in 2025.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Transactions are complex and expensive.
  • Very small businesses can be unable to absorb transaction cost.
  • Institutional investors and established CDEs dominate the programme.
  • Minority-led entities historically had lower award success.
  • Community accountability can be advisory rather than controlling.
  • Project location does not guarantee benefits to residents.
  • Reported jobs can include temporary, projected, retained, or indirectly supported positions.
  • Projects frequently combine multiple public subsidies.
  • Investor returns and fees can be difficult to evaluate.
  • Some projects may have proceeded without the credit.
  • Eligible tracts vary widely in actual distress.
  • Investment can contribute to gentrification and displacement.
  • Residential rental housing is generally restricted.
  • The seven-year compliance period complicates financing and exit.
  • Data do not always establish long-term outcomes or resident benefit.
  • Tax expenditure is less visible than direct appropriations.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Permanent Implementation

Treasury is transitioning from repeated temporary extensions to a permanent annual $5 billion allocation framework with five-year unused-authority carryover.

2024–2025 Award Closing

The 142 allocatees announced in December 2025 are closing allocation agreements, raising QEIs, developing project pipelines, and beginning deployment.

Allocation and QEI Data Modernisation

The CDFI Fund is seeking public input in 2026 on its Allocation and Qualified Equity Investment Tracking System.

Rural and Manufacturing Priorities

Treasury is emphasising rural investment, domestic manufacturing, small businesses, job creation, rural hospitals, and essential community infrastructure.

Anti-Discrimination Compliance

Treasury has announced stronger allocation-agreement provisions and monitoring concerning federal anti-discrimination law and current executive policy.

Community Ownership

Advocates continue seeking greater Black, Latino, Native, local, worker, and community ownership rather than project location alone.

Fee and Subsidy Transparency

Policymakers continue examining whether complex structures, stacked subsidies, and investor pricing deliver sufficient net benefit to projects.

Outcome Measurement

Permanent authorization increases the importance of measuring poverty reduction, job quality, resident benefit, business survival, service access, and displacement—not only investment volume.

Census Data Transitions

CDEs and projects must track the CDFI Fund’s applicable census datasets, transition rules, and eligibility maps as geographic data change.

Relationship to Opportunity Zones

NMTC and permanent Opportunity Zones can overlap geographically but use different eligibility, investor, compliance, and community-benefit structures.

The maximum amount of private equity that a CDE may designate as eligible Qualified Equity Investments.

CDE
A certified Community Development Entity with a low-income community mission and community accountability.

A certified Community Development Financial Institution serving underserved markets through financial products and services.
QEI
A Qualified Equity Investment in a CDE that supports investor eligibility for NMTC.

An eligible census tract or targeted population satisfying Section 45D standards.

Qualifying low-income persons or other authorised groups treated as a low-income community under specialised rules.

The regulatory requirement that generally at least 85 percent of QEI proceeds remain invested in QLICIs.

Debt combined with investor equity to form a larger Qualified Equity Investment.

A special-purpose entity pooling tax-credit equity and leverage debt before investing in a CDE.

Financing conditions more favourable than a borrower could obtain conventionally.

Tax recovery triggered by specified noncompliance during the seven-year period.

The extent to which the credit causes investment, terms, timing, location, or community benefits that otherwise would not occur.
Tax expenditure
Federal revenue forgone through a tax preference rather than direct appropriated spending.

Summary

The New Markets Tax Credit Program is a federal community-development tax incentive created in 2000 and made permanent in 2025.

The CDFI Fund competitively awards allocation authority to certified Community Development Entities. CDEs raise private Qualified Equity Investments and use the capital to finance businesses and facilities in low-income communities.

Investors receive a federal tax credit equal to 39 percent of the original investment over seven years: five percent in each of the first three years and six percent in each of the final four.

In general, at least 85 percent of QEI proceeds must remain invested in Qualified Low-Income Community Investments, principally loans or equity investments in Qualified Active Low-Income Community Businesses.

Eligible low-income communities generally have poverty of at least 20 percent or median family income at or below 80 percent of the applicable benchmark. Targeted-population rules provide additional pathways.

NMTC has financed manufacturing, healthcare, education, grocery, childcare, cultural, nonprofit, commercial, rural, tribal, and disaster-recovery projects.

The combined 2024–2025 round awarded $10 billion to 142 CDEs. Across twenty-one rounds, the CDFI Fund had awarded $81 billion in authority, while approximately $71 billion had been invested through fiscal year 2024.

Public Law 119-21 made the programme permanent at $5 billion in annual allocation authority and established a five-year carryover limit for unused authority.

The programme’s principal strength is its ability to use private tax-motivated capital to close financing gaps. Its principal weakness is a complex intermediary structure that can obscure fees, investor returns, public-subsidy stacking, additionality, and who ultimately benefits.

The programme should therefore be judged not only by investment volume but by business survival, job quality, community ownership, service access, racial and geographic equity, displacement prevention, and durable benefit to low-income residents.
