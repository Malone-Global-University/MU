Women, Infants, and Children Program

The federal maternal and child nutrition program officially known as WIC, providing tailored supplemental foods, nutrition education, breastfeeding support, health screening, and referrals to eligible pregnant and postpartum participants, infants, and children younger than five.

What Is the Women, Infants, and Children Program?

WIC is a public-health nutrition program serving low-income pregnant, postpartum, and breastfeeding people, infants, and children younger than five who are found to be at nutritional risk.

WIC does not provide a general grocery allowance. Each participant receives a food package tailored to age, pregnancy or postpartum status, breastfeeding category, medical needs, and nutritional assessment.

The program combines food benefits with nutrition education, breastfeeding assistance, growth and anemia screening, and referrals to health care and social services.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains WIC’s origins, law, financing, administration, eligibility, certification, nutrition-risk assessment, food packages, breastfeeding services, infant formula policy, eWIC system, vendors, cost containment, program integrity, Farmers’ Market Nutrition Program, outcomes, and current status.

It does not replace a WIC clinic’s individualized eligibility or food-package determination. State food lists, clinic procedures, authorized brands, substitutions, and benefit issuance vary.

Geographic Scope

WIC operates through state agencies in the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Participating Indian Tribal Organizations administer separate WIC state agencies for approved populations and service areas.

Local access is provided through public-health departments, community health centres, hospitals, tribal programs, mobile clinics, nonprofit agencies, and other approved institutions.

Historical Context

WIC emerged from 1960s and early-1970s investigations documenting maternal malnutrition, infant mortality, anemia, inadequate prenatal care, and childhood hunger.

Physicians, public-health researchers, civil-rights organizations, anti-hunger advocates, and congressional committees argued that ordinary food assistance did not fully address the nutritional needs of pregnancy, infancy, and early childhood.

The program was designed as an adjunct to health care rather than merely a commodity or income-support program.

Child Nutrition Act of 1966

The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 established the statutory framework later amended to include WIC.

Section 17 now contains WIC’s purposes, definitions, eligibility rules, funding structure, food-package authority, nutrition-education requirements, breastfeeding provisions, vendor rules, and Farmers’ Market Nutrition Program.

Pilot Authorization in 1972

Public Law 92-433 created the Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children as a two-year pilot.

Congress authorized grants for supplemental foods and related services for nutritionally vulnerable pregnant people, infants, and young children.

The pilot reflected concern that critical nutritional deficiencies during pregnancy and early development could produce lasting health consequences.

The First WIC Clinic

The first official WIC clinic opened in Pineville, Kentucky, in January 1974.

Early sites demonstrated clinic-based certification, food prescription, nutrition counselling, and coordination with maternal and child health services.

Permanent Authorization in 1975

Public Law 94-105 made WIC a permanent national health and nutrition program in 1975.

Permanent status allowed broader state participation, long-term appropriations, standardized administration, and continuing development of nutrition-risk and food-package policy.

The 1994 Name Change

The Healthy Meals for Healthy Americans Act of 1994 changed the official name from the Special Supplemental Food Program to the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program.

The change emphasized that WIC includes nutrition assessment, education, breastfeeding support, and referrals rather than food distribution alone.

Statutory Purpose

Congress found that low-income pregnant, postpartum, and breastfeeding participants, infants, and young children face special physical and mental health risks from inadequate nutrition or health care.

WIC is intended to provide supplemental foods, nutrition education, breastfeeding promotion and support, and an adjunct to good health care during critical periods of growth and development.

Discretionary Program Rather Than an Entitlement

WIC depends on annual federal appropriations.

An applicant meeting all eligibility conditions does not possess the same federal entitlement to benefits that exists under SNAP.

Congress has historically attempted to appropriate enough funding to serve projected eligible participants, but state agencies must use priority systems if resources are insufficient.

Federal Administration

The Food and Nutrition Administration establishes national policy, distributes grants, approves state plans, reviews food-delivery systems, oversees EBT, monitors vendors and cost containment, conducts research, and enforces federal requirements.

The federal agency was called the Food and Nutrition Service until June 1, 2026.

WIC State Agencies

WIC “state agencies” include state and territorial governments and approved Indian Tribal Organizations.

State agencies develop food lists, contract for infant-formula rebates, allocate funds, authorize vendors, maintain management-information and EBT systems, train local agencies, and monitor compliance.

USDA reported eighty-eight WIC state agencies operating in 2026.

Local Agencies and Clinics

Local agencies conduct certification, nutrition assessment, food-package prescription, nutrition education, breastfeeding support, referrals, and benefit issuance.

Clinics may be operated by health departments, tribal governments, hospitals, community health centres, nonprofit organizations, and other approved entities.

Categorical Eligibility

WIC serves only people in specified maternal and child categories.

The term “categorical eligibility” in WIC means membership in an eligible participant category. It differs from SNAP categorical eligibility, which concerns links to other assistance programs.

Pregnant Participants

A person determined to have one or more fetuses in utero can qualify during pregnancy.

For household-size purposes, pregnancy generally adds one person, with additional treatment available under state policy for multiple fetuses.

Certification ordinarily continues through pregnancy and a short period after pregnancy ends before postpartum status is determined.

Breastfeeding Participants

A breastfeeding participant can remain eligible through the infant’s first birthday while breastfeeding continues.

Fully and partially breastfeeding categories receive different food packages, with the fully breastfeeding package providing the most extensive adult supplemental foods.

Nonbreastfeeding Postpartum Participants

A nonbreastfeeding postpartum participant can generally qualify through six months after pregnancy ends.

The category includes pregnancy termination, miscarriage, stillbirth, and live birth under applicable definitions.

Infants

An infant is a person from birth through the day before the first birthday.

Infant food packages differ by age and by fully breastfed, partially breastfed, or fully formula-fed status.

Children

A WIC child has reached the first birthday but has not reached the fifth birthday.

Eligibility ends at age five even when the child remains income eligible and at nutritional risk.

Families may transition to school meals, Head Start, SNAP, Medicaid, and other programs.

Residency Requirement

An applicant must live in the state, territory, tribal service area, or local jurisdiction where applying.

Federal WIC rules do not require a minimum length of residence.

The program’s core categorical criteria do not include a federal citizenship test.

Income Eligibility

An applicant ordinarily must have family income within the guidelines adopted by the WIC state agency.

The maximum standard is the income limit for reduced-price school meals—185 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

A state may use identical free or reduced-price health-care standards when they fall between 100 and 185 percent of the poverty guidelines.

Alaska and Hawaii Income Standards

WIC applies higher poverty-based income limits in Alaska and Hawaii.

Family and Household Size

WIC generally treats related or unrelated people living as one economic unit as a family when they share income and expenses.

Foster children are commonly treated as separate economic units.

Pregnancy increases family size for income testing under federal law.

Income Period

State agencies may consider current income or income during the preceding twelve months, using the measure that more accurately represents the family’s economic circumstances.

Current income can be important after job loss, reduced work hours, separation, or another sudden change.

What Counts as Income?

WIC income generally includes wages, self-employment earnings, Social Security, pensions, unemployment compensation, public assistance, child support, alimony, interest, dividends, and regular cash contributions.

Federal law excludes specified payments, loans, in-kind housing, certain military allowances, disaster aid, tax-exempt program payments, and other protected income.

No General WIC Asset Test

WIC ordinarily tests family income rather than imposing a separate national resource limit on savings, vehicles, or a home.

This distinguishes WIC from SSI and some state cash-assistance programs.

Adjunctive Income Eligibility

An applicant can satisfy the income requirement through participation in another qualifying means-tested program.

Federal adjunctive programs include:

  • Medicaid;
  • Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program;
  • Temporary Assistance for Needy Families meeting federal comparability requirements; and
  • specified state-administered programs approved under federal rules.

Adjunctive eligibility does not eliminate categorical, residence, or nutritional-risk requirements.

Medicaid and WIC Eligibility

Receipt of Medicaid by the applicant can establish WIC income eligibility.

A pregnant participant or infant can also create adjunctive eligibility for family members under statutory rules.

WIC and Medicaid remain separate programs and generally require separate enrollment.

SNAP and TANF Links

Receipt of SNAP or qualifying TANF assistance can establish WIC income eligibility.

WIC benefits do not count as income for SNAP, TANF, Medicaid, or other federal means-tested programs under ordinary rules.

Nutritional Risk

Income eligibility alone is insufficient. A competent professional authority must determine that each participant is at nutritional risk.

Nutritional risk includes medically based conditions and dietary conditions that threaten health, growth, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or development.

Medically Based Nutritional Risk

Medically based risks can include anemia, underweight, overweight, abnormal growth, pregnancy complications, poor birth outcomes, chronic disease, oral-health conditions, and medically restricted diets.

Risk assessment uses anthropometric, biochemical, medical, and health-history information.

Dietary Nutritional Risk

Dietary risks can include inadequate intake, inappropriate feeding practices, limited dietary variety, excessive intake of specified foods, unsafe formula preparation, or feeding inconsistent with developmental needs.

Food insecurity and conditions predisposing a person to inadequate nutrition can also support certification under approved criteria.

Competent Professional Authority

Nutritional risk must be determined by a qualified professional such as a physician, nutritionist, dietitian, registered nurse, medically trained health official, or properly designated staff member operating under federal standards.

Nutrition Assessment

WIC certification generally includes height or length, weight, health and diet history, and a hematological test for anemia when required.

Assessment should be participant centred and used to tailor education, referrals, and the food package.

Presumptive Eligibility for Pregnant Applicants

A state may immediately certify an income-eligible pregnant applicant before the final nutrition-risk evaluation.

The nutritional-risk assessment must then be completed within sixty days.

Presumptive eligibility supports early prenatal nutrition intervention.

Priority System

When resources are insufficient, WIC state agencies must serve participants according to a federally structured nutrition-risk priority system.

Highest priority generally goes to pregnant and breastfeeding participants and infants with serious medically based nutritional risks.

Lower-priority applicants can be placed on waiting lists rather than denied as categorically ineligible.

Application Process

A person contacts a local WIC clinic or state intake system and requests an appointment.

Certification commonly requires:

  • proof of identity;
  • proof of residence;
  • income documentation or proof of adjunctive eligibility;
  • presence of the applicant unless waived;
  • health and diet information;
  • height or length and weight;
  • blood testing when required; and
  • a nutrition-risk determination.

Notification and Processing Time

Local agencies generally must notify applicants of eligibility or ineligibility within twenty days after an oral or written request during office hours.

Shorter processing standards apply to applicants with urgent nutritional risks.

Physical-Presence Requirement

Federal law generally requires each applicant to be physically present at certification and recertification.

Local agencies can waive physical presence when it would create an unreasonable barrier for specified infants, children, disabled applicants, hospitalised people, and other authorized cases.

Remote WIC Services

Pandemic-era and modernization waivers expanded telephone, video, remote-document, and remote-benefit services.

Many American Rescue Plan modernization waivers remained active through September 30, 2026, or the end of federal evaluation, whichever was later.

Remote service does not eliminate the requirement for reliable nutritional assessment and periodic measurements.

Certification Periods

Certification duration depends on participant category and state options.

Breastfeeding participants can be certified through one year postpartum while breastfeeding continues, and children can be certified for as long as one year when required assessments are maintained.

Pregnant and postpartum periods reflect pregnancy and postpartum eligibility windows.

Participant Rights and Responsibilities

Participants receive notice concerning benefits, approved foods, appointments, reporting, civil rights, fair hearings, dual participation, sale or exchange of benefits, and sanctions.

Participants must provide truthful information, use benefits only for authorised purposes, protect the eWIC card, and avoid selling or transferring benefits.

Notices and Fair Hearings

Applicants and participants may request a fair hearing concerning denial, termination, disqualification, benefit reduction, or another adverse action.

State procedures must provide timely notice, an impartial hearing, evidence review, representation, and a written decision.

Nutrition Education

Nutrition education is a core statutory service rather than an optional add-on.

Education addresses healthy eating, infant and child feeding, pregnancy nutrition, food safety, physical activity, shopping, meal preparation, and individual health concerns.

Federal law requires services to respect personal and cultural preferences.

Value Enhanced Nutrition Assessment

Value Enhanced Nutrition Assessment is a WIC practice model emphasizing open communication, participant goals, health context, and individualized nutrition services.

The model seeks to move certification away from a checklist-only process while preserving objective eligibility requirements.

Health and Social-Service Referrals

WIC refers families to prenatal care, paediatric care, immunizations, dental services, Medicaid, SNAP, TANF, Head Start, child care, substance-use treatment, domestic-violence services, housing aid, and other community resources.

Referral does not guarantee eligibility or immediate access to the receiving program.

Immunization Screening and Referral

WIC clinics assess immunization status and refer infants and children to appropriate health providers.

WIC does not ordinarily administer a national vaccination requirement as a condition of food benefits.

Breastfeeding Promotion and Support

Federal law directs USDA and WIC agencies to promote breastfeeding and provide support, counselling, equipment, and referrals.

Services can include prenatal education, latch and positioning support, peer counselling, lactation consultation, breast pumps, workplace planning, and referral for medical concerns.

Breastfeeding Support and Participant Choice

WIC describes breastfeeding as the optimal source of infant nutrition while serving families who fully breastfeed, partially breastfeed, or use formula.

Food-package policy should support informed choice, medical necessity, infant safety, and the participant’s feeding goals without withholding appropriate formula.

Breastfeeding Peer Counselling

Peer counsellors are trained people with personal breastfeeding experience who provide culturally responsive encouragement and practical assistance.

Peer programmes supplement rather than replace clinical lactation and medical care.

Breast Pumps

WIC agencies may provide manual or electric breast pumps and related supplies according to participant need, state policy, and available resources.

The 2024 food-package rule codified breast pumps as a WIC benefit and treated sale or attempted sale as a participant violation.

WIC Food Packages

WIC uses seven science-based food packages tailored to participant category and nutritional need.

Packages are supplemental. They are not designed to provide every food or all formula a participant consumes during the month.

Food Package I: Infants Birth Through Five Months

Food Package I serves infants from birth through five months.

The prescription depends on whether the infant is fully breastfed, partially breastfed, or fully formula fed.

Fully breastfed infants receive no formula through WIC.

Food Package II: Infants Six Through Eleven Months

Food Package II serves infants ages six through eleven months.

Packages can include infant cereal, infant fruits and vegetables, formula according to feeding category, and infant meat for fully breastfed infants.

Food Package III: Qualifying Medical Conditions

Food Package III serves participants with documented qualifying medical conditions requiring exempt infant formula, WIC-eligible nutritionals, or modified supplemental foods.

Appropriate health-care-provider documentation is generally required.

Food Package IV: Children Ages One Through Four

Food Package IV provides foods for children from the first birthday until age five.

Typical components include milk or approved alternatives, cereal, eggs or permitted substitutes, whole grains, legumes or peanut butter, juice, and a fruit-and-vegetable cash-value benefit.

Food Package V: Pregnancy and Partial Breastfeeding

Food Package V serves pregnant participants and partially breastfeeding participants.

The package contains greater quantities than the nonbreastfeeding postpartum package and is tailored according to clinical need and breastfeeding intensity.

Food Package VI: Nonbreastfeeding Postpartum

Food Package VI serves nonbreastfeeding postpartum participants during the eligible postpartum period.

It is designed for maternal nutrition recovery but contains fewer supplemental foods than the fully breastfeeding package.

Food Package VII: Fully Breastfeeding

Food Package VII provides the largest adult food package to participants fully breastfeeding an infant.

It can include additional milk, eggs, legumes, peanut butter, whole grains, canned fish, and the highest fruit-and-vegetable benefit.

Individual Food-Package Tailoring

WIC professionals can tailor packages to nutritional need, food allergy, medical condition, breastfeeding status, age, storage, preparation capacity, and approved substitutions.

Tailoring cannot exceed federal maximums or authorize foods outside applicable federal and state rules.

Common WIC-Approved Foods

Depending on participant category and state food list, approved foods can include:

  • milk and lactose-free milk;
  • cheese and yogurt;
  • approved plant-based milk alternatives;
  • eggs;
  • beans, peas, lentils, peanut butter, tofu, and approved nut or seed butters;
  • whole-grain bread, tortillas, rice, oats, pasta, barley, cornmeal, and other approved whole grains;
  • breakfast cereal;
  • fruit and vegetable juice;
  • fresh, frozen, canned, or dried fruits and vegetables;
  • infant cereal and infant foods;
  • infant formula;
  • canned fish; and
  • medical nutrition products under Food Package III.

Fruit-and-Vegetable Cash-Value Benefit

The cash-value benefit allows participants to purchase eligible fruits and vegetables up to a monthly dollar amount.

Unlike other WIC foods, the benefit is based primarily on price rather than a prescribed quantity.

Unused value generally expires at the end of the benefit period.

The 2024 Food-Package Final Rule

USDA published a comprehensive WIC food-package final rule on April 18, 2024.

The rule aligned benefits with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans and recommendations of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine.

Major changes expanded fruit and vegetable benefits, whole-grain options, substitutions, culturally appropriate choices, plant-based alternatives, canned fish, and flexibility for special dietary needs while revising quantities of juice, milk, infant foods, and other products.

Implementation of the 2024 Food-Package Rule

State agencies received twenty-four months from publication to implement most provisions, with a sixty-day deadline for the revised fruit-and-vegetable benefit.

The vitamin D specification for yogurt has a later deadline of April 26, 2027.

By August 2026, most required food-package changes were in effect, subject to the temporary fiscal-year 2026 milk law.

Whole Grains

WIC food packages include whole-grain foods and require state food lists to offer whole-wheat or whole-grain bread.

The 2024 rule expanded options including additional culturally common whole grains and package sizes.

Milk and Dairy Substitutions

State agencies must authorize regular and lactose-free milk and may authorize permitted plant-based milk alternatives, yogurts, cheeses, and tofu under federal standards.

Fat content depends on age, pregnancy, postpartum status, and individualized nutrition assessment.

Egg, Legume, and Protein Substitutions

Federal rules permit or require specified substitutions among eggs, dry or canned legumes, peanut butter, tofu, and approved nut or seed butters.

These options support vegetarian diets, allergies, cultural food patterns, and participant choice.

Juice

WIC food packages include limited quantities of one-hundred-percent juice for eligible participant categories.

The 2024 rule reduced several juice quantities and allowed specified substitution of part of the juice benefit for additional fruit-and-vegetable value.

Canned Fish

Canned fish is included for fully breastfeeding participants and was expanded to additional participant categories under the 2024 rule.

State lists must account for federal mercury guidance, package sizes, and approved species.

Infant Formula

WIC supplies contract-brand infant formula to eligible fully or partially formula-fed infants when prescribed.

The program is supplemental and may not provide every ounce an infant consumes.

Exempt formula and medical nutritionals require qualifying conditions and documentation.

Infant-Formula Rebate Contracts

Most WIC state agencies use competitive sole-source rebate contracts with infant-formula manufacturers.

The winning manufacturer pays a rebate for each unit purchased with WIC benefits, substantially reducing net federal food cost.

Savings allow WIC to serve more participants, but contract concentration affects brands, retailers, supply chains, and market competition.

The 2022 Infant-Formula Shortage

A major formula recall and manufacturing shutdown in 2022 disrupted national supplies and disproportionately affected WIC families tied to contract brands.

USDA issued waivers allowing substitutions, alternate sizes, imported formula, and broader vendor flexibility.

The crisis exposed vulnerabilities created by market concentration and rigid benefit specifications.

Access to Baby Formula Act of 2022

The Access to Baby Formula Act strengthened USDA authority during recalls, supply-chain disruptions, public-health emergencies, and disasters.

It required contingency planning, federal coordination, waiver procedures, and stronger contractual protections for formula availability.

Food Safety

Nutrition education includes safe formula preparation, breast-milk storage, age-appropriate feeding, choking prevention, sanitation, allergy awareness, and recall information.

WIC agencies coordinate with health departments, USDA, the Food and Drug Administration, and manufacturers during product recalls.

Electronic WIC Benefits

eWIC uses an electronic benefit account and card to store prescribed foods and quantities.

At checkout, the system compares the participant’s remaining benefits, the state’s approved product list, and the retailer’s scanned items.

Nationwide WIC EBT implementation was required by October 1, 2020, subject to limited technical circumstances.

eWIC and SNAP EBT Are Different

SNAP generally provides a dollar balance usable for any SNAP-eligible food.

eWIC provides specified products, quantities, sizes, brands, and dollar fruit-and-vegetable benefits according to the participant’s prescription.

One card may not substitute for the other even when both operate electronically.

Benefit Issuance Periods

WIC benefits are issued for defined monthly periods with a first and last date of use.

Unredeemed benefits generally do not carry forward.

Families should distinguish the certification period from the monthly food-benefit period.

Approved Product Lists

Each state agency maintains an approved product list identifying eligible universal product codes and product categories.

A food that meets federal nutrition standards is not automatically purchasable unless the state authorizes the product and the EBT system recognizes it.

Shopping Applications

Many state agencies provide mobile applications showing benefit balances, approved products, clinic information, and barcode scanning.

Applications assist shopping but do not override the official eWIC transaction system or state food list.

Online Ordering and Shopping

WIC online shopping has expanded through modernization grants, pilot projects, curbside pickup, and retailer-system development.

Nationwide online redemption was not yet universal in 2026 because WIC’s product-specific EBT structure requires complex retailer and state-system integration.

Authorized WIC Vendors

State agencies authorize retail stores, pharmacies, direct-distribution sites, home-delivery contractors, and other approved vendors.

Vendors must stock required foods, charge competitive prices, use approved systems, train staff, maintain records, and comply with transaction and civil-rights rules.

Vendor Selection and Peer Groups

State agencies use vendor-selection criteria involving business integrity, stocking, location, prices, ownership, sanctions, and capacity.

Peer-group and competitive-price systems compare similar stores to control food costs.

Minimum Stocking Requirements

Vendors must maintain minimum varieties and quantities of approved foods.

The 2024 rule requires vendor applicants to stock at least two different fruits, three different vegetables, and at least one approved whole-grain cereal, subject to state peer-group policies.

Vendor Monitoring

State agencies monitor vendors through transaction analysis, compliance purchases, inventory audits, price reviews, complaints, and onsite visits.

Sanctions can include warning, claims, civil money penalties, temporary disqualification, or permanent disqualification.

Participant Violations

Violations include intentionally misrepresenting eligibility, dual participation, selling or offering to sell benefits or breast pumps, exchanging benefits for cash, and using benefits for unauthorized purposes.

Penalties can include repayment, suspension, disqualification, and referral for civil or criminal action.

Dual Participation

A participant cannot receive WIC benefits from more than one clinic, state agency, or WIC jurisdiction for the same benefit period.

States use data matching, transfer procedures, and identity controls to prevent duplicate issuance while allowing families to move.

Errors and Fraud Are Different

Incorrect issuance can result from staff error, system configuration, misunderstood reporting, vendor coding, or participant misrepresentation.

An error is not automatically fraud. Intentional conduct requires evidence of knowing violation.

Civil Rights and Nondiscrimination

WIC agencies, clinics, and vendors must comply with federal civil-rights requirements.

Programs must provide nondiscriminatory service, language assistance, disability access, complaint procedures, and equal treatment at authorized stores.

Language Access

Clinics must take reasonable steps to provide meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency.

Translation, interpretation, culturally responsive counselling, and accessible written materials are important to informed food use and safe infant feeding.

Disability Access

WIC agencies must provide reasonable modifications, effective communication, accessible facilities, and physical-presence waivers where authorized and necessary.

Food Package III and individual tailoring address qualifying medical and dietary needs.

Migrant Services

Federal law reserves a portion of WIC appropriations for services to eligible members of migrant populations.

Mobile families face challenges involving records, benefit transfer, clinic access, language, transportation, and continuity of infant formula and health care.

Indian Tribal Organization WIC Programs

Indian Tribal Organizations can administer WIC as state agencies under approved federal plans.

Tribal administration can improve sovereignty, cultural relevance, traditional-food connections, language access, and service delivery in rural areas.

Retail access and long travel distances remain important challenges.

Rural Access

Rural participants may travel long distances to clinics and authorized retailers.

Mobile clinics, remote certification, extended appointments, local vendors, farmers markets, and transportation support can reduce barriers.

Urban Access

Urban WIC programs serve large and diverse caseloads but can face clinic congestion, housing instability, language diversity, retailer turnover, and transportation barriers across neighbourhoods.

Military Families

Military families can qualify under ordinary WIC rules, and some allowances are excluded from income.

The Department of Defense operates a separate overseas WIC program for eligible military families outside covered domestic jurisdictions.

WIC Overseas

WIC Overseas is administered by the Department of Defense rather than USDA’s domestic WIC programme.

It serves eligible members of the military community at designated overseas locations and uses separate delivery and administrative systems.

WIC Farmers’ Market Nutrition Program

The WIC Farmers’ Market Nutrition Program is a related but separately funded program established nationally in 1992.

Participating state agencies issue seasonal benefits for fresh, unprepared, locally grown fruits, vegetables, and herbs from authorized farmers, farmers markets, roadside stands, and community-supported agriculture programmes where approved.

Farmers’ Market Program Eligibility

State agencies select WIC participants to receive Farmers’ Market Nutrition Program benefits.

Infants generally do not receive the benefit before four months of age, and state priorities can apply when funding is limited.

WIC Cash-Value Benefits and FMNP Are Different

The ordinary WIC fruit-and-vegetable cash-value benefit is part of the monthly food package and is primarily redeemed at WIC vendors.

FMNP supplies an additional seasonal benefit through a separate grant and authorized direct-marketing network.

Federal Funding Allocation

Federal grants cover food costs and nutrition services and administration.

USDA allocates funds using participation, food-cost, administrative, and statutory formulas and can recover and reallocate unspent funds.

States do not ordinarily provide a general matching share for the core WIC grant.

Nutrition Services and Administration Funds

Nutrition Services and Administration funds support certification, nutrition assessment, breastfeeding services, clinics, outreach, EBT, vendor management, data systems, staff, referrals, and program oversight.

Minimum Breastfeeding Expenditure

Federal law requires minimum national and state expenditures for breastfeeding promotion and support.

The formula is based partly on the number of pregnant and breastfeeding participants and is adjusted for inflation.

Cost Containment

WIC uses formula rebates, vendor price controls, competitive bidding, food specifications, approved brands, peer groups, and EBT data to control cost.

Cost containment increases programme reach but can reduce brand choice or create supply vulnerability.

Fiscal Year 2025 Program Scale

USDA reported that WIC participation averaged approximately 6.9 million people per month in fiscal year 2025.

  • Approximately 1.5 million were adult participants;
  • approximately 1.5 million were infants;
  • approximately 3.8 million were children ages one through four;
  • federal spending totalled approximately $7.7 billion; and
  • monthly food cost averaged $64.71 per participant.

Participation History

WIC served approximately 88,000 people in 1974, 1.9 million in 1980, 3.1 million in 1985, 4.5 million in 1990, and 7.2 million in 2000.

Participation later exceeded nine million before declining during the 2010s and increasing again from fiscal years 2023 through 2025.

Eligibility and Coverage Rates

Not every eligible person participates.

Coverage is generally highest among infants and lower among children ages one through four, who often leave the program after infancy.

Application burden, clinic access, stigma, employment schedules, benefit value, retailer experience, and awareness affect participation.

Retention of Children Ages One Through Four

Children constitute the largest participant group but have lower coverage than infants.

WIC agencies coordinate with Head Start, Medicaid, paediatric clinics, SNAP, child care, and community organizations to retain eligible children after the first birthday.

Pregnancy and Birth Outcomes

Research generally associates prenatal WIC participation with improved birth outcomes, including lower risk of low birth weight and preterm birth in many study populations.

Observational findings require care because participants and nonparticipants can differ in health, income, timing of prenatal care, and other conditions.

Infant Health

WIC supports infant nutrition through breastfeeding assistance, safe formula access, growth assessment, complementary feeding guidance, and health referrals.

Its strongest effects arise from combining food support with early contact with health and nutrition professionals.

Child Diet Quality

Studies associate WIC food-package participation with higher consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and selected priority nutrients.

The 2009 package revision was associated with improved dietary patterns and changes in the foods available in stores serving WIC communities.

Childhood Obesity and Growth

WIC monitors growth and provides nutrition counselling concerning feeding practices, beverages, physical activity, and healthy weight.

Research has documented declining obesity prevalence among young WIC children in many jurisdictions after earlier increases, although causation is influenced by multiple policies and social conditions.

Breastfeeding Outcomes

WIC breastfeeding initiation and duration have increased over time, supported by peer counselling, enhanced food packages, pumps, education, and clinic practice.

Breastfeeding rates still vary by race, geography, employment, hospital practice, income, family support, and access to lactation care.

Health-Care and Economic Effects

Improved pregnancy and infant outcomes can reduce medical costs associated with low birth weight, prematurity, anaemia, and nutrition-related illness.

WIC food purchases support grocery retailers, manufacturers, farms, and local food systems.

Effects on the Retail Food Environment

Vendor stocking requirements and participant purchasing can increase availability of whole grains, low-fat dairy, infant foods, and fruits and vegetables in low-income neighbourhoods.

Small stores can face costs associated with inventory, product identification, technology, and minimum stocking.

COVID-19 Pandemic

Federal legislation allowed USDA to waive physical-presence and other requirements during the COVID-19 pandemic.

States expanded remote appointments, remote benefit issuance, product substitutions, and electronic communication.

The pandemic accelerated WIC modernization and demonstrated both the value and limitations of remote nutrition services.

American Rescue Plan Modernization

The American Rescue Plan provided $390 million for WIC and FMNP outreach, innovation, and modernization.

Projects supported remote services, online shopping, data sharing, participant portals, clinic technology, benefit access, and outreach.

Disasters and Programme Disruption

Hurricanes, wildfires, floods, public-health emergencies, power outages, recalls, and supply-chain disruptions can interrupt clinics, EBT, vendors, formula, and approved foods.

State plans must include alternate operations, communication, product substitution, and continuity procedures.

Program Integrity

Program integrity includes accurate certification, nutrition-risk determination, benefit issuance, vendor transactions, cost containment, data security, protection against dual participation, and correction of improper denials.

Integrity is not limited to fraud enforcement; it also requires accessible clinics, reliable EBT, accurate food lists, and timely benefits.

Data Sharing

WIC agencies exchange information with Medicaid, SNAP, TANF, vital records, health systems, and other programmes when authorised.

Data sharing can streamline certification and outreach but must comply with WIC confidentiality law, consent requirements, and approved uses.

Confidentiality

WIC applicant and participant information is confidential and may be used or disclosed only for programme administration and other authorised purposes.

State agencies must govern access, data-sharing agreements, security, notices, and records retention.

Stigma and Public Understanding

WIC is sometimes incorrectly described as food stamps for pregnant people.

Its clinic services, nutrition-risk requirement, tailored foods, breastfeeding support, and health-care role distinguish it from SNAP.

eWIC reduced the visibility of paper vouchers but shopping complexity and misinformation can still discourage participation.

Administrative Burden

Required appointments, measurements, blood tests, income proof, recertification, limited clinic hours, retailer rules, and product-specific shopping can burden families.

Modernization seeks to reduce unnecessary burden without eliminating nutritional assessment or programme integrity.

Funding Risk and Waiting Lists

Because WIC is discretionary, unexpected increases in participation or food prices can create funding pressure.

State reserves, federal reallocation, contingency authority, and supplemental appropriations can protect service.

If funding becomes insufficient, federal priority rules determine who is served first.

Major Achievements

  • Created a national maternal and child nutrition programme focused on critical developmental periods.
  • Combined supplemental foods with professional nutrition assessment and education.
  • Built a major national breastfeeding-support infrastructure.
  • Connected millions of families with health care and social services.
  • Reduced the cost of infant formula through competitive rebates.
  • Developed tailored food packages for medical, cultural, and developmental needs.
  • Expanded fruit, vegetable, and whole-grain access.
  • Converted paper vouchers to electronic benefits.
  • Created tribal administration and migrant-service pathways.
  • Supported farmers and local food systems through FMNP.

Limitations and Failures

  • WIC is not an individual entitlement and depends on annual appropriations.
  • Only a portion of eligible people participate.
  • Coverage drops sharply after infancy.
  • Clinic appointments and certification can burden working families.
  • Product-specific benefits can be difficult to redeem.
  • Food lists and benefit access differ among states.
  • Retailer shortages and long travel distances limit rural and tribal access.
  • Formula rebate concentration creates supply-chain vulnerability.
  • Benefits are supplemental and do not cover all household food needs.
  • Online shopping remains incomplete.
  • Food-package changes can impose technology and inventory costs.
  • Nutrition support cannot overcome inadequate housing, health care, wages, or paid leave by itself.

Major Controversies

Breastfeeding and formula
WIC promotes breastfeeding while remaining a major source of formula for families who use it.

Rebates save billions but contribute to brand concentration and supply dependence.
Discretionary funding
Annual appropriations allow budget control but do not guarantee service to every eligible applicant.
Food-package prescription
Tailored foods reinforce nutrition goals but reduce consumer flexibility compared with SNAP.
Milk and juice quantities
Scientific recommendations, congressional directives, industry concerns, and participant preferences have produced repeated disputes.

Remote access reduces burden but can complicate measurements, laboratory testing, and personal counselling.

The 185-percent ceiling reaches working families but excludes some families facing high living costs.
Participation data
Caseload counts measure people receiving benefits, not every eligible person or every service outcome.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

WIC remained active under Section 17 of the Child Nutrition Act and 7 C.F.R. Part 246.

The 2026–2027 income guidelines had taken effect on July 1, 2026.

Most provisions of the 2024 food-package final rule had reached their implementation deadline, while the yogurt vitamin D specification remained scheduled for April 26, 2027.

Temporary higher fluid-milk allowances remained in force through September 30, 2026.

Fiscal year 2025 data showed average monthly participation of approximately 6.9 million people and total federal spending of approximately $7.7 billion.

Why the Women, Infants, and Children Program Matters

WIC targets nutrition during pregnancy, infancy, and early childhood—periods when inadequate nutrition can affect birth outcomes, growth, cognition, and long-term health.

Its design differs from general income assistance. A clinic identifies risk, prescribes supplemental foods, provides education and breastfeeding support, and connects the participant with health care.

WIC also demonstrates the strengths and limitations of cooperative federalism. National law creates a common health and nutrition framework, while state and tribal agencies determine food lists, clinic systems, vendors, and many delivery details.

The programme’s continuing challenge is to preserve scientific nutrition standards and integrity while making certification, shopping, breastfeeding support, formula access, and health referrals practical for millions of families.

Summary

The Women, Infants, and Children Program is the federal maternal and child nutrition programme officially known as the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children.

Congress authorised WIC as a pilot in 1972, the first clinic opened in 1974, and the programme became permanent in 1975. Its authority is Section 17 of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966.

WIC serves income-eligible pregnant, breastfeeding, and postpartum participants, infants, and children younger than five who are determined to be at nutritional risk. Medicaid, SNAP, or qualifying TANF participation can establish income eligibility, but nutritional risk and categorical eligibility remain necessary.

Participants receive tailored supplemental foods through eWIC, nutrition education, breastfeeding support, breast pumps where appropriate, health screening, and referrals.

In fiscal year 2025, WIC served approximately 6.9 million people in an average month and spent approximately $7.7 billion. Children ages one through four formed the largest participant group.

The 2024 food-package final rule expanded fruits, vegetables, whole grains, cultural choice, substitutions, and breastfeeding support. In fiscal year 2026, the monthly fruit-and-vegetable benefit was $26 for children, $48 for pregnant and postpartum participants, and $52 for breastfeeding participants.

As of August 2, 2026, most food-package revisions were implemented, 2026–2027 income guidelines were in force, and temporary higher fluid-milk allowances remained effective through September 30, 2026.
