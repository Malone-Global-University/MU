Emergency Rental Assistance Program

The temporary United States Treasury pandemic program that channelled more than $46 billion through state, local, territorial, and Tribal governments to help low-income renters pay rent, rental arrears, utilities, home-energy costs, relocation expenses, and housing-stability services.

Terminology Note

The Emergency Rental Assistance Program, commonly abbreviated ERA, consisted of two legally separate federal programs administered by the United States Department of the Treasury.

ERA1 was created by section 501 of Division N of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 and appropriated $25 billion.

ERA2 was created by section 3201 of the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 and appropriated $21.55 billion.

ERA was not a permanent housing subsidy, a federally guaranteed loan, or a direct entitlement administered uniformly from Washington. Treasury awarded funds to eligible governmental grantees, which designed and operated local programs within federal requirements.

The programs should not be confused with Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing, the Emergency Solutions Grants Program, the Emergency Rental Assistance Demonstration of the Great Recession, the Homeowner Assistance Fund, FEMA shelter programs, or state and local rental-aid programs financed from other sources.

The phrase rental assistance included payments to landlords, utility providers, or tenants for qualifying housing costs. It also included housing-stability services such as eviction diversion, legal assistance, counselling, case management, and housing navigation.

ERA1 and ERA2 are closed programs. ERA1 periods of performance ended in 2022. ERA2's period of performance ended September 30, 2025. As of August 2, 2026, ERA grantees could no longer use award funds to provide new assistance to renters.

Overview

Congress created ERA during the winter of 2020–2021 as millions of renters accumulated arrears because of unemployment, reduced hours, illness, caregiving obligations, school closures, business shutdowns, and other pandemic disruptions.

Temporary eviction moratoria delayed many removals but did not cancel rent. Tenants continued to owe rent, landlords continued to face mortgage and operating costs, and utility arrears accumulated.

ERA provided federal funds to governmental grantees that built or expanded local rental-assistance systems.

The programs paid landlords and utility providers, reimbursed eligible tenants, financed relocation and security deposits, supported eviction-court diversion, and funded legal and case-management services.

ERA1 was designed for urgent short-term deployment. ERA2 added a longer period of performance, more flexible tenant payments, additional administrative funding, and eventual authority for affordable rental housing and eviction-prevention investment.

Treasury repeatedly revised guidance to reduce documentary barriers, permit self-attestation in defined circumstances, encourage direct tenant applications, enable bulk landlord and utility payments, and accelerate assistance.

Because local programs differed in forms, priorities, documentation, payment methods, and landlord conditions, a household's experience depended substantially on its jurisdiction.

Classification and Scope

This page classifies ERA under Government Programs because it was a temporary but nationally significant federal assistance system with statutory appropriations, beneficiary standards, governmental grantees, allowable uses, reporting, reallocation, auditing, and closeout.

The Department of the Treasury and Office of Capital Access belong under Institutions.

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 and American Rescue Plan Act belong under Laws and Legislation.

The COVID-19 pandemic, national eviction moratorium, and associated Supreme Court litigation belong under Historical Events and Legal Cases.

Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing, Emergency Solutions Grants, Homeowner Assistance Fund, Low Income Home Energy Assistance, and long-term affordable-housing programs warrant separate program pages.

Historical Context

Before 2020, the United States lacked a permanent national entitlement providing emergency cash assistance for rental arrears.

Federal rental policy relied primarily on long-term HUD subsidies, public housing, homelessness grants, community-development funds, and smaller state or local emergency programs.

Most low-income renters eligible for long-term housing assistance did not receive it because appropriations were insufficient to serve all eligible households.

Many renters entered the pandemic already paying a high share of income for housing and holding little emergency savings.

Black, Latino, Native, female-headed, disabled, immigrant, and low-wage households faced disproportionate exposure to job loss, rent burden, illness, and eviction risk.

The unprecedented national emergency produced an equally unprecedented federal rental-arrears intervention.

COVID-19 Rental Housing Crisis

The pandemic closed businesses, reduced employment, disrupted schooling and childcare, and increased household costs.

Renters were more likely than homeowners to have low incomes, work in affected service industries, and lack accumulated housing equity.

By late 2020, national surveys indicated that a substantial share of renters were behind on rent and feared eviction.

Landlords, particularly small property owners, experienced unpaid rent while remaining responsible for mortgages, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, and employees.

Utility shutoff moratoria protected some households temporarily but allowed arrears to grow.

Eviction Moratoria

Federal, state, local, and judicial eviction restrictions operated during portions of the pandemic.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued a national moratorium on specified residential evictions for nonpayment.

Moratoria delayed eviction proceedings or enforcement but generally did not forgive rent or eliminate lawful landlord claims.

ERA supplied money intended to resolve arrears accumulated during the protected period.

The Supreme Court ended the CDC's extended nationwide moratorium in August 2021, increasing pressure to accelerate rental assistance and court diversion.

Creation of ERA1

Congress enacted ERA1 on December 27, 2020 as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021.

The law appropriated $25 billion for fiscal year 2021.

Treasury launched the program on January 5, 2021 and began distributing funds rapidly to nearly seven hundred eligible governmental grantees.

ERA1 used a large population-based allocation structure adapted from the Coronavirus Relief Fund.

It included states, qualifying local governments, territories, Indian Tribes or their designated housing entities, Alaska Native corporations under the statutory definition, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

ERA1 Funding Reservations

The $25 billion appropriation included:

  • $400 million reserved for Puerto Rico and the other United States territories;
  • $800 million reserved for Tribal communities and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands;
  • $15 million for Treasury administration and grantee technical assistance; and
  • $6.5 million separately appropriated for Treasury Inspector General oversight and recoupment.

Remaining funds were allocated to states, the District of Columbia, and qualifying local governments.

Creation of ERA2

Congress enacted ERA2 on March 11, 2021 through the American Rescue Plan Act.

ERA2 appropriated $21.55 billion and extended federal rental support beyond ERA1's initial emergency horizon.

The program used many ERA1 administrative structures but altered allocation, payment, eligibility, documentation, and permissible-use provisions.

ERA2 did not provide a new direct Tribal allocation equivalent to ERA1.

States and local governments could continue administering assistance through the same agency, contractors, housing authorities, nonprofit subrecipients, or coordinated systems.

ERA2 Funding Reservations

The $21.55 billion ERA2 appropriation reserved:

  • $305 million for Puerto Rico and the other territories;
  • $30 million for Treasury administration, financial assistance, technical assistance, and grantee support;
  • $3 million for Treasury Inspector General oversight; and
  • $2.5 billion for high-need grantees.

High-need factors included severe rent burden among very-low-income renters, substandard or overcrowded housing, rental-market costs, and employment change after February 2020.

Eligible Grantees

States and District of Columbia

Every state and the District of Columbia received an allocation, subject to statutory formulas and minimums.

Local Governments

Counties, cities, towns, townships, villages, parishes, boroughs, and other qualifying general-purpose local governments with more than 200,000 residents could elect direct funding.

Territories

Puerto Rico, United States Virgin Islands, Guam, Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa received reserved allocations.

Tribal Communities

ERA1 included Indian Tribes, Tribally Designated Housing Entities, Alaska Native corporations within the statutory definition, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

State and Local Program Overlap

Direct local allocations reduced the amount otherwise available to the state under the allocation formula.

A renter could therefore live within a city or county operating its own program while the state operated a separate program elsewhere.

Treasury encouraged overlapping grantees to coordinate eligibility, outreach, duplicate-payment controls, utility payments, and public information.

Some local governments declined direct ERA2 allocations and transferred responsibility to their state or another locality.

Fragmentation sometimes confused tenants, landlords, courts, and service providers.

Local Program Delivery

Grantees could administer ERA directly or use public agencies, housing authorities, nonprofit organisations, contractors, community groups, legal-service providers, and intergovernmental agreements.

Local programs established application portals, call centres, paper applications, landlord systems, payment processes, outreach, appeals, prioritisation, fraud controls, and court partnerships.

Subrecipients were subject to federal subaward management and monitoring requirements.

Contractors were subject to federal procurement standards.

The governmental grantee remained responsible to Treasury for all award funds, including amounts administered by outside entities.

Household Eligibility

A household generally had to satisfy four elements:

  1. an obligation to pay rent on a residential dwelling;
  2. qualifying unemployment or pandemic-related financial hardship;
  3. risk of homelessness or housing instability; and
  4. income within the applicable low-income limit.

Grantees had discretion to define reasonable evidence and adopt consistent policies where Treasury guidance did not dictate a single method.

Housing-stability services under ERA2 could be provided without proving every household financial-assistance eligibility element when no ERA2 financial assistance was paid.

Rental Obligation

The household had to be obligated to pay rent on a residential dwelling.

Eligible arrangements included conventional leases and other lawful rental or subrental obligations documented under local program policy.

A household was not required to have occupied the unit when the national emergency began.

The program did not require an eviction filing.

Homeowners seeking mortgage assistance were not eligible for ERA household payments.

Pandemic Financial Hardship

Qualifying circumstances included unemployment benefits, reduced household income, significant costs, or other financial hardship.

ERA1 required the hardship to result directly or indirectly from the COVID-19 outbreak.

ERA2 included hardship experienced during the pandemic as well as hardship caused directly or indirectly by it.

Examples included job loss, reduced hours, business interruption, illness, medical costs, childcare needs, caregiving, increased household expenses, or inability to work because of public-health restrictions.

Written self-attestation could be used under grantee procedures.

Risk of Homelessness or Housing Instability

Evidence could include:

  • past-due rent;
  • past-due utility or home-energy bills;
  • an eviction notice;
  • a utility shutoff notice;
  • unsafe or unhealthy living conditions;
  • overcrowding;
  • housing displacement;
  • risk of losing a current unit;
  • difficulty securing replacement housing; or
  • other evidence accepted under the grantee's policies.

Income Limits

ERA1 limited assistance to households at or below 80 percent of area median income.

ERA2 used the Housing Act definition of a low-income family, which generally applies the same 80-percent ceiling with HUD adjustments and program rules.

Area median income varies by location and household size.

HUD annually publishes income limits used by housing programs.

A household could qualify using annual income for the relevant calendar year or current monthly income under a grantee's approved method.

Current Monthly Income Method

ERA1 permitted a grantee to annualise current monthly household income.

When eligibility was based on current monthly income, the grantee had to redetermine income every three months during continuing assistance.

ERA2 permitted reasonable methods and generally treated continued use of an ERA1 method as compliant.

This method helped households whose annual pre-pandemic tax records overstated their current ability to pay rent.

Documentation Flexibility

Treasury encouraged grantees to reduce unnecessary barriers while maintaining a reasonable basis for eligibility.

Evidence could include:

  • pay stubs;
  • tax returns;
  • W-2 forms;
  • bank statements;
  • unemployment records;
  • employer statements;
  • benefit-program determinations;
  • caseworker attestations;
  • lease and landlord records;
  • utility bills;
  • eviction notices;
  • self-attestations; and
  • fact-specific income proxies.

Self-Attestation

Written self-attestation could establish pandemic hardship, housing instability, and—in specified circumstances—income.

Grantees had to state when self-attestation was permitted and maintain validation or fraud-prevention procedures.

Where self-attestation established most household income, periodic reassessment could be required.

Self-attestation was intended to serve households without conventional wage records, tax filings, internet access, or cooperative landlords.

It did not eliminate the grantee's responsibility to make and document an eligibility determination.

Categorical Eligibility

Grantees could rely on recent income determinations made by other federal, state, or local assistance programs.

A determination letter showing income at or below the relevant limit could reduce duplicate verification.

The underlying determination generally had to have been made on or after January 1, 2020.

Programs using categorical eligibility still had to verify the remaining ERA requirements.

Fact-Specific Proxies

A grantee could combine an applicant's income attestation with a reasonable proxy, such as average income in the household's census tract.

Proxies helped streamline applications from high-poverty areas or populations with limited documentation.

A proxy had to be connected reasonably to the household's circumstances and applied through a documented policy.

Geographic poverty alone did not establish every other eligibility condition.

Priority Households

Grantees had to prioritise:

  • households with income below 50 percent of area median income; and
  • households containing a person unemployed for at least ninety days before application.

Grantees were required to document and publish their priority system.

Local programs could establish further lawful priorities, including eviction risk, imminent utility shutoff, disability, homelessness risk, or other urgent conditions.

Prioritisation did not permit arbitrary denial of otherwise eligible applicants.

Citizenship and Immigration Status

The federal ERA statutes did not condition household eligibility on citizenship or immigration status.

Treasury guidance therefore did not require Social Security numbers or proof of lawful immigration as general household eligibility elements.

Grantees still had to verify identity, residency, rent, and other eligibility information through reasonable procedures.

Language access and trusted-community outreach were particularly important for immigrant households concerned about interacting with government programs.

Rent and Rental Arrears

ERA could pay current rent, past-due rent, and prospective rent.

Arrears accrued before March 13, 2020 were generally ineligible.

ERA1 required a grantee providing prospective rent to address existing rental arrears as well.

ERA2 did not impose the same arrears-first rule.

A grantee could pay all eligible arrears or establish a lower programme cap.

Prospective Rent

Households did not need arrears to receive prospective assistance.

ERA1 permitted no more than three months of prospective rent based on one application.

Additional prospective months required another application and available funds.

Prospective payments could stabilise households after arrears were resolved, support relocation, or prevent a new delinquency.

Eighteen-Month Aggregate Limit

ERA1 generally provided as many as twelve months plus three additional months when necessary.

ERA2 financial assistance, combined with any ERA1 assistance, could not exceed eighteen months.

The limit applied to financial assistance rather than every contact with a housing-stability service provider.

Grantees had to prevent duplicate coverage of the same month and expense across overlapping programs.

Utilities and Home-Energy Costs

ERA could pay current and past-due utility and home-energy expenses.

Eligible services could include:

  • electricity;
  • gas;
  • water and sewer;
  • trash removal;
  • heating oil;
  • wood or other home fuel; and
  • other separately stated residential utility costs accepted by the grantee.

Telecommunications services were not treated as ordinary statutory utilities, though internet costs could qualify as another housing-related expense under defined conditions.

A household could receive utility assistance without receiving rent assistance.

Other Housing-Related Expenses

Treasury defined additional eligible costs connected to maintaining or obtaining housing.

Depending on grantee policy, these could include:

  • relocation expenses;
  • security deposits;
  • reasonable application or screening fees;
  • hotel or motel stays when displacement made them necessary;
  • internet service used for distance learning, telework, telemedicine, or government services;
  • rental bonds;
  • late fees that were lawful and documented;
  • utility deposits;
  • moving costs; and
  • other necessary housing expenses approved under Treasury guidance.

Relocation and New Housing

ERA could help a household move after eviction, displacement, unsafe housing, domestic violence, landlord refusal, or loss of a prior unit.

Assistance could cover security deposits, prospective rent, utility deposits, moving expenses, application fees, and temporary lodging.

A household without a current lease could receive a commitment letter stating the assistance available if it entered a qualifying lease.

This tool helped applicants convince prospective landlords that rent and deposits would be paid.

Security Deposits

Grantees could pay a reasonable security deposit.

Treasury generally advised that the amount should not exceed one month's rent unless a higher amount was customary and reasonable in the local market.

Grantees were expected to adopt a policy governing return and disposition of deposits.

A minimum rental period of at least four months could be required before the tenant became entitled to a returned ERA-funded deposit.

Landlord Applications

A landlord could assist a tenant or apply on the tenant's behalf.

ERA1 required tenant signature, provision of application documentation to the tenant, and use of payment to satisfy the tenant's rental obligation.

Landlord participation could improve documentation and payment speed.

It also created a risk that tenants would not know an application had been filed or how funds were credited.

Grantees were responsible for protecting tenant information and preventing duplicate or unauthorised claims.

Tenant Applications

Treasury strongly encouraged ERA1 programs to allow tenants to apply directly.

ERA2 required a direct tenant application option even when a landlord refused to participate.

Tenant applications were critical where landlords were unresponsive, refused conditions, sought eviction, lacked records, or would not accept government payment.

Programs could not operate solely as landlord reimbursement systems under ERA2.

Payments to Landlords and Utility Providers

Grantees ordinarily paid landlords and utility providers directly on behalf of eligible households.

The household had to receive documentation of payments made on its behalf.

Landlords and providers had to commit to apply funds to the eligible household's obligation.

Grantees could require confirmation that paid arrears would be treated as satisfied and no longer reported as outstanding.

Direct Payments to Tenants

Under ERA1, a grantee could pay the household after making reasonable efforts to obtain landlord or utility-provider cooperation.

Treasury treated outreach as complete through specified mailed, telephone, text, email, or written-refusal procedures.

ERA2 did not require prior landlord or utility outreach before paying a tenant directly.

The tenant had to agree to use the payment for an eligible purpose.

If a landlord later refused the tenant's payment, the grantee could permit another eligible housing use under its policies.

Landlord Conditions and Eviction Protection

A landlord receiving ERA funds could not evict the tenant for nonpayment during the period covered by the assistance.

Treasury encouraged grantees to require an additional thirty-to-ninety-day nonpayment-eviction restriction.

Programs could require waiver or reduction of late fees, dismissal of pending cases, acceptance of payment as full satisfaction, or other lawful terms.

ERA did not invalidate legitimate eviction grounds unrelated to the paid nonpayment obligation.

Arrears after the Tenant Vacated

ERA could pay qualifying rental or utility arrears after the tenant left the unit.

Clearing old debt could remove barriers to new housing, improve credit records, and end collection.

Treasury encouraged grantees to require the landlord or utility provider to cease further collection for amounts paid and confirm resolution to credit-reporting agencies.

Duplication of Benefits

ERA1 prohibited payment of costs already covered by another federal rental-assistance source.

Grantees could rely on applicant attestations and coordination with overlapping jurisdictions.

ERA2 contained different statutory language, but Treasury continued to encourage prevention of duplicate assistance.

Duplicate payment of the same rent month, utility bill, or housing expense could become an improper payment.

Federally Subsidised Housing

Households receiving Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing assistance, or other rental subsidies could qualify for ERA for the tenant-paid portion of rent and eligible utilities.

ERA could not duplicate the subsidy paid by HUD or another program.

Grantees were encouraged to partner with public housing agencies and subsidised owners to reach tenants in arrears.

ERA assistance did not alter the household's underlying subsidy rights or obligations.

Housing Stability Services

Both ERA1 and ERA2 permitted up to 10 percent of an award for services helping households maintain or obtain housing.

Services could include:

  • eviction prevention;
  • eviction diversion;
  • landlord–tenant mediation;
  • housing counselling;
  • fair-housing counselling;
  • housing navigation;
  • case management;
  • legal services and attorney fees;
  • services for survivors of domestic violence or human trafficking;
  • services for seniors or people with disabilities;
  • benefit coordination; and
  • other housing-stability support.

ERA1 and ERA2 Service Differences

ERA1 housing-stability services had to serve eligible households and relate to the COVID-19 outbreak.

ERA2 services did not have to be related to COVID-19.

ERA2 also did not restrict service-only assistance to households meeting every financial-assistance eligibility element.

These differences allowed local governments to use ERA2 for broader eviction-prevention infrastructure near the end of the program.

Eviction Diversion

Grantees integrated ERA with courts, clerks, judges, sheriffs, legal-aid organisations, landlord attorneys, mediators, and housing agencies.

Programs could screen cases before judgment, pause proceedings while applications were processed, negotiate repayment or dismissal, and issue payments directly through court partnerships.

Diversion systems were intended to prevent displacement, judgments, collections, and damage to tenant records.

Their effectiveness depended on local law, landlord participation, application speed, court procedures, and available funds.

Legal Services

ERA could pay attorney fees and related legal services connected to eviction proceedings and housing stability.

Legal assistance helped tenants understand notices, assert defences, negotiate with landlords, correct accounting errors, preserve subsidies, address unlawful lockouts, and obtain reasonable accommodations.

ERA did not create a universal right to counsel.

Local programs selected providers and defined service coverage within federal grant rules.

Survivors of Domestic Violence, Sexual Assault, and Trafficking

Housing-stability services could support survivors needing confidential relocation, legal assistance, safety planning, deposits, rental aid, and navigation.

ERA1 reporting law required confidentiality protections for information concerning intimate-partner violence, sexual assault, and stalking.

Programs had to balance identity and eligibility verification with survivor safety and privacy.

Disability Access

Grantees were required to comply with federal disability nondiscrimination and accessibility law.

Reasonable accommodations could address documentation, online portals, communication, application assistance, deadlines, and service delivery.

Housing-stability funds could support specialised navigation and services helping disabled renters maintain accessible housing.

Treasury allowed documentation exceptions for disability and other extenuating circumstances while preserving the eligibility determination.

Language Access and Community Outreach

Programs used translated materials, bilingual staff, community navigators, promotoras, faith organisations, tenant groups, schools, healthcare systems, utilities, and door-to-door outreach.

Language and digital barriers could prevent eligible households from learning about or completing assistance.

Community-based subrecipients often had stronger trust and contact with affected populations than central government agencies.

Outreach expenditures had to be connected to eligible administration or housing-stability activity.

Digital Access and Application Portals

Many grantees created online portals because offices were closed or operating remotely.

Portals accelerated document submission and status tracking for some applicants.

They excluded or burdened others without broadband, computers, smartphones, scanners, email, literacy, or technical assistance.

Effective programs maintained telephone, paper, in-person, disability-accessible, and community-partner options.

Utility Partnerships

Grantees worked with utilities to verify arrears, stop disconnections, restore service, perform outreach, and process payments.

Bulk payments could speed assistance where providers served many eligible households.

Treasury permitted estimated bulk payments under conditions requiring later household documentation, provider certification, and repayment of ineligible amounts.

Grantees were encouraged to coordinate ERA with LIHEAP and other energy programs.

Bulk Landlord and Utility Payments

A grantee could combine assistance for several households into one payment.

Estimated payments required a reasonable provider estimate, later collection of household documentation, and a written agreement to return amounts associated with ineligible households.

Treasury encouraged partial estimated payments to limit federal loss.

Bulk processing reduced transaction time but increased reconciliation and improper-payment risk.

ERA1 Funding Restrictions

At least 90 percent of ERA1 funds had to provide direct financial assistance.

No more than 10 percent could support housing-stability services.

Administrative costs were limited to 10 percent.

Housing-stability services had to relate to COVID-19 and serve eligible households.

ERA1 did not authorise long-term affordable-housing construction or preservation.

ERA2 Funding Restrictions

ERA2 supported financial assistance, housing-stability services, administrative costs, and—after specified conditions were met—affordable rental housing and eviction-prevention purposes.

Housing-stability services remained limited to 10 percent.

Administrative expenses were limited to 15 percent.

ERA2 provided greater flexibility for direct tenant payment and service-only households.

Administrative Costs

Allowable administrative expenses included staff, application systems, call centres, payment processing, data collection, reporting, fraud controls, outreach, contractors, subrecipient oversight, legal review, and closeout.

Direct and indirect costs counted toward the statutory ceiling.

A subrecipient could spend more than the percentage of its individual subaward if total grantee and subrecipient administrative costs remained within the overall federal cap.

Administrative expenses had to be reasonable, allocable, documented, and connected to the award.

ERA2 Affordable Rental Housing Authority

ERA2 allowed unobligated funds remaining on or after October 1, 2022 to support affordable rental housing and eviction-prevention purposes after the grantee obligated at least 75 percent of its adjusted allocation to financial assistance, housing-stability services, and eligible administration.

Eligible uses included construction, rehabilitation, preservation, real-property acquisition, predevelopment, and operation of qualifying projects.

Projects had to serve very-low-income families and conform to one of several recognised federal affordable-housing program frameworks.

The authority converted a portion of temporary emergency aid into longer-term housing supply and eviction-prevention investment.

Affordable Housing Conditions

ERA2-supported units had to be restricted to very-low-income families.

The income restriction had to be enforced through a covenant, land-use restriction agreement, or comparable legal requirement for at least twenty years.

Projects had to align with requirements of programs such as:

  • Low-Income Housing Tax Credit;
  • HOME Investment Partnerships;
  • HOME-ARP;
  • Housing Trust Fund;
  • Public Housing Capital Fund;
  • Indian Housing Block Grant;
  • Section 202 housing for elderly persons;
  • Section 811 housing for persons with disabilities;
  • USDA Farm Labor Housing; or
  • USDA multifamily preservation programs.

Forms of Affordable Housing Financing

Grantees could provide ERA2 funds through grants, loans, deferred-payment loans, no-interest loans, interest subsidies, and other approved arrangements.

ERA2 funds could not establish or capitalise a revolving loan fund or other structured fund.

Mixed-income projects could receive ERA2 funding only for the proportion of development cost attributable to qualifying very-low-income units.

Program income received after September 30, 2025 must continue to support eligible affordable rental housing or eviction-prevention purposes.

Reallocation of Funds

Congress authorised Treasury to recapture funds from slow-spending grantees and reallocate them to jurisdictions demonstrating need and capacity.

ERA1 reallocation began after September 30, 2021.

Treasury also established ERA2 tranches, voluntary reallocations, recapture, high-need payments, and additional payment conditions.

Reallocation sought to prevent federal funds from expiring in jurisdictions with limited demand or administrative capacity while renters elsewhere faced exhausted programs.

Treasury ultimately reallocated billions of dollars.

ERA2 Payment Tranches

Treasury initially paid no less than 40 percent of each ERA2 allocation.

Additional tranches depended on procedures established by Treasury and generally required substantial obligation of previously disbursed funds.

The statutory structure encouraged rapid deployment while reducing the amount held by grantees that were unable to spend.

Tranche and reallocation rules changed as the program matured.

End of ERA1

ERA1 initial-allocation funds generally remained available through September 30, 2022.

Grantees receiving reallocated funds could use them through December 29, 2022.

Grantees then reconciled expenditures, liquidated obligations, returned unspent or disallowed funds, submitted final reports, and retained records for audit.

Treasury reported that all ERA1 awards were closed by January 31, 2025.

End of ERA2

ERA2's period of performance ended September 30, 2025.

After that date, grantees could no longer provide renter financial assistance, housing-stability services, or new affordable-housing and eviction-prevention obligations using ERA2 award funds.

Obligations incurred by the deadline had to be liquidated during closeout.

Final reports were due January 28, 2026.

Treasury continued closeout review, financial confirmation, audit resolution, and recovery of remaining balances.

Current Status in 2026

ERA no longer accepts household applications or provides new federal ERA payments.

Current Treasury work includes:

  • ERA2 final-report review;
  • financial confirmation;
  • closeout notices;
  • return of unobligated and unexpended funds;
  • audit and Single Audit follow-up;
  • improper-payment correction;
  • fraud referrals and recovery;
  • record retention;
  • property disposition; and
  • oversight of continuing affordable-housing program income.

Renters needing current assistance must use non-ERA federal, state, local, charitable, legal-aid, or housing-agency resources.

Closeout Process

Grantees reconcile the federal award and confirm that every reported expenditure was allowable, supported, and incurred within the performance period.

Closeout requires grantees to:

  • liquidate valid obligations;
  • identify unobligated and unspent balances;
  • reconcile payment and household data;
  • resolve subrecipient and contractor balances;
  • report equipment, supplies, and real property;
  • submit final financial and program reports;
  • return funds owed to Treasury;
  • address improper payments; and
  • retain records for later review.

Record Retention

Closeout does not end the grantee's recordkeeping duties.

Grantees and subrecipients must retain financial, household, eligibility, payment, procurement, property, subaward, audit, and compliance records for the period required by federal law and award terms.

Records associated with litigation, audit, claim, investigation, or unresolved findings must be retained longer when required.

Personally identifiable information remains subject to privacy and security protections.

Program Reporting

ERA1 law required Treasury to publish quarterly information concerning:

  • households assisted;
  • application acceptance rates;
  • types of assistance;
  • average household payment;
  • household income category;
  • average number of months covered; and
  • race, ethnicity, and gender of primary applicants.

ERA2 reporting expanded to cumulative financial, household, demographic, subaward, housing-stability, affordable-housing, and compliance information.

Privacy and Data Security

Grantees had to protect applicant and household information.

Personally identifiable information was to be collected and used for lawful program, reporting, audit, and research purposes.

Special confidentiality protection applied to survivors of intimate-partner violence, sexual assault, and stalking.

Grantees were responsible for securing portals, contractors, subrecipients, payment files, bank information, and document storage.

Incomplete and Inconsistent Data

Treasury did not receive complete household and payment data during important periods of program operation.

GAO found that data were missing for 26 percent of household payments reported for 2021.

Later cumulative reporting allowed grantees to correct prior submissions, but gaps remained.

Treasury subsequently completed ERA1 closeout data for all but one required grantee and reported substantially improved ERA2 submission coverage.

Data limitations complicate precise estimates of unique households, demographic distribution, duplicate payments, and program outcomes.

Improper Payments

An improper payment includes assistance paid to an ineligible household, for an ineligible cost, in the wrong amount, without required support, or in duplication of another payment.

Grantees remain responsible for improper payments made by themselves, subrecipients, contractors, landlords, utilities, and tenant-payment processes.

Treasury recognised that recovering nonfraudulent mistakes directly from low-income tenants could create renewed housing instability.

Guidance therefore permitted alternative correction methods under specified circumstances.

Improper-Payment Remedies after Program End

After both periods of performance ended, grantees lost several corrective options that depended on making new ERA expenditures.

Remaining remedies generally include:

  • identifying compliant assistance previously paid with the grantee's own funds during the applicable performance period and recharacterising it when allowed; or
  • returning an equivalent amount of nonfederal funds to Treasury.

Fraudulent payments require appropriate recovery, mandatory disclosure, and referral rather than accommodation as an innocent administrative error.

Fraud Risks

Fraud schemes could include:

  • false leases;
  • invented tenants or landlords;
  • stolen identities;
  • inflated arrears;
  • duplicate applications in several jurisdictions;
  • false income or unemployment claims;
  • fabricated utility bills;
  • payments diverted from landlords;
  • landlords failing to credit tenant accounts;
  • conflicts of interest;
  • kickbacks;
  • contractor overbilling;
  • misuse of administrative funds; and
  • collusion among applicants, owners, employees, or vendors.

Fraud Detection and Response

Grantees were expected to maintain reasonable fraud-prevention and validation procedures.

Controls included:

  • identity verification;
  • property-ownership verification;
  • duplicate-payment matching;
  • lease and utility review;
  • bank-account validation;
  • conflict-of-interest policies;
  • data analytics;
  • hotlines;
  • post-payment review;
  • subrecipient monitoring;
  • provider repayment agreements; and
  • referral to Treasury OIG and law enforcement.

Treasury Inspector General Oversight

The ERA1 statute specifically directed the Treasury Inspector General to monitor receipt, disbursement, and use of funds.

Misused amounts could be recorded as debts owed by the governmental grantee to the United States.

Treasury OIG receives fraud, waste, and abuse reports, conducts investigations and audits, and coordinates with federal prosecutors and other inspectors general.

Grantees must report potential federal criminal violations involving fraud, bribery, or gratuities under the Uniform Guidance.

Single Audit and Compliance Supplement

ERA expenditures were subject to federal Single Audit requirements for eligible governmental and nonprofit recipients.

The annual Office of Management and Budget Compliance Supplement identified program objectives, eligible uses, reporting, period of performance, reallocation, subrecipient monitoring, and other compliance requirements.

Audit findings could result in questioned costs, corrective action, recovery, and additional Treasury review.

Closeout did not prevent later audit of funds spent during the program.

Government Accountability Office Findings

GAO found that many grantees were slow to begin payments because they lacked staff, technology, administrative systems, clear guidance, and efficient documentation procedures.

GAO also found incomplete reporting, weaknesses in Treasury's improper-payment risk assessment, and reallocations that did not always align consistently with renter need.

Treasury later improved cumulative reporting, completed risk-assessment work, and closed GAO recommendations.

The findings illustrate the difficulty of creating a large national benefit through hundreds of newly built local programs during an emergency.

Reported Program Scale

Treasury's January 2025 ERA1 closeout report stated that ERA1 deployed $23.6 billion through approximately eight million payments and served more than three million households.

As of September 30, 2025, Treasury reported approximately sixteen million combined ERA1 and ERA2 payments totalling $42.8 billion in financial assistance and housing-stability support.

A payment is not the same as a unique household. One household could receive several monthly rent, utility, or other payments.

Total appropriations exceeded household financial-assistance expenditures because funding also supported administration, services, affordable-housing activity, Inspector General oversight, reallocations, and returned balances.

Demographic Reach

Treasury reported that ERA disproportionately reached very-low-income renters and communities at elevated risk of eviction.

Published program data indicated large shares of assistance went to Black and Latino applicants and female-headed households.

More than 60 percent of early ERA1 household payments went to households at or below 30 percent of area median income.

Demographic findings must be interpreted with caution because not every grantee reported complete household data.

Effects on Eviction

Eviction filings did not produce the national surge widely predicted for the end of pandemic moratoria.

ERA operated alongside eviction moratoria, expanded unemployment benefits, stimulus payments, legal protections, state programs, economic recovery, and landlord forbearance.

Research cited by Treasury and GAO indicates that ERA contributed to lower eviction risk and reached census tracts with high pre-pandemic eviction rates.

It is difficult to isolate the precise share of avoided evictions attributable only to ERA.

Effects on Landlords

ERA converted unpaid tenant obligations into government payments and helped property owners meet mortgages, taxes, utilities, insurance, maintenance, and payroll.

Landlord participation improved when programs simplified documentation, communicated processing times, provided bulk options, and avoided excessive conditions.

Some landlords refused participation because of delays, paperwork, required tenant cooperation, eviction restrictions, fee waivers, or distrust.

Direct tenant-payment authority reduced a landlord's ability to block household access.

Effects on Utilities

ERA paid accumulated electric, gas, water, sewer, and home-energy arrears.

Utility partnerships prevented shutoffs, restored service, reduced uncollectible accounts, and coordinated customers with LIHEAP.

Bulk payment and data-sharing arrangements increased speed but required privacy and reconciliation controls.

Tribal ERA1 Programs

ERA1 reserved $800 million for Indian Tribes, Tribally Designated Housing Entities, Alaska Native corporations included by statute, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

Tribal programs could serve eligible Tribal members outside Tribal lands and eligible nonmembers living on Tribal lands, provided assistance was not duplicated.

Programs addressed rent, utilities, remoteness, limited housing stock, overcrowding, infrastructure deficiencies, and administrative capacity.

ERA2 did not include a comparable Tribal allocation, making ERA1 the principal Treasury rental-assistance appropriation specifically directed to Tribal communities.

Racial and Economic Equity

The pandemic intensified pre-existing racial disparities in rent burden, employment, health exposure, savings, housing quality, and eviction.

ERA eligibility was based on income, pandemic hardship, rental obligation, and housing instability rather than race.

Grantees used targeted outreach, language access, geographic proxies, and partnerships to reach communities facing high eviction risk.

Program data suggested substantial delivery to communities of colour, although incomplete reporting limited certainty.

Women, Children, and Female-Headed Households

Female-headed households experienced high exposure to rent burden, job loss, childcare disruption, and eviction.

Treasury reported that women headed a large share of households receiving assistance.

Preventing displacement helped preserve school continuity, employment access, healthcare, custody arrangements, and family stability.

Housing-stability programs also supported survivors of domestic abuse and trafficking.

Program Achievements

  • Created the largest federal emergency rental-arrears program in United States history.
  • Delivered approximately sixteen million payments by the end of ERA2.
  • Paid rent, utilities, energy costs, and relocation expenses.
  • Reached millions of very-low-income renter households.
  • Provided funds directly through state, local, territorial, and Tribal systems.
  • Enabled direct tenant applications and payments when landlords refused participation.
  • Supported legal assistance, mediation, counselling, navigation, and case management.
  • Integrated rental assistance with eviction courts and diversion programs.
  • Helped landlords and utilities recover arrears.
  • Used self-attestation and categorical eligibility to reduce documentation barriers.
  • Reallocated billions from slower jurisdictions to areas with greater demand.
  • Supported Tribal and Hawaiian Home Lands rental assistance through ERA1.
  • Created local housing-stability infrastructure that continued beyond individual payments.
  • Allowed a portion of ERA2 funds to support long-term affordable rental housing.
  • Contributed to avoidance of the predicted national eviction wave.

Limitations and Failures

  • Programs took months to build while households faced immediate arrears.
  • Eligibility and documentation differed substantially among jurisdictions.
  • Complex applications excluded some eligible renters.
  • Online-only systems created digital-access barriers.
  • Landlord nonparticipation delayed or blocked some assistance.
  • State and local overlap caused confusion and duplicate-payment risk.
  • Administrative capacity varied widely.
  • Some grantees spent funds slowly while others exhausted allocations.
  • Treasury reporting was incomplete during major phases of the program.
  • Improper-payment risks were not assessed fully at the outset.
  • Assistance was temporary and did not solve long-term rent affordability.
  • ERA2 did not include a separate Tribal allocation.
  • Program rules changed repeatedly.
  • Many households remained ineligible because they were homeowners, lacked a rental obligation, exceeded income limits, or had arrears outside the eligible period.
  • Temporary funds could not create a permanent right to rental assistance.
  • Closing the program left local eviction-prevention systems without a comparable federal funding stream.

Major Controversies

Speed versus Verification

Simplified documentation accelerated aid but increased the risk of error, duplication, and fraud.

Landlord Conditions

Tenant advocates favoured eviction protections and fee waivers, while some landlords viewed conditions as a barrier to accepting payment.

Local Variation

Federal flexibility allowed programmes to fit local law but produced unequal access and inconsistent treatment.

Reallocation

Recapture promoted faster spending but could remove funds from jurisdictions still building capacity or facing later demand.

Data Quality

Incomplete reports limited oversight and reliable demographic conclusions.

Temporary versus Permanent Assistance

ERA demonstrated the effect of large-scale rental assistance but ended without creating a permanent emergency-rent entitlement.

Affordable Housing Conversion

Using ERA2 for long-term housing supply extended public benefit but diverted some remaining funds away from immediate household payments.

Documented Outcomes

Treasury reported $23.6 billion in ERA1 deployment, eight million payments, and service to more than three million households.

By the September 30, 2025 conclusion of ERA2, the two programs had produced approximately sixteen million payments totalling $42.8 billion.

Research found that assistance was concentrated in places with high poverty, pre-pandemic eviction filings, Black renter populations, renting households with children, and single mothers.

Eviction filings remained below historical averages during important periods after the national moratorium ended.

Treasury and GAO both concluded that ERA helped prevent evictions, while acknowledging the contribution of other pandemic policies and limitations in program data.

Historical Significance

ERA represented a major shift from relying almost entirely on eviction delay toward paying the underlying rent and utility debt.

It demonstrated that the federal government could build a national emergency-rental system through state, local, territorial, and Tribal institutions.

The program connected direct cash assistance with legal services, court reform, mediation, housing navigation, and long-term housing investment.

It also revealed the consequences of fragmented administration, incomplete data, weak national infrastructure, and unequal local capacity.

ERA remains a central model for future proposals involving eviction prevention, emergency rent insurance, universal vouchers, and permanent housing-stability systems.

Research Cautions

  • ERA1 and ERA2 were separate programs. Their grantees, deadlines, payment structures, and allowable uses differed.
  • ERA is closed. The historical Treasury program no longer accepts rental-assistance applications.
  • A payment is not a unique household. One household could receive several payments.
  • Appropriations do not equal household assistance. Funds also supported administration, services, housing development, oversight, and returned balances.
  • ERA did not forgive rent universally. It paid qualifying obligations for approved households.
  • Eviction moratoria and ERA were different. Moratoria delayed certain removals; ERA paid eligible housing costs.
  • ERA was not a HUD voucher. Treasury administered grants through temporary local programs.
  • Homeowners were ineligible. The Homeowner Assistance Fund and other programs served mortgage borrowers.
  • Local rules varied. Treasury guidance allowed substantial grantee discretion.
  • Demographic data are incomplete. Published percentages do not represent every payment.
  • ERA2 affordable-housing funds can have continuing effects. Loans and program income remain subject to eligible-use restrictions after award closeout.
  • Closeout is not the end of liability. Audits, record retention, recovery, and enforcement continue.

Definitions

Area median income
Median family income for a metropolitan area or nonmetropolitan county, adjusted by HUD for household size and other factors.
Eligible household
A renter household meeting the applicable hardship, housing-instability, and income requirements.
Eligible grantee
A governmental entity authorised by the ERA1 or ERA2 statute to receive a direct Treasury award.
ERA1
The $25 billion Emergency Rental Assistance program created by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021.
ERA2
The $21.55 billion Emergency Rental Assistance program created by the American Rescue Plan Act.
Fact-specific proxy
A reasonable indicator, such as neighbourhood income data, used with applicant attestation to support an income determination.
Financial assistance
Payments for rent, arrears, utilities, energy, and other eligible housing costs.
Housing stability services
Eviction prevention, legal help, counselling, navigation, mediation, case management, and related services helping households maintain or obtain housing.
Improper payment
A payment made in the wrong amount, to an ineligible person, for an ineligible purpose, without required support, or in duplication of another payment.
Obligation
A binding commitment such as an approved payment, contract, subaward, grant, or loan requiring later expenditure.
Period of performance
The time during which a grantee could incur eligible award obligations and perform authorised activities.
Prospective rent
Rent due for future months rather than arrears.
Reallocation
Treasury recapture or voluntary transfer of funds from one grantee and redistribution to another grantee with demonstrated need and capacity.
Rental arrears
Past-due rent owed by a tenant household.
Subrecipient
A public or nonprofit entity receiving a subaward to administer part of a grantee's ERA program.
Very-low-income family
A family generally at or below 50 percent of area median income under the United States Housing Act definition.

Summary

The Emergency Rental Assistance Program was a temporary Treasury grant system created to prevent pandemic-related eviction, utility disconnection, homelessness, and housing instability.

ERA1 appropriated $25 billion in December 2020. ERA2 added $21.55 billion in March 2021, producing a combined federal appropriation of $46.55 billion.

Treasury distributed funds to states, the District of Columbia, territories, qualifying local governments, and—under ERA1—Tribal governments, designated housing entities, Alaska Native corporations covered by the statute, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

Eligible households generally had to owe rent on a residential dwelling, experience unemployment or pandemic-related financial hardship, face housing instability, and have low income.

Households below 50 percent of area median income and households with a member unemployed for at least ninety days received statutory priority.

ERA paid rent, rental arrears, utilities, home-energy costs, relocation expenses, deposits, and other housing costs. Combined ERA1 and ERA2 financial assistance could not exceed eighteen months.

Landlords and utilities ordinarily received payment directly, but tenants could receive direct payment when providers would not participate. ERA2 required programmes to offer tenants a direct application route.

Housing-stability funds supported eviction diversion, mediation, legal services, counselling, case management, housing navigation, and specialised services for survivors, seniors, and people with disabilities.

Treasury used flexible documentation, self-attestation, categorical eligibility, fact-specific proxies, bulk payments, and reallocation to accelerate delivery.

ERA2 later allowed qualifying grantees to invest remaining funds in affordable rental housing and eviction-prevention activity serving very-low-income families.

ERA1 ended in 2022. ERA2 ended September 30, 2025. As of August 2, 2026, no ERA grantee could use award funds to provide new renter assistance.

Treasury reported approximately sixteen million combined payments totalling $42.8 billion by the end of ERA2. ERA1 alone deployed $23.6 billion through eight million payments and served more than three million households.

Research indicates that ERA reached many renters at high risk of eviction and contributed to preventing a national eviction surge.

The program also experienced slow startup, local inconsistency, documentation barriers, incomplete federal data, reallocation disputes, and improper-payment risk.

The remaining federal work involves closeout, audits, records retention, improper-payment resolution, fraud recovery, and continuing restrictions on affordable-housing program income.
