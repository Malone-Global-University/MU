Federal Public Housing Program

The federal–local housing program established during the New Deal to provide publicly owned, income-restricted rental housing for eligible low-income households.

Overview

```

The Federal Public Housing Program is the oldest continuing federal rental-assistance program in the United States. It was established by the United States Housing Act of 1937 to help state and local governments provide decent, safe, and sanitary rental housing for households whose incomes were insufficient to obtain suitable housing through the private market.

Although commonly called “federal housing,” most public housing developments are not directly owned or managed by the federal government. Local public housing agencies generally own and operate the properties. The United States Department of Housing and Urban Development provides funding, establishes national requirements, monitors performance, and enforces federal housing and civil-rights laws.

Public housing has provided long-term housing stability to millions of families, older adults, and people with disabilities. Its history also includes racial segregation, discriminatory site selection, concentrated poverty, uneven maintenance, demolition, resident displacement, and recurring disputes over local control, federal funding, public safety, tenant rights, and neighborhood integration.
```

Timeline

```
Date Development
1933 The Public Works Administration begins financing and constructing limited-dividend and publicly owned housing projects, establishing an important federal precedent for permanent public housing.
1937 The United States Housing Act of 1937 establishes the United States Housing Authority and creates the federal–local low-rent public housing system.
1942–1947 Federal housing functions are reorganized during and after World War II, eventually placing public housing administration within the Housing and Home Finance Agency.
1949 The Housing Act of 1949 declares a national housing objective and authorizes a major expansion of low-rent public housing alongside slum-clearance and urban-redevelopment programs.
1950s–1960s Thousands of public housing units are constructed. Local site-selection, tenant-assignment, and urban-development policies frequently reinforce racial and economic segregation.
1964 Title VI of the Civil Rights Act prohibits racial discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance.
1965 The Department of Housing and Urban Development is created and assumes federal responsibility for the public housing program.
1968 The Fair Housing Act prohibits specified forms of housing discrimination and gives HUD major enforcement responsibilities.
1969 The Brooke Amendment limits the amount that public housing residents can be required to contribute toward rent. The statutory percentage is later revised.
1974 Congress creates the Section 8 program, beginning a broader federal shift toward subsidizing rents in privately owned housing as well as supporting publicly owned developments.
1981 Federal legislation generally raises the principal income-based tenant contribution standard from 25 percent to 30 percent of adjusted income.
1992 Congress establishes the Urban Revitalization Demonstration, later known as HOPE VI, to redevelop severely distressed public housing.
1998 The Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act restructures major aspects of public housing policy, expands local flexibility, promotes mixed-income occupancy, changes demolition and replacement rules, and establishes the limitation commonly known as the Faircloth Amendment.
2010 Choice Neighborhoods begins as a broader neighborhood-redevelopment successor to HOPE VI.
2012 Congress authorizes the Rental Assistance Demonstration, permitting participating public housing properties to convert to long-term Section 8 assistance to finance rehabilitation and preservation.
2023 HUD begins applying the National Standards for the Physical Inspection of Real Estate to public housing, emphasizing health, safety, and functional conditions.
Present Public housing remains active, but the traditional inventory continues to face aging buildings, unmet repair needs, funding constraints, redevelopment pressures, and conversion to alternative subsidy structures.
```

Definitions

```
Public housing
Rental housing generally owned or controlled by a public housing agency and operated under the United States Housing Act of 1937 for eligible low-income households.
Public housing agency
A state-chartered, county, municipal, regional, or other public body authorized to develop or administer federally assisted housing programs. The abbreviation PHA is commonly used.
Public housing development
A building, group of buildings, or scattered-site collection of homes operated as public housing. Developments may include high-rise apartments, walk-up buildings, rowhouses, townhouses, duplexes, or detached homes.
Operating Fund
Federal formula funding intended to help public housing agencies cover the difference between tenant rental revenue and the cost of operating and maintaining public housing.
Capital Fund
Federal formula funding used for major repairs, modernization, development activities, management improvements, demolition, replacement, and other eligible capital needs.
Income-based rent
A rental calculation tied to household income and allowable deductions. Public housing rent is generally structured around statutory percentages, minimum-rent provisions, utility allowances, and other federal requirements.
Flat rent
A rent based on the rental value of the unit rather than directly on the household’s income. Eligible households may be permitted to select a flat-rent option under applicable rules.
Area median income
A local income benchmark calculated for metropolitan areas and nonmetropolitan counties and adjusted for household size. Federal housing eligibility categories are commonly expressed as percentages of area median income.
Extremely low-income household
A household whose income does not exceed the applicable statutory extremely low-income threshold, generally connected to 30 percent of area median income or the federal poverty guideline.
Faircloth limit
The statutory limitation restricting HUD from funding a net increase in traditional public housing units above the number a public housing agency owned, assisted, or operated on October 1, 1999, subject to specified exceptions and related development mechanisms.
Rental Assistance Demonstration
A federal preservation initiative allowing eligible public housing properties to convert from the traditional Operating Fund and Capital Fund structure to long-term Section 8 project-based assistance.
Mixed-finance public housing
Public housing developed or preserved using a combination of federal public housing resources and other public or private financing.
Scattered-site public housing
Public housing units distributed among multiple smaller properties rather than concentrated within one large development.
```

Historical Development

```

New Deal Origins

The modern public housing system emerged during the Great Depression, when unemployment, foreclosure, overcrowding, deteriorating housing, and collapsed private construction intensified longstanding housing problems. Early federal housing work conducted through the Public Works Administration demonstrated that the national government could finance and construct low-cost housing, but the direct federal model produced a limited number of developments.

The United States Housing Act of 1937 established a more durable institutional arrangement. The federal government would provide loans, subsidies, and oversight, while state and local governments would create housing authorities to select sites, acquire land, develop projects, admit tenants, collect rent, and manage the properties.

The law linked public housing to the elimination of unsafe housing and slums. Early policy frequently required the removal of an approximately equivalent number of substandard units when new public housing was created. This approach limited the extent to which public housing added to the total housing supply and connected the program to clearance and redevelopment policy.

Postwar Expansion

The Housing Act of 1949 expanded the national commitment to housing and authorized a large increase in public housing construction. During the postwar decades, local housing authorities constructed developments for families, older adults, and other qualifying residents.

Public housing design varied considerably. Some communities constructed low-rise rowhouses, garden apartments, duplexes, or detached homes. Other cities adopted superblock plans and high-rise towers. Large high-rise developments became the most visible symbol of the program, but they never represented the complete range of public housing forms.

Shift Toward Rental Subsidies

Beginning in the 1960s and accelerating after the creation of Section 8 in 1974, federal policy increasingly supported privately owned rental housing through contracts, subsidies, tax incentives, and tenant-based vouchers. Traditional public housing remained active, but it was no longer the principal mechanism through which the federal government expanded rental assistance.

Redevelopment and Conversion

By the late twentieth century, some public housing developments had severe physical, financial, management, and public-safety problems. HOPE VI financed the demolition and redevelopment of many distressed developments, frequently replacing high-density projects with lower-density mixed-income communities.

These redevelopment efforts removed some severely deteriorated housing and attracted new investment, but they also generated controversy. Common concerns included the loss of deeply affordable units, resident displacement, incomplete one-for-one replacement, restrictive readmission standards, and the inability of some former residents to return to redeveloped communities.

The Rental Assistance Demonstration introduced another major transformation. Rather than remaining under the traditional public housing funding structure, converted properties receive long-term project-based Section 8 assistance. Conversion can provide access to private financing for repairs, but it changes the property’s legal, ownership, financing, or subsidy structure and requires continued oversight of resident protections.

```

Program Structure and Administration

```

Federal Responsibilities

HUD establishes national program requirements, allocates operating and capital funding, approves major development and disposition actions, conducts or supervises inspections, reviews public housing agency plans, monitors financial and management performance, and enforces applicable housing and civil-rights requirements.

Local Responsibilities

Public housing agencies generally perform the direct administrative functions of the program. Their responsibilities may include:

  • Maintaining applicant waiting lists
  • Verifying household income and eligibility
  • Establishing admissions preferences consistent with federal and local rules
  • Assigning eligible households to available units
  • Calculating rent and utility allowances
  • Executing and enforcing residential leases
  • Maintaining buildings, grounds, and building systems
  • Providing reasonable accommodations for residents with disabilities
  • Enforcing health, safety, occupancy, and conduct requirements
  • Preparing annual and five-year public housing agency plans
  • Consulting resident advisory boards and recognized tenant organizations
  • Managing redevelopment, demolition, disposition, or conversion proposals

Federal–Local Relationship

The program combines federal funding and regulation with substantial local control. This structure permits adaptation to local conditions, but it has also produced major differences in property quality, admissions policy, maintenance performance, waiting-list administration, redevelopment strategy, and resident treatment.

Local discretion has historically been a central strength and a central risk. Competent housing authorities have used it to provide stable, well-maintained housing. Other authorities have used or tolerated discriminatory assignments, segregated site selection, arbitrary administration, inadequate maintenance, or weak financial controls.

```

Eligibility, Admissions, and Rent

```

Income Eligibility

Public housing is restricted to households meeting applicable income limits. Federal law generally permits admission of low-income households, while requiring a substantial share of newly available units to be occupied by extremely low-income households.

Eligibility determinations may also consider household composition, citizenship or eligible immigration status, prior rental conduct, criminal history, debts owed to housing authorities, and other factors permitted by federal law and local admissions policies.

Waiting Lists

Demand commonly exceeds the number of available units. Public housing agencies therefore maintain waiting lists that may remain closed for extended periods. Agencies may establish lawful preferences for categories such as people experiencing homelessness, residents displaced by government action, veterans, working households, local residents, people with disabilities, or other groups identified in agency policy.

Waiting-list rules can materially affect access. Transparency, accurate recordkeeping, nondiscrimination, notice, and consistent application are therefore important administrative safeguards.

Rent Calculation

Public housing rent is generally income-based. A household commonly pays an amount connected to its adjusted income, gross income, applicable welfare rent, or a minimum-rent requirement, subject to utility allowances, hardship exemptions, deductions, and other statutory rules.

Some households may select or be subject to flat-rent provisions. Because income, deductions, household composition, utility responsibility, and local policy can change, residents generally undergo periodic income reexaminations.

Continued Occupancy

Public housing is not necessarily terminated immediately when a household’s income increases. Federal law and agency policy establish procedures for over-income households, including specified periods and the possible payment of higher or market-based rent.

```

Funding, Maintenance, and Property Conditions

```

Operating Assistance

Tenant rents generally do not cover the full cost of operating public housing. HUD therefore provides operating subsidies intended to support routine management, utilities, maintenance, security, insurance, administrative functions, and other eligible expenses.

Capital Assistance

Public housing agencies receive capital funding for major rehabilitation, building systems, accessibility improvements, lead-hazard reduction, modernization, demolition, replacement, and other long-term physical needs.

Capital Backlog

Many public housing properties are decades old and require substantial investment in roofs, plumbing, elevators, heating and cooling systems, electrical systems, accessibility, environmental remediation, and unit interiors. Federal capital appropriations have frequently been insufficient to eliminate accumulated repair needs.

Inspection and Enforcement

Public housing properties are subject to federal physical inspection and performance requirements. Inspection findings can influence corrective-action plans, funding decisions, agency ratings, receivership, redevelopment proposals, and other federal responses.

Inspection systems do not replace the housing authority’s continuing duty to maintain habitable properties or the rights residents may possess under federal, state, and local law.

```

Racial Segregation and African American Communities

```

Segregated Development

Public housing developed within an already segregated housing market shaped by restrictive covenants, exclusionary zoning, racial violence, discriminatory lending, redlining, real-estate steering, and municipal segregation. Federal and local administrators frequently accommodated or reinforced those conditions rather than dismantling them.

Many housing authorities constructed separate developments for Black and white residents, assigned tenants according to race, or selected sites that reproduced existing residential boundaries. Housing intended for African American residents was often concentrated in or near established Black neighborhoods and areas affected by industrial hazards, infrastructure barriers, disinvestment, or limited municipal services.

Housing Opportunity and Material Benefit

Public housing nevertheless provided significant benefits to many African American households excluded from private rental and homeownership markets. Well-maintained developments offered stable rents, utilities, indoor plumbing, safer structures, community institutions, playgrounds, and greater residential security than many overcrowded or substandard private-market alternatives.

The program’s historical meaning cannot therefore be reduced either to a complete success or a complete failure. Public housing supplied indispensable housing while operating within—and sometimes actively maintaining—a racially unequal metropolitan system.

Concentration of Poverty

Changes in admissions policy, employment patterns, urban population, suburbanization, local tax bases, building design, management practices, and federal funding contributed to the increasing concentration of extremely poor households in some developments. African American residents were disproportionately exposed to developments located in high-poverty and racially isolated neighborhoods.

Desegregation and Mobility Remedies

Civil-rights litigation and federal enforcement challenged discriminatory site selection, tenant assignment, and metropolitan exclusion. Remedies have included scattered-site housing, nondiscriminatory admissions, mobility counselling, regional housing opportunities, revised site-selection rules, and federal monitoring.

Redevelopment and Displacement

Demolition and mixed-income redevelopment removed some of the country’s most deteriorated housing. However, African American communities frequently bore the costs of relocation, community-network disruption, reduced unit counts, altered eligibility standards, and delayed or unrealized rights of return.

```

Resident Rights and Responsibilities

```

Resident Rights

Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, public housing residents may possess rights involving:

  • Written leases and notice of lease requirements
  • Income-based rent calculations and applicable utility allowances
  • Administrative grievance procedures
  • Notice and an opportunity to respond before specified adverse actions
  • Nondiscrimination under federal civil-rights laws
  • Reasonable accommodations and accessibility protections
  • Participation through resident councils and resident advisory boards
  • Relocation assistance and specified protections during redevelopment or conversion
  • Inspection, maintenance, and habitability protections
  • Judicial review or other remedies where authorized by law

Resident Responsibilities

Residents are generally required to pay rent, report household and income changes, avoid serious or repeated lease violations, maintain units in reasonable condition, avoid disturbing other residents, and comply with lawful occupancy, safety, and community requirements.

Federal law requires public housing leases to address certain criminal and drug-related conduct. Enforcement of these provisions has generated continuing debate over public safety, family responsibility, due process, proportionality, and the eviction of tenants who did not personally engage in prohibited conduct.

```

Major Policy Debates

```

Direct Public Provision or Private-Market Subsidy

Public housing provides a permanently designated stock of deeply affordable units under public control. Critics argue that public ownership can create management inefficiency, political interference, concentrated poverty, and large long-term capital obligations. Supporters argue that vouchers alone cannot guarantee access where rents are high, vacancies are scarce, landlords refuse participation, or discriminatory barriers remain.

Preservation or Conversion

Conversion programs can generate financing for urgent repairs, but they may move properties outside the traditional public housing framework. Debate therefore concerns long-term affordability, ownership control, tenant protections, public accountability, foreclosure risk, and whether converted units remain functionally equivalent to public housing.

Redevelopment and Right to Return

Redevelopment can improve property conditions and neighborhood infrastructure. It can also reduce the number of deeply subsidized units or create barriers preventing former residents from returning. Effective policy must therefore address relocation, replacement housing, admissions standards, resident participation, construction schedules, and enforceable return rights.

Local Control and Federal Accountability

Local administration permits housing authorities to respond to local needs. It can also permit exclusionary, discriminatory, or poorly managed practices. Federal oversight seeks to establish national standards without assuming direct responsibility for every local operational decision.

Public Safety and Due Process

Housing authorities must protect residents from violence, drug activity, harassment, and dangerous conditions. Enforcement systems must also guard against collective punishment, arbitrary eviction, inaccurate allegations, discriminatory policing, and the removal of innocent household members.

Geographic Concentration

Locating public housing only in areas of concentrated poverty can restrict access to employment, transportation, high-performing schools, healthcare, environmental quality, and other resources. Proposals for scattered-site or opportunity-area development often encounter local political resistance, zoning barriers, land costs, and community opposition.

```

Key Distinctions

```

Public Housing and Housing Choice Vouchers

Public housing generally provides a specific unit owned or controlled by a public housing agency. A Housing Choice Voucher generally provides a portable rental subsidy that an eligible household uses in qualifying privately owned housing.

Public Housing and Project-Based Section 8

Traditional public housing is funded principally through Public Housing Operating and Capital Funds. Project-based Section 8 assistance is attached by contract to specified units, which are often privately or nonprofit owned. Properties converted through the Rental Assistance Demonstration receive project-based Section 8 assistance rather than traditional public housing subsidies.

Public Housing and FHA Mortgage Insurance

The Federal Housing Administration primarily insures qualifying mortgages made by approved lenders. FHA mortgage insurance does not generally create or operate public housing and is principally associated with privately owned housing and mortgage finance.

Public Housing and Urban Renewal

Public housing provides rental units for eligible residents. Urban renewal was a broader redevelopment and land-clearance system that acquired and cleared designated areas for public or private redevelopment. The programs frequently overlapped, but they were legally and operationally distinct.

Public Housing and Affordable Housing

Affordable housing is a broad category that may include public housing, subsidized private housing, nonprofit housing, tax-credit developments, rent-restricted units, cooperative housing, community land trusts, and naturally occurring lower-cost housing.

Public Housing and Social Housing

Social housing is a broader international concept encompassing publicly, municipally, cooperatively, or nonprofit owned housing that is permanently or substantially insulated from market pricing. United States public housing is one specific and comparatively targeted form of social housing.
```

Geographic Scope

```

The Federal Public Housing Program operates through participating public housing agencies across the United States. Public housing is found in major metropolitan centres, midsized cities, small towns, suburban jurisdictions, rural communities, and United States territories.

The size and form of local programs vary substantially. Large-city housing authorities may administer thousands of apartments across numerous developments, while small authorities may operate only a few buildings or scattered-site homes.

Public housing has often been associated with central-city high-rise developments, but the national inventory also includes rowhouses, townhouses, garden apartments, walk-up buildings, duplexes, detached homes, senior buildings, and accessible housing for people with disabilities.

Geographic location strongly affects resident opportunity. Access to employment, transportation, education, healthcare, grocery stores, environmental quality, public safety, and social networks can differ significantly depending on where a public housing development was placed and how surrounding neighborhoods have changed.
```

Legal Context

```

United States Housing Act of 1937

The United States Housing Act of 1937, codified principally at 42 U.S.C. § 1437 and following sections, remains the foundational federal statute governing public housing. Section 9 authorizes the principal operating and capital funding structure.

Housing Act of 1949

The Housing Act of 1949 established a broad national housing policy, expanded public housing authorization, and connected housing production to urban redevelopment and slum clearance.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programs or activities receiving federal financial assistance. It applies to federally assisted public housing administration.

Department of Housing and Urban Development Act of 1965

This law created HUD as a Cabinet-level department and consolidated major federal housing and urban-development responsibilities.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

The Fair Housing Act prohibits specified forms of discrimination in the sale, rental, financing, and provision of housing. Its protections apply to public housing as well as most other housing.

Brooke Amendment

Enacted in 1969 and subsequently amended, the Brooke Amendment established an income-based ceiling on the rent contribution required from residents of federally assisted housing.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination in federally funded programs and requires covered housing providers to meet accessibility and reasonable-accommodation obligations.

Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998

This statute made extensive changes to public housing admissions, rents, planning, resident participation, demolition, mixed-income policy, agency flexibility, and federal funding. It also established the development limitation commonly known as the Faircloth Amendment.

Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act of 2016

This law revised income calculations, asset rules, over-income provisions, inspection procedures, and other requirements affecting public housing and federal rental assistance.

Selected Legal Cases

  • Thorpe v. Housing Authority of the City of Durham (1969): Applied a HUD requirement that housing authorities provide tenants with reasons for proposed eviction actions.
  • Hills v. Gautreaux (1976): Held that a federal court could consider metropolitan-area relief to remedy federal participation in racially discriminatory public housing administration in Chicago.
  • Wright v. Roanoke Redevelopment and Housing Authority (1987): Recognized that public housing tenants could pursue a federal civil-rights action to enforce statutory rent-ceiling protections, including qualifying utility charges.
  • Department of Housing and Urban Development v. Rucker (2002): Upheld statutory authority permitting lease termination for specified drug-related activity by household members or guests, including circumstances in which the tenant lacked personal knowledge of the activity.
```

Summary

```

The Federal Public Housing Program created a permanent federal–local system for providing publicly owned rental housing to households unable to secure suitable housing through the private market. Established under the United States Housing Act of 1937, the program relies on HUD funding and regulation but is administered principally by local public housing agencies.

Public housing has supplied affordable and stable homes to generations of low-income families, older adults, and people with disabilities. At the same time, discriminatory site selection, segregated tenant assignment, concentrated poverty, inadequate maintenance, demolition, and displacement have shaped its history—particularly for African American communities.

The program remains an important component of the national housing safety net. Its future depends on decisions involving preservation, capital investment, tenant protections, fair housing, geographic integration, redevelopment, local accountability, and the continued availability of deeply affordable rental homes.
```
