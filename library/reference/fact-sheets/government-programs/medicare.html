Medicare

The federal health-insurance program established in 1965 for older Americans and later extended to certain people with disabilities, end-stage renal disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, operating through hospital, medical, private-plan, and prescription-drug coverage.

What Is Medicare?

Medicare is the national health-insurance program created by Congress in 1965 under Title XVIII of the Social Security Act. Its original purpose was to protect older Americans against hospital and physician costs at a time when private insurance coverage commonly ended with retirement or was unavailable because of age and medical risk.

Medicare is a federal program, but it is not one uniform insurance policy. It consists of several legally distinct parts, separate trust-fund accounts, federal payment systems, private-plan contracts, prescription-drug plans, beneficiary premiums, provider participation rules, and enrollment periods.

Original Medicare is administered through the federal government and generally pays covered providers on a fee-for-service basis. Medicare Advantage allows beneficiaries to receive Medicare-covered benefits through private organizations operating under federal contracts and payment rules. Part D provides outpatient prescription-drug coverage through private plans approved and regulated by Medicare.

Medicare should not be defined as a program exclusively for retirees. Age remains the largest eligibility pathway, but the program also covers certain younger people with disabilities and specific severe medical conditions. Nor should Medicare be treated as free comprehensive care. The program protects beneficiaries against many large medical expenses, but it includes premiums and cost sharing and excludes or limits important categories of service.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines Medicare as a federal institution: its origins, legal design, eligibility categories, coverage components, administration, financing, provider-payment systems, civil-rights role, racial and regional effects, legislative development, limitations, and continuing policy debates.

It is not an individual enrollment guide, benefits determination, insurance recommendation, or statement that a specific service will be covered. Premiums, deductibles, plan networks, formularies, payment rates, covered technologies, and enrollment rules can change through legislation and annual administration.

Individual beneficiaries should use current Medicare, Social Security, plan, provider, and State Health Insurance Assistance Program materials when making personal coverage decisions.

Geographic Scope

Medicare is national in statutory authority and financing, but access and delivery vary by location. Original Medicare can generally be used with participating physicians, hospitals, and other eligible providers throughout the United States and participating jurisdictions. Medicare Advantage and Part D plans operate through approved service areas, networks, formularies, and contracts that may differ by county or region.

Geographic variation affects provider supply, travel distance, hospital availability, specialist access, home-health capacity, prescription-drug access, plan competition, payment adjustments, and the cost of delivering services. Rural areas, tribal communities, island jurisdictions, medically underserved communities, and neighborhoods affected by hospital closures may experience coverage on paper without equivalent practical access.

Medicare generally provides limited coverage outside the United States. Certain emergencies, border-area services, shipboard situations, and supplemental policies can create exceptions, but international coverage is not equivalent to domestic coverage.

Historical Context

Before Medicare, older Americans faced a structural insurance problem. Retirement reduced access to employer-sponsored coverage, private insurers charged more for age and illness, and older people used more hospital and physician services while often living on fixed incomes.

The growth of modern hospital medicine increased the value and cost of medical treatment. Blue Cross hospital plans, Blue Shield physician plans, commercial insurance, union benefits, and employer coverage expanded during the mid-twentieth century, but coverage remained incomplete and unequal.

Social Security created a federal system for retirement income in 1935, but the original statute did not establish national health insurance. Health coverage became a recurring subject of New Deal, wartime, Fair Deal, labor, civil-rights, medical-profession, insurance-industry, and congressional debate.

President Harry S. Truman proposed national health insurance in 1945. Opposition from organized medicine, conservative legislators, insurance interests, and critics of centralized government prevented enactment. Later proposals narrowed the issue from national insurance for the general population to hospital insurance for older people through Social Security.

The Kerr-Mills Act of 1960 expanded federal support for state medical assistance to certain older people with limited means. Because Kerr-Mills depended heavily on state participation and means testing, supporters of social insurance continued to seek a national program based on age and insured status rather than poverty alone.

Creation of Medicare

Medicare emerged from a legislative compromise combining three approaches. The first became Part A, a compulsory hospital-insurance system financed primarily through payroll contributions. The second became Part B, a voluntary supplementary medical-insurance program financed through beneficiary premiums and general revenues. The third expanded medical assistance for low-income people through Medicaid under Title XIX.

Congress enacted the Social Security Amendments of 1965 after the 1964 election produced large congressional majorities supportive of President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society agenda. Representative Wilbur D. Mills, chair of the House Ways and Means Committee, played a central role in assembling the legislative package.

President Johnson signed the amendments on July 30, 1965, at the Harry S. Truman Presidential Library in Independence, Missouri. Truman and former First Lady Bess Truman received the first Medicare cards in recognition of Truman’s earlier health-insurance advocacy.

Medicare was enacted alongside Medicaid, but the programs were designed differently. Medicare operated primarily as federal social insurance connected to age, disability, and covered status. Medicaid operated as a federal-state assistance program with eligibility and administration shared among federal and state governments.

Initial Implementation in 1965–1966

Implementing Medicare required the federal government to identify and enroll millions of older people, certify hospitals and other providers, develop payment procedures, educate beneficiaries, coordinate with insurance intermediaries, and establish administrative systems capable of processing a national volume of claims.

The Social Security Administration led much of the initial enrollment effort. Federal officials worked with hospitals, professional associations, insurers, state agencies, local organizations, and the United States Postal Service to reach eligible people before coverage began on July 1, 1966.

The program relied partly on existing insurance organizations to perform claims-administration functions. Blue Cross plans, Blue Shield plans, commercial carriers, and other contractors helped process payments under federal standards. This public-private administrative model continued in later forms through fiscal intermediaries, carriers, and Medicare Administrative Contractors.

The launch demonstrated the federal government’s capacity to create a nationwide health-insurance system rapidly, but it also embedded existing medical institutions, provider practices, regional inequalities, and payment arrangements within the new program.

Medicare and Hospital Desegregation

Medicare’s implementation was a major civil-rights event. Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited racial discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance. Hospitals seeking Medicare reimbursement therefore had to demonstrate compliance with federal nondiscrimination requirements.

Before 1966, many hospitals maintained racially separate wards, rooms, waiting areas, blood supplies, staff privileges, patient assignments, and admission practices. Black physicians were frequently denied privileges, and Black patients could be excluded, segregated, transferred, or treated in inferior facilities.

Federal teams inspected hospitals, reviewed policies, investigated complaints, and required corrective action before certification. Social Security personnel, Public Health Service officials, civil-rights staff, and local volunteers contributed to the compliance effort.

Medicare created a powerful enforcement mechanism because hospitals depended on the new federal reimbursement stream. A hospital could preserve segregation and risk exclusion from Medicare, or desegregate and gain access to a large national source of payment.

The certification campaign did not eliminate every form of discrimination. Informal segregation, physician referral patterns, residential inequality, hospital location, unequal treatment, occupational barriers, and differences in institutional resources persisted. Medicare nevertheless helped dismantle formal hospital segregation on a national scale in a period far shorter than many other desegregation processes.

This history demonstrates that Medicare was not only an insurance program. It also expanded federal leverage over hospitals and connected public payment to civil-rights compliance.

Eligibility

Medicare eligibility is based on federal law and differs by category. Enrollment in one part does not automatically establish entitlement to every other part under identical terms.

Age-based eligibility
People age 65 or older may qualify when they meet applicable citizenship or residency requirements and Social Security, Railroad Retirement, or premium-payment conditions.
Disability-based eligibility
Certain people under age 65 become eligible after receiving qualifying Social Security or Railroad Retirement disability benefits for the required period. Special rules apply to particular disability categories.
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
People entitled to disability benefits because of ALS receive Medicare under an accelerated statutory pathway without the ordinary disability waiting period for Medicare coverage.
End-stage renal disease
People of any age with permanent kidney failure requiring dialysis or a kidney transplant may qualify under special work-history, family-relationship, treatment, and enrollment rules.
Premium-free Part A
Most beneficiaries receive Part A without a monthly premium because they or a qualifying family member accumulated sufficient covered employment. People without enough quarters may be able to purchase Part A.
Part B enrollment
Part B generally requires enrollment and payment of a monthly premium. Some people are enrolled automatically; others must apply.
Late enrollment
Delaying Part B or Part D without qualifying coverage can produce continuing premium penalties, subject to statutory exceptions and special enrollment periods.

Eligibility, entitlement, enrollment, effective dates, premium liability, and coverage are related but distinct determinations.

Program Structure: Parts A, B, C, and D

Part A: Hospital Insurance
Covers qualifying inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care following applicable conditions, hospice care, and certain home-health services.
Part B: Medical Insurance
Covers physicians’ services, outpatient care, preventive services, diagnostic testing, durable medical equipment, ambulance services, mental-health services, and other medically necessary items and services defined by law and policy.
Part C: Medicare Advantage
Allows Medicare-approved private plans to provide Part A and Part B benefits, usually together with Part D and sometimes additional benefits, under federal contracts and payment rules.
Part D: Prescription-Drug Coverage
Provides outpatient prescription-drug coverage through private stand-alone plans and Medicare Advantage prescription-drug plans.

“Part C” does not mean that beneficiaries receive a separate third set of benefits in addition to Parts A and B. Medicare Advantage is an alternative delivery pathway through which an approved private plan assumes responsibility for furnishing Medicare-covered Part A and Part B services.

Original Medicare

Original Medicare is the federally administered fee-for-service pathway consisting of Part A and Part B. Beneficiaries generally may obtain covered services from any eligible provider that accepts Medicare, subject to coverage rules, medical necessity, provider participation, and assignment.

Medicare pays an established amount for a covered service, and the beneficiary may owe a deductible, coinsurance, or copayment. Some providers accept assignment and agree to receive the Medicare-approved amount as full payment for covered professional services. Other participating rules and limits govern providers that do not accept assignment.

Original Medicare does not include an annual out-of-pocket maximum for all Part A and Part B services. Beneficiaries frequently rely on retiree coverage, Medicaid, employer or union coverage, military-related coverage, or a Medigap policy to reduce financial exposure.

Beneficiaries in Original Medicare may add a stand-alone Part D plan for outpatient prescription drugs. They may also purchase a standardized Medicare Supplement Insurance policy when eligible and available under federal and state rules.

Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage is the modern name for the private-plan program established as Medicare+Choice under the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 and reorganized by the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003.

Medicare pays private organizations to furnish covered Part A and Part B services to enrolled beneficiaries. Plans may operate as health maintenance organizations, preferred provider organizations, private fee-for-service plans, special needs plans, medical savings account plans, or other authorized models.

Medicare Advantage plans generally must cover the benefits available under Original Medicare, but they may use networks, referrals, prior authorization, utilization management, cost-sharing structures, and plan-specific service rules. Most plans include Part D coverage, and many advertise supplemental benefits such as dental, vision, hearing, transportation, fitness, meals, or over-the-counter allowances.

Additional benefits are not necessarily uniform, unlimited, or available to every enrollee under the same conditions. Beneficiaries must compare provider networks, formularies, authorization rules, maximum out-of-pocket limits, service areas, quality information, and total expected costs rather than relying on the premium alone.

Medicare Advantage is a major policy field because federal payment methods must account for beneficiary health risk without rewarding plans for unsupported diagnoses, avoiding costly patients, restricting necessary care, or retaining excess public payments.

Medicare Part D

Congress created Medicare Part D through the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003, with coverage beginning in 2006. Before Part D, Original Medicare generally lacked a comprehensive outpatient prescription-drug benefit.

Part D is delivered through private plans approved by Medicare. Stand-alone prescription-drug plans serve people in Original Medicare, while Medicare Advantage prescription-drug plans combine drug coverage with Part A and Part B benefits.

Plans maintain formularies identifying covered drugs and may use tiers, prior authorization, step therapy, quantity limits, preferred pharmacies, and negotiated prices within federal requirements. Beneficiaries must evaluate whether their medications and pharmacies are covered under the plan selected for the coming year.

The Affordable Care Act gradually reduced the former Part D coverage gap commonly called the “donut hole.” The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 added major affordability reforms, including limits on certain insulin cost sharing, elimination of beneficiary cost sharing in the catastrophic phase, an annual Part D out-of-pocket cap beginning in 2025, inflation rebates, and federal negotiation of prices for selected high-expenditure drugs.

The first negotiated prices under the Medicare Drug Price Negotiation Program took effect in 2026. The selection, negotiation, and effective-price cycles continue under statutory schedules and CMS guidance.

Medigap and Other Supplemental Coverage

Medicare Supplement Insurance, commonly called Medigap, is private insurance designed to pay certain beneficiary cost-sharing obligations under Original Medicare. Federal law standardizes benefit categories in most states, while state insurance departments regulate important aspects of sale and availability.

Medigap is not the same as Medicare Advantage. A Medigap policy supplements Original Medicare; it does not replace Parts A and B with a private-plan delivery system. A beneficiary generally cannot use Medigap to pay Medicare Advantage cost sharing.

Other sources of supplemental protection include employer or union retiree coverage, Medicaid, the Federal Employees Health Benefits Program, TRICARE for Life, Veterans Health Administration services, and specialized state assistance programs. Coordination rules determine which payer is primary and what costs remain.

Administers Medicare coverage policy, payment systems, plan contracts, provider enrollment, quality programs, data systems, demonstrations, program integrity, and regulatory implementation.
Social Security Administration
Determines entitlement and performs major enrollment, premium, and income-related adjustment functions for most beneficiaries.
Railroad Retirement Board
Performs related functions for eligible railroad workers and their families.
Medicare Administrative Contractors
Process Original Medicare claims, enroll providers, conduct review, issue local coverage determinations, and perform other delegated functions.
Private Medicare Advantage and Part D organizations
Administer benefits, networks, formularies, claims, utilization controls, customer service, appeals, and reporting under federal contracts.
State insurance departments
Regulate Medigap insurers and other insurance matters within the boundaries of federal law.
State Health Insurance Assistance Programs
Provide free, individualized counselling to Medicare beneficiaries, families, and caregivers.
Department of Justice, HHS Office of Inspector General, and other oversight bodies
Investigate fraud, false claims, kickbacks, civil-rights violations, program abuse, and administrative misconduct.

Financing

Medicare is financed through two trust-fund structures and several revenue sources rather than one account.

Hospital Insurance Trust Fund

Part A is financed primarily through payroll taxes paid by workers and employers, with additional revenue from taxation of certain Social Security benefits, premiums paid by some beneficiaries, interest, and other statutory sources. Part A finances covered hospital, skilled nursing facility, hospice, and related services.

Supplementary Medical Insurance Trust Fund

Parts B and D are financed primarily through general federal revenues and beneficiary premiums, together with state payments for certain beneficiaries, drug-related payments, and other receipts. Premiums are set to finance a defined share of program costs, and higher-income beneficiaries pay income-related premium adjustments.

Medicare Advantage Financing

Medicare Advantage payments draw from the Part A and Part B accounts according to the services and populations covered. Federal payments are adjusted through benchmarks, bids, quality provisions, risk adjustment, and other statutory formulas.

The Hospital Insurance Trust Fund receives the greatest public attention because its dedicated revenue can be compared with projected Part A spending. Parts B and D do not face insolvency in the same technical form because their financing is reset through premiums and general-revenue transfers, but their growth places increasing pressure on beneficiaries and the federal budget.

Enrollment and Annual Coverage Decisions

Some people are enrolled in Medicare automatically because they receive Social Security or Railroad Retirement benefits before becoming eligible. Others must apply. The Initial Enrollment Period generally surrounds the month of first age-based eligibility, while special rules apply to disability, ESRD, employer coverage, and other circumstances.

A person who delays Part B because of current employer group-health coverage may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period after employment or coverage ends. Retiree coverage, COBRA, Marketplace coverage, and other insurance do not necessarily create the same protections against late-enrollment penalties.

Medicare’s annual Open Enrollment Period runs from October 15 through December 7. During that period, beneficiaries can review and change Medicare Advantage and Part D coverage for the following year.

Medicare Advantage enrollees also have a separate annual period during which they may make specified changes. Additional Special Enrollment Periods apply after qualifying events such as moving, losing coverage, gaining Medicaid eligibility, entering an institution, or experiencing certain plan changes.

Annual review matters because premiums, cost sharing, provider networks, formularies, drug tiers, prior-authorization requirements, supplemental benefits, service areas, and plan contracts can change.

Covered Services

Medicare coverage depends on the beneficiary’s enrolled parts, the service category, medical necessity, provider eligibility, setting, frequency limits, documentation, and national or local coverage policy.

  • inpatient hospital services;
  • physician and qualified practitioner services;
  • outpatient hospital care;
  • diagnostic laboratory and imaging services;
  • preventive screenings, vaccines, and wellness services authorized by law;
  • mental-health and substance-use-disorder treatment;
  • limited skilled nursing facility care after applicable conditions are met;
  • home-health services for qualifying beneficiaries;
  • hospice care for qualifying terminal illness;
  • durable medical equipment and supplies;
  • ambulance services under defined conditions;
  • dialysis and kidney-transplant services;
  • approved outpatient prescription drugs through Part D; and
  • additional plan benefits when offered under Medicare Advantage.

Coverage does not guarantee that every provider will accept every Medicare patient, that every plan will include every provider, or that an item will be paid without prior authorization, medical-necessity review, cost sharing, or appeal.

Coverage Gaps and Exclusions

Medicare is broad health insurance but not comprehensive long-term social care. Important exclusions and limitations include:

  • most long-term custodial care in nursing homes, assisted-living facilities, or private residences;
  • routine dental care under Original Medicare, except when a covered medical circumstance brings a service within statutory authority;
  • routine eye examinations for eyeglasses and most ordinary eyewear under Original Medicare, subject to limited exceptions;
  • routine hearing examinations and hearing aids under Original Medicare;
  • most care received outside the United States;
  • private-duty nursing in ordinary circumstances;
  • services that are not reasonable and necessary or that fall outside a covered benefit category;
  • unlimited skilled nursing facility or home-care services;
  • room and board for most long-term residential care; and
  • complete protection from premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, copayments, and uncovered charges.

Medicare Advantage plans may offer dental, vision, hearing, meal, transportation, or in-home support benefits, but those benefits remain plan-specific and do not convert Medicare into a universal long-term-care program.

Medicare and Medicaid

Medicare and Medicaid were created by the same 1965 legislation but serve different legal functions.

Medicare
A federal health-insurance program based principally on age, disability, ESRD, ALS, insured status, and enrollment.
Medicaid
A federal-state assistance program based on income, eligibility category, state plan rules, and federal requirements.

Medicare is generally the primary payer for Medicare-covered acute and medical services for people enrolled in both programs. Medicaid may pay Medicare premiums and cost sharing and may cover services Medicare excludes, including substantial long-term services and supports.

Confusing the two programs can obscure major differences in eligibility, administration, financing, provider payment, benefits, state variation, and beneficiary rights.

Dual Eligibility

People enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid are commonly called dually eligible beneficiaries. This population includes older adults and people with disabilities who have limited income or resources and often experience complex health and social needs.

Dual eligibility can reduce beneficiary costs and expand access to services, but coordination is administratively difficult. Medicare and Medicaid use different payment systems, provider networks, appeals, data structures, and governmental responsibilities.

Medicare Savings Programs help qualifying people pay Part A or Part B premiums and, in some categories, deductibles and coinsurance. The Part D Low-Income Subsidy, commonly called Extra Help, reduces prescription-drug premiums and cost sharing for qualifying beneficiaries.

Federal and state governments have developed special needs plans, demonstrations, aligned enrollment arrangements, and integrated-care models to reduce fragmentation, but results and availability vary.

Provider Payment and Health-System Influence

Medicare is one of the largest purchasers of health care in the United States. Its payment rules influence hospital finance, physician practice, post-acute care, medical technology, quality measurement, documentation, and private insurance.

Inpatient hospitals
Generally paid through prospective payment systems using diagnosis-related groups and statutory adjustments.
Physicians and practitioners
Generally paid under the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule, subject to coverage rules, geographic adjustments, quality programs, and legislative updates.
Outpatient hospitals
Paid through an outpatient prospective payment system and related fee schedules.
Post-acute providers
Skilled nursing facilities, home-health agencies, inpatient rehabilitation facilities, long-term-care hospitals, and hospices operate under distinct payment systems.
Medicare Advantage plans
Receive risk-adjusted federal payments and negotiate payment arrangements with contracted providers.
Part D plans
Operate through federal subsidies, beneficiary premiums, plan liability, manufacturer discounts or rebates, and statutory risk-sharing arrangements.

Payment policy can change provider behaviour. A formula may encourage efficiency, but it may also create incentives for coding intensity, service volume, patient selection, consolidation, site-of-service shifts, or avoidance of poorly compensated care.

Beneficiary Rights and Protections

Medicare beneficiaries have rights involving information, nondiscrimination, privacy, emergency care, coverage decisions, appeals, complaints, continuity, and access to plan materials.

Original Medicare and private Medicare plans use different procedural pathways, but both must provide notice when coverage or payment is denied. Beneficiaries may request reconsideration and proceed through multiple levels of administrative and judicial review when statutory thresholds are met.

Hospitals, skilled nursing facilities, home-health agencies, hospices, and plans must provide specified notices in circumstances involving discharge, termination, noncoverage, or plan action. Quality Improvement Organizations perform review functions in certain expedited appeals.

Civil-rights protections apply independently of whether a beneficiary receives care through Original Medicare or a private plan. Discrimination based on race, color, national origin, disability, age, sex, or other protected status may violate federal law, depending on the program and circumstances.

Significance for African American Communities

Medicare materially expanded insurance coverage for older Black Americans who had experienced lower lifetime earnings, occupational exclusion, segregated employment, limited employer coverage, unequal medical access, and discrimination in private insurance and health institutions.

Its greatest immediate civil-rights effect was hospital desegregation. By tying federal reimbursement to Title VI compliance, Medicare changed the financial consequences of maintaining segregated facilities. Hospitals that had resisted moral appeals or litigation faced exclusion from a major new payment system.

Medicare also strengthened the financial position of hospitals and physicians serving older patients, including facilities in communities that had previously supplied uncompensated or undercompensated care. The distribution of those gains, however, was unequal. Historically Black hospitals, Black physicians, and underserved neighborhoods did not automatically receive equal capital, referrals, institutional authority, or reimbursement opportunities.

Coverage did not eliminate health disparities. Black beneficiaries have continued to experience differences in chronic disease burden, hospital use, preventive care, mental-health follow-up, treatment quality, provider access, wealth available for supplemental insurance, and exposure to under-resourced health systems.

Medicare policy therefore has two distinct racial meanings. It was a decisive instrument against formal hospital segregation, and it remains a system operating within broader inequalities in income, housing, environment, transportation, provider supply, institutional trust, and accumulated health risk.

Health Equity and Disparities

Equal insurance entitlement does not guarantee equal outcomes. Beneficiaries differ in health status, language, disability, geography, transportation, income, housing stability, digital access, caregiver support, provider availability, and exposure to discrimination.

CMS collects and publishes data on racial, ethnic, sex, disability, socioeconomic, and geographic differences in access, quality, utilization, and outcomes. The agency also maintains tools for mapping disparities among Medicare populations.

Policy responses include improved demographic data, quality reporting, accountable-care models, language access, rural-health programs, value-based payment, health-equity measures, community partnerships, and targeted outreach.

Equity initiatives must be evaluated carefully. A reporting requirement can reveal disparities without correcting them, and a payment model can unintentionally penalize providers serving patients with greater social and medical needs if risk adjustment is inadequate.

Rural and Regional Access

Medicare is particularly important in rural areas because older adults constitute a substantial share of many rural populations and private insurance markets may offer fewer options.

Rural beneficiaries can face long travel distances, hospital closures, limited specialist supply, ambulance shortages, weak broadband, fewer home-health agencies, nursing shortages, and reduced competition among Medicare Advantage or Part D plans.

Congress and CMS use special payment classifications and programs for critical access hospitals, rural health clinics, federally qualified health centers, telehealth, ambulance services, and other rural providers. These policies can support access but do not eliminate every workforce, transportation, or infrastructure constraint.

Regional differences also affect urban beneficiaries. Segregated neighborhoods, hospital consolidation, safety-net closures, provider networks, and transportation barriers can produce medical underservice within densely populated metropolitan areas.

Major Legislative Development

Social Security Amendments of 1965
Established Medicare Parts A and B and Medicaid under Titles XVIII and XIX.
Social Security Amendments of 1972
Extended Medicare to certain people under age 65 with long-term disabilities and to people with end-stage renal disease.
Social Security Amendments of 1983
Advanced prospective payment for inpatient hospitals through diagnosis-related groups, changing Medicare from retrospective cost reimbursement toward predetermined payment.
Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act of 1988
Expanded benefits and beneficiary protections but generated opposition to its financing; Congress repealed most of the expansion in 1989.
Balanced Budget Act of 1997
Established Medicare+Choice under Part C and made major changes to provider payment and program administration.
Medicare Modernization Act of 2003
Created Part D, renamed and revised the private-plan program as Medicare Advantage, and established additional payment and savings-account provisions.
Affordable Care Act of 2010
Reduced the Part D coverage gap, expanded preventive benefits, revised Medicare Advantage payments, created payment and delivery reforms, and strengthened program-integrity provisions.
Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act of 2015
Replaced the sustainable growth rate physician-payment formula and established new physician quality and alternative-payment pathways.
Inflation Reduction Act of 2022
Added prescription-drug negotiation, inflation rebates, insulin and vaccine affordability measures, catastrophic-phase reform, and an annual Part D out-of-pocket limit.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Social insurance
A public program providing protection through statutory entitlement connected to covered status, contributions, age, disability, or other defined conditions rather than financial need alone.
Original Medicare
The federally administered Part A and Part B fee-for-service pathway.
Medicare Advantage
The Part C pathway through which a Medicare-approved private plan provides Part A and Part B benefits and usually Part D.
Part D plan
A private plan approved by Medicare to provide outpatient prescription-drug coverage.
Medigap
Private supplemental insurance designed to pay specified Original Medicare cost-sharing obligations.
Premium
A recurring amount paid to maintain coverage.
Deductible
An amount the beneficiary must pay before a coverage component begins paying under applicable rules.
Coinsurance
A percentage of the approved cost owed by the beneficiary.
Copayment
A fixed amount owed for a service, item, or prescription.
Assignment
An agreement by a provider or supplier to accept the Medicare-approved amount as full payment for a covered service, subject to beneficiary cost sharing.
Formulary
A Part D or Medicare Advantage plan’s list of covered prescription drugs and applicable coverage conditions.
Prior authorization
A requirement to obtain plan approval before specified services or drugs will be covered.
Dual eligibility
Enrollment in both Medicare and Medicaid.
National coverage determination
A CMS decision establishing whether and under what conditions Medicare covers a service or item nationally.
Local coverage determination
A decision by a Medicare Administrative Contractor addressing coverage within its jurisdiction when national policy does not fully resolve the issue.

Major Achievements

  • Created near-universal access to health insurance for eligible older Americans.
  • Extended federal health-insurance protection to qualifying people with disabilities, ESRD, and ALS.
  • Reduced the financial consequences of hospital and physician care for populations with high medical need.
  • Used federal payment to accelerate desegregation of hospitals under Title VI.
  • Established national payment and coverage systems affecting nearly every major health-care sector.
  • Added outpatient prescription-drug coverage through Part D.
  • Supported preventive, hospice, dialysis, home-health, rehabilitation, and chronic-care services.
  • Created a national administrative and data infrastructure for health-care claims, quality, research, and fraud control.
  • Provided a stable payer for hospitals, physicians, and other providers serving older and disabled populations.
  • Developed beneficiary appeals, notices, counselling, and plan-comparison systems.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Original Medicare lacks a general annual out-of-pocket maximum for Part A and Part B services.
  • Most long-term custodial care is excluded.
  • Routine dental, vision, and hearing benefits remain limited under Original Medicare.
  • Coverage rules and administrative complexity can be difficult for beneficiaries and caregivers to navigate.
  • Low-income beneficiaries may be unable to afford premiums or cost sharing without Medicaid or other assistance.
  • Provider shortages and network restrictions can limit practical access.
  • Racial, ethnic, disability, income, and geographic disparities persist despite common insurance entitlement.
  • Payment systems can reward coding, volume, consolidation, or service-location changes rather than health improvement.
  • Medicare Advantage creates oversight challenges involving risk adjustment, prior authorization, denials, marketing, supplemental benefits, and plan payments.
  • Part D beneficiaries must navigate formularies, plan changes, pharmacy networks, and drug-management rules.
  • Program spending places growing pressure on payroll-tax revenue, general federal revenue, beneficiary premiums, and the broader budget.
  • Fraud, waste, abuse, improper payments, and billing complexity require extensive enforcement and program-integrity systems.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Prescription-Drug Pricing

Implementation of the Inflation Reduction Act remains a major administrative priority. CMS is applying negotiated prices for the first selected drugs in 2026 while conducting later selection and negotiation cycles.

Medicare Advantage Oversight

Policymakers continue to examine payment benchmarks, coding intensity, risk adjustment, prior authorization, encounter-data accuracy, supplemental-benefit use, marketing, plan quality ratings, and differences between federal payments and the cost of comparable Original Medicare beneficiaries.

Hospital Insurance Financing

The long-term balance of the Part A trust fund depends on employment, wages, payroll-tax revenue, demographic change, health-care prices, service use, productivity, and legislation.

Part B and Part D Budget Pressure

Because general revenue finances a large share of Parts B and D, growth affects federal spending even when those accounts remain technically financed under current-law formulas.

Provider Payment

Physicians, hospitals, nursing facilities, home-health agencies, pharmacies, ambulance suppliers, and other providers continue to dispute whether payment updates reflect inflation, workforce costs, technology, quality expectations, and access needs.

Health Equity

CMS and other institutions continue to measure and address disparities, but controversy remains over data quality, risk adjustment, mandatory reporting, payment incentives, and the federal role in addressing social conditions affecting health.

Benefit Design

Debate continues over adding or standardizing dental, vision, hearing, long-term-care, and out-of-pocket protections and over whether benefits should be delivered through Original Medicare, private plans, Medicaid, tax subsidies, or separate programs.

Public–Private Balance

The growth of Medicare Advantage raises a foundational question: whether Medicare should remain primarily a federally administered social-insurance program, evolve toward private-plan management, or maintain a competitive dual structure with stronger payment neutrality and oversight.

Summary

Medicare is the federal health-insurance program created by the Social Security Amendments of 1965. Coverage began on July 1, 1966, initially for eligible people age 65 or older. Congress later extended the program to certain people with disabilities, end-stage renal disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.

The program consists of Part A hospital insurance, Part B medical insurance, Part C Medicare Advantage, and Part D prescription-drug coverage. Beneficiaries receive care through Original Medicare or through a Medicare-approved private plan, with supplemental coverage and financial-assistance programs available under separate rules.

Medicare transformed American health care by creating a national payer for older and disabled populations, shaping provider payment, supporting hospitals and physicians, and establishing federal standards for coverage and administration. Its implementation also became a major civil-rights intervention because hospitals had to comply with Title VI nondiscrimination requirements to receive Medicare funds.

Medicare does not eliminate every barrier to care. Beneficiaries remain exposed to premiums, cost sharing, excluded services, plan complexity, provider shortages, regional inequality, and racial disparities. The program’s future depends on sustainable financing, accurate payment, strong beneficiary protections, equitable access, effective private-plan oversight, and adaptation to changing medical and demographic conditions.
