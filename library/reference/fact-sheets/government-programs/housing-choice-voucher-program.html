Housing Choice Voucher Program

The principal federal tenant-based rental-assistance program, enabling eligible low-income households to lease approved privately owned housing with an income-related subsidy administered by public housing agencies.

Overview

The Housing Choice Voucher Program is the federal government’s principal program for providing tenant-based rental assistance to low-income households. Commonly called “Section 8,” it operates under Section 8(o) of the United States Housing Act of 1937 and is administered nationally by the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development.

HUD provides federal funding and oversight to approximately 2,000 state, regional, and local public housing agencies. These agencies maintain waiting lists, determine eligibility, issue vouchers, approve units and rents, inspect housing, make assistance payments to property owners, conduct income reviews, administer portability, and enforce participant and owner obligations.

An assisted household generally selects its own qualifying housing in the private rental market. Eligible units may include apartments, townhouses, duplexes, detached homes, manufactured homes, and certain special housing types. The owner remains responsible for screening the household as a prospective tenant, while the public housing agency independently determines whether the household, unit, rent, lease, and owner satisfy federal program requirements.

The public housing agency pays a housing assistance payment directly to the owner. The household pays the remaining approved rent and any tenant-paid utilities. Assistance is calculated from household income, allowable deductions, the agency’s payment standard, the unit’s gross rent, utility allowances, and federal affordability rules.

Because assistance is generally assigned to the household rather than permanently attached to one property, a participant can ordinarily move to another eligible unit while retaining assistance. Portability also allows eligible households to move between public housing agency jurisdictions, although administrative procedures and housing-market conditions can affect the practical ability to relocate.

The program assists more than 2.3 million American families. It serves families with children, older adults, people with disabilities, veterans, people experiencing homelessness, young adults leaving foster care, survivors of domestic violence and related harm, and other eligible households. It also includes specialized initiatives, a homeownership option, Family Self-Sufficiency, and a Project-Based Voucher component.

The program’s reach remains limited by annual appropriations. Eligibility does not create an individual entitlement to assistance, and many qualified applicants remain on waiting lists or cannot apply because local lists are closed. Voucher holders may also face landlord refusal, discrimination, security-deposit barriers, insufficient payment standards, high rents, inspection delays, limited accessible housing, and difficulty locating units before their vouchers expire.

Definitions

Housing Choice Voucher
A document issued by a public housing agency authorizing an eligible household to search for qualifying housing under the tenant-based Section 8 program for a specified term and bedroom size.
Tenant-based assistance
Rental assistance assigned to an eligible household rather than permanently attached to one property. The assistance can ordinarily continue when the household moves to another approved unit.
Section 8
The broader statutory rental-assistance framework authorized under Section 8 of the United States Housing Act of 1937. The Housing Choice Voucher Program is its principal tenant-based component.
Public housing agency
A state, regional, county, municipal, or other public body authorized to administer Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing, Project-Based Vouchers, or related federal housing programs. The abbreviation PHA is commonly used.
Annual contributions contract
The agreement under which HUD provides Housing Choice Voucher funding to a public housing agency and the agency agrees to administer the program in accordance with federal requirements.
Housing assistance payment
The subsidy payment made by a public housing agency to a participating owner on behalf of an assisted household. The abbreviation HAP is commonly used.
Housing assistance payments contract
The contract between the public housing agency and the property owner establishing the assisted tenancy, monthly payment, rent, unit requirements, owner obligations, and grounds for payment suspension or termination.
Request for tenancy approval
The form submitted by the household and owner asking the public housing agency to approve a proposed unit, rent, lease, utilities, and tenancy.
Payment standard
The amount established by a public housing agency as the subsidy benchmark for a unit size and payment-standard area. It is used in calculating the maximum monthly housing assistance payment before subtracting the household’s income-based contribution.
Fair market rent
A HUD estimate of gross rent for standard-quality rental housing within a metropolitan area or nonmetropolitan county. The abbreviation FMR is commonly used.
Small Area Fair Market Rent
A ZIP-code-level fair market rent intended to reflect rental-price differences within a metropolitan area. The abbreviation SAFMR is commonly used.
Basic payment-standard range
The ordinary range within which a public housing agency sets payment standards, generally from 90 percent through 110 percent of the applicable fair market rent unless another amount is authorized.
Exception payment standard
A payment standard outside the basic range, approved or otherwise authorized under federal rules for high-cost areas, reasonable accommodation, mobility objectives, or other permitted circumstances.
Gross rent
The rent payable to the owner plus the public housing agency’s utility allowance for utilities and appliances paid directly by the household.
Rent to owner
The total monthly rent payable to the property owner under the approved lease, excluding tenant-paid utilities.
Utility allowance
A public housing agency’s estimate of reasonable monthly expenses for utilities and services paid directly by an assisted household.
Utility reimbursement
A payment made to or on behalf of a household when its utility allowance exceeds its total tenant payment.
Total tenant payment
The income-based amount calculated under federal rules and used to determine the household’s responsibility for rent and utilities.
Family rent to owner
The portion of the approved rent that the assisted household pays directly to the property owner.
Rent reasonableness
The requirement that an assisted rent be reasonable in comparison with rents for similar unassisted units, considering location, size, type, quality, amenities, services, maintenance, and utilities.
Subsidy standard
The public housing agency’s policy for determining the number of bedrooms authorized for a household based on household size and composition.
Voucher term
The period during which a household may search for housing and submit a request for tenancy approval. Agencies may establish extensions consistent with federal requirements and reasonable-accommodation obligations.
Portability
The ability of a voucher household to move from one public housing agency jurisdiction to another while retaining tenant-based assistance.
Initial public housing agency
The agency that first issues the voucher and may continue funding the household after a portability move.
Receiving public housing agency
The agency serving the area to which the household moves. It may administer the voucher on behalf of the initial agency or absorb the household into its own program.
Absorption
A receiving public housing agency’s decision to use its own available voucher funding for a household that moved into its jurisdiction.
Billing
A portability arrangement under which the receiving agency administers the voucher and charges the initial agency for housing assistance payments and an administrative-fee share.
Special-purpose voucher
A voucher funded or designated for a defined population or policy objective, such as veterans, non-elderly people with disabilities, families involved with child welfare, or households facing homelessness.
Project-Based Voucher
A component of the Housing Choice Voucher Program under which a public housing agency commits voucher funding to designated units through a long-term contract. The assistance remains with the unit when a household leaves.

Program Structure and Administration

Federal Responsibilities

HUD establishes national requirements, allocates housing assistance and administrative funding, publishes fair market rents, issues regulations and guidance, approves certain exceptions, monitors public housing agency performance, administers shortfall and reserve policies, collects program data, and enforces federal civil-rights requirements.

Public Housing Agency Responsibilities

Participating agencies generally:

  • Adopt an administrative plan governing local program policies
  • Open and manage waiting lists
  • Establish lawful admission preferences
  • Verify income, assets, deductions, family status, and immigration eligibility
  • Determine subsidy standards and issue vouchers
  • Approve or reject proposed units and owners
  • Set payment standards and utility allowances
  • Determine rent reasonableness
  • Inspect units and enforce physical-condition requirements
  • Execute housing assistance payments contracts
  • Pay monthly subsidy to owners
  • Conduct reexaminations and process household changes
  • Administer moves and portability
  • Provide informal reviews and hearings for specified decisions
  • Prevent fraud, improper payments, and conflicts of interest
  • Report leasing, expenditure, and household data to HUD

Owner Responsibilities

Owners select tenants under lawful screening criteria, execute leases, maintain assisted units, comply with the HAP contract, collect only approved charges, enforce the lease, notify the agency of material changes, and avoid prohibited conflicts, discrimination, side payments, or false claims.

Household Responsibilities

Households provide accurate information, report required changes, search for housing, comply with the lease and family obligations, pay their assigned rent and utilities, permit inspections, avoid unauthorized occupants or subleasing, and follow required procedures before moving.

Local Discretion

Federal law establishes the program’s core requirements, but public housing agencies retain substantial discretion. Local policy can affect preferences, payment standards, voucher extensions, screening of owners, occupancy standards, interim income reporting, portability administration, landlord incentives, security-deposit assistance, and mobility services.

Application and Waiting Lists

Local Applications

There is no single national Housing Choice Voucher application. A household applies to one or more public housing agencies when those agencies accept applications. Each agency manages its own waiting list unless it participates in a regional or centralized system.

Open and Closed Lists

Demand commonly exceeds available vouchers. Agencies may close waiting lists when the number of applicants is sufficient to fill anticipated openings for an extended period. Some lists open only for short periods and receive far more applications than can be served.

Selection Methods

Agencies may select applicants according to application date, lottery, local preference, targeted referral, or a lawful combination of methods. Preferences can include people experiencing homelessness, veterans, residents displaced by government action, people with disabilities, local residents, working households, or other categories adopted in the administrative plan.

Maintaining Applicant Status

Applicants generally must keep contact information current, respond to agency notices, report relevant changes, and comply with waiting-list update procedures. Failure to respond can result in removal, subject to applicable notice and reinstatement rules.

Eligibility Interview

When an applicant approaches selection, the agency verifies household identity, income, assets, deductions, composition, citizenship or eligible immigration status, prior assistance, criminal-history factors permitted by law, debts to housing agencies, and other program requirements.

Denial and Review

An agency can deny assistance for statutory or regulatory reasons, including ineligibility, fraud, eviction from federally assisted housing for specified drug-related activity, certain criminal conduct, or failure to provide required information. Applicants generally receive written notice and an opportunity for an informal review of specified denials.

Eligibility and Income Targeting

Income Limits

HUD establishes local income limits adjusted for household size. A household is generally eligible if it is very low-income, although federal law authorizes admission of certain low-income households and other specially eligible categories.

Extremely Low-Income Targeting

At least 75 percent of the families admitted by a public housing agency during its fiscal year generally must be extremely low-income. This requirement directs newly available assistance toward households with the greatest income constraints.

Family Status

Eligible families may include households with children, married or unmarried partners, older adults, people with disabilities, displaced persons, and qualifying single individuals. Federal and local rules govern household composition and recognition.

Citizenship and Immigration Status

Assistance is generally limited to United States citizens and noncitizens with eligible immigration status. Mixed-status households may receive prorated assistance rather than full subsidy.

Income and Assets

Agencies verify wages, self-employment, benefits, pensions, child support, unemployment compensation, investment income, and other countable sources. Deductions and exclusions apply under federal law. HOTMA revised asset limitations, income reviews, deductions, and treatment of certain sources.

Criminal and Rental History

Federal law requires denial in certain circumstances and permits denial in others. Public housing agencies must distinguish mandatory prohibitions from discretionary screening and must apply policies consistently with fair-housing, disability, due-process, and individualized-assessment requirements where applicable.

Prior Program Violations

Outstanding debts, fraud, unauthorized assistance, eviction from federally assisted housing, threatening agency personnel, or failure to comply with previous family obligations can affect admission or continued assistance.

Voucher Issuance and Housing Search

Briefing

Before issuance, the public housing agency provides an oral briefing and information packet explaining the search process, household obligations, payment standards, unit size, portability, prohibited discrimination, reasonable accommodation, and procedures for requesting tenancy approval.

Bedroom Size

The voucher identifies the household’s authorized bedroom size under the agency’s subsidy standard. The household can select a different-sized unit if the unit meets occupancy rules and the subsidy calculation remains permissible.

Search Period

The agency establishes the voucher term, subject to federal minimum requirements. Extensions may be available under local policy, and reasonable accommodation may require additional search time for a person with a disability.

Eligible Housing

Housing may include:

  • Privately owned apartments
  • Townhouses and rowhouses
  • Duplexes and small multifamily buildings
  • Detached single-family homes
  • Manufactured homes
  • Cooperative housing
  • Single-room occupancy housing
  • Shared housing
  • Group homes and congregate housing
  • Homeownership units under an approved local option

Search Barriers

Households may encounter application fees, screening criteria, security deposits, transportation costs, limited listings, landlord refusal, discrimination, inaccessible units, insufficient payment standards, credit requirements, prior evictions, and rapidly rising rents.

Voucher Expiration

If a household does not submit an approvable tenancy before the voucher expires, the agency may allow the voucher to lapse and return the funding to the program. Search extensions, tolling during agency review, and reasonable accommodations can prevent expiration in qualifying circumstances.

Leasing Process

Owner Screening

The owner determines whether to accept the household as a tenant using lawful rental criteria. The public housing agency does not ordinarily guarantee the household’s suitability, rent payment, conduct, or property care.

Request for Tenancy Approval

The household and owner submit information concerning the proposed rent, utilities, lease term, unit address, owner identity, and tenancy conditions. The agency reviews the owner and unit for program eligibility.

Inspection

The agency determines whether the unit meets applicable HUD physical-condition standards. Life-threatening or serious deficiencies require correction within applicable deadlines. The agency can withhold or abate payments when an owner fails to correct owner-responsible deficiencies.

Rent Reasonableness

The proposed rent must be reasonable compared with rents for similar unassisted units. The owner cannot charge the voucher household more than the owner charges for comparable unassisted units on the premises.

Affordability Review

The agency calculates the household’s share. When a family initially leases a unit with gross rent above the payment standard, its total contribution generally cannot exceed 40 percent of adjusted monthly income.

Lease

The household and owner execute a written lease. The initial lease term is ordinarily at least one year unless the agency determines that a shorter term improves housing opportunities and the arrangement is a prevailing local practice.

Tenancy Addendum

HUD’s tenancy addendum becomes part of the lease and establishes federal requirements. When a conflicting lease provision is inconsistent with the tenancy addendum, the federal addendum controls.

HAP Contract

The public housing agency and owner execute a housing assistance payments contract. Assistance generally begins only after the agency approves the unit and tenancy and the required documents are effective.

Rent and Subsidy Calculation

Total Tenant Payment

The income-based calculation generally begins with the highest applicable amount among:

  • Thirty percent of adjusted monthly income
  • Ten percent of gross monthly income
  • A designated welfare rent where applicable
  • An authorized minimum rent

Deductions, exclusions, utility allowances, hardship exemptions, and program-specific provisions affect the final household responsibility.

Payment Standard

The public housing agency adopts payment standards for each unit size and payment-standard area. Payment standards generally must fall within 90 to 110 percent of the applicable fair market rent unless HUD or federal rules authorize an exception.

Housing Assistance Payment

The monthly subsidy is generally the lower of:

  • The payment standard minus the household’s total tenant payment; or
  • The unit’s gross rent minus the household’s total tenant payment.

Family Rent to Owner

The household pays the difference between the approved rent to owner and the housing assistance payment. The household also pays utilities assigned to it under the lease.

Unit Below the Payment Standard

When gross rent is below the payment standard, the subsidy is based on the lower gross rent. The household generally benefits from selecting a less expensive unit because it does not pay the amount by which the payment standard exceeds actual gross rent.

Unit Above the Payment Standard

When gross rent exceeds the payment standard, the household pays the income-based amount plus the difference between gross rent and the payment standard. This increases the household’s rent burden and can make the tenancy unaffordable.

Utility Reimbursement

If the utility allowance exceeds the total tenant payment, the household may receive a utility reimbursement paid directly or through an authorized utility-payment arrangement.

Representative Calculations

Example One: Gross Rent Equal to the Payment Standard

  • Adjusted monthly income: $1,200
  • Income-based total tenant payment: $360
  • Payment standard: $1,500
  • Rent to owner: $1,400
  • Utility allowance: $100
  • Gross rent: $1,500
  • Housing assistance payment: $1,140
  • Family rent to owner: $260
  • Estimated tenant-paid utilities: $100

The household’s combined rent and estimated utilities equal its $360 total tenant payment.

Example Two: Gross Rent Above the Payment Standard

  • Adjusted monthly income: $1,200
  • Income-based total tenant payment: $360
  • Payment standard: $1,500
  • Rent to owner: $1,550
  • Utility allowance: $100
  • Gross rent: $1,650
  • Housing assistance payment: $1,140
  • Family rent to owner: $410
  • Estimated tenant-paid utilities: $100

The household’s total contribution is approximately $510, or 42.5 percent of adjusted monthly income. This tenancy would ordinarily exceed the 40-percent affordability cap at initial lease-up and would not be approved without a lower rent, higher authorized payment standard, or other qualifying adjustment.

These examples are simplified. Actual calculations depend on current income rules, deductions, minimum rent, utility treatment, household composition, payment-standard effective dates, and local public housing agency policy.

Payment Standards and Small Area Fair Market Rents

Purpose of Payment Standards

Payment standards determine the subsidy benchmark and influence where households can successfully lease housing. A higher standard can expand access to more expensive units and neighborhoods, but it also raises the average subsidy cost and can reduce the number of households served within fixed funding.

Basic Range

Public housing agencies generally establish payment standards from 90 through 110 percent of the applicable fair market rent. Agencies can vary standards by bedroom size and geographic area.

Small Area Fair Market Rents

Small Area Fair Market Rents establish ZIP-code-level rent benchmarks within metropolitan areas. They can increase payment standards in higher-rent neighborhoods and decrease them in lower-rent areas, aligning subsidy more closely with local market variation.

Mobility Objective

HUD identifies SAFMRs as one tool for improving access to low-poverty and high-opportunity neighborhoods. Their effectiveness depends on landlord participation, search assistance, security-deposit resources, transportation, school access, discrimination enforcement, and the supply of qualifying units.

Reasonable Accommodation

A public housing agency may need to approve a higher payment standard as a reasonable accommodation when a person with a disability requires a unit with accessibility features, additional space for equipment, a live-in aide, proximity to treatment, or another disability-related characteristic.

Funding Pressure

Payment-standard policy creates a recurring operational trade-off. Standards that lag behind rents can produce search failure and concentration. Standards that rise faster than federal renewal funding can create shortfall risk and force an agency to limit issuance, reduce standards prospectively, or adopt other cost controls.

Inspections and Housing Quality

Federal Physical Standards

Voucher-assisted units must comply with HUD physical-condition requirements throughout the assisted tenancy. Standards address health, safety, sanitation, structural conditions, building systems, utilities, access, lead hazards, and the condition of the unit and related areas.

Initial Inspection

The agency must determine that the proposed unit meets applicable requirements before or within the time permitted for assistance to begin. Certain non-life-threatening deficiencies can be corrected under authorized procedures, while serious hazards require prompt correction.

Recurring Inspection

Agencies inspect assisted housing on a recurring schedule and can also inspect in response to complaints, reports of deterioration, owner noncompliance, or other program concerns.

Owner-Caused Deficiencies

When an owner fails to correct an owner-responsible deficiency, the agency may suspend or abate housing assistance payments, terminate the HAP contract, prohibit future participation, or require relocation.

Household-Caused Deficiencies

The household may be responsible for damage or violations caused by household members or guests beyond ordinary wear. Failure to correct qualifying household-caused deficiencies can affect continued assistance.

Lead-Based Paint

Additional federal requirements apply to many pre-1978 properties occupied by families with children. Elevated blood-lead findings, deteriorated paint, and other hazards can trigger owner, agency, and public-health responses.

Inspection Is Not a Warranty

Program approval does not guarantee that a unit will remain free from defects or replace rights available under state and local landlord–tenant law. Residents should report serious conditions to both the owner and administering agency.

Landlord Participation

Federal Voluntary Structure

Federal law generally does not require every private landlord to participate in the tenant-based voucher program. State and local source-of-income laws may prohibit refusal or unequal treatment based on an applicant’s use of a voucher or other lawful income.

Owner Benefits

Participation can provide:

  • A recurring public subsidy payment
  • Access to a large pool of prospective tenants
  • Longer resident tenure in some markets
  • Damage, security-deposit, signing-bonus, or vacancy-loss incentives where locally funded
  • Public housing agency support resolving program questions

Owner Concerns

Owners commonly identify inspection timing, paperwork, payment delays, rent limitations, local administrative differences, tenant screening, property damage, lease enforcement, and uncertainty over federal funding as participation concerns.

Landlord Recruitment

Public housing agencies can recruit owners through outreach, dedicated staff, expedited inspections, electronic documentation, direct deposit, signing incentives, damage mitigation, security-deposit assistance, and targeted engagement in low-poverty neighborhoods.

Owner Screening

The agency can deny or terminate an owner’s participation for fraud, violent or drug-related activity, repeated physical-condition failures, conflicts of interest, unauthorized payments, discrimination, or other disqualifying conduct.

Non-Rent Fees

Owners may charge only lawful and permitted fees applied consistently with the lease, program requirements, and comparable unassisted tenancies. Undisclosed or discriminatory charges can violate the HAP contract or other law.

Moves and Portability

Moves Within a Jurisdiction

A household can generally move to another eligible unit within the same public housing agency’s jurisdiction after complying with lease, notice, and program requirements.

Interjurisdictional Moves

Portability permits eligible households to lease in another jurisdiction served by a participating agency. It is a defining feature of tenant-based assistance and can support access to employment, education, family support, medical care, disability services, safety, or lower-poverty neighborhoods.

Initial Residency Restrictions

An applicant who did not reside within the issuing public housing agency’s jurisdiction when applying may be required to lease within that jurisdiction for the first year of assistance unless the agency permits an immediate portability move.

Billing and Absorption

The receiving agency can administer the household on behalf of the initial agency and bill it for costs, or it can absorb the household into its own program when funding and policy permit.

Receiving Agency Policies

The receiving agency generally applies its own payment standards, subsidy standards, utility allowances, inspection procedures, and administrative policies to the relocated household.

Practical Barriers

Portability can be slowed by paperwork transfer, inconsistent agency procedures, delayed appointments, different payment standards, housing-search difficulty, funding constraints, and disputes over billing or absorption.

Income Reviews and Continued Assistance

Regular Reexaminations

Public housing agencies periodically verify income, assets, deductions, household composition, student status, and continued eligibility. Federal reforms permit triennial income examinations for certain households with fixed income while retaining annual or interim procedures for others.

Interim Changes

Households must report specified changes between regular reviews. The timing and effect of an interim reexamination depend on federal rules, the agency’s administrative plan, the type and amount of income change, and applicable hardship protections.

Adding or Removing Household Members

Agency approval is generally required before adding a household member other than through birth, adoption, or court-awarded custody, subject to reporting rules. Unauthorized occupants can create lease and program violations.

Zero Assistance

If household income increases enough that the housing assistance payment falls to zero, the household can remain in the program for a limited period under applicable rules. Assistance can resume if income decreases before the period expires.

Termination

An agency can terminate assistance for material violations, fraud, failure to provide information, prolonged absence, unauthorized occupancy, serious lease violations, certain criminal conduct, or other authorized grounds. Federal law and local policy govern notice, evidence, mitigating circumstances, disability accommodation, VAWA protections, and hearing rights.

Administrative Reviews and Hearings

Applicant Reviews

Applicants generally have access to an informal review for specified denials, including eligibility determinations and waiting-list removal in covered circumstances.

Participant Hearings

Participants generally have access to an informal hearing for specified decisions involving income, utility allowances, unit size, termination, or family obligations.

Notice

The agency must provide notice explaining the decision and applicable review or hearing rights. Notices should be accessible to people with disabilities and meaningful to people with limited English proficiency.

Evidence and Decision

Households can generally review relevant documents, present evidence, question adverse information, and receive a written determination from an impartial hearing officer or other authorized decision-maker.

Limitations

Not every agency action requires an informal hearing. Matters of general policy, funding availability, unit physical-condition determinations in specified circumstances, and discretionary administrative decisions may be excluded.

Special-Purpose Vouchers

HUD–Veterans Affairs Supportive Housing

HUD–VASH combines Housing Choice Vouchers with Department of Veterans Affairs case management and clinical services for eligible veterans experiencing homelessness.

Mainstream Vouchers

Mainstream vouchers assist households that include a non-elderly person with a disability. They use Housing Choice Voucher rules with additional population and funding requirements.

Family Unification Program

Family Unification Program vouchers assist families for whom inadequate housing is a primary factor in the placement or delayed return of a child and eligible young adults leaving foster care.

Foster Youth to Independence

Foster Youth to Independence provides time-limited voucher assistance and supportive-service coordination for eligible young adults who have recently left foster care and face homelessness or housing instability.

Emergency Housing Vouchers

Emergency Housing Vouchers assist eligible households experiencing or at risk of homelessness, recently homeless households, and people fleeing or attempting to flee domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, stalking, or human trafficking.

Tenant-Protection Vouchers

Tenant-protection vouchers respond to authorized displacement or loss of federal housing assistance, including public housing demolition or disposition, project-based contract termination, owner opt-out, or certain preservation events.

Enhanced Vouchers

Enhanced vouchers can permit eligible residents to remain in a property after specified affordability restrictions end, even when the new rent exceeds an ordinary payment standard, subject to statutory conditions and the household’s income-related contribution.

Project-Based Voucher Component

Project-Based Vouchers are a component of the Housing Choice Voucher Program, but they operate differently from tenant-based vouchers. A public housing agency commits a portion of its voucher budget authority to designated units under a long-term housing assistance payments contract.

The subsidy remains attached to the unit when a resident moves. The next eligible household selected from the applicable waiting list receives the assistance.

Public housing agencies generally can project-base up to 20 percent of authorized voucher units, with authority for additional units under specified circumstances. Not every agency operates a Project-Based Voucher program.

Project-Based Vouchers can support new construction, rehabilitation, preservation, supportive housing, housing for older adults or people with disabilities, and development in areas where tenant-based vouchers are difficult to use.

After occupying a Project-Based Voucher unit for the required period, a household generally can request the next available form of comparable tenant-based assistance, allowing it to move while the project-based subsidy remains with the unit.

Project-Based Vouchers should not be confused with Project-Based Rental Assistance. PBVs are administered by public housing agencies using Housing Choice Voucher funding. PBRA is administered through HUD’s multifamily housing system under different Section 8 authorities and contracts.

Homeownership and Self-Sufficiency

Homeownership Option

A public housing agency may operate a Housing Choice Voucher homeownership program. Qualifying households can use monthly assistance toward eligible homeownership expenses rather than rent.

Eligibility generally involves first-time-homeowner status, employment or disability-related exceptions, minimum income, homeownership counselling, unit inspection, financing, and compliance with agency requirements. Agencies are not required to offer the option.

Family Self-Sufficiency

The Family Self-Sufficiency Program coordinates employment and supportive services for participating voucher and public housing households. As earned income and rent increase, an agency may deposit qualifying amounts into an escrow account.

A household that completes its contract and meets program requirements can receive the accumulated escrow funds. The program seeks to reduce the immediate loss of subsidy associated with increased earnings and support longer-term economic advancement.

Limitations

Homeownership and self-sufficiency programs require administrative capacity, household readiness, counselling, local partnerships, and suitable housing markets. They serve a small share of the total voucher population.

Civil Rights and Racial Context

Housing Choice Objective

The voucher model was intended to enable households to obtain housing in the private market and avoid limiting assistance to fixed public housing locations. In principle, portability and owner choice can increase access to neighborhoods, schools, employment, transportation, healthcare, and services.

Residential Segregation

Formal freedom to search does not eliminate residential segregation. Voucher holders encounter housing markets shaped by racial discrimination, redlining, exclusionary zoning, unequal wealth, concentrated poverty, landlord participation, credit screening, eviction history, and neighborhood disparities.

African American Households

African American households are substantially represented among voucher participants because of longstanding inequalities in income, housing access, wealth, discrimination, and exposure to rent burden. Voucher policy therefore has significant consequences for African American residential stability and geographic opportunity.

Source-of-Income Discrimination

The federal Fair Housing Act does not establish a general nationwide protected class for voucher status alone. State and local laws increasingly prohibit discrimination based on lawful source of income. Where such protections do not apply, owners may often decline voucher participation unless another legal obligation controls.

Disparate Treatment

An owner cannot reject or mistreat a voucher household because of race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability, or another protected characteristic. Neutral-looking screening standards can also raise legal concerns when applied selectively or when they unlawfully discriminate.

Mobility Programs

Mobility programs combine vouchers with counselling, landlord recruitment, search assistance, flexible payment standards, security deposits, application-fee assistance, transportation, and post-move support. Their goal is to make access to lower-poverty or higher-opportunity neighborhoods practically attainable.

Disability Rights

Public housing agencies and owners must provide reasonable accommodations when necessary for a person with a disability to use and enjoy housing or participate in the program. Accommodations can involve search extensions, additional bedrooms, live-in aides, higher payment standards, communication methods, accessible units, or modification of policies.

Limited English Proficiency

Recipients of federal financial assistance must take reasonable steps to provide meaningful program access to people with limited English proficiency. Agencies commonly use translated materials, interpreters, language-access plans, and notices identifying available assistance.

Violence Against Women Act Protections

Covered applicants and participants cannot be denied admission, terminated from assistance, or evicted solely because they are victims of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, stalking, or another covered form of harm.

Housing providers can request documentation under authorized procedures but must protect confidentiality. Survivors can use specified forms of self-certification or other permitted evidence.

Owners can bifurcate a lease to remove a person who committed covered violence while allowing the survivor and other lawful household members to remain, subject to applicable law.

Public housing agencies must maintain emergency-transfer plans. A transfer remains subject to the availability of an appropriate unit, voucher, or other housing resource.

VAWA does not prevent eviction or termination for a serious lease violation unrelated to victim status, but a housing provider cannot apply a more demanding standard to a survivor than to other residents.

Benefits, Outcomes, and Limitations

Housing Affordability

The program reduces the difference between low household income and private-market rent. Assistance can prevent severe rent burden, eviction, overcrowding, and homelessness.

Housing Stability

Long-term assistance can provide stability for families, older adults, people with disabilities, and households with unpredictable or fixed incomes.

Private-Market Flexibility

Tenant-based assistance allows households to choose among eligible private units and to move when employment, family, education, health, disability, or safety needs change.

Insufficient Coverage

The program does not serve every eligible household. Limited appropriations create long waiting lists, closed applications, and competition for each available voucher.

Search Failure

Some households receive vouchers but cannot use them before expiration. Search failure can result from insufficient rents, landlord refusal, screening barriers, discrimination, inaccessible housing, security deposits, limited time, or administrative delay.

Geographic Concentration

Voucher use can remain concentrated in low-rent, high-poverty, or racially segregated neighborhoods. Payment standards, landlord participation, local boundaries, zoning, and lack of mobility services influence location outcomes.

Administrative Fragmentation

Approximately 2,000 local agencies administer the program. This enables local adaptation but produces variation in rules, customer service, technology, waiting lists, inspections, landlord outreach, payment standards, and portability.

Funding Volatility

Annual appropriations, rent inflation, reserve offsets, administrative-fee levels, and per-unit costs can affect voucher issuance and agency operations. Agencies facing shortfall risk may stop issuing new vouchers or adopt cost controls to avoid terminating current participants.

Landlord Participation

The program depends on private owners. A voucher has limited value when few landlords participate or when legal protections against source-of-income discrimination are absent or weakly enforced.

Work-Incentive Debate

Because rent contributions generally rise with income, increased earnings can produce an immediate rent increase. Family Self-Sufficiency escrow accounts and other policy tools seek to reduce this effect.

Key Distinctions

Housing Choice Voucher Program and Section 8

Section 8 is the broader statutory framework. The Housing Choice Voucher Program is its principal tenant-based component. Project-Based Rental Assistance and several legacy programs also operate under Section 8 but are not Housing Choice Vouchers.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Project-Based Vouchers

A tenant-based voucher generally follows the household. A Project-Based Voucher is attached to designated units under a public housing agency contract.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Project-Based Rental Assistance

Housing Choice Vouchers are administered by public housing agencies under HUD’s Office of Public and Indian Housing. Project-Based Rental Assistance is administered through HUD’s multifamily housing system and remains attached to specific properties.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Public Housing

Public housing generally consists of units owned or controlled by a public housing agency. Housing Choice Vouchers subsidize eligible households renting privately owned units selected in the market.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Low-Income Housing Tax Credits

A voucher subsidizes a household’s rent. The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit finances rent-restricted housing through tax incentives. A household can use a voucher in an eligible tax-credit unit.

Housing Choice Vouchers and Emergency Rental Assistance

Housing Choice Vouchers provide continuing assistance subject to annual funding and eligibility. Emergency rental assistance generally addresses temporary arrears, utilities, or acute housing instability.

Payment Standard and Rent Limit

The payment standard is a subsidy benchmark, not an absolute cap on the rent an owner may request. The proposed rent must still be reasonable, and the household must satisfy initial affordability requirements.

Voucher and Lease

The voucher authorizes a household to search for housing. It is not itself a lease, guarantee of tenancy, or promise that a particular unit will be approved.

Geographic Scope

The Housing Choice Voucher Program operates throughout the United States, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and other participating jurisdictions. Approximately 2,000 public housing agencies administer vouchers under HUD funding agreements.

Administrative geography varies. A voucher may be administered by a city agency, county authority, regional authority, state agency, consortium, or another authorized public body. Overlapping agencies may operate separate waiting lists and policies within the same metropolitan area.

Portability allows eligible households to use assistance across jurisdictional boundaries. A participating household can generally lease anywhere in the United States where a public housing agency administers tenant-based assistance, subject to initial-residency and procedural rules.

Market conditions differ substantially. High-cost cities may have rents above ordinary payment standards and low vacancy rates. Rural areas may have few qualifying rentals or limited agency capacity. Suburban jurisdictions may offer higher-opportunity locations but present transportation, landlord-participation, zoning, and application barriers.

Small Area Fair Market Rents and local payment-standard areas attempt to reflect submarket differences within metropolitan regions. Geographic choice nevertheless depends on unit supply, owner participation, affordability, discrimination enforcement, and the household’s ability to search and move.

Legal Context

United States Housing Act of 1937

The Housing Choice Voucher Program is governed principally by Section 8(o), codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1437f(o). Related provisions address public housing agency organization, income, rents, eligibility, civil rights, data, family obligations, and assisted-housing administration.

Housing and Community Development Act of 1974

This law added Section 8 to the United States Housing Act and established the Existing Housing Certificate Program that became a principal predecessor of modern vouchers.

Housing and Urban-Rural Recovery Act of 1983

This law introduced the voucher approach as federal policy shifted from certificate rent limits toward a household subsidy calculated from a payment standard.

Housing and Community Development Act of 1987

This law established a permanent rental voucher program and expanded tenant-protection and preservation policy.

Cranston–Gonzalez National Affordable Housing Act of 1990

This statute revised rental assistance and established Family Self-Sufficiency as a strategy connecting assisted housing with employment and supportive services.

Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998

QHWRA merged certificates and vouchers, established the Housing Choice Voucher Program, revised income targeting, expanded portability, created the modern Project-Based Voucher structure, and reorganized public housing agency responsibilities.

Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act of 2016

HOTMA revised income and asset calculations, inspection procedures, project-based voucher authority, owner rules, and other Housing Choice Voucher requirements. HUD has implemented the statute through regulations, notices, forms, and phased compliance dates.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

The Fair Housing Act prohibits specified forms of discrimination in rental housing, advertising, financing, and related activity. It applies to most owners participating in the voucher program and to public housing agency administration.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programs receiving federal financial assistance, including public housing agencies administering HUD funds.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination in federally assisted programs and requires reasonable accommodation, effective communication, program accessibility, and other protections.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II applies to public housing agencies as public entities. Other provisions may apply to covered offices, services, and housing-related activities.

Violence Against Women Act

VAWA establishes admission, termination, eviction, confidentiality, lease-bifurcation, notice, and emergency-transfer protections for covered applicants and participants.

Summary

The Housing Choice Voucher Program is the federal government’s principal tenant-based rental-assistance system. Created through the merger of the Section 8 certificate and voucher programs, it allows eligible low-income households to lease approved privately owned housing while a public housing agency pays an income-related subsidy to the owner.

The program’s operating structure divides responsibilities among HUD, approximately 2,000 public housing agencies, private and nonprofit owners, and assisted households. Agencies determine eligibility, issue vouchers, establish payment standards, inspect units, approve rents and leases, administer portability, make monthly housing assistance payments, and monitor continued compliance.

Housing Choice Vouchers provide affordability, residential stability, and potential geographic mobility to more than 2.3 million American families. Specialized voucher programs extend assistance to veterans, people with disabilities, young adults leaving foster care, households experiencing homelessness, and other defined populations.

The program’s effectiveness depends on more than voucher issuance. Participating households must locate an available unit, find an owner willing or legally required to participate, satisfy lawful screening, secure a reasonable rent, pass inspection, and complete the process before the voucher expires.

Long waiting lists, limited appropriations, rising rents, landlord refusal, racial and source-of-income discrimination, administrative fragmentation, payment-standard constraints, and neighborhood inequality prevent the program from fully achieving universal housing choice. These limitations make landlord participation, fair-housing enforcement, adequate funding, mobility services, efficient administration, and realistic payment standards central to its continuing performance.
