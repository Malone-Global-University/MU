What Are USDA Farm Loan Programs?

USDA Farm Loan Programs are federal agricultural-credit programmes administered principally by FSA.

They are designed as a lender of first opportunity for some beginning and underserved producers and a lender of last resort or transitional lender for family farmers who cannot obtain enough conventional credit on reasonable terms.

The programmes do not operate as grants. Borrowers sign promissory notes, pledge collateral, make scheduled payments, report financial information, maintain security, and remain subject to servicing and collection.

FSA’s stated goal is to help borrowers develop sufficient financial strength, records, equity, and management capacity to graduate to commercial credit when possible.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet covers the principal direct and guaranteed Farm Ownership and Operating Loan programmes and related FSA credit tools, including Microloans, Down Payment Loans, Joint Financing, Emergency Loans, Youth Loans, Land Contract Guarantees, Conservation Loans, tribal land programmes, and Farm Storage Facility Loans.

It examines history, eligibility, credit standards, application, interest, terms, collateral, supervised credit, servicing, appeals, discrimination, distressed-borrower reform, and current issues through August 3, 2026.

It does not determine a particular applicant’s eligibility, repayment ability, collateral value, interest rate, loan amount, environmental compliance, or legal rights. Current statutes, regulations, handbooks, forms, notices, appropriations, and individual loan documents control.

Historical Roots of Federal Farm Credit

Federal farm lending emerged from recurring problems in agricultural credit.

Farmers require large amounts of capital before harvest, often pledge land and crops as security, face volatile prices and weather, and can lose repayment ability through events outside their control.

Private lenders historically concentrated credit among established landowners with clear title, substantial equity, commodity experience, and predictable markets.

Tenant farmers, sharecroppers, Black farmers, Native producers, immigrants, beginning farmers, small farms, and disaster-affected borrowers frequently lacked equal access.

New Deal Farm Ownership and Rehabilitation Credit

During the Great Depression, federal agencies made rehabilitation and resettlement loans to distressed rural families.

The Bankhead–Jones Farm Tenant Act of 1937 authorised federal loans helping eligible tenant farmers purchase farms.

These programmes established enduring principles:

  • public credit can help tenants become owners;
  • loan supervision can accompany financing;
  • family-farm structure can guide eligibility;
  • long repayment terms are necessary for land; and
  • credit policy affects land ownership and rural class structure.

Farmers Home Administration

USDA consolidated rural lending functions within the Farmers Home Administration in 1946.

FmHA administered farm ownership, operating, emergency, housing, community, and rural-development credit.

Farm loans were characterised as supervised credit: the agency financed borrowers while reviewing farm plans, management, income, family living, security, and repayment.

FmHA became crucial during drought, recession, commodity-price collapse, and the 1980s farm crisis, but its administration also became a central site of civil-rights complaints.

Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act

Congress enacted the Consolidated Farmers Home Administration Act in 1961, later renamed the Consolidated Farm and Rural Development Act.

The statute provides much of the modern authority for:

  • Farm Ownership Loans;
  • Operating Loans;
  • Emergency Loans;
  • guaranteed loans;
  • beginning-farmer targeting;
  • socially disadvantaged producer provisions;
  • loan servicing;
  • conservation contracts;
  • land-contract guarantees; and
  • tribal land credit.

Creation of the Farm Service Agency

USDA reorganisation in 1994 created FSA and transferred major farm-credit responsibilities from FmHA.

FSA combined farm lending with commodity, conservation, disaster, and county-office functions.

Direct and guaranteed farm lending became organisationally distinct from USDA Rural Development housing and community programmes.

Farm Ownership Loans

Farm Ownership Loans finance long-term real-estate and structural needs.

Eligible purposes can include:

  • purchasing a farm or ranch;
  • enlarging an existing operation;
  • constructing or repairing farm dwellings;
  • constructing barns, service buildings, fencing, wells, and permanent facilities;
  • improving farmland;
  • soil and water conservation;
  • paying closing costs;
  • refinancing qualifying bridge or interim debt; and
  • other authorised real-estate purposes.

The operation must be an eligible agricultural enterprise rather than primarily a nonfarm residence or speculative land investment.

Direct Farm Ownership Loans

FSA makes and services Direct Farm Ownership Loans.

The 2026 maximum is $600,000, including outstanding direct Farm Ownership, Conservation, and related real-estate balances under applicable combined limits.

Terms can extend to forty years.

FSA evaluates:

  • purchase price and appraisal;
  • farm size and family-farm status;
  • repayment ability;
  • farm experience;
  • ownership and title;
  • environmental compliance;
  • water and access rights;
  • security value;
  • business plan; and
  • credit elsewhere.

Guaranteed Farm Ownership Loans

A commercial lender makes the loan and requests an FSA guarantee.

The fiscal year 2026 maximum standard guarantee loan amount is $2,343,000.

The lender underwrites cash flow, collateral, management, and credit history, while FSA determines federal eligibility, environmental compliance, guarantee conditions, and loss-protection requirements.

Guaranteed Farm Ownership loans can have terms up to forty years.

Beginning Farmer Down Payment Program

The Down Payment Program helps qualified beginning and socially disadvantaged or targeted underserved producers purchase a farm with a small borrower contribution and shared financing.

The structure generally requires:

  • at least five percent cash down payment from the applicant;
  • FSA financing up to forty-five percent of the lesser of purchase price, appraised value, or $667,000;
  • a maximum FSA portion of $300,150;
  • remaining financing from a commercial lender, cooperative lender, state programme, seller, or private party;
  • a maximum twenty-year term for the FSA loan;
  • a rate four percentage points below the ordinary direct FO rate, but not below the statutory floor; and
  • long-term amortisation and balloon-payment protections for participating financing.

Joint Financing

Joint Financing combines an FSA Direct Farm Ownership Loan with another lender or seller.

FSA can provide up to fifty percent of the total amount financed, subject to the $600,000 direct limit.

The other lender can finance the remaining purchase or improvement cost with or without an FSA guarantee.

The FSA portion receives a reduced interest rate, while the other lender negotiates its own rate and terms.

Joint financing stretches limited direct-loan appropriations and introduces borrowers to commercial credit.

Land Contract Guarantees

FSA can guarantee a private seller’s land contract with a qualified beginning or socially disadvantaged farmer or rancher.

The applicant generally contributes at least five percent of the purchase price.

The seller can select:

  • a prompt-payment guarantee covering specified annual instalments; or
  • a standard guarantee protecting a percentage of the outstanding principal and interest.

The maximum guaranteed contract amount is $500,000.

The programme encourages retiring owners to sell to new producers while reducing seller credit risk.

Farm Operating Loans

Operating Loans finance production, livestock, equipment, and working-capital needs.

Eligible purposes can include:

  • seed, feed, fertiliser, fuel, and chemicals;
  • livestock and poultry;
  • farm machinery and equipment;
  • crop and livestock insurance;
  • land rent;
  • marketing and processing expenses;
  • family living expenses;
  • minor building and fencing improvements;
  • water and land development;
  • reorganisation of the farm business;
  • refinancing qualifying farm debt; and
  • other authorised operating costs.

Direct Operating Loans

FSA makes direct operating term loans and, where authorised, lines of credit.

The maximum outstanding direct Operating Loan principal is $400,000.

Repayment generally ranges from one to seven years.

Annual production loans are often repaid when crops, livestock, or products are sold.

Equipment or breeding-livestock loans can use longer terms matched to useful life and cash flow.

Guaranteed Operating Loans

Guaranteed Operating Loans enable commercial lenders to finance operating costs when the applicant does not meet ordinary lender standards without federal risk protection.

The fiscal year 2026 maximum is $2,343,000, subject to combined outstanding-balance limits.

The lender can structure:

  • single-advance term loans;
  • revolving or nonrevolving lines of credit;
  • annual production financing;
  • equipment loans;
  • livestock loans; and
  • qualifying refinancing.

EZ Guarantee

EZ Guarantee simplifies lender application and underwriting for smaller guaranteed requests.

It is available up to $100,000 for eligible Farm Ownership or Operating purposes.

The lender uses streamlined underwriting similar to a comparable nonguaranteed loan, but federal eligibility, purpose, security, environmental, and servicing requirements remain.

The programme is intended to improve early access for small, beginning, niche, and underserved family farms.

Microloan Programs

FSA offers Direct Operating and Farm Ownership Microloans up to $50,000.

Microloans use simplified forms, flexible management experience, smaller security needs, and underwriting tailored to modest operations.

They are intended for:

  • beginning farmers;
  • small farms;
  • specialty-crop producers;
  • organic farms;
  • direct-market operations;
  • urban agriculture;
  • community-supported agriculture;
  • small livestock operations;
  • farmers markets; and
  • nontraditional enterprises.

Operating Microloan

Operating Microloans can finance livestock, equipment, supplies, feed, seed, minor improvements, marketing, and family living expenses.

Terms range from one to seven years.

Applicants can use small-business, apprenticeship, mentorship, military, education, and agricultural experience to demonstrate management capacity.

Alternative records can be important for direct-market and cash-based operations.

Farm Ownership Microloan

Farm Ownership Microloans can finance modest land purchases, farm improvements, buildings, wells, fencing, and conservation needs.

The maximum is $50,000 and the term can extend to twenty-five years under applicable rules.

The smaller amount can serve urban, peri-urban, specialty, and beginning operations but may be insufficient in high-cost land markets.

Emergency Farm Loans

Emergency Loans help eligible producers recover from a qualifying natural disaster.

The farm must be located in a county covered by a presidential major-disaster declaration, secretarial disaster designation, or authorised contiguous-county rule.

Funds can:

  • restore or replace essential property;
  • replace livestock or equipment;
  • repair farm structures;
  • pay disaster-year production costs;
  • cover essential family living expenses;
  • reorganise the farm;
  • refinance certain disaster-related debt; and
  • restore farm real estate.

Emergency Loan Eligibility and Amount

The applicant must have suffered a qualifying physical or production loss directly related to the disaster and demonstrate repayment ability after available insurance and assistance.

The maximum is the lesser of:

  • one hundred percent of qualifying actual loss;
  • the amount needed to restore the operation and develop a feasible plan; or
  • $500,000.

Crop-insurance indemnities, disaster payments, grants, and other duplicate compensation reduce eligible loss.

Youth Loans

Youth Loans finance modest agricultural income-producing projects for young people ages ten through twenty at loan closing.

The project must be connected with:

  • 4-H;
  • Future Farmers of America;
  • tribal youth organisations;
  • school agricultural education;
  • county extension; or
  • another recognised agricultural group.

A project advisor supervises planning and operation.

Loans can finance livestock, seed, equipment, supplies, tools, rent, and operating expenses up to $10,000.

Guaranteed Conservation Loans

Conservation Loans finance conservation activities included in a USDA-approved conservation or forestry plan.

Eligible purposes can include:

  • erosion control;
  • water conservation;
  • manure management;
  • fencing and prescribed grazing;
  • forest management;
  • water-quality improvements;
  • renewable-energy or efficiency measures tied to conservation plans; and
  • other approved practices.

Conservation applicants do not have to satisfy the ordinary family-farm or inability-to-obtain-credit-without-a-guarantee tests, but other eligibility requirements apply.

Farm Storage Facility Loans

Farm Storage Facility Loans are Commodity Credit Corporation loans administered by FSA rather than ordinary direct Farm Ownership or Operating Loans.

They finance eligible:

  • grain bins;
  • cold storage;
  • hay storage;
  • handling equipment;
  • drying and processing components;
  • portable structures;
  • storage and handling trucks; and
  • facilities for eligible commodities.

The maximum is generally $500,000 for facilities and $100,000 for eligible trucks, with terms from three to twelve years.

Indian Tribal Land Acquisition Loans

This programme assists federally recognised tribes in purchasing land within reservation boundaries.

Loans can support:

  • land consolidation;
  • agricultural production;
  • economic development;
  • restoration of tribal land bases;
  • resource management; and
  • long-term sovereignty and community use.

Terms can extend to thirty years. Loan size depends on the acquisition, security, statutory authority, and repayment ability.

Highly Fractionated Indian Land Loan Program

HFIL addresses land ownership divided among many heirs through generations of federal allotment policy.

FSA lends to eligible intermediary lenders, which re-lend to tribes, tribal entities, and tribal members to purchase fractional interests in trust or restricted land.

The programme can:

  • consolidate ownership;
  • increase usable acreage;
  • improve agricultural management;
  • reduce title fragmentation;
  • support leasing and collateral; and
  • strengthen tribal economic control.

Current programme information lists ultimate borrower loans up to $500,000 and terms up to thirty years.

Beginning Farmers and Ranchers

A beginning farmer generally has not operated a farm or ranch for more than ten years and materially participates in the operation.

For entity applicants, qualifying rules apply to members, ownership, participation, and management.

Beginning-farmer assistance includes:

  • targeted direct and guaranteed funds;
  • Down Payment Loans;
  • Joint Financing;
  • Land Contract Guarantees;
  • Microloans;
  • flexible farm-experience substitutions;
  • inventory property preference;
  • state and private lender coordination; and
  • outreach and technical assistance.

Underserved and Socially Disadvantaged Producers

Congress and USDA target loan funds to address historic and continuing credit barriers.

FSA commonly identifies targeted underserved groups as:

  • women;
  • African Americans;
  • American Indians and Alaska Natives;
  • Hispanics;
  • Asians; and
  • Pacific Islanders.

Targeting does not waive repayment, feasibility, legal capacity, purpose, or other eligibility requirements.

It reserves funds, supports outreach, and can provide access to specialised ownership tools.

Family-Farm Requirement

Most Farm Ownership and Operating Loans support family farms rather than passive investment, industrial ownership disconnected from operators, or primarily nonagricultural enterprises.

FSA considers:

  • community standards;
  • who makes day-to-day management decisions;
  • family labour;
  • hired and seasonal labour;
  • scale and enterprise type;
  • ownership and tenancy;
  • operator participation; and
  • whether the operation is primarily agricultural.

A family farm can be large or use substantial hired labour, especially for labour-intensive commodities, but the family must remain the genuine management centre.

Citizenship and Legal Status

Eligible applicants generally must be United States citizens, noncitizen nationals, or qualified legal residents under programme rules.

Entity eligibility depends on the citizenship or immigration status of members and controlling interests.

The programmes operate in the states and designated territories, including Puerto Rico and the United States Virgin Islands, subject to local office and programme authority.

Legal Capacity and Federal Debt

The applicant must have legal capacity to incur the debt and sign enforceable loan and security documents.

An applicant generally cannot be delinquent on federal debt unless an authorised exception, repayment arrangement, bankruptcy treatment, or programme rule applies.

Prior federal debt forgiveness can restrict eligibility, particularly after April 4, 1996, subject to statutory exceptions.

Credit History

FSA and guaranteed lenders evaluate whether the applicant has acted responsibly in meeting obligations.

Poor credit does not automatically require denial.

Decision-makers consider:

  • circumstances beyond the applicant’s control;
  • medical emergencies;
  • divorce or family crisis;
  • temporary unemployment;
  • disaster;
  • disputed accounts;
  • isolated late payments;
  • recent corrective conduct;
  • identity theft; and
  • overall pattern rather than credit score alone.

Credit Elsewhere

Most direct and guaranteed applicants must show that sufficient credit is unavailable without FSA assistance.

The test does not always require formal rejection letters, but FSA can request documentation and use the Market Placement Program to explore guaranteed or commercial credit.

Credit can be considered unavailable when:

  • the lender refuses the loan;
  • the approved amount is insufficient;
  • collateral requirements are unreasonable;
  • repayment terms make the farm infeasible;
  • the lender requires an FSA guarantee; or
  • rates and conditions are not reasonable for the operation.

Management and Farm Experience

FSA must determine that the applicant can manage the proposed farm successfully.

Experience can include:

  • farm ownership or tenancy;
  • farm employment with management responsibility;
  • apprenticeship;
  • agricultural education;
  • Extension and incubator programmes;
  • SCORE or approved mentorship;
  • military leadership and logistics;
  • small-business management;
  • 4-H or FFA enterprise projects;
  • urban agriculture; and
  • production and marketing experience.

Direct Farm Ownership generally requires three years of qualifying participation within the previous ten years, with authorised substitutions.

Feasible Farm Operating Plan

A feasible plan projects enough income to pay:

  • ordinary farm operating expenses;
  • family living expenses;
  • taxes;
  • capital replacement;
  • loan instalments;
  • other debts;
  • required reserves; and
  • reasonable savings or working capital under current policy.

FSA reviews prices, yields, markets, production history, costs, contracts, risk management, debt structure, and management assumptions.

Optimistic projections must be supported by evidence.

Farm Business Plan

The business plan connects the loan purpose to production and repayment.

It commonly includes:

  • enterprise description;
  • land and resource base;
  • ownership and leases;
  • production methods;
  • historical and projected yields;
  • market channels;
  • contracts and prices;
  • equipment and labour;
  • insurance;
  • income and expense projections;
  • balance sheet;
  • cash-flow budget;
  • family living budget; and
  • risk and contingency planning.

Direct Loan Application

A direct applicant works with the local FSA Farm Loan Team.

Application information can include:

  • identity and entity documents;
  • loan request and purpose;
  • farm business plan;
  • balance sheet;
  • income and expense history;
  • tax returns;
  • credit authorisation;
  • production records;
  • leases and deeds;
  • purchase contracts;
  • equipment lists;
  • debts and payment history;
  • environmental information; and
  • eligibility certifications.

Online Loan Tools

USDA provides a Loan Assistance Tool that guides applicants through potential direct-loan options and application requirements.

The online direct-loan application can support:

  • electronic signatures;
  • document upload;
  • balance-sheet preparation;
  • farm operating plans;
  • application tracking; and
  • paperless communication.

Applicants can still seek local office assistance, reasonable accommodation, language access, and paper alternatives.

Complete Application and Processing

FSA reviews the submission for completeness before making a credit decision.

A complete application includes all information needed to decide:

  • eligibility;
  • loan purpose;
  • repayment ability;
  • security;
  • farm experience;
  • environmental review;
  • credit elsewhere;
  • civil-rights compliance; and
  • funding availability.

FSA must notify the applicant when information is missing and issue a written approval or adverse decision.

Guaranteed Loan Application

The borrower applies to a commercial lender rather than directly requesting money from FSA.

The lender:

  1. underwrites the request;
  2. determines that a guarantee is needed;
  3. prepares the Application for Guarantee;
  4. submits eligibility and credit information to FSA;
  5. receives a conditional commitment;
  6. closes and funds the loan; and
  7. obtains the final guarantee after meeting conditions.

The guaranteed loan remains the lender’s asset and responsibility.

Limited-Resource Interest Rates

FSA can provide limited-resource rates to qualifying direct borrowers who cannot develop a feasible plan at the regular rate and meet regulatory criteria.

The rate is reviewed periodically and can change when the borrower’s repayment ability improves.

Limited-resource treatment is not automatic and does not waive principal, security, or repayment obligations.

Collateral and Security

FSA and guaranteed lenders ordinarily take liens on property purchased or produced with loan funds.

Security can include:

  • farm real estate;
  • crops;
  • livestock;
  • equipment;
  • farm products;
  • accounts and contract rights;
  • insurance proceeds;
  • government payments;
  • water rights; and
  • other available assets.

The 2024 rule reduced additional collateral requirements and the frequency with which a personal residence must secure a direct farm loan.

Appraisal and Property Review

Real-estate loans require a valuation meeting federal standards.

Review can address:

  • market value;
  • agricultural productivity;
  • comparable sales;
  • buildings and improvements;
  • water rights;
  • access;
  • easements;
  • environmental conditions;
  • title defects;
  • zoning;
  • mineral rights; and
  • highest and best agricultural use.

FSA cannot finance an inflated price merely because the applicant and seller agree.

Environmental and Conservation Review

Federal lending actions must comply with environmental and conservation law.

Review can include:

  • National Environmental Policy Act;
  • highly erodible land and wetland conservation;
  • endangered species;
  • historic preservation;
  • floodplains and wetlands;
  • hazardous substances;
  • water quality;
  • cultural resources;
  • concentrated animal feeding operations; and
  • environmental justice.

The applicant must not begin construction or irreversible work before required review and approval.

Borrower Training

Direct borrowers can be required to complete production and financial-management training unless FSA grants a waiver based on demonstrated competence.

Training can cover:

  • recordkeeping;
  • cash flow;
  • balance sheets;
  • enterprise analysis;
  • marketing;
  • taxes;
  • credit management;
  • risk management;
  • production planning; and
  • family living budgets.

The goal is borrower success rather than punishment, but failure to complete required training can place the loan in nonmonetary default.

Supervised Credit

FSA direct lending includes continuing guidance after closing.

Supervision can involve:

  • annual farm-plan review;
  • financial statements;
  • production and marketing analysis;
  • security inspections;
  • controlled proceeds;
  • training;
  • graduation review;
  • loan restructuring;
  • disaster response; and
  • coordination with Extension, conservation, and lenders.

Effective supervision should support independent management rather than reproduce paternalistic or discriminatory control.

Graduation to Commercial Credit

Direct Farm Loan Programs are intended to be temporary when a borrower becomes able to obtain conventional credit.

FSA can review whether a borrower can refinance with:

  • a commercial bank;
  • Farm Credit System lender;
  • credit union;
  • cooperative lender;
  • state beginning-farmer authority;
  • private seller; or
  • an FSA guaranteed lender.

Graduation frees direct funds for other applicants, but refinancing must be reasonable and not undermine the farm’s viability.

Loan Approval and Closing

Approval is conditioned on completion of legal and financial requirements.

Conditions can include:

  • acceptable title;
  • appraisal;
  • insurance;
  • lien priority;
  • leases;
  • entity authority;
  • environmental review;
  • down payment;
  • other lender commitment;
  • debt payoff;
  • security instruments;
  • borrower training agreement; and
  • updated farm plan.

The borrower should review the promissory note, security agreement, mortgage, repayment schedule, and borrower responsibilities before signing.

Use and Supervision of Loan Funds

Loan proceeds must be used for approved purposes.

FSA can use supervised bank accounts, joint checks, invoices, inspections, or staged advances.

Material changes in purpose, vendor, property, construction, or farm plan require prior approval.

Unauthorised use can create default, repayment, liquidation, or fraud consequences.

Repayment Structure

Payments should match the timing of agricultural income.

Schedules can be:

  • monthly;
  • quarterly;
  • semiannual;
  • annual;
  • seasonal;
  • amortised; or
  • structured around livestock, crop, or contract proceeds.

The 2024 rule expanded flexibility to permit reasonable working-capital and savings margins rather than designing every feasible plan to leave no financial cushion.

Sale of Security and Release of Proceeds

A borrower generally cannot sell mortgaged crops, livestock, equipment, or real estate and retain proceeds without lender approval.

Proceeds can be released for:

  • ordinary operating expenses;
  • family living expenses in the approved plan;
  • replacement of essential security;
  • payment of superior liens;
  • loan instalments;
  • taxes and insurance;
  • approved capital improvements; and
  • other authorised purposes.

Conversion of secured property can result in severe civil or criminal consequences.

Financial Distress

A borrower should contact FSA or the guaranteed lender before missing a payment.

Early intervention can identify:

  • cash-flow shortage;
  • disaster loss;
  • commodity-price decline;
  • medical crisis;
  • family transition;
  • equipment failure;
  • lease loss;
  • market disruption;
  • excess debt; and
  • need for restructuring or additional credit.

Distressed Borrower Set-Aside

Effective September 25, 2024, DBSA created a streamlined servicing option for eligible financially distressed direct borrowers.

It can defer one annual instalment on each qualifying loan and place the amount at the end of the schedule at a reduced interest rate.

Unlike Disaster Set-Aside, DBSA does not require a designated natural disaster.

The borrower must meet eligibility, feasibility, security, and documentation requirements.

Disaster Set-Aside

Disaster Set-Aside can move one qualifying direct-loan instalment to the end of the loan after a designated disaster causes repayment difficulty.

It is intended for temporary disaster-related inability rather than long-term insolvency.

The borrower generally must be current or within authorised delinquency limits, show disaster impact, and demonstrate future repayment ability.

Primary Loan Servicing

Delinquent or financially distressed direct borrowers can receive notice of servicing options.

Primary servicing can include:

  • reamortisation;
  • rescheduling;
  • deferral;
  • interest-rate reduction;
  • write-down;
  • conservation contract;
  • combination servicing;
  • current-market-value buyout; and
  • debt settlement when other options fail.

FSA uses statutory priority and feasibility calculations rather than allowing the borrower to select any remedy without qualification.

Conservation Contracts

A conservation contract can reduce or cancel part of direct farm-loan debt in exchange for a long-term conservation, recreation, or wildlife easement or contract on qualifying land.

The arrangement can protect:

  • wetlands;
  • highly erodible land;
  • wildlife habitat;
  • riparian areas;
  • historic resources;
  • open space; and
  • other conservation values.

It can be used with or without other servicing when programme requirements are met.

Shared Appreciation Agreements

When FSA writes down real-estate-secured debt, the borrower can be required to share part of later appreciation in the property.

The agreement protects the public from granting a write-down followed by rapid sale at a large gain.

Valuation, maturity, payment, servicing, and dispute rules have produced significant borrower controversy and litigation.

Liquidation and Foreclosure

When no feasible servicing option exists or a borrower fails to comply, FSA or the guaranteed lender can liquidate security.

Steps can include:

  • demand and acceleration;
  • voluntary sale;
  • assignment of proceeds;
  • chattel liquidation;
  • foreclosure;
  • government acquisition;
  • homestead protection review;
  • deficiency collection; and
  • debt settlement.

Notice, servicing review, appeal, bankruptcy, and state foreclosure law can affect timing and rights.

FSA Inventory Farm Property

FSA can acquire farm property through foreclosure, voluntary conveyance, or liquidation.

Statute provides preference or special opportunities for beginning and socially disadvantaged farmers in leasing or purchasing qualifying inventory property.

Inventory availability is limited and varies geographically.

Guaranteed Loan Servicing

The commercial lender remains responsible for servicing.

The lender must:

  • service the loan as a prudent lender;
  • monitor collateral and repayment;
  • document protective advances;
  • consider restructuring;
  • obtain FSA approval for major actions;
  • liquidate security when required;
  • submit loss claims accurately; and
  • mitigate federal loss.

FSA can reduce a loss payment when the lender fails to follow the guarantee or prudent-servicing standards.

Reconsideration, Mediation, and Appeals

An adverse decision should identify the factual and legal reasons and available review rights.

Depending on the issue, an applicant or borrower can seek:

  • informal review;
  • reconsideration by FSA;
  • state-certified agricultural mediation;
  • appeal to the USDA National Appeals Division;
  • review by the NAD Director;
  • judicial review; and
  • equal-credit or civil-rights complaint procedures.

Deadlines can be short and should be preserved even while the parties negotiate.

Agricultural Mediation

USDA-certified state mediation programmes provide neutral assistance in agricultural-credit disputes.

Mediation can address:

  • loan denial;
  • servicing;
  • collateral;
  • repayment plans;
  • foreclosure;
  • leases;
  • creditor negotiations;
  • farm transitions;
  • USDA programme disputes; and
  • other authorised agricultural conflicts.

Mediation does not force settlement or extend every appeal deadline automatically.

Civil-Rights History

USDA has acknowledged a long history of discrimination against farmers and ranchers in farm lending and programme delivery.

Discrimination included:

  • denial or delay of loans;
  • smaller loan amounts;
  • restrictive conditions;
  • unequal servicing;
  • lack of technical assistance;
  • retaliation;
  • county-committee exclusion;
  • loss of complaint records;
  • foreclosure while complaints remained unresolved; and
  • systemic exclusion from land and credit.

These practices contributed to land loss, reduced farm scale, intergenerational wealth loss, and distrust of USDA.

Pigford Litigation

Pigford v. Glickman addressed discrimination complaints by Black farmers who sought USDA farm loans and benefits.

The 1999 consent decree created an alternative dispute-resolution process for qualifying claims.

Later legislation and Pigford II addressed many people who had missed the original filing deadline.

The settlements compensated some historic harm but did not restore all lost land, income, credit opportunities, or institutional trust.

Other Farm-Lending Civil-Rights Cases

Native American, Hispanic, and women farmers pursued litigation and administrative claims concerning USDA discrimination.

Major proceedings included Keepseagle, Garcia, and Love-related claims processes.

USDA has publicly recognised that overt discrimination and embedded policies limited access to credit across multiple groups.

Discrimination Financial Assistance

The Inflation Reduction Act authorised financial assistance for people who experienced discrimination in USDA farm-lending programmes before January 1, 2021.

USDA completed the main application and payment process in 2024.

The programme was not a new loan, debt cancellation entitlement, or admission concerning every individual claim.

It reflected congressional recognition that historic discrimination required assistance beyond ordinary current loan eligibility.

Inflation Reduction Act Distressed-Borrower Assistance

Section 22006 authorised assistance for distressed direct and guaranteed farm-loan borrowers.

USDA provided automatic and case-by-case payments to reduce delinquency, prevent acceleration and foreclosure, address shared-appreciation obligations, and help financially distressed borrowers remain viable.

The temporary assistance complemented, but did not replace, permanent servicing rights.

Significance for African American Farmers

Access to affordable ownership and operating credit is central to Black farm survival.

Black farmers have faced:

  • historic exclusion from land ownership;
  • racial violence and dispossession;
  • heirs’ property;
  • discriminatory lending;
  • underfunded farm operations;
  • weak access to markets and equipment;
  • delayed USDA decisions;
  • foreclosure and forced sale;
  • county-level political exclusion; and
  • intergenerational wealth loss.

FSA lending can provide entry and preservation capital, but equitable outcomes require timely decisions, adequate amounts, fair appraisals, flexible documentation, respectful service, and effective appeal rights.

Heirs’ Property

Heirs’ property is family land inherited without clear probate or partition, leaving multiple owners with undivided interests.

Credit problems can include:

  • unclear authority to mortgage;
  • missing heirs;
  • fractional ownership;
  • title defects;
  • partition-sale risk;
  • inability to prove farm control;
  • appraisal difficulty; and
  • weak collateral value.

USDA relending and legal-assistance programmes can help resolve ownership and succession, but title work can be lengthy and expensive.

Native American and Tribal Producers

Native producers can face trust land, restricted title, fractionation, Bureau of Indian Affairs approval, remote markets, and limited collateral.

FSA tools include:

  • direct and guaranteed farm loans;
  • Indian Tribal Land Acquisition Loans;
  • Highly Fractionated Indian Land Loans;
  • Youth Loans through tribal organisations;
  • Microloans;
  • targeted underserved funds; and
  • outreach partnerships.

Administration should respect tribal sovereignty, trust responsibilities, and tribal land-tenure systems.

Women Farmers and Ranchers

Women can qualify for targeted underserved funds while meeting ordinary credit requirements.

Barriers have included:

  • land titled in a spouse’s name;
  • farm labour not recognised as management experience;
  • lack of independent credit history;
  • gender stereotyping;
  • smaller and diversified operations;
  • caregiving responsibilities;
  • unequal agent and lender treatment; and
  • inheritance and succession disputes.

FSA must evaluate actual ownership, management, and repayment ability rather than traditional gender roles.

Hispanic, Immigrant, and Limited-English-Proficient Producers

Producers can face language barriers, limited financial records, nontraditional markets, land-access constraints, and unfamiliarity with federal credit.

Meaningful access requires:

  • translated vital documents;
  • qualified interpretation;
  • clear explanation of debt and collateral;
  • nondiscriminatory citizenship review;
  • reasonable documentation;
  • protection from predatory intermediaries; and
  • effective complaint and appeal processes.

Small, Urban, and Diversified Farms

Conventional agricultural underwriting often assumes commodity acreage, standard markets, long production histories, and substantial collateral.

Small and diversified farms can rely on:

  • farmers markets;
  • community-supported agriculture;
  • direct retail;
  • restaurants and institutions;
  • specialty crops;
  • organic premiums;
  • value-added products;
  • urban leases;
  • greenhouses; and
  • mixed livestock and crops.

Microloans, alternative records, whole-farm planning, and flexible experience rules improve access.

Relationship to Disaster Assistance and Crop Insurance

Emergency Loans are debt and must be repaid; disaster payments and crop-insurance indemnities are assistance or contractual insurance proceeds.

A producer can use several programmes, but duplicate compensation for the same loss is prohibited.

FSA considers:

  • crop-insurance indemnity;
  • Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance;
  • livestock disaster payments;
  • supplemental disaster relief;
  • insurance on buildings and equipment;
  • Federal Emergency Management Agency assistance;
  • Small Business Administration disaster loans; and
  • other compensation.

Relationship to the Farm Credit System

The Farm Credit System is a nationwide borrower-owned network of specialised agricultural lenders.

It is federally chartered but not the same as FSA.

Farm Credit institutions can:

  • make conventional farm loans;
  • participate with FSA direct loans;
  • make FSA guaranteed loans;
  • refinance borrowers graduating from direct credit; and
  • provide young, beginning, and small-farmer programmes.

Relationship to Small Business Administration Loans

SBA generally excludes or limits financing for primary agricultural production that is eligible for FSA farm lending, while supporting many nonfarm and value-added rural businesses.

A farm enterprise can require coordination among FSA, SBA, Rural Development, Farm Credit, commercial lenders, and state finance authorities depending on whether the purpose is production, processing, retail, housing, energy, or nonfarm business.

Bankruptcy

Farmers can seek bankruptcy protection under Chapters 7, 11, 12, or 13, with Chapter 12 designed for qualifying family farmers and fishermen.

Bankruptcy can:

  • stay collection;
  • restructure secured debt;
  • modify payment schedules;
  • sell property;
  • address tax claims;
  • preserve operations; or
  • liquidate assets.

FSA eligibility, servicing, liens, setoff, and future credit must be analysed with bankruptcy orders and federal law.

Programme Performance and Public Purpose

Farm Loan Programs are expected to balance several goals:

  • expand access to credit;
  • protect public funds;
  • support family farms;
  • help beginning farmers enter agriculture;
  • address historic discrimination;
  • respond to disasters;
  • support viable farm businesses;
  • graduate borrowers to commercial credit;
  • avoid unnecessary foreclosure; and
  • maintain fair and timely administration.

Tension arises because strict loss prevention can exclude the producers the programme exists to serve, while weak underwriting can burden borrowers with unpayable debt.

Fraud and False Statements

Fraud can include:

  • false assets or debts;
  • fabricated leases or purchase contracts;
  • hidden ownership;
  • false production or income;
  • misuse of loan funds;
  • sale of collateral without remitting proceeds;
  • duplicate disaster compensation;
  • false disaster losses;
  • straw borrowers;
  • lender collusion; and
  • concealment in servicing applications.

Consequences can include denial, acceleration, liquidation, civil penalties, False Claims Act liability, debarment, and criminal prosecution.

Privacy and Information Sharing

Loan files contain tax returns, Social Security numbers, financial statements, land records, credit reports, family information, and business plans.

FSA can share information for authorised:

  • credit reporting;
  • debt collection;
  • lender coordination;
  • programme eligibility;
  • fraud investigation;
  • civil-rights enforcement;
  • audits;
  • legal proceedings; and
  • statutory reporting.

Applicants should understand privacy notices and avoid giving sensitive documents to unverified private intermediaries.

Major Achievements

  • Provides federal credit to family farmers unable to obtain adequate commercial financing.
  • Helps beginning farmers purchase land and enter agriculture.
  • Supports annual production, livestock, equipment, and working capital.
  • Uses guarantees to leverage private lender funds.
  • Provides long-term ownership financing up to forty years.
  • Offers reduced-rate Down Payment and Joint Financing tools.
  • Provides simplified Microloans for small and nontraditional operations.
  • Supports agricultural youth projects.
  • Responds to natural disasters through Emergency Loans.
  • Supports tribal land consolidation and acquisition.
  • Targets funds to beginning and underserved applicants.
  • Provides supervised credit and borrower training.
  • Offers servicing intended to prevent unnecessary foreclosure.
  • Created a non-disaster distressed-borrower set-aside in 2024.
  • Coordinates with commercial banks, Farm Credit, state programmes, and sellers.
  • Supports storage, conservation, and farm infrastructure.
  • Provides written appeal and mediation rights.
  • Can preserve farm ownership and intergenerational rural wealth.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Direct loan limits can be inadequate in high-cost land and equipment markets.
  • Funding depends on annual appropriations and can be exhausted.
  • Applications require extensive records and planning.
  • Processing delays can cause loss of land purchases or production seasons.
  • Credit does not solve poor markets, high land cost, or inadequate farm income.
  • Debt can worsen distress when projections fail.
  • Collateral and appraisal can disadvantage small and heirs’ property farms.
  • Beginning-farmer experience rules can exclude people without formal opportunities.
  • Commercial lenders can remain reluctant even with guarantees.
  • Guaranteed rates can be substantially higher than direct rates.
  • Supervised credit can become intrusive or inconsistently administered.
  • Historic discrimination has caused lasting distrust and land loss.
  • County-level service quality varies.
  • Language and disability access can be uneven.
  • Loan servicing is complex and deadline driven.
  • Foreclosure can destroy family land and community stability.
  • Emergency Loans add debt after disaster.
  • Targeted funds do not guarantee approval or adequate capital.
  • Online systems can disadvantage applicants without broadband or digital records.
  • Graduation pressure can move borrowers to costlier credit.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Loan Limits

Guaranteed limits adjust annually, while the $600,000 Direct Farm Ownership and $400,000 Direct Operating limits face pressure from rising land, machinery, livestock, and input costs.

Interest Rates

Monthly direct rates increased during the first half of 2026, affecting feasibility and the value of Down Payment and Joint Financing discounts.

Distressed Borrower Set-Aside

FSA continues implementing the 2024 low-interest instalment-deferral programme and evaluating whether it prevents deeper delinquency.

Flexible Repayment and Savings

Current rules permit farm plans to include working-capital reserves and household savings rather than requiring cash flow to be consumed entirely by debt service.

Collateral Reform

FSA is applying reduced additional-security requirements intended to protect personal residences and allow borrowers to leverage equity for productive investment.

Online Application Modernization

USDA continues digitising applications and customer service while maintaining paper, language, disability, and in-person access.

Beginning Farmer Land Access

High land values and investor competition limit the practical reach of direct ownership caps and the $300,150 Down Payment Loan maximum.

Racial Equity

USDA faces continuing demands for timely targeted lending, fair servicing, public demographic data, effective complaints, and restoration of trust after documented discrimination.

Heirs’ Property and Succession

Relending and legal-assistance programmes are increasingly important for resolving title and transferring farms across generations.

Climate and Disaster Risk

Repeated drought, flood, wildfire, hurricane, and livestock loss test whether new debt is appropriate after disaster and whether loan underwriting should incorporate long-term adaptation.

Guaranteed Lender Participation

FSA continues lender training, streamlined guarantees, and process improvements to expand the number of institutions serving smaller and riskier farms.

Appropriations and Staffing

Programme effectiveness depends on sufficient loan funds, experienced loan officers, appraisers, servicing staff, and local technical support.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Direct loan
A loan made and serviced by FSA with federal funds.
Guaranteed loan
A private lender loan for which FSA guarantees an authorised portion of loss.
Farm Ownership Loan
Long-term credit for farmland, buildings, improvements, and conservation.
Operating Loan
Credit for annual production, livestock, equipment, family living, and related farm needs.
Microloan
A streamlined direct loan up to $50,000.
Emergency Loan
Direct credit for qualifying production or physical loss after an authorised natural disaster.
Family farm
An operation in which family members make management decisions and provide substantial labour under community and programme standards.
Credit elsewhere
Sufficient financing available without FSA assistance at reasonable rates and terms.
Feasible plan
A projected farm plan showing reasonable ability to pay expenses, family living, and debt.
Supervised credit
Lending combined with planning, training, review, and account servicing.
Beginning farmer
A qualifying operator with no more than ten years of farm operation and material participation.
Targeted underserved applicant
A member of an FSA-identified group historically subjected to racial, ethnic, or gender prejudice for targeted funding purposes.
Down Payment Loan
A reduced-rate direct ownership loan financing up to forty-five percent of a qualifying purchase.
Joint Financing
A Farm Ownership arrangement combining an FSA direct loan with another lender or seller.
Loan guarantee
Federal protection reimbursing an approved lender for an authorised share of loss after proper servicing and liquidation.
Distressed Borrower Set-Aside
A servicing option deferring one annual instalment per eligible direct loan at reduced interest.
Primary loan servicing
Statutory restructuring options for financially distressed or delinquent direct borrowers.
Shared Appreciation Agreement
An agreement requiring a borrower who received real-estate debt write-down to share later property appreciation.
Graduation
Refinancing FSA direct debt when commercial credit becomes available.
Heirs’ property
Inherited land held by multiple relatives as undivided interests without clear title division.

Summary

USDA Farm Loan Programs provide direct federal loans and guarantees of private loans for eligible family farmers and ranchers who cannot obtain sufficient conventional credit on reasonable terms.

The principal programmes are Farm Ownership and Operating Loans. Related tools include Microloans, Down Payment Loans, Joint Financing, Emergency Loans, Youth Loans, Conservation Loans, Land Contract Guarantees, tribal land programmes, and Farm Storage Facility Loans.

For fiscal year 2026, the maximum standard guaranteed loan is $2,343,000. Direct Farm Ownership Loans are capped at $600,000, Direct Operating Loans at $400,000, Microloans at $50,000, Emergency Loans at $500,000, and Youth Loans at $10,000.

The latest FSA rate page available on August 3, 2026, listed rates effective July 1: 5.125 percent for Direct Operating, 6.000 percent for Direct Farm Ownership, 4.000 percent for Joint Financing, 2.000 percent for Down Payment Loans, and 3.750 percent for Emergency Loans.

Applicants generally must operate a family farm, possess legal capacity and managerial ability, show a feasible plan, provide security, satisfy federal-debt and eligibility rules, and demonstrate that adequate credit is unavailable without FSA assistance.

Beginning and underserved applicants receive targeted funds and specialised ownership tools, but they remain subject to repayment and feasibility standards.

Direct lending is supervised credit. FSA assists with planning, training, annual analysis, servicing, and eventual graduation to commercial credit.

When borrowers experience distress, permanent options can include Distressed Borrower Set-Aside, Disaster Set-Aside, rescheduling, reamortisation, deferral, interest reduction, write-down, conservation contracts, and debt settlement.

USDA’s lending history includes documented discrimination against Black, Native American, Hispanic, women, and other farmers, producing litigation, settlements, financial assistance, and continuing demands for institutional reform.

The programmes’ principal strength is their ability to supply patient capital where ordinary markets fail. Their principal challenge is ensuring that public credit builds viable and equitable farm ownership rather than adding unsustainable debt or reproducing historic discrimination.
