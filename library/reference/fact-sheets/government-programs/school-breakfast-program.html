School Breakfast Program

The federal school-meal program that reimburses participating schools and residential child care institutions for serving nutritionally regulated breakfasts at free, reduced-price, paid, and severe-need reimbursement levels.

Overview

The School Breakfast Program is a federally assisted meal program operating in public schools, nonprofit private schools, and qualifying residential child care institutions. Commonly abbreviated SBP, it provides federal cash reimbursement and related food assistance for breakfasts that satisfy federal nutrition, eligibility, accountability, food-safety, procurement, and civil-rights requirements.

Congress established the SBP as a pilot program through the Child Nutrition Act of 1966. The program initially emphasized schools in poor economic areas, schools serving children who travelled long distances, and schools with special need for improved nutrition among children from low-income and working families. Congress removed the temporary authorization and made the program permanent in 1975.

The SBP is administered through a federal–state–local partnership. The United States Department of Agriculture establishes national policy, meal standards, reimbursement rates, income guidelines, program-integrity requirements, and food-assistance rules. State agencies approve and monitor local operators. School food authorities purchase food, prepare or contract for meals, determine student eligibility, claim reimbursement, maintain records, manage nonprofit food-service accounts, and serve breakfasts.

Participation by schools is voluntary under federal law unless a state or local law requires breakfast service. Every enrolled child in a participating school may purchase or receive a reimbursable breakfast. Children from qualifying households receive meals free or at a reduced price, while other students may purchase paid breakfasts at locally established prices.

Children from households with incomes at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty guidelines are generally eligible for free meals. Children from households above 130 percent but at or below 185 percent are generally eligible for reduced-price meals. The maximum student charge for a reduced-price breakfast is thirty cents.

Eligibility can be established through household application, direct certification, categorical eligibility, or a school-wide alternative. Children can be directly certified through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other qualifying assistance systems. Foster children and qualifying homeless, migrant, runaway, and Head Start participants can receive free meals without an ordinary income application.

The Community Eligibility Provision allows eligible schools, groups of schools, and local educational agencies to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every enrolled student. Rather than collecting household meal applications, the school uses the share of students directly identified through public-benefit and categorical systems to calculate federal reimbursement.

A reimbursable breakfast generally includes fruit, a combined grains or meats and meat-alternates component, and fluid milk. Schools must offer minimum daily and weekly quantities for grade groups and comply with calorie, saturated-fat, sodium, trans-fat, added-sugar, whole-grain, and milk requirements.

Breakfast can be served before the school day in a cafeteria or through alternative models such as breakfast in the classroom, grab-and-go breakfast, second-chance breakfast, mobile carts, bus or arrival service, and breakfast after the bell. These models seek to reduce transportation, schedule, stigma, and convenience barriers that can suppress participation.

Schools serving high percentages of low-income children can receive severe-need reimbursement. A school generally qualifies when at least 40 percent of lunches served during the relevant second preceding school year were served free or at a reduced price. Severe-need payments are higher for free and reduced-price breakfasts but do not increase the paid-breakfast rate.

For school year 2026–2027, ordinary contiguous-state reimbursement is $2.54 for a free breakfast, $2.24 for a reduced-price breakfast, and $0.42 for a paid breakfast. Severe-need schools receive $3.05 for a free breakfast and $2.75 for a reduced-price breakfast. Alaska and the jurisdictions grouped with Guam, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and the United States Virgin Islands receive higher rates.

Current nutrition rules are in transition. Product-based added-sugar limits for breakfast cereals, yogurt, and flavored milk took effect July 1, 2025. A weekly added-sugars limit and a further breakfast sodium reduction are scheduled for July 1, 2027. Federal rules effective in June 2026 restored whole and reduced-fat milk as permissible choices for participants age two and older.

The SBP is one of the nation’s largest child-nutrition programs. In fiscal year 2025, it served approximately 2.6 billion breakfasts and federal spending reached approximately $6.1 billion. Most meals are served free or at a reduced price, reflecting the program’s concentration in schools and communities with substantial economic need.

The program can reduce morning hunger, support dietary quality, improve readiness to learn, stabilize household food budgets, and create a reliable institutional market for food. It also faces persistent challenges involving early arrival times, transportation, stigma, scheduling, labor, kitchen infrastructure, reimbursement adequacy, student participation, food waste, certification, procurement, and unequal access to universal free meals.

Definitions

School Breakfast Program
The federal program reimbursing participating schools and residential child care institutions for qualifying breakfasts served to enrolled children.
School food authority
The governing body responsible for operating the school meal program in one or more schools. The abbreviation SFA is commonly used.
Local educational agency
A public board of education or other public authority legally responsible for administrative control of public elementary or secondary schools. The abbreviation LEA is commonly used.
State agency
The state education, agriculture, health, or other designated agency administering the SBP within a state or territory.
Residential child care institution
A qualifying public or nonprofit private residential institution serving children, including certain group homes, residential schools, shelters, detention facilities, and similar institutions. The abbreviation RCCI is commonly used.
Reimbursable breakfast
A breakfast offered and selected in accordance with the applicable federal meal pattern, service, recordkeeping, and counting requirements.
Free breakfast
A reimbursable breakfast served without charge to a child certified for free meal benefits or enrolled in a school-wide free-meal option.
Reduced-price breakfast
A reimbursable breakfast served to an eligible child at a student charge not exceeding thirty cents.
Paid breakfast
A reimbursable breakfast served to a child not claimed in the free or reduced-price category. The meal still receives a smaller federal reimbursement.
Severe-need school
A school meeting statutory criteria for enhanced free and reduced-price breakfast reimbursement, generally because at least 40 percent of lunches in the relevant second preceding school year were served free or at a reduced price.
Severe-need reimbursement
The higher federal payment for free and reduced-price breakfasts served in a qualifying severe-need school.
Income Eligibility Guidelines
Annual USDA household-income standards used to determine eligibility for free and reduced-price school meals. The abbreviation IEGs is commonly used.
Categorical eligibility
Eligibility established through participation in another qualifying public-assistance program or through a protected child category rather than an ordinary household-income calculation.
Direct certification
Certification of a child for free or reduced-price meals by matching school enrollment data with records from SNAP, TANF, FDPIR, Medicaid demonstrations, foster care, or another authorized source.
Household application
A paper or electronic form through which a household reports income, household members, assistance-program information, and required certification for meal eligibility.
Verification
The annual process through which an SFA reviews a sample of approved household applications and requests documentation of income or categorical eligibility.
Overt identification
Any practice revealing or signaling a child’s free or reduced-price status to students, staff without a need to know, or the public. Federal law prohibits overt identification.
Community Eligibility Provision
A four-year meal-service option allowing eligible high-poverty schools, groups, or LEAs to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to all enrolled students and claim reimbursement through an identified-student formula. The abbreviation CEP is commonly used.
Identified student percentage
The percentage of enrolled students directly certified or otherwise identified without a household application for purposes of the Community Eligibility Provision. The abbreviation ISP is commonly used.
Provision 2
An alternative allowing a school to establish free, reduced-price, and paid claiming percentages during a base year and serve meals without charge to all students in later years of a four-year cycle.
Provision 3
An alternative providing a fixed federal payment based on reimbursement and enrollment data from a base year, adjusted for enrollment and inflation, while meals are served without charge.
Meal component
A required food category within the federal breakfast pattern: fruit, grains or meats and meat alternates, and fluid milk.
Food item
A specific food offered within a meal component, such as an orange, oatmeal, eggs, yogurt, or milk.
Offer versus Serve
A meal-service flexibility permitting students to decline specified food items while still selecting a reimbursable breakfast. The abbreviation OVS is commonly used.
Breakfast after the bell
A service model in which breakfast is offered after the official school day begins rather than only before first period.
Breakfast in the classroom
A service model in which students eat breakfast in their classroom, commonly during attendance, announcements, or opening instructional routines.
Grab-and-go breakfast
A service model using portable packaged foods distributed from carts, kiosks, entrances, cafeterias, or other locations for consumption in an approved area.
Second-chance breakfast
A breakfast service offered after first period or during a morning break for students who did not eat before school.
Meal pattern
The federal requirements concerning components, quantities, grade groups, calories, nutrients, menu planning, and service.
Combined grains and meats or meat-alternates component
The breakfast component through which schools may offer grains, meats or meat alternates, or a combination of both to satisfy daily and weekly ounce-equivalent requirements.
USDA Foods
Domestically produced foods purchased by USDA and made available to school food authorities through the broader school-meal system.
Nonprofit school food-service account
The restricted account containing revenue and expenditures of the school meal program. Funds must be used for operation or improvement of the nonprofit food service.
Buy American requirement
The requirement that school food authorities purchase domestic agricultural commodities and food products to the maximum extent practicable, subject to documented exceptions.
Administrative review
A state-agency examination of SFA compliance involving meal access, meal patterns, counting and claiming, resource management, civil rights, wellness policy, procurement, food safety, and related requirements.
Meal-counting system
The point-of-service or other approved process identifying each reimbursable breakfast and its correct free, reduced-price, paid, or school-wide claiming category.
Universal school breakfast
Breakfast provided without charge to every student through CEP, Provision 2 or 3, or state and local funding.

Historical Origins

Child Hunger and Education

The program emerged from growing recognition that children arriving at school without breakfast could have difficulty concentrating, participating, and learning. The problem was especially visible in communities experiencing poverty, racial inequality, rural isolation, and limited access to school food service.

Rural Travel and Early Meals

Congress explicitly considered children who ate very early, completed farm or household work, travelled long distances, and arrived at school hungry again. The original statute gave priority to schools drawing attendance from poor areas and schools where children travelled long distances.

Working Families

The original law also referenced children of working mothers, reflecting changing labor patterns and concern about whether children received adequate morning nutrition when parents left home early.

War on Poverty

The School Breakfast Program formed part of the Great Society and War on Poverty expansion of federal food assistance. It followed the permanent National School Lunch Program and accompanied broader efforts involving food stamps, community action, Head Start, health centers, and civil-rights enforcement.

Hunger Investigations

Congressional hearings, medical investigations, civil-rights advocacy, and national media reporting during the late 1960s documented severe hunger in poor urban and rural communities. These findings increased pressure to expand and permanently authorize federal child-nutrition programs.

Permanent National Policy

The 1975 amendments declared it national nutrition and health policy that breakfast should be available in schools where needed to provide adequate nutrition. The law directed USDA and state agencies to promote program availability.

Program Purpose and Scale

Morning Nutrition

The SBP provides a meal early in the school day. For children who lack reliable breakfast at home, the program can prevent a long period without food before lunch.

Educational Readiness

Reliable breakfast access can reduce hunger-related fatigue, distraction, irritability, tardiness, and difficulty participating in classroom instruction.

Household Economic Support

Free and reduced-price breakfast lowers household food costs and reduces the need for families to prepare food during early work and school routines.

Public Health

Breakfast standards can increase consumption of fruit, whole grains, dairy, protein foods, fiber, vitamins, and minerals while limiting sodium, saturated fat, trans fat, and added sugars.

Agricultural Policy

School breakfast purchasing and USDA Foods assistance create demand for domestic agricultural commodities and food products.

Fiscal Year 2025

The program served approximately 2.6 billion breakfasts in fiscal year 2025. Federal spending totaled approximately $6.1 billion.

Low-Income Concentration

Most breakfasts are served free or at a reduced price. In fiscal year 2024, approximately 80 percent of breakfasts were in those two categories.

Long-Term Growth

The program grew from a limited pilot into a national system serving tens of millions of children across the school year. Expansion was driven by federal reimbursement, severe-need payments, state mandates, direct certification, CEP, and alternative breakfast service.

Administration and Operating Structure

Federal Administration

The Food and Nutrition Administration:

  • Implements federal statutes and appropriations
  • Issues regulations, policy memoranda, forms, and technical assistance
  • Publishes annual income guidelines and reimbursement rates
  • Allocates federal funds to states
  • Establishes meal-pattern and nutrition standards
  • Monitors state agencies and regional operations
  • Collects participation and expenditure data
  • Administers civil-rights and program-integrity requirements
  • Provides grants for equipment, expansion, training, technology, and farm-to-school activity

State Agencies

State agencies approve school food authorities, distribute federal funds, provide training, review applications and agreements, conduct administrative reviews, resolve findings, oversee procurement and resource management, monitor civil rights, and report meal counts to USDA.

School Food Authorities

SFAs operate the local program. Responsibilities include:

  • Preparing annual agreements and policies
  • Determining eligibility or implementing a school-wide option
  • Purchasing food and services under federal procurement rules
  • Planning menus and maintaining production records
  • Training food-service and other responsible staff
  • Counting reimbursable meals at the point of service
  • Submitting accurate reimbursement claims
  • Managing the nonprofit school food-service account
  • Maintaining food safety and sanitation
  • Protecting confidential eligibility information
  • Providing reasonable modifications for children with disabilities
  • Implementing civil-rights and nondiscrimination requirements

Schools

Individual schools coordinate breakfast schedules, student arrival, cafeteria or classroom service, supervision, transportation, point-of-sale procedures, cleanup, accommodations, and communication with households.

Private Contractors

An SFA may contract with a food-service management company, vendor, cooperative, or other provider. The SFA remains responsible for federal compliance and cannot transfer its legal accountability to the contractor.

Regional Offices

USDA regional offices supervise state agencies, provide technical assistance, review state operations, and coordinate policy implementation across multistate regions.

Eligible Schools and Institutions

Public Schools

Public elementary and secondary schools can participate through an approved state agreement. Public charter schools can participate when they satisfy state and federal requirements.

Nonprofit Private Schools

Nonprofit private schools can participate when they possess qualifying nonprofit status, operate an eligible educational program, and comply with federal rules.

Residential Child Care Institutions

Eligible RCCIs can include residential schools, group homes, juvenile facilities, shelters, orphanages, and other qualifying institutions serving children.

Voluntary Federal Participation

Federal law does not require every school to operate the SBP. A school or SFA elects participation unless state law imposes a breakfast-service mandate.

State Mandates

Many states require schools to offer breakfast when a specified share of students qualifies for free or reduced-price meals. Some require breakfast after the bell or universal free breakfast in high-poverty schools.

Nonprofit Food Service

Participating institutions must operate a nonprofit school food service. Revenue can cover allowable costs and improve the program but cannot be distributed as profit to the school or private parties.

Program Agreement

The SFA enters into a written agreement with the state agency establishing responsibilities for meals, eligibility, claims, records, civil rights, procurement, audits, reviews, and corrective action.

Student Eligibility

Free Meals by Household Income

A child is generally eligible for free breakfast when household income is at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Reduced-Price Meals by Household Income

A child is generally eligible for reduced-price breakfast when household income exceeds 130 percent but does not exceed 185 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Paid Meals

Children not certified for free or reduced-price meals may purchase breakfast at a price established by the SFA. The federal government still provides a smaller reimbursement for the qualifying paid meal.

Gross Income

Household income is generally evaluated before deductions. Wages, self-employment, pensions, unemployment, Social Security, child support, rental income, and other cash income can count, subject to federal exclusions and household rules.

Duration

Eligibility generally remains effective for the school year and a carryover period at the beginning of the next school year, unless a change improves the household’s benefit or another authorized action applies.

No Adverse Change Reporting

Households generally are not required to report increases in income or loss of categorical eligibility during the certification period.

Direct and Categorical Certification

Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program

Children identified as members of households receiving SNAP benefits are directly certified for free school breakfast and lunch.

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families

Children in qualifying TANF households can be directly certified for free meals under federal and state data systems.

Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations

Children in households receiving FDPIR benefits are categorically eligible for free meals.

Medicaid Demonstrations

States approved for direct-certification demonstrations can use Medicaid data to identify children eligible for free or reduced-price meals under specified procedures.

Foster Children

A foster child formally placed by a state or court is categorically eligible for free meals and can be certified independently of other household members.

Homeless, Migrant, and Runaway Children

Children identified through the McKinney–Vento, migrant education, or runaway youth systems can receive free meals without an ordinary income application.

Head Start

Children enrolled in federally funded Head Start or comparable state-funded prekindergarten programs using equivalent eligibility standards can qualify categorically.

Extended Eligibility

When one child is directly certified through SNAP, TANF, or FDPIR, other children in the same household can generally receive the same free-meal status after the household connection is established.

Program Benefits

Direct certification reduces household paperwork, lowers certification error, increases access, supports CEP eligibility, and links breakfast with broader nutrition and income-assistance systems.

Data-Matching Challenges

Name differences, addresses, custody arrangements, missing records, delayed updates, and incomplete enrollment data can leave eligible children unmatched.

Community Eligibility Provision

Purpose

CEP permits eligible schools to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every student, eliminate ordinary meal applications, reduce stigma, simplify meal counting, and increase participation.

Eligibility Threshold

A school, group of schools, or LEA generally must have an identified student percentage of at least 25 percent as of the applicable eligibility date.

Identified Students

Identified students generally include children directly certified through qualifying assistance programs and children categorically eligible through foster, homeless, migrant, runaway, or Head Start status. Students approved through ordinary income applications do not count toward the ISP.

Reimbursement Formula

The ISP is multiplied by the federal multiplier, generally 1.6, to calculate the percentage of breakfasts and lunches reimbursed at the free rate. Remaining meals receive the paid rate.

Example

A school with a 50-percent ISP claims 80 percent of breakfasts at the free rate and 20 percent at the paid rate. A school with an ISP of 62.5 percent or higher generally reaches 100-percent free-rate claiming under the 1.6 multiplier.

Four-Year Cycle

CEP operates through a four-year cycle. The school can use the ISP from the election year or an updated higher ISP from a later year within the cycle. A grace year may be available under specified conditions.

Severe-Need Interaction

CEP changes the reimbursement claiming method but does not eliminate severe-need status. A qualifying school can receive severe-need breakfast rates for the share of meals claimed at the free rate.

Local Funding Gap

CEP does not guarantee that federal reimbursement covers the full cost of serving meals without charge. The LEA must cover any difference with nonfederal funds unless state or local universal-meal funding applies.

Advantages

  • No student meal charges
  • No ordinary household meal applications
  • Reduced stigma and overt identification
  • Higher breakfast participation
  • Simplified point-of-service counting
  • Reduced unpaid meal debt
  • Stronger compatibility with breakfast in the classroom and grab-and-go service

Limitations

Schools near the 25-percent threshold can face a substantial nonfederal funding gap. Declines in SNAP or other directly certified enrollment can reduce the ISP even when community poverty remains high.

Breakfast Service Models

Traditional Cafeteria Breakfast

Students eat in the cafeteria before the official school day begins. This model is familiar and operationally concentrated but can exclude students whose buses arrive late, whose families cannot provide early transportation, or who avoid the cafeteria because of stigma.

Breakfast in the Classroom

Meals are delivered to or collected by classrooms and eaten during attendance, announcements, or opening activities. The model can produce high participation but requires teacher cooperation, cleanup, waste control, allergy planning, and instructional integration.

Grab-and-Go

Students select portable meals from carts, kiosks, entrances, hallways, cafeterias, or other approved locations. The model can reduce lines and serve students moving toward class.

Second-Chance Breakfast

Breakfast is served after first period or during a morning break. This model is especially useful in middle and high schools where students arrive close to the bell or are not hungry early.

Breakfast After the Bell

This umbrella term includes classroom, grab-and-go, and second-chance service occurring after instructional time begins. Several states require or encourage this model in high-poverty schools.

Mobile Breakfast

Meals are distributed through carts or vehicles across a campus. Mobile service can reach separate buildings, alternative programs, or large high-school facilities.

Breakfast on the Bus or at Arrival

Some schools provide packaged breakfast during transportation or immediately after students exit buses. Food safety, supervision, waste, and vehicle policy require careful planning.

Hybrid Models

A school may offer traditional breakfast before school and a limited second-chance option later. Hybrid systems serve students with different schedules and preferences.

Operational Principle

Alternative service changes where and when breakfast is available, not the nutrition, counting, eligibility, food-safety, or civil-rights requirements for a reimbursable meal.

Barriers to Breakfast Participation

Transportation

School buses can arrive too close to the opening bell for students to eat in the cafeteria.

Parent and Student Schedules

Families may lack time or transportation to bring children early. Older students may arrive independently immediately before class.

Stigma

Students can perceive before-school breakfast as a program only for poor children, particularly where participation is low or the cafeteria population visibly differs from the broader school.

Morning Appetite

Some students are not hungry immediately after waking but will eat later in the morning.

Short Service Period

Long lines, security procedures, late buses, and limited staffing can leave insufficient time to select and eat breakfast.

Open Campus and High-School Culture

Older students may purchase food elsewhere, skip breakfast, socialize, attend activities, or avoid institutional food service.

Meal Quality and Preference

Participation depends on taste, appearance, temperature, cultural relevance, variety, product quality, and student input.

School Scheduling

Early classes, zero periods, athletics, tutoring, testing, and staggered starts can complicate service.

Information

Families and students may not know that breakfast is available, that paid meals receive subsidy, or that universal breakfast is open to all students.

Meal Service and Counting

Breakfast Period

Breakfast must be served at or near the beginning of the school day, subject to approved alternative models and state-agency requirements.

Point of Service

The school counts a meal as reimbursable only after determining that the student selected the required components or items. Attendance counts, production totals, or pre-service estimates cannot substitute for an approved counting system.

Offer versus Serve

At breakfast, schools offering four food items from the required components can permit a student to decline one item. The student must select at least three items, including at least one-half cup of fruit, vegetables, or a combination.

Food Items and Components

A school can offer more than the minimum number of items. Staff must understand how each item credits toward fruit, combined grains and meat alternates, and milk.

Second Meals

Federal reimbursement is ordinarily available for only one breakfast per child per meal service. Second breakfasts can be sold but generally are not reimbursable except under limited emergency procedures.

Meals to Adults

Meals served to teachers, staff, visitors, or other adults cannot be claimed as reimbursable student breakfasts. Their price must cover applicable costs.

Field Trips

Reimbursable breakfasts can be served during qualifying school field trips when the SFA maintains required meal-pattern, counting, food-safety, and recordkeeping controls.

Classroom Counting

Breakfast in the classroom requires a reliable method for identifying reimbursable selections and recording one meal per child without exposing benefit status.

Breakfast Meal Pattern

Required Components

A complete breakfast offers:

  • Fruit
  • Grains, meats or meat alternates, or a combination
  • Fluid milk

Fruit

Schools must offer one cup daily. Fresh, frozen, canned, and dried fruit can credit. Pasteurized full-strength juice can satisfy no more than half of the weekly fruit offering.

Vegetable Substitution

Vegetables can replace some or all of the breakfast fruit requirement under current rules. Schools can use the substitution to offer savory breakfast menus and increase vegetable variety.

Combined Grains and Meat Alternates

Schools may offer grains, meats or meat alternates, or any combination to satisfy the daily and weekly ounce equivalents. Eggs, cheese, yogurt, beans, tofu, nuts, seeds, meat, poultry, fish, and grain products can contribute when they meet crediting requirements.

Whole Grains

At least 80 percent of weekly grains offered must be whole grain-rich. Remaining grain items must be enriched or otherwise meet federal grain criteria.

Fluid Milk

Schools generally offer at least two milk choices, including an unflavored option, in quantities and varieties permitted by federal law.

Nutrition Standards

Calories

Weekly menus must fall within age-appropriate average daily calorie ranges. Individual breakfasts may vary when the weekly average complies.

Saturated Fat

Saturated fat is generally limited to less than 10 percent of total calories across the school week. Under current federal treatment, saturated fat from milk used to meet the required milk component is excluded from the weekly specification.

Trans Fat

Foods and ingredients generally must contain zero grams of trans fat per serving as shown on nutrition labels or manufacturer documentation, excluding naturally occurring trans fat.

Sodium

Breakfasts must satisfy grade-specific weekly sodium limits. Current limits remain in place through school year 2026–2027. A further reduction is scheduled for July 1, 2027 under the 2024 final rule, subject to subsequent law.

Breakfast Cereal Added Sugars

Breakfast cereal served in school meals may contain no more than six grams of added sugars per dry ounce.

Yogurt Added Sugars

Yogurt may contain no more than twelve grams of added sugars per six ounces, equivalent to two grams per ounce.

Flavored Milk Added Sugars

Flavored milk served as a meal component may contain no more than ten grams of added sugars per eight fluid ounces.

Weekly Added Sugars

Beginning July 1, 2027, breakfast menus are scheduled to limit added sugars to less than 10 percent of weekly calories.

Dietary Guidelines

Federal law requires school breakfast standards to be consistent with the goals of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. USDA updates enforceable requirements through legislation and notice-and-comment rulemaking.

Menu Flexibility

Schools determine recipes, preparation methods, local foods, cultural menus, vegetarian options, and service models within federal requirements.

Fluid Milk Policy

Required Offering

Participating schools generally must offer fluid milk as a breakfast component. Students are not required to select milk when Offer versus Serve applies and the meal otherwise qualifies.

2026 Milk Options

Federal law and regulations effective in 2026 permit conventional or organic whole, reduced-fat, low-fat, and fat-free milk for participants age two and older. Lactose-free and lactose-reduced varieties can also qualify.

Flavored Milk

Flavored milk is permitted subject to the federal added-sugar limit, the requirement to offer an unflavored choice, and any stricter state or local policy.

Disability Modification

A school must provide an appropriate substitute or modification when a child’s disability restricts ordinary milk consumption and the modification is necessary for equal program access.

Non-Disability Request

Schools may offer qualifying nondairy substitutes for non-disability requests when allowed by federal law and local policy. The substitute must satisfy USDA nutrient standards, and the school generally bears any additional cost.

Policy Debate

Milk policy has changed repeatedly in response to dietary guidance, dairy policy, student acceptance, food waste, saturated fat, added sugars, and congressional priorities.

Federal Reimbursement

Per-Meal Payments

USDA reimburses states for each properly claimed free, reduced-price, or paid breakfast. States reimburse school food authorities under approved claim systems.

Alaska Rates

For school year 2026–2027, Alaska receives $4.09 for a non-severe-need free breakfast, $3.79 for a non-severe-need reduced-price breakfast, and $0.64 for a paid breakfast. Severe-need free and reduced-price rates are $4.90 and $4.60.

These jurisdictions receive $3.29 for a non-severe-need free breakfast, $2.99 for a non-severe-need reduced-price breakfast, and $0.52 for a paid breakfast. Severe-need free and reduced-price rates are $3.94 and $3.64.

Annual Inflation Adjustment

Breakfast rates are adjusted each July using the Food Away From Home component of the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers.

Reduced-Price Difference

The reduced-price reimbursement is thirty cents below the corresponding free-breakfast rate because federal law limits the student charge to thirty cents.

Claims

SFAs submit monthly claims based on meal counts. Late claims, upward revisions, downward adjustments, and exceptions are governed by federal deadlines.

Entitlement Status

The SBP is an entitlement to states and participating institutions for breakfasts properly served and claimed. Federal reimbursement does not depend on a fixed annual number of children.

Severe-Need Assistance

Purpose

Severe-need assistance recognizes that schools serving large numbers of low-income children can face higher costs and limited revenue from student payments.

Eligibility Test

A school generally qualifies when 40 percent or more of lunches served in the relevant second preceding school year were served free or at a reduced price.

New or Non-Lunch Schools

When a school did not serve lunch during the relevant year, USDA or the state agency can determine severe need through alternative documentation authorized by federal law.

School-Level Determination

Severe need is generally determined for an individual school rather than automatically for every school in an SFA.

Payment Effect

The higher rate applies to free and reduced-price breakfasts. Paid breakfasts receive the ordinary paid rate.

CEP Schools

A CEP school can receive severe-need reimbursement for meals claimed at the free rate when the school satisfies the severe-need criteria.

Policy Significance

Severe-need reimbursement makes breakfast more financially viable in high-poverty schools and helps explain why the SBP serves a larger proportion of low-income children than the lunch program.

Food Assistance and Procurement

USDA Foods

The SBP does not receive a separate large commodity entitlement calculated solely from breakfast counts. Schools commonly use USDA Foods made available through their participation in the National School Lunch Program and other federal food-distribution authorities.

Direct Delivery

Schools can use fruit, grains, dairy products, meat, poultry, beans, and other USDA Foods within breakfast menus when the products credit under the meal pattern.

Full and Open Competition

SFAs must conduct procurement in a manner providing full and open competition and must follow federal, state, and local requirements.

Methods

Procurement can involve micro-purchases, small purchases, sealed bids, competitive proposals, noncompetitive procurement under authorized conditions, cooperatives, and food-service management contracts.

Buy American

SFAs must purchase domestic agricultural commodities and food products to the maximum extent practicable.

Exceptions

An SFA can use a documented exception when a domestic product is unavailable in sufficient quantity or quality or when the cost difference satisfies the applicable accommodation process.

Local Foods

Schools can use geographic preference for qualifying unprocessed locally grown or raised agricultural products and can participate in farm-to-school purchasing, school gardens, and agriculture education.

Breakfast-Specific Procurement

Breakfast menus often depend on portable products, cereal, yogurt, milk, fruit, sandwiches, baked goods, eggs, and reheatable items. Alternative service models can increase packaging, cold-storage, transport, and waste-management needs.

Financial Management

Nonprofit Account

Federal reimbursements, student payments, catering revenue, a la carte sales, and other school food-service income are deposited into the nonprofit account.

Allowable Costs

Funds can pay for food, labor, supplies, equipment, utilities, contracts, training, technology, transportation, and other costs necessary and reasonable for program operation.

Unallowable Costs

Costs unrelated to school food service, unsupported charges, excessive compensation, improper transfers, entertainment, and other prohibited expenditures cannot be paid from the account.

Breakfast Revenue

Because most breakfasts are free or reduced-price, breakfast operations depend heavily on federal reimbursement and state or local subsidies rather than student payments.

Participation and Fixed Cost

Higher participation can spread labor, equipment, and administrative costs across more meals. Low participation can make a traditional before-school program financially difficult even when the school qualifies for severe-need rates.

Alternative-Service Costs

Breakfast in the classroom and grab-and-go can require carts, insulated containers, point-of-sale technology, custodial time, trash collection, and additional packaging. Increased reimbursement from higher participation can offset some costs.

Net Cash Resources

State agencies monitor account balances. Excess cash can require an approved spending plan involving equipment, meal quality, staffing, technology, or other program improvements.

Indirect Costs

School districts can charge allowable direct and indirect costs under federal cost principles and approved methodologies. Unclear or excessive charges can weaken food-service finances.

Program Integrity and Accountability

Eligibility Accuracy

Schools must correctly approve free and reduced-price applications, maintain direct-certification records, extend benefits to qualifying household members, and protect confidential information.

Verification

Each year, SFAs review a federally prescribed sample of approved applications. Households may provide pay records, assistance-program documentation, or other evidence.

Meal Counting

Claims must be based on actual reimbursable breakfasts served to eligible enrolled children. Attendance counts, production totals, or classroom rosters alone cannot substitute for an approved service and counting system.

Alternative-Service Controls

Classroom and grab-and-go models require controls preventing duplicate meals, counting incomplete meals, serving ineligible adults, or claiming food that was distributed but not selected by an enrolled child.

Administrative Reviews

State agencies review each SFA on a federal cycle and can examine eligibility, meal access, meal patterns, counting, claiming, fiscal management, procurement, civil rights, food safety, wellness policy, and professional standards.

Corrective Action

Findings can require repayment, revised procedures, retraining, fiscal action, follow-up review, claim adjustment, or termination.

Fraud

Intentional false claims, falsified meal counts, procurement collusion, kickbacks, theft, or misuse of program funds can lead to civil, administrative, or criminal enforcement.

Record Retention

SFAs must retain applications, benefit lists, menus, production records, invoices, contracts, claims, reviews, and other documentation for the federal retention period or longer when an audit remains unresolved.

Workforce and Infrastructure

Professional Standards

Federal regulations establish hiring and annual training requirements for school nutrition directors, managers, employees, and staff with program responsibilities.

Early Work Hours

Breakfast requires employees to begin food preparation, receiving, transport, and service before most of the school workforce arrives.

Split Shifts and Limited Hours

Some breakfast employees work short or split shifts, which can complicate recruitment, benefits, and retention.

Kitchen Capacity

Schools can lack ovens, refrigeration, preparation space, storage, electrical capacity, dishwashing, carts, and loading areas required for varied breakfast menus.

Classroom Service

Breakfast in the classroom requires coordination among nutrition staff, teachers, custodians, administrators, nurses, and transportation personnel.

Culinary Skills

Fresh preparation and culturally relevant breakfast require trained staff, recipes, equipment, procurement systems, and time.

Technology

Mobile point-of-sale devices, student information systems, menu software, direct-certification matching, production records, and financial systems support accurate decentralized service.

Food Safety, Allergies, and Disability Access

Food-Safety Program

Participating schools must implement a food-safety program based on Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point principles.

Temperature Control

Breakfast service can involve milk, yogurt, eggs, meat, fruit, and prepared foods distributed away from the cafeteria. Time and temperature controls remain essential.

Classroom Sanitation

Schools must plan delivery, hand hygiene, surface cleaning, trash collection, pest prevention, spills, and storage when meals are eaten outside the cafeteria.

Food Allergies

Schools must prevent allergen exposure through accurate information, staff training, communication, cleaning, preparation procedures, and emergency response.

Disability Modifications

When a child’s disability restricts diet, the SFA must make reasonable modifications necessary for equal access. A medical statement can be requested when needed to identify the required modification.

Not a Medical Program

The SBP provides meals under nutrition and civil-rights rules but does not replace individualized medical care or emergency planning.

Civil Rights and Nondiscrimination

Federal Nondiscrimination

Participating institutions must operate the SBP without unlawful discrimination under the Child Nutrition Act, Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act, Title VI, Section 504, the Age Discrimination Act, Title IX where applicable, disability law, and USDA civil-rights requirements.

Protected Access

Civil-rights compliance includes:

  • Equal access to breakfast service
  • Reasonable modifications for disability
  • Meaningful access for households with limited English proficiency
  • Nondiscriminatory meal counting and discipline
  • Equal treatment across service locations
  • Public notification and complaint procedures
  • Collection and analysis of racial and ethnic participation data
  • Staff civil-rights training

Overt Identification

Students cannot be separated, publicly listed, given visibly different credentials, required to perform work, or otherwise identified because they receive free or reduced-price meals.

Alternative Service and Equality

Breakfast after the bell should not be organized in a manner that isolates low-income students or deprives one group of instructional time, food choices, or dignified service.

Complaints

Students and households can file civil-rights complaints with USDA and other responsible agencies. Schools must display required nondiscrimination information and inform the public of complaint procedures.

African American Communities and Racial Context

Origins During the Civil-Rights Era

The School Breakfast Program was created while Black communities and civil-rights organizations were documenting hunger, segregated schools, unequal public services, and poverty in both the rural South and northern cities.

School Segregation

The federal program entered school systems still engaged in desegregation litigation and resistance. Black schools and recently desegregated schools often lacked adequate kitchens, cafeterias, equipment, transportation, and local funding.

Black Panther Free Breakfast Program

Beginning in 1969, the Black Panther Party organized free breakfast programs for children in several cities. These community programs demonstrated both the scale of child hunger and the capacity of local organizations to serve meals before school. They were separate from the federal SBP but became an important part of the historical relationship among food, education, community self-help, and Black political organizing.

Disproportionate Eligibility

African American children have been disproportionately represented among students eligible for free and reduced-price meals because of racial inequalities in employment, wages, wealth, housing, school finance, and neighborhood opportunity.

Stigma

A before-school cafeteria serving only a small group of low-income children can expose benefit status. Universal breakfast and breakfast after the bell can reduce racialized and class-based stigma.

Transportation and Urban Form

Long bus rides, unstable housing, parental work schedules, school choice, and transportation inequality can make traditional before-school breakfast inaccessible.

Community Eligibility

CEP has particular significance in high-poverty urban and rural schools serving substantial Black populations. It can eliminate applications and student charges while supporting higher participation.

Black Farmers and Food Businesses

Farm-to-school, local purchasing, cooperatives, and technical assistance can connect breakfast purchasing with Black farmers and food businesses. Ordinary federal procurement rules do not guarantee that outcome.

Equity Measure

Program equity should be assessed through access, participation, meal quality, cultural relevance, student dignity, staffing, kitchen investment, educational effect, and community economic benefit rather than eligibility alone.

Universal Free Breakfast and State Policy

Federal School-Wide Options

CEP, Provision 2, and Provision 3 allow qualifying schools to provide breakfast without charge to all students under federal reimbursement systems.

State Universal-Meal Laws

Several states provide state funds so public-school students receive breakfast and lunch without charge regardless of household income. State approaches differ in school coverage, reimbursement, private-school eligibility, and participation requirements.

Breakfast Mandates

Some states require schools with specified poverty levels to operate the SBP. Others require breakfast after the bell or universal breakfast in high-poverty schools.

Advantages

  • Reduced stigma
  • Higher participation
  • Lower application burden
  • No student breakfast debt
  • Simplified service
  • Greater compatibility with classroom and grab-and-go models

Cost

Federal free and paid reimbursement under CEP may not cover the full cost of every meal. State or local funds are necessary when federal reimbursement falls below the cost of universal service.

Administrative Data

Schools using universal meals must maintain other valid measures of economic disadvantage for Title I, state funding, research, and accountability when individual meal applications are not collected.

Policy Debate

Supporters describe universal breakfast as an educational service comparable to transportation or textbooks. Critics emphasize public cost and argue that higher subsidies should remain targeted toward children with lower household incomes.

Benefits and Evidence

Food Security

Free and reduced-price breakfast increases reliable access to food for children from households with limited resources.

Diet Quality

Research generally finds that students eating a school breakfast consume more morning nutrients and can have stronger overall diet quality than comparable children who skip breakfast.

Attendance and Tardiness

Breakfast after the bell can reduce tardiness incentives and allow late-arriving students to eat. Some evaluations associate school breakfast expansion with improved attendance, although results vary by setting and research design.

Academic Readiness

Reducing morning hunger can support attention, memory, behavior, and readiness to learn. Academic effects depend on meal participation, educational conditions, household circumstances, and study method.

Participation Effects

Breakfast in the classroom, grab-and-go, second-chance breakfast, CEP, and state universal meals generally increase participation relative to a traditional before-school cafeteria model.

Household Budget

Breakfast without charge reduces household food expenses and morning preparation demands.

Emergency Capacity

School nutrition departments form part of the national emergency food-delivery infrastructure, as demonstrated during natural disasters and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Limits of Evidence

Program effects vary by student need, menu quality, timing, participation, comparison group, school implementation, and whether the meal replaces breakfast from home or prevents breakfast skipping.

Operational Challenges

Reimbursement Adequacy

Federal reimbursement must cover food, labor, benefits, equipment, transportation, packaging, cleanup, administration, and compliance. Local costs can exceed national rates.

Low Participation

A school can operate breakfast for a small number of students while incurring substantial fixed labor and facility costs.

Early Staffing

Employees must prepare and serve food before the school day, making recruitment and transportation difficult.

Kitchen Infrastructure

Older schools can lack refrigeration, storage, preparation space, ovens, carts, electrical capacity, and classroom-service equipment.

Time

Late buses, short arrival windows, security screening, and early class schedules reduce eating time.

Food Waste

Waste can result from unwanted foods, morning appetite, rushed service, unfamiliar products, packaging, or inaccurate production forecasting.

Classroom Concerns

Teachers and custodians can object to spills, pests, allergies, trash, lost instructional time, and responsibility for counting meals.

High-School Participation

Adolescents often have the lowest participation because of later appetite, peer culture, off-campus food, early classes, and schedule complexity.

Administrative Burden

Eligibility, verification, menu documentation, procurement, civil rights, claims, direct certification, CEP, professional standards, and reviews require substantial capacity.

Major Policy Debates

Universal or Means-Tested Breakfast

Supporters of universal breakfast emphasize access, dignity, participation, administrative simplicity, and educational readiness. Critics emphasize federal cost and targeting of larger subsidies toward children with lower incomes.

Before School or After the Bell

Traditional service preserves classroom time and confines food service to the cafeteria. Breakfast after the bell reaches more students but requires integration with instruction and school operations.

Nutrition Standards and Student Acceptance

National standards promote consistency and public health. Schools argue that product limits and menu rules can increase cost, procurement difficulty, and waste when students reject available foods.

Fruit and Juice

One cup of fruit improves breakfast quality, while schools report cost, storage, waste, and student acceptance challenges. Federal rules limit the share served as juice.

Protein at Breakfast

The combined grains and meat-alternates component allows more eggs, yogurt, cheese, beans, and other protein foods. Cost and equipment affect how extensively schools use that flexibility.

Sodium and Added Sugars

Public-health advocates support stronger limits. Schools and manufacturers emphasize reformulation, availability, taste, student participation, and implementation time.

Whole Milk and Saturated Fat

Supporters of expanded milk options emphasize student consumption, nutrients, preference, and reduced waste. Critics emphasize saturated fat and consistency with dietary guidance.

State Mandates

Mandates can ensure breakfast access but impose staffing, facility, schedule, and funding obligations on schools.

Educational Time

Breakfast in the classroom can support attendance and readiness, while critics argue that food distribution and cleanup can reduce instructional time.

Key Distinctions

School Breakfast Program and National School Lunch Program

The SBP is authorized by the Child Nutrition Act and reimburses breakfast. The NSLP is authorized by the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act and reimburses lunch and qualifying afterschool snacks.

Ordinary and Severe-Need Breakfast

Ordinary reimbursement applies to participating schools generally. Severe-need schools receive higher payments for free and reduced-price breakfasts because they serve high percentages of low-income children.

Breakfast Before School and Breakfast After the Bell

Before-school breakfast occurs prior to the official instructional day. Breakfast after the bell occurs after the school day begins through classroom, grab-and-go, second-chance, or another approved model.

Free Breakfast and Universal Free Breakfast

An individually free breakfast is claimed for a certified student. Universal free breakfast is offered without charge to every student through CEP, Provision 2 or 3, or state and local funding.

Community Eligibility and State Universal Meals

CEP is a federal reimbursement option based on directly identified students. State universal-meal laws use state funds to cover federal reimbursement gaps or provide no-charge meals beyond CEP eligibility.

Reimbursable Breakfast and A La Carte Food

A reimbursable breakfast contains the required federal food items. A la carte items are sold separately and do not receive SBP meal reimbursement.

Offer versus Serve and Full Meal Selection

Offer versus Serve permits a student to decline one of at least four offered food items. Without OVS, the student must receive the full required meal.

Direct Certification and Household Application

Direct certification uses public records to establish eligibility. A household application relies on household-reported income or qualifying assistance-program information.

School Breakfast and Summer Meals

The SBP serves breakfast during school operations and through the Seamless Summer Option. The Summer Food Service Program is a separate program for free meals in eligible communities when school is out.

School Breakfast and SNAP

The SBP provides prepared meals through schools. SNAP provides household grocery benefits. SNAP participation can directly certify children for free school breakfast.

Geographic Scope

The SBP operates in every state, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, and other participating jurisdictions under applicable federal arrangements.

Administration varies by jurisdiction. Most states use an education agency, while some use agriculture or other departments. Tribal schools can participate through state or federal administrative structures depending on governance.

Federal reimbursement rates are higher in Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands because of higher food-service costs. Income guidelines are also adjusted for Alaska and Hawaii.

Rural schools face transportation, workforce, purchasing, enrollment, and distance challenges. The program’s original design specifically recognized children travelling long distances to school.

Urban districts manage scale, centralized kitchens, high student need, complex transportation, large labor systems, and breakfast distribution across multiple school buildings.

State breakfast mandates and universal-meal policies create major geographic differences. A child can receive breakfast without charge in one state while a similarly situated child in another state depends on individual eligibility or local CEP participation.

Legal Context

Child Nutrition Act of 1966

Section 4 created the School Breakfast Program and governs federal payments, severe-need assistance, nutrition requirements, state administration, priorities, and national breakfast policy.

National School Lunch Act and Child Nutrition Act Amendments of 1975

Public Law 94-105 removed the breakfast program’s temporary authorization and declared a national policy that breakfast should be available where needed.

Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act

Many eligibility, direct-certification, Community Eligibility, procurement, food-safety, civil-rights, and program-integrity requirements apply jointly to lunch and breakfast through cross-references and implementing regulations.

Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010

Public Law 111-296 strengthened meal standards, created CEP, expanded direct certification, established professional standards, and revised program integrity.

Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025

Public Law 119-69 expanded permissible milk varieties and directed USDA to revise child-nutrition regulations.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits race, color, and national-origin discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination and requires reasonable modifications and program accessibility.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II applies to public schools and public entities. Other provisions can apply to covered private institutions and services.

McKinney–Vento Homeless Assistance Act

Children identified as homeless under federal education law receive categorical access to free school meals.

Summary

The School Breakfast Program is the permanent federal system supporting nutritionally regulated breakfasts in public schools, nonprofit private schools, and residential child care institutions. Congress created it as a pilot in 1966 and made it permanent in 1975.

The program operates through a federal–state–local partnership. USDA establishes national rules and provides reimbursement. State agencies approve and monitor local operators. School food authorities purchase food, plan menus, certify students, serve breakfasts, count meals, claim reimbursement, manage finances, and protect civil rights.

Children generally qualify for free meals at or below 130 percent of the poverty guidelines and reduced-price meals at or below 185 percent. The maximum reduced-price breakfast charge is thirty cents. Direct certification, categorical eligibility, household applications, and school-wide alternatives establish access.

Schools with high concentrations of low-income children can receive severe-need reimbursement. For school year 2026–2027, ordinary contiguous-state rates are $2.54 for free breakfast, $2.24 for reduced-price breakfast, and $0.42 for paid breakfast. Severe-need free and reduced-price rates are $3.05 and $2.75.

The Community Eligibility Provision allows eligible schools to serve breakfast and lunch without charge to every student. Breakfast in the classroom, grab-and-go, second-chance breakfast, and other after-the-bell models reduce schedule, transportation, convenience, and stigma barriers.

Reimbursable breakfasts offer fruit, grains or meats and meat alternates, and fluid milk. Weekly menus must satisfy grade-based quantities, calories, whole-grain rules, and nutrient limits. Current standards include product-based added-sugar limits, sodium controls, and expanded milk choices effective in 2026.

In fiscal year 2025, the program served approximately 2.6 billion breakfasts at a federal cost of approximately $6.1 billion. Most meals were served free or at a reduced price.

The program can reduce morning hunger, improve dietary quality, support readiness to learn, and ease household food costs. Its effects depend on whether students can access and will eat the meal, making service time, location, quality, culture, staffing, and school climate central to program success.

The program’s history is also tied to the civil-rights era, school desegregation, the War on Poverty, and community responses to child hunger, including the Black Panther Party’s separate free breakfast programs. Modern equity requires attention to participation, dignity, kitchen investment, culturally relevant food, universal access, and community economic benefit.

The continuing effectiveness of the School Breakfast Program depends on adequate reimbursement, accurate eligibility, severe-need support, strong civil-rights enforcement, trained workers, modern kitchens, responsible procurement, attractive menus, flexible service, food safety, transparent administration, and coordination between nutrition and education.
