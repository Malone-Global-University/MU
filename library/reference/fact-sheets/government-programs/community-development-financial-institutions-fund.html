Community Development Financial Institutions Fund

The United States Treasury institution that certifies mission-driven community lenders and investors and administers grants, tax-credit allocations, bank incentives, long-term bond guarantees, housing capital, Native initiatives, and other tools supporting underserved people and economically distressed communities.

Terminology Note

The Community Development Financial Institutions Fund, commonly called the CDFI Fund, is a federal institution within the United States Department of the Treasury. It is distinct from the private and nonprofit financial institutions that it certifies as Community Development Financial Institutions.

A CDFI may be a regulated bank, credit union, depository institution holding company, nonprofit or for-profit loan fund, or venture-capital fund that satisfies federal certification requirements. Certification is an official Treasury designation; it is not a bank charter, deposit-insurance guarantee, endorsement of financial strength, or promise of a federal award.

A Community Development Entity, abbreviated CDE, is a separate certification used principally for the New Markets Tax Credit Program. CDFI Certification and CDE Certification should not be treated as interchangeable.

The CDFI Fund generally provides capital to or through financial intermediaries rather than making ordinary consumer, mortgage, or small-business loans directly to members of the public. Individuals and businesses seeking financing usually apply to a participating CDFI, bank, credit union, loan fund, CDE, or other local institution.

Several programs administered by the Fund are permanent or recurring, while others were temporary emergency initiatives created in response to the COVID-19 pandemic or a specific congressional appropriation.

As of August 2, 2026, Treasury was implementing revised certification, annual reporting, responsible-financing, and federal nondiscrimination requirements. Application and award documents for a specific fiscal year control where they differ from general descriptions on this page.

Overview

The CDFI Fund was created to strengthen specialised financial institutions that serve borrowers, businesses, housing projects, and communities often underserved by conventional financial markets.

Rather than replacing private finance, the Fund seeks to increase the capacity, liquidity, credibility, and reach of local lenders and investors.

CDFIs combine financial products with a community-development mission. They may offer small-business loans, affordable mortgages, consumer credit, community-facility finance, charter-school loans, affordable-housing finance, real-estate development loans, credit-building products, equity investments, and technical assistance.

The Fund certifies qualifying institutions, awards grants and investments, allocates tax-credit authority, administers long-term federal bond guarantees, rewards banks for increased community activity, supports Native finance, and collects industry performance data.

Some Fund programs require CDFI Certification. Others are open to FDIC-insured banks, qualified nonprofit housing organisations, or certified Community Development Entities.

The Fund does not replace the banking regulators. Insured banks and credit unions remain subject to their chartering, deposit-insurance, consumer-protection, capital, safety-and-soundness, and supervisory authorities.

Nondepository loan and venture funds remain subject to applicable state law, federal consumer and securities law, assistance agreements, and the Fund’s certification and award conditions.

Classification and Scope

This page classifies the CDFI Fund under Institutions because the subject is a continuing federal entity with statutory governance, officers, an advisory board, regulations, certification responsibilities, and several distinct programs.

The CDFI Program, Bank Enterprise Award Program, New Markets Tax Credit Program, Capital Magnet Fund, Native American CDFI Assistance Program, CDFI Bond Guarantee Program, and Small Dollar Loan Program are separate government programs.

The Riegle Community Development and Regulatory Improvement Act of 1994 belongs under Laws and Legislation.

Community development financial institutions as an industry, community development banking, mission-driven finance, and financial inclusion may receive separate concept entries.

Historical Context

Community-development finance in the United States developed through credit unions, minority-owned banks, community development corporations, revolving loan funds, faith-based lenders, housing organisations, community loan funds, and local investment institutions.

These organisations responded to redlining, racial discrimination, rural isolation, reservation credit shortages, disinvestment, bank closures, weak collateral, deteriorated housing, and limited business finance.

The Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 encouraged regulated depository institutions to help meet the credit needs of their communities, but it did not create a dedicated federal capital source for specialised community lenders.

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, community-development practitioners and lawmakers proposed a national fund that could certify institutions, match private capital, provide technical assistance, and reward bank partnerships.

The proposal attracted bipartisan support and was incorporated into broader legislation addressing community development and regulatory reform.

Riegle Community Development and Regulatory Improvement Act of 1994

President Bill Clinton signed Public Law 103-325 on September 23, 1994.

Title I contained the Community Development Banking and Financial Institutions Act.

The law:

  • created the CDFI Fund;
  • defined Community Development Financial Institutions;
  • authorised financial and technical assistance;
  • required matching funds for specified assistance;
  • created the Bank Enterprise Award Program;
  • established the Community Development Advisory Board;
  • authorised training and data collection;
  • required annual reports and audits;
  • required a study of capital and credit in Native communities;
  • provided enforcement authority;
  • preserved the authority of federal banking regulators; and
  • specified that assisted institutions do not become federal instrumentalities.

Federal Corporate Status and Treasury Relationship

The statute established the Fund as a wholly owned government corporation in the executive branch.

The original text stated that the Fund would not be affiliated with or within another agency or department, while providing a transition role for the Secretary of the Treasury.

In practice, the Fund operates within the Treasury Department and is presented publicly as a Treasury institution.

Its corporate form gives it authority to enter agreements, make investments, adopt rules, maintain accounts, collect information, and administer assistance.

The liability of the Fund and federal government for an equity investment is generally limited to the amount invested.

Community Development Advisory Board

The Riegle Act created a fifteen-member Community Development Advisory Board.

The board includes federal officials and private members with experience in community development, financial services, investment, banking, credit unions, Native communities, consumer and public interests, and other relevant fields.

The board advises the Fund on policy but does not decide whether a particular applicant receives an award.

Its meetings, recommendations, and membership provide a record of changing community-finance priorities.

Initial Implementation

The Fund published a notice opening the first CDFI Program and Bank Enterprise Award rounds on October 19, 1995.

The Community Development Advisory Board held its first meeting on February 2, 1996.

On July 31, 1996, the Fund announced $35.5 million for thirty-one organisations in the first CDFI Program round.

On October 1, 1996, thirty-eight banks and thrifts received $13.1 million through the first Bank Enterprise Award round.

The initial years established certification, assistance agreements, matching requirements, bank incentives, annual reporting, and a national community-finance network.

Types of Certified CDFIs

Community Development Banks

Federally or state-chartered banks that combine deposit taking and lending with a community-development mission.

Community Development Credit Unions

Member-owned cooperatives serving eligible fields of membership, often with savings, consumer, mortgage, and small-business products.

Loan Funds

Nondepository nonprofit or for-profit lenders financing housing, businesses, consumers, community facilities, and real estate.

Venture-Capital Funds

Investment entities providing equity or equity-like capital to businesses in underserved markets.

Depository Institution Holding Companies

Eligible holding companies whose structure and activities satisfy the current certification framework.

These categories differ in charter, regulation, capital structure, products, risk, and ability to accept deposits.

Purpose of CDFI Certification

Certification identifies financing entities that meet a national community-development standard.

Certified institutions may become eligible for several Fund award programs, specialised federal opportunities, private investments, deposits, grants, guarantees, and partnerships.

Certification can improve credibility with banks, foundations, investors, government agencies, and community partners.

Certification does not state that the institution is profitable, solvent, safe, well managed, or appropriate for every borrower.

CDFI Certification Requirements

An applicant must demonstrate that it:

  • is a legal entity at the time of application;
  • has a primary mission of promoting community development;
  • is a financing entity;
  • primarily serves one or more eligible target markets;
  • provides development services in conjunction with financing;
  • maintains accountability to the target market;
  • is not a government entity or controlled by government, subject to the Tribal-government exception;
  • meets responsible-financing and product standards in the current application;
  • provides complete data and documentation; and
  • agrees to annual certification and compliance reporting.

Primary Community-Development Mission

The organisation’s governing documents, policies, strategy, activities, products, and historical operations must show that community development is its primary mission.

The mission must be more than a marketing statement added shortly before application.

The Fund examines whether activities are purposefully directed toward improving social or economic conditions for underserved people or residents of distressed communities.

Responsible financing is treated as part of demonstrating a genuine community-development mission.

Financing-Entity Requirement

A certified CDFI must predominantly engage in arm’s-length, on-balance-sheet financing products or eligible financial services.

The applicant generally must have completed at least one full fiscal year of qualifying financing activity.

Referral, counselling, advocacy, grantmaking, or development work without qualifying financing is not sufficient by itself.

The financing entity must assume genuine financial risk and responsibility rather than acting solely as a broker or pass-through.

Target Markets

A CDFI must direct its financing to one or more eligible target markets.

Target markets can include:

  • Investment Areas consisting of economically distressed geographic areas;
  • Low-Income Targeted Populations;
  • Other Targeted Populations meeting current eligibility criteria;
  • Native American, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian populations and communities where applicable; and
  • approved combinations of geographic and population markets.

The applicant must define, document, and verify its selected market using the Fund’s current mapping and data rules.

Target-Market Activity Test

The certification framework generally requires at least 60 percent of both the number and dollar volume of qualifying financing transactions to serve eligible target-market components.

The dual test prevents an institution from placing many small transactions in a target market while reserving most dollars for other customers.

The Fund may apply current calculation, reporting-period, affiliate, product, and exception rules contained in the certification application and guidance.

Development Services

CDFIs provide development services that support the successful use of financing.

Services can include:

  • homebuyer education;
  • financial counselling;
  • credit-building assistance;
  • small-business planning;
  • loan-readiness assessment;
  • accounting and cash-flow guidance;
  • technical assistance for affordable housing or community facilities;
  • training for Native entrepreneurs;
  • post-closing business assistance; and
  • referrals to legal, tax, government, and social-service resources.

Development services may be provided directly or through a formal partner where current certification rules are satisfied.

Accountability to the Target Market

A CDFI must demonstrate that people connected to its target market can influence institutional governance or advice.

Accountability is ordinarily shown through a governing board, advisory board, or approved combination.

The Fund examines residence, employment, organisational leadership, business ownership, service, or other recognised connections to the target market.

Merely serving on the board of an unrelated community organisation does not automatically establish accountability.

Nongovernmental Status

A certified CDFI generally cannot be a government entity or be controlled by a government entity.

Tribal governments receive a statutory exception that recognises Tribal sovereignty and collective community ownership.

Public agencies can sponsor or support CDFIs but must structure governance and control consistently with current certification rules.

Responsible Financing and Consumer Protection

The revised certification application places greater emphasis on responsible products, transparency, affordability, borrower ability to repay, and consistency with community-development purposes.

A mission-driven institution can still cause harm through excessive pricing, hidden fees, unaffordable repayment structures, abusive collection, repeated refinancing, or opaque small-business financing.

The Fund postponed the certification requirement for written small-business loan disclosures concerning periodic payment, total repayment, total finance charges, and annual percentage rate until October 1, 2028.

The delay does not eliminate obligations imposed by consumer, commercial-finance, fair-lending, state disclosure, or other applicable laws.

Revised Certification Application

The Fund released a substantially revised certification application on December 7, 2023.

The revision changed documentation and standards involving mission, financing activity, target markets, development services, accountability, affiliates, collective review, responsible financing, and annual data.

Organisations certified as of the release date were required to reapply according to scheduled submission windows beginning in 2024.

Failure to reapply by the assigned deadline can result in loss of certification.

New applicants may submit applications under the current process without waiting for an annual funding round.

Annual Certification and Data Reporting

Certified CDFIs submit an Annual Certification and Data Collection Report.

The ACR permits the Fund to evaluate continued certification and collect information concerning mission, governance, financing, target markets, products, assets, and organisational changes.

Current schedules generally require submission within ninety days after the end of the organisation’s fiscal year, subject to the reporting calendar placed in the Awards Management Information System.

CDFIs may also submit abbreviated or full Transaction Level Reports depending on certification and award obligations.

Failure to report can result in certification loss, award noncompliance, withheld payments, remedies, or future ineligibility.

Material Events and Continuing Compliance

Certification and award recipients must report events that may affect eligibility or performance.

Material events can include:

  • merger or acquisition;
  • charter conversion;
  • loss of deposit insurance;
  • regulatory enforcement action;
  • bankruptcy or insolvency;
  • mission or governance change;
  • target-market decline;
  • change in control;
  • fraud investigation;
  • civil-rights finding;
  • failure to meet award performance goals;
  • audit finding; and
  • other events specified in the agreement.

Community Development Financial Institutions Program

The CDFI Program is the Fund’s foundational award program.

It provides Financial Assistance and Technical Assistance through competitive notices of funding availability.

Financial Assistance expands lending and investment capital, while Technical Assistance builds organisational capacity.

Certified CDFIs are generally required for Financial Assistance. Emerging organisations may be eligible for Technical Assistance under the current notice.

Since 1994, the program has awarded more than $3 billion in Financial and Technical Assistance.

Financial Assistance Awards

Financial Assistance can be structured as:

  • grants;
  • loans;
  • equity investments;
  • deposits;
  • credit-union shares; or
  • other authorised investment instruments.

The form of the federal award is linked to eligible matching funds and the applicant’s financial structure.

Recipients deploy capital for mortgages, businesses, consumers, facilities, housing, real estate, Native communities, and other approved community-development purposes.

Assistance agreements impose performance goals, deployment periods, reporting, audit, recordkeeping, and compliance duties.

Matching-Funds Requirement

The original statute generally required Financial Assistance to be matched dollar for dollar with nonfederal funds comparable in form and value.

Matching sources can include grants, loans, equity, retained earnings, deposits, credit-union shares, secondary capital, and other eligible resources.

Congress has periodically waived or modified the requirement for specific fiscal years, award types, or institutions.

Applicants must follow the current notice and matching-funds guidance rather than assume that one historical rule applies to every round.

Technical Assistance Awards

Technical Assistance grants support the organisational infrastructure needed to become or remain an effective CDFI.

Eligible uses may include:

  • staff and training;
  • technology and data systems;
  • consultants;
  • market studies;
  • audit and accounting systems;
  • loan policies and underwriting;
  • board development;
  • certification preparation;
  • product design;
  • compliance systems;
  • impact measurement; and
  • other capacity-building expenses.

Supplemental CDFI Program Awards

Congress and the Fund have used supplemental awards to direct financing toward identified needs.

Examples include:

  • Healthy Food Financing Initiative Financial Assistance;
  • Disability Funds Financial Assistance;
  • Persistent Poverty Counties Financial Assistance;
  • Housing Production Financial Assistance; and
  • other fiscal-year-specific initiatives.

Supplemental awards have their own eligibility, use, matching, reporting, and performance conditions.

Bank Enterprise Award Program

The Bank Enterprise Award Program rewards FDIC-insured banks and thrifts that increase qualifying community-development activity.

Awards are based on the increase from one assessment period to another rather than on the institution’s total historic activity alone.

Qualified activities include:

  • investments, loans, deposits, grants, and technical assistance to CDFIs;
  • small-business and affordable-housing finance in distressed communities;
  • home mortgages and home-improvement lending;
  • education and consumer lending;
  • commercial real-estate finance;
  • financial services; and
  • other approved community-development activity.

FY 2026 made as much as $40 million available for the program.

BEA Distressed Communities

The BEA Program uses a particularly high-distress standard.

Eligible areas generally have at least 30 percent of residents with incomes below the national poverty level and unemployment at least 1.5 times the national rate.

This standard is narrower than many other low-income or investment-area definitions.

Award recipients must reinvest BEA awards in future eligible activities.

Native Initiatives

The statute required the Fund to study barriers to capital and credit on Indian reservations and trust lands.

The resulting research documented limited banking access, collateral complications, remoteness, trust-land issues, weak infrastructure, and difficulty using mainstream public and private programs.

The Fund developed Native Initiatives to support Native CDFIs, emerging Native CDFIs, sponsoring entities, training, and technical assistance.

The program supports economic self-determination, housing, small business, consumers, community facilities, and local financial capacity.

Native American CDFI Assistance Program

The Native American CDFI Assistance Program, commonly called NACA, provides Financial Assistance and Technical Assistance.

A Financial Assistance applicant must be a certified CDFI and generally demonstrate that at least 50 percent of its activities serve Native American, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian communities.

Technical Assistance is available to Native CDFIs, emerging Native CDFIs, and sponsoring entities seeking to create a certified institution.

The Fund reports more than $220 million in NACA Financial and Technical Assistance awards.

New Markets Tax Credit Program

Congress created the New Markets Tax Credit Program in the Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000.

The Fund competitively allocates tax-credit authority to certified Community Development Entities.

Investors receive a federal income-tax credit equal to 39 percent of a qualified equity investment claimed over seven years.

CDEs use the investment capital for qualified low-income community investments, including businesses, real estate, community facilities, healthcare, education, manufacturing, and other approved projects.

Public Law 119-21 made the program permanent in 2025.

By August 2026, the Fund reported $81 billion in cumulative allocation authority.

Community Development Entity Certification

A CDE must be a domestic corporation or partnership with a primary mission of serving or providing investment capital for low-income communities or low-income persons.

It must maintain accountability to residents of low-income communities through representation on a governing or advisory board.

CDE Certification permits an entity to apply for NMTC allocation authority but does not guarantee an allocation.

A business seeking project finance should contact an allocatee rather than apply for certification merely to receive a loan.

New Markets Tax Credit Compliance

Allocatees sign allocation agreements and must deploy capital within required periods, maintain qualified investments, serve approved markets, and report transaction data.

Investors face credit recapture if statutory compliance fails during the seven-year period.

Treasury announced enhanced monitoring and nondiscrimination controls in connection with the 2024–2025 double allocation round announced in December 2025.

Complex leveraged structures, fees, exit arrangements, subsidised benefits, and community outcomes remain major oversight subjects.

Capital Magnet Fund

Congress created the Capital Magnet Fund through the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008.

The Fund awards grants to certified CDFIs and qualified nonprofit housing organisations.

Awards support affordable rental and homeownership housing, preservation, revolving funds, loan-loss reserves, risk-sharing, economic development, and community-service facilities associated with affordable housing.

Awardees generally must finance or support eligible project costs equal to at least ten times the federal award.

The program is financed through a statutory allocation from assessments on new mortgage purchases by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac rather than ordinary annual discretionary appropriations alone.

Capital Magnet Fund Financing Mechanism

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac contribute a statutory assessment based on the unpaid principal balance of new mortgage purchases.

Thirty-five percent of the resulting amount is allocated to the Capital Magnet Fund, with the remainder supporting the Housing Trust Fund.

The Federal Housing Finance Agency can suspend contributions under statutory conditions involving financial safety and soundness.

By the end of fiscal year 2024, Capital Magnet Fund awards totalled nearly $1.4 billion.

CDFI Bond Guarantee Program

The Small Business Jobs Act of 2010 created the CDFI Bond Guarantee Program.

The Secretary of the Treasury provides a 100-percent guarantee on bonds issued by approved Qualified Issuers and purchased by the Federal Financing Bank.

Bond proceeds are lent to eligible CDFIs for community and economic development purposes.

The program provides fixed-rate, long-term capital with maturities that can approach thirty years.

Guarantees are generally issued in amounts of at least $100 million, although several eligible CDFIs may participate in one bond with minimum individual commitments.

Nearly $3 billion had been guaranteed by 2026.

Bond Security and Federal Risk

The bond program is debt, not a grant.

Eligible CDFIs borrow and must repay principal, interest, fees, and other obligations.

Secondary loans and collateral are pledged within the approved structure.

Qualified Issuers, master servicers, trustees, the Federal Financing Bank, Treasury, and the Fund divide underwriting, servicing, monitoring, and enforcement functions.

Federal guarantees expose the government to losses if collateral and recovery are insufficient after default.

Small Dollar Loan Program

The Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 authorised the Small Dollar Loan Program.

It provides grants to certified CDFIs to establish or expand alternatives to high-cost small-dollar credit.

Program loans to consumers may not exceed $2,500.

The Fund provides:

  • Loan Loss Reserve grants to absorb qualifying losses; and
  • Technical Assistance grants for technology, staff, systems, and program operations.

The FY 2026 round made up to $9 million available.

Pandemic Response Programs

Congress used the CDFI Fund network to deploy emergency capital during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

CDFIs were viewed as institutions able to reach small businesses, consumers, nonprofits, and communities not fully served by conventional relief channels.

The principal one-time Fund programs were the CDFI Rapid Response Program and the CDFI Equitable Recovery Program.

The Treasury Department also administered the Emergency Capital Investment Program separately from the CDFI Fund, although many participating institutions were CDFIs or minority depository institutions.

CDFI Rapid Response Program

The Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021 provided emergency funding for a streamlined award round.

The program was designed to distribute capital broadly and quickly to certified CDFIs.

The Fund used existing certification, Financial Assistance, and Awards Management Information System infrastructure to meet accelerated statutory deadlines.

The award round has ended, but recipients continue to report and comply with assistance agreements.

CDFI Equitable Recovery Program

The CDFI Equitable Recovery Program was created to address disproportionate pandemic effects in low- and moderate-income communities and among borrowers with unmet capital and financial-service needs.

Congress made as much as $1.75 billion available for a single award round.

Eligibility required CDFI Certification and a demonstrated history of financing in eligible geographies.

Awards support recovery, business, housing, consumers, facilities, and long-term community investment.

Although no new general award round was expected, compliance and transaction reporting continued in 2026.

The Fund has administered or supported additional initiatives at different times, including:

How a Borrower Uses the CDFI System

  1. Locate an institution. The borrower searches the certified CDFI or CDE directory and local community-development networks.
  2. Confirm products and geography. Not every CDFI offers every loan or serves every location.
  3. Apply to the local institution. The CDFI, not the federal Fund, ordinarily underwrites the loan.
  4. Complete development services. Counselling, business planning, credit improvement, or homebuyer education may be required.
  5. Receive a decision. Approval depends on the CDFI’s underwriting, capital, policy, and product.
  6. Repay or meet investment terms. Federal support does not generally convert the transaction into free money.

Deposit Insurance and Financial Regulation

CDFI Certification does not insure deposits.

A CDFI bank may have Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation coverage, and a credit union may have National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund or private coverage, but the borrower or depositor must verify the institution’s status.

Loan funds and venture funds do not ordinarily accept insured retail deposits.

The appropriate federal and state regulators retain authority over safety and soundness, fair lending, consumer protection, securities, chartering, and insurance.

Relationship to the Community Reinvestment Act

The Community Reinvestment Act and the CDFI Fund are distinct but complementary.

The CRA evaluates how regulated banks help meet community credit needs consistent with safe and sound operations.

Banks may receive CRA consideration for eligible loans, investments, services, deposits, and partnerships involving CDFIs.

The Bank Enterprise Award Program provides a separate direct federal monetary incentive for increased qualifying activity.

Minority Depository Institutions

Minority Depository Institution status and CDFI Certification are separate.

Some banks and credit unions hold both designations.

MDIs are identified under federal banking statutes and agency definitions based on ownership, board, membership, or community served.

CDFI Certification focuses on mission, financing, target markets, development services, accountability, and organisational structure.

Dual-designated institutions played important roles in pandemic capital programs and community finance.

Rural Communities

Rural areas may lack bank branches, affordable mortgages, small-business credit, specialised underwriting, and investment capital.

CDFIs finance farms, rural businesses, housing, healthcare, childcare, broadband, manufacturing, community facilities, and local services.

Challenges include distance, sparse population, limited collateral values, small transaction size, ageing infrastructure, and thin secondary markets.

The Fund uses awards, Native initiatives, tax credits, bond guarantees, housing grants, and target-market rules to support rural finance.

Urban Communities

Urban CDFIs serve neighbourhoods affected by redlining, vacancy, disinvestment, displacement, high housing costs, and limited small-business capital.

Financing may support affordable housing, commercial corridors, minority-owned businesses, community facilities, grocery stores, healthcare, cultural institutions, and property acquisition.

Community finance can revitalise neighbourhoods but may also contribute to rising property values and displacement if affordability and community control are not protected.

Native Communities

Native CDFIs respond to geographic isolation, limited bank presence, trust-land collateral rules, infrastructure gaps, low household wealth, and culturally inappropriate underwriting.

They may provide consumer loans, business loans, homeownership finance, credit building, financial education, agricultural finance, and community facilities.

Tribal governance and community accountability can shape products in ways not available through distant financial institutions.

Racial Wealth and Financial Access

CDFIs frequently serve Black, Latino, Native, Asian American, immigrant, and other underserved borrowers, but certification is not itself limited to institutions serving one racial group.

Historical redlining, segregation, dispossession, discriminatory lending, unequal inheritance, and exclusion from ownership contribute to present capital gaps.

Current federal policy requires CDFIs and award recipients to comply with fair-lending and nondiscrimination law.

In April 2026, Treasury announced new agreement provisions requiring policies and annual certifications designed to prevent unlawful preferences or set-asides based on race, ethnicity, or sex.

Race-neutral administration does not eliminate the need to measure whether historically underserved communities receive access to responsible finance.

Impact and Leverage

CDFI Fund awards are intended to leverage private, philanthropic, state, local, and institutional capital.

Financial Assistance can strengthen net assets and borrowing capacity. Bank Enterprise Awards reward new activity. Tax credits attract equity investment. Bond guarantees create long-term debt. Capital Magnet Fund grants require substantial leverage.

Leverage ratios should not be interpreted as proof that the federal award caused every dollar of the resulting transaction.

Accurate evaluation distinguishes award amount, institution capital, financing originated, project cost, private investment, outstanding balance, jobs, units, and community outcomes.

Reported Results

By fiscal year 2024, the Fund reported more than $8 billion in monetary awards, $81 billion in New Markets Tax Credit allocation authority, and nearly $3 billion in bond guarantees.

The certified network exceeded 1,400 institutions.

Fiscal year 2024 CDFI Program Financial Assistance recipients included hundreds of institutions, while Native CDFIs received separate NACA awards.

Since fiscal year 2010, CDFI Program Financial Assistance recipients reported originating nearly $300 billion in loans and investments through September 30, 2024.

Reported originations include institutional capital beyond the federal award and should not be treated as direct Treasury spending.

Oversight and Compliance

The Fund uses application review, risk scoring, assistance agreements, allocation agreements, annual reports, audits, transaction-level data, site visits, certification review, and enforcement.

High-risk applicants may be excluded from awards.

Recipients must document use of federal assistance, achievement of performance goals, matching funds, target-market activity, leverage, eligible projects, and other program conditions.

Bank and credit-union regulators coordinate where Fund remedies could affect safety and soundness.

Treasury’s Office of Inspector General audits the Fund and investigates fraud, waste, abuse, award management, and financial reporting.

Enforcement and Remedies

Remedies can include:

  • withholding or suspending payments;
  • corrective-action plans;
  • repayment or recovery;
  • termination of an assistance agreement;
  • loss of certification;
  • loss of unused tax-credit allocation authority;
  • tax-credit recapture under federal tax law;
  • default remedies under guaranteed bonds;
  • suspension or debarment;
  • False Claims Act liability;
  • civil penalties; and
  • criminal prosecution.

Risks and Potential Abuse

  • False target-market data or transaction reporting.
  • Mission drift after certification.
  • Predatory or opaque financing inconsistent with community development.
  • Inflated leverage or job claims.
  • Use of pass-through entities to obtain awards or tax credits.
  • Excessive fees in complex New Markets Tax Credit structures.
  • Insider transactions and conflicts of interest.
  • Weak underwriting or concentrated risk.
  • Failure to maintain required matching funds.
  • Duplicate counting across federal programs.
  • Inadequate community accountability.
  • Use of public subsidy without durable community benefit.
  • Unlawful discrimination.
  • Cybersecurity, data, and servicing failures.
  • Default on long-term bond obligations.

Achievements

  • Created a national standard for mission-driven community financial institutions.
  • Expanded the number and capacity of CDFIs across the United States.
  • Provided flexible capital for lenders serving markets often avoided by conventional institutions.
  • Supported affordable housing, businesses, consumers, and community facilities.
  • Created a dedicated federal Native finance initiative.
  • Rewarded banks for expanding community-development activity.
  • Attracted private equity to low-income communities through the New Markets Tax Credit.
  • Created long-term, fixed-rate capital through federal bond guarantees.
  • Supported affordable housing through the Capital Magnet Fund.
  • Provided alternatives to high-cost small-dollar loans.
  • Used an established network to deploy pandemic recovery capital rapidly.
  • Improved community-finance data, mapping, reporting, and public directories.
  • Connected banks, foundations, governments, and private investors with local institutions.
  • Made community accountability a formal certification requirement.

Limitations and Failures

  • CDFI Certification does not guarantee financial strength or responsible conduct.
  • The Fund does not directly approve most local borrowers.
  • Award demand substantially exceeds available appropriations.
  • Reporting burdens can be substantial for small institutions.
  • Complex certification changes can cause institutions to lose status.
  • Target-market percentages do not alone prove community impact.
  • Federal awards can be small compared with local financing needs.
  • Loan funds may lack stable, low-cost, long-term capital.
  • Tax-credit transactions can be legally and financially complex.
  • Bond guarantees create repayment and federal-loss risk.
  • Some products can be unaffordable despite a community-development label.
  • Rural and Native institutions face particularly high operating costs.
  • Community revitalisation can contribute to displacement.
  • Outcome attribution is difficult when several subsidies and investors finance one project.
  • Temporary emergency programs create long compliance periods without new award rounds.
  • Political and legal changes can alter eligibility, nondiscrimination, reporting, and investment priorities.

Major Controversies

Effectiveness and Additionality

Researchers debate how much financing would have occurred without the Fund and whether public awards reach projects lacking other options.

Responsible Financing

Certification standards must balance product flexibility with transparency, affordability, and borrower protection.

New Markets Tax Credit Complexity

Supporters emphasise private investment and major projects; critics cite fees, layered structures, and uncertain net subsidy.

Mission Drift

Growth and outside capital can move an institution away from its original community mission.

Nondiscrimination

Treasury seeks to enforce federal civil-rights law while many CDFIs were created to address historically racialised exclusion.

Federal Risk

Grants, tax expenditures, and guarantees create different public costs and oversight requirements.

Administrative Capacity

Rapid program expansion, certification reform, large emergency appropriations, and detailed data systems strain both the Fund and recipients.

The Fund collects institution, transaction, borrower, project, geographic, product, performance, and compliance data.

Major systems and tools include:

Research Cautions

  • The Fund and a CDFI are different. The federal institution certifies and supports local financial institutions.
  • Certification is not deposit insurance. Verify FDIC, NCUA, or private insurance separately.
  • Certification is not an award. Most awards are competitive and subject to annual funding.
  • The Fund does not usually lend directly to the public. Borrowers apply to a local institution.
  • CDFI and CDE Certification are different. CDEs are central to the New Markets Tax Credit Program.
  • Award dollars, tax-credit authority, and guaranteed bonds are not comparable. They represent grants or investments, tax expenditure authority, and repayable debt.
  • Financing originated is not federal spending. CDFIs combine Fund awards with other capital.
  • Program requirements change by round. Notices, agreements, and current guidance control.
  • Temporary programs may remain on the website after awards end. Compliance can continue for years.
  • Community-development impact is not automatic. Affordability, ownership, displacement, repayment, and local benefit require separate analysis.
  • Historical counts may include institutions later decertified or merged. Use dated certified lists.
  • Current FY 2026 policy is evolving. Certification, reporting, public-benefit, and nondiscrimination rules should be checked against current Treasury documents.

Definitions

Annual Certification and Data Collection Report
The annual filing used to verify continued CDFI Certification and collect institution-level data.
Bank Enterprise Award
A monetary award to an eligible insured bank or thrift for increasing qualifying CDFI or distressed-community activity.
Capital Magnet Fund
A grant program supporting leveraged affordable-housing and related economic-development financing.
CDFI
A federally certified financing entity meeting mission, target-market, development-service, accountability, and organisational requirements.
CDFI Bond Guarantee
A Treasury guarantee supporting long-term bonds purchased by the Federal Financing Bank and used to finance eligible CDFIs.
CDFI Fund
The federal government corporation within Treasury that certifies and supports community-development financial institutions.
Community Development Entity
A domestic corporation or partnership certified for New Markets Tax Credit purposes because it serves low-income communities and maintains community accountability.
Development services
Counselling, education, technical assistance, and other support provided with financing.
Financial Assistance
CDFI or NACA Program capital provided through grants, loans, equity, deposits, credit-union shares, or other authorised instruments.
Investment Area
An eligible economically distressed geographic target market.
Native CDFI
A CDFI primarily serving Native American, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian communities under current program standards.
New Markets Tax Credit
A federal tax credit equal to 39 percent of a qualified equity investment in a certified CDE, claimed over seven years.
Target Market
An eligible investment area or targeted population that receives the required share of CDFI financing.
Technical Assistance
Grant support for staff, systems, training, certification, product development, and organisational capacity.
Transaction Level Report
A filing containing loan, investment, borrower, project, and geographic information used for certification, compliance, and impact analysis.

Summary

The Community Development Financial Institutions Fund is a wholly owned federal government corporation administered within the United States Department of the Treasury.

Congress created it through the Riegle Community Development and Regulatory Improvement Act of 1994 to promote economic revitalisation and community development through specialised financial institutions.

The Fund certifies banks, credit unions, loan funds, venture funds, and eligible holding companies that maintain a primary community-development mission, operate as financing entities, primarily serve target markets, provide development services, maintain community accountability, and satisfy organisational requirements.

CDFI Certification is not deposit insurance, a bank charter, a federal loan approval, or a guarantee that the institution will receive an award.

The CDFI Program provides Financial and Technical Assistance. The Bank Enterprise Award Program rewards increased bank activity. Native Initiatives support Native CDFIs. The New Markets Tax Credit attracts private equity. The Capital Magnet Fund supports affordable housing. The Bond Guarantee Program provides long-term federal credit. The Small Dollar Loan Program supports alternatives to high-cost lending.

Temporary Rapid Response and Equitable Recovery programs used the network to distribute pandemic capital and continue to generate compliance obligations.

By fiscal year 2024, the Fund reported more than $8 billion in monetary awards, $81 billion in tax-credit allocation authority, nearly $3 billion in guaranteed bonds, and more than 1,400 certified CDFIs.

The revised certification application released in 2023 required previously certified organisations to reapply through phased windows and strengthened mission, target-market, accountability, responsible-financing, and data standards.

In 2026, Treasury added agreement provisions concerning federal nondiscrimination compliance and postponed the certification-related small-business loan disclosure requirement until October 1, 2028.

The Fund has expanded responsible finance in underserved urban, rural, Native, and territorial communities, but it continues to face challenges involving limited appropriations, reporting burden, mission drift, product affordability, complex tax-credit structures, federal guarantee risk, impact attribution, and changing legal requirements.
