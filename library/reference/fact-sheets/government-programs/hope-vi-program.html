HOPE VI Program

The federal public-housing redevelopment initiative that replaced severely distressed developments with lower-density, mixed-income communities while relocating residents, leveraging private investment, and restructuring the physical and social organization of public housing.

Overview

The HOPE VI Program was a competitive federal grant program created in 1992 to address severely distressed public housing. Originally known as the Urban Revitalization Demonstration, it provided grants to public housing agencies for demolition, rehabilitation, replacement housing, site redevelopment, management reform, resident relocation, community and supportive services, and the creation of mixed-income communities.

The program emerged from the work of the National Commission on Severely Distressed Public Housing. In its 1992 final report, the Commission estimated that approximately 86,000 units—about 6 percent of the national public housing inventory—were severely distressed. It proposed a national action plan combining physical improvements, stronger management, resident services, and concentrated federal intervention.

Congress first funded the Urban Revitalization Demonstration through the Departments of Veterans Affairs and Housing and Urban Development, and Independent Agencies Appropriations Act, 1993. The program received an initial appropriation of $300 million. Congress continued it through annual appropriations before placing it on a more formal statutory basis in Section 24 of the United States Housing Act of 1937 through the Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998.

HOPE VI became the principal federal instrument for replacing large, obsolete, and highly distressed public housing developments. Local housing agencies commonly demolished superblocks, high-rise towers, barracks-style buildings, and isolated developments and replaced them with lower-density townhouses, apartments, streets, parks, community facilities, and mixed-income housing.

The redevelopment model combined public housing replacement units with other affordable rentals, Low-Income Housing Tax Credit units, market-rate rentals, and homeownership. Public housing agencies used federal grants to leverage private equity, mortgages, tax-exempt bonds, local funds, land contributions, philanthropic support, and other public resources.

HOPE VI also attempted to address resident and institutional conditions. Grantees funded job training, childcare, education, healthcare, case management, youth programs, financial literacy, homeownership preparation, transportation, and other Community and Supportive Services. Housing authorities were expected to strengthen property management, reduce vacancy, improve security, and create developments financially and physically capable of long-term operation.

The program produced major physical improvements at numerous sites and helped normalize mixed-finance and mixed-income redevelopment within federal housing policy. HUD ultimately maintained a working portfolio of 262 revitalization grants awarded from fiscal years 1993 through 2010, totaling approximately $6.2 billion. Federal grants leveraged nearly $11 billion in additional financing according to later program analysis.

HOPE VI was also controversial. Redevelopment frequently demolished more deeply subsidized public housing units than it rebuilt on the original site. Former residents often received vouchers or relocated to other public housing, and only a portion returned to the redeveloped community. Screening standards, occupancy requirements, lease enforcement, reduced density, long construction periods, and limited replacement units created barriers to return.

Supporters emphasize improved housing quality, lower crime, stronger property management, private investment, restored street networks, and reduced concentrations of poverty. Critics emphasize displacement, community disruption, loss of public housing, incomplete one-for-one replacement, exclusion of the poorest former residents, weak resident control, and the use of mixed-income redevelopment as a vehicle for neighborhood repositioning.

The federal government stopped making new standard HOPE VI awards after the final fiscal-year 2010–2011 competition. Choice Neighborhoods succeeded HOPE VI as the principal competitive neighborhood-transformation program, expanding eligibility beyond public housing to certain privately owned HUD-assisted housing and placing greater formal emphasis on neighborhood institutions, education, and resident outcomes.

Definitions

HOPE VI
The common name for the Urban Revitalization Demonstration and later statutory program providing competitive grants for revitalization of severely distressed public housing. Federal and local materials commonly expand HOPE as “Housing Opportunities for People Everywhere,” while the original HOPE-grants statutory lineage used “Homeownership and Opportunity for People Everywhere.”
Urban Revitalization Demonstration
The original formal name of the program created by the fiscal-year 1993 HUD appropriations law.
Severely distressed public housing
Public housing experiencing serious physical deterioration, uninhabitable conditions, high vacancy, weak management, concentrated poverty, crime, social isolation, or other conditions making ordinary modernization insufficient.
Revitalization grant
A competitive HOPE VI grant supporting demolition, rehabilitation, construction, replacement housing, relocation, site improvement, management reform, supportive services, and related redevelopment activities.
Demolition grant
A HOPE VI grant focused principally on demolition of distressed public housing and relocation of affected residents rather than comprehensive site redevelopment.
Planning grant
A smaller grant supporting resident consultation, physical and financial planning, development-team formation, environmental review, and preparation of a revitalization proposal.
Public housing agency
A state-chartered, regional, county, municipal, or other public body authorized to own or administer public housing and apply for HOPE VI assistance. The abbreviation PHA is commonly used.
Revitalization plan
The HUD-approved redevelopment strategy establishing demolition, relocation, financing, unit production, resident services, management, schedule, budget, and long-term operating commitments.
Replacement housing
Public housing, other assisted housing, homeownership units, tenant-based vouchers, or an authorized combination used to replace housing opportunities affected by demolition or disposition.
Public housing replacement unit
A unit remaining within the federal public housing program and reserved for eligible public housing households under applicable operating, rent, occupancy, and use restrictions.
Tenant-based assistance
Rental assistance, commonly a Housing Choice Voucher, assigned to a household for use in qualifying privately owned housing rather than attached to the redeveloped site.
Mixed-income housing
A development containing residents or units associated with multiple income categories, commonly combining public housing, other affordable housing, market-rate rental housing, and sometimes homeownership.
Mixed-finance public housing
Public housing developed through a combination of HUD public housing resources and private or other public financing, commonly using ownership entities, tax credits, loans, bonds, or grants.
Deconcentration of poverty
A policy objective of reducing the geographic concentration of very low-income households through mixed-income development, tenant mobility, dispersed replacement housing, or changes in admissions and occupancy.
Community and Supportive Services
The HOPE VI component funding case management, employment, education, childcare, health, youth, transportation, financial, and related services for residents. The abbreviation CSS is commonly used.
Resident relocation
The process of moving households from buildings scheduled for demolition or rehabilitation to temporary or permanent housing, including other public housing, vouchers, or replacement units.
Right to return
A policy or contractual right permitting an original resident to return to a redeveloped site after satisfying applicable eligibility, lease, screening, and occupancy requirements.
Return rate
The proportion of original residents who later occupy units within the redeveloped HOPE VI community. Definitions and denominators vary among reports and sites.
Relocatee
A resident household displaced or moved as part of demolition, rehabilitation, or redevelopment.
One-for-one replacement
A requirement that each demolished public housing unit be replaced by another public housing unit. The general federal requirement was repealed before many major HOPE VI redevelopments.
Homeownership unit
A dwelling developed for sale to an eligible purchaser, which could include public housing residents, other low- or moderate-income households, or market-rate buyers depending on the project.
New Urbanism
An urban-design approach emphasizing connected streets, traditional blocks, mixed housing types, pedestrian access, porches, public space, and integration with surrounding neighborhoods. It influenced many HOPE VI plans.
Defensible space
A design concept using building orientation, entrances, visibility, territorial definition, and public-private boundaries to support resident control and reduce opportunities for crime.
Leverage
Non-HOPE VI funds committed to a redevelopment, including tax-credit equity, private debt, bonds, local funds, philanthropic resources, land, infrastructure, and other federal or state assistance.
Section 3
A federal requirement directing certain HUD-funded employment, training, and contracting opportunities toward low-income persons and businesses connected to HUD-assisted communities.
HOPE VI Main Street
A related grant authority created for smaller communities to convert unused commercial space in historic or traditional downtown buildings into affordable housing while supporting economic development.

Origins and Policy Context

Public Housing Distress

Public housing conditions varied substantially by city and development. Many properties remained stable and valuable, but a smaller group experienced severe structural deterioration, vacancies, crime, weak management, financial distress, and social isolation.

Large developments frequently housed extremely poor populations because of broader economic change, suburbanization, racial discrimination, federal admissions policy, declining urban employment, and limited alternatives for households with the lowest incomes.

Racial Segregation

Federal and local public housing had often been developed or operated within racially segregated systems. Site selection, tenant assignment, municipal boundaries, discriminatory lending, urban renewal, highway construction, and suburban exclusion concentrated many African American residents within isolated central-city developments.

Failure of Ordinary Modernization

Traditional modernization repaired roofs, plumbing, heating systems, elevators, kitchens, and other physical components. Policymakers concluded that some developments required more fundamental reconfiguration because their design, density, management, location, vacancy, and neighborhood conditions could not be corrected through repairs alone.

National Commission

Congress created the National Commission on Severely Distressed Public Housing in 1989. The Commission held hearings, reviewed developments, consulted residents and officials, and produced a national action plan in 1992.

The Commission emphasized physical reconstruction, improved management, resident services, and coordinated intervention. Its estimate that 86,000 units were severely distressed became a central benchmark for HOPE VI.

Mixed-Income Precedents

Harbor Point in Boston demonstrated that an isolated and distressed public housing development could be redeveloped through a public-private partnership into a mixed-income community. Its physical design, management structure, financing, and income mix influenced the evolving HOPE VI model.

Federal Housing Reform

HOPE VI developed during a wider shift toward vouchers, private ownership, tax-credit finance, mixed-income development, deregulation, local flexibility, and reduced reliance on construction of traditional public housing.

Program Purposes

Section 24 and annual funding notices identified several interrelated purposes:

  • Improve the living environment of residents in severely distressed public housing
  • Revitalize public housing sites and surrounding neighborhoods
  • Demolish or rehabilitate obsolete and distressed buildings
  • Provide replacement housing for affected households
  • Avoid or lessen concentrations of very low-income families
  • Build sustainable mixed-income communities
  • Improve public housing management and financial viability
  • Promote resident self-sufficiency and upward mobility
  • Leverage private and other public investment
  • Encourage high-quality architecture, urban design, and neighborhood integration
  • Support resident participation in redevelopment planning
  • Expand rental and homeownership opportunities

These purposes did not always align. Reducing density and creating mixed-income housing could conflict with replacement of every public housing unit. Private financing could require rents, unit types, schedules, and screening practices different from traditional public housing. Neighborhood transformation could conflict with preserving an existing resident community in place.

Application and Grant Selection

Competitive Process

Public housing agencies submitted applications to HUD under annual Notices of Funding Availability. Applications identified a target development, documented severe distress, proposed a redevelopment plan, described relocation and resident services, identified partners, and presented financing commitments.

Evidence of Distress

Applicants documented building deterioration, design problems, vacancy, crime, poverty, management weakness, operating costs, capital needs, and barriers to ordinary modernization.

Housing Authority Capacity

HUD evaluated the housing agency’s financial, development, procurement, management, and implementation capacity. Agencies with weak performance often needed private development partners, receivership arrangements, or substantial technical assistance.

Resident and Community Participation

Applications described consultation with residents, local government, service providers, schools, businesses, neighborhood organizations, and other stakeholders. The quality and influence of participation varied substantially.

Leveraging

HUD increasingly rewarded commitments of non-HOPE VI resources. Applicants assembled tax credits, bonds, loans, land, infrastructure, public funds, philanthropic support, and private investment.

Relocation and Replacement

Applicants proposed where residents would live during redevelopment, what assistance they would receive, how replacement housing would be structured, and whether original residents could return.

Community and Supportive Services

Plans identified resident needs, service providers, employment strategies, case management, education, childcare, transportation, and other supports.

Selection Limits

Funding was insufficient to serve every severely distressed development. Later research found that a substantial share of eligible or comparable applicants never received a revitalization grant.

Redevelopment Process

Planning and Development Team

The public housing agency selected developers, architects, planners, contractors, financial consultants, property managers, service providers, legal counsel, and other partners. Procurement and developer selection could become major sources of delay or controversy.

Resident Consultation

Housing agencies held meetings, surveys, design workshops, advisory committees, and negotiations with resident councils. Participation could influence unit design, relocation, services, amenities, phasing, and return policies, but final control generally remained with the housing agency, HUD, funders, and development partners.

Environmental and Historic Review

Projects underwent applicable environmental review, lead and contamination assessment, floodplain analysis, historic-preservation consultation, and related federal or local processes.

Relocation

Residents moved to other public housing, private units using vouchers, temporary units, or housing outside the immediate neighborhood. Relocation could occur in phases or through complete site clearance.

Demolition and Site Preparation

Housing agencies demolished buildings, removed infrastructure, remediated contamination, regraded land, restored street grids, installed utilities, and prepared parcels for new development.

Mixed-Finance Closing

Development phases commonly required separate ownership entities, Low-Income Housing Tax Credit allocations, loans, bonds, public-housing declarations, operating arrangements, land leases, guarantees, and HUD approvals.

Construction

Redevelopment often proceeded through multiple rental and homeownership phases. Long schedules exposed projects to financing changes, construction inflation, market downturns, contamination, infrastructure costs, political transition, and partner failure.

Lease-Up and Return

Public housing replacement units were leased to qualifying applicants, including eligible former residents. Market-rate and other affordable units followed separate eligibility and leasing systems.

Long-Term Management

Redeveloped properties commonly used private or nonprofit management, mixed-income operating standards, homeowners associations, design controls, public-housing agreements, and multiple subsidy systems.

Physical Design and Neighborhood Form

Demolition of Superblocks

Many HOPE VI projects replaced inward-facing superblocks with connected streets, conventional blocks, individual addresses, front doors, porches, sidewalks, and public spaces tied to the surrounding city.

Lower Density

Redevelopments often reduced the number of units on the original site. Lower density was intended to improve design, safety, management, marketability, and neighborhood integration, but it also reduced the number of deeply subsidized units available for returning residents.

Housing-Type Diversity

Projects used townhouses, duplexes, garden apartments, mid-rise buildings, senior housing, accessible units, detached homes, and market-rate or affordable homeownership.

Tenure Integration

Rental and homeownership units were sometimes distributed throughout the redevelopment. Other projects separated unit types, income categories, buildings, phases, or amenities.

Community Facilities

Plans could include schools, childcare centres, health clinics, community buildings, parks, recreation, retail, police facilities, libraries, and service offices.

Design Uniformity

Many projects attempted to make public housing units visually indistinguishable from tax-credit and market-rate units. The objective was to reduce stigma and create a unified neighborhood appearance.

Defensible Space

Designs increased visibility, private entrances, resident-controlled yards, clear boundaries, street-facing windows, and reduced common corridors. Supporters associated these features with lower crime and stronger territorial control.

Housing Mix and Financing

Public Housing Units

Public housing replacement units remained subject to federal public housing requirements, income eligibility, operating subsidies, use restrictions, and housing authority oversight.

Other Affordable Units

Developments frequently included Low-Income Housing Tax Credit units, HOME-assisted units, Project-Based Vouchers, other subsidized rentals, and units restricted under state or local programs.

Market-Rate Rentals

Market-rate units were intended to attract households with higher incomes, reduce poverty concentration, support operating income, and strengthen market demand.

Homeownership

Some developments included affordable and market-rate homes for sale. Programs offered counselling, down-payment assistance, soft loans, and other support to qualifying buyers.

Low-Income Housing Tax Credit

LIHTC became one of the principal tools for converting HOPE VI grants into larger mixed-finance transactions. Investor equity financed restricted rental units within redevelopment phases.

Private Debt and Bonds

Projects used mortgages, tax-exempt bonds, construction loans, credit enhancement, and other financing supported by restricted and market rents.

Local and State Resources

Cities and states contributed land, infrastructure, tax increment, grants, housing funds, roads, utilities, parks, schools, and related investments.

Leverage Debate

Leverage expanded total development resources and attracted private expertise. It also increased transaction complexity, subordinated some decisions to investor and lender requirements, and made schedules dependent on multiple funding systems.

Resident Relocation and Return

Relocation Options

Residents commonly received one or more of the following:

  • Transfer to another public housing unit
  • Housing Choice Voucher assistance
  • Temporary relocation during rehabilitation
  • Placement in newly constructed off-site replacement housing
  • Opportunity to return to a completed public housing replacement unit
  • Homeownership counselling or assistance for eligible households

Relocation Assistance

Housing agencies provided notices, counselling, moving expenses, search assistance, transportation, utility support, landlord outreach, and other services under grant agreements and applicable relocation law.

Right-to-Return Conditions

Return commonly required continued income eligibility, good standing, compliance with lease obligations, criminal-history standards, household-size compatibility, and completion of application procedures. Requirements varied by grant, site, year, and local policy.

Reduced On-Site Capacity

Because many projects reduced density and added market-rate or other non-public housing, the redeveloped site often contained fewer public housing units than the former development. Not every original household could return on-site even when eligible.

Long Development Periods

Relocated residents could remain away for many years while demolition, financing, and construction proceeded. Household circumstances, contact information, health, family composition, preferences, and eligibility could change during that period.

Tracking Problems

GAO and researchers identified inconsistent data on resident location, services, outcomes, and return. Housing authorities sometimes lost contact with former residents or used different definitions of return eligibility and successful relocation.

Community Disruption

Relocation separated neighbors, extended families, childcare networks, congregations, informal support systems, resident organizations, and local businesses. Physical redevelopment could therefore improve the site while dissolving parts of the prior community.

Resident Choice

Some residents preferred vouchers, other public housing, or different neighborhoods and did not seek return. Others wanted to return but faced insufficient units, screening barriers, relocation failures, or changed circumstances. Return rates cannot be interpreted without distinguishing voluntary nonreturn from exclusion or inability to return.

Community and Supportive Services

Case Management

Many sites assigned case managers to assess household needs, establish plans, coordinate services, track progress, and support relocation or return.

Employment

Programs included job readiness, vocational training, placement, apprenticeships, construction employment, employer partnerships, Section 3 opportunities, and employment retention.

Education

Activities included adult education, literacy, General Educational Development preparation, tutoring, college access, youth enrichment, scholarships, and school partnerships.

Childcare and Youth Programs

Sites funded childcare, after-school programs, recreation, mentoring, summer employment, violence prevention, arts, and youth leadership.

Health and Social Services

Services could include primary healthcare, mental health, substance-use treatment, domestic-violence support, nutrition, senior services, disability assistance, and benefits access.

Financial and Homeownership Services

Programs offered budgeting, credit repair, banking access, savings, tax preparation, entrepreneurship, homebuyer education, and foreclosure prevention.

Transportation

Relocation and employment plans sometimes included transit passes, shuttle service, vehicle assistance, or coordination with transportation agencies.

Funding Limits

Supportive-services funding was time-limited and generally smaller than housing-development resources. Long-term continuation often depended on other agencies, grants, philanthropy, or local institutions.

Outcome Limits

Research found meaningful improvements in housing conditions and safety for many residents, but employment, income, and health gains were inconsistent. Residents with serious health, disability, caregiving, trauma, or labor-market barriers often required longer and more intensive assistance.

African American Communities and Racial Effects

Disproportionate Exposure

Many targeted developments were predominantly African American because public housing had been shaped by segregation, discriminatory siting, urban renewal displacement, white suburban exclusion, lending discrimination, municipal fragmentation, and concentrated disinvestment.

Material Improvement

HOPE VI replaced dangerous and deteriorated buildings in numerous African American communities. Redeveloped sites often provided higher-quality units, functioning systems, improved streets, parks, lower vacancy, and stronger management.

Loss of Deeply Subsidized Housing

Demolition reduced the number of traditional public housing units at many sites. Because African American households were substantially represented among residents, they bore a disproportionate share of relocation and the loss of on-site public housing capacity.

Mixed-Income Integration

Mixed-income plans introduced households with higher incomes and, in some locations, greater racial diversity. Integration outcomes varied and could reflect both genuine inclusion and replacement of former residents by households with greater economic resources.

Neighborhood Revaluation

Redevelopment improved market perception and attracted public and private investment. In appreciating neighborhoods, this could protect affordable units within revitalizing areas but also contribute to broader displacement pressure on nearby renters and businesses.

Resident Political Power

Resident councils and neighborhood organizations sometimes negotiated return rights, unit counts, services, design, and employment. In other cases, residents had advisory roles without decisive authority over demolition, finance, density, or ownership.

Screening and Exclusion

Lease compliance, criminal-history review, housekeeping standards, employment expectations, occupancy rules, and other screening practices could exclude original residents. Neutral standards could have unequal effects when applied to communities already subject to intensive policing and economic disadvantage.

Mobility and Segregation

Vouchers allowed some families to move to safer or lower-poverty neighborhoods. Others relocated to racially segregated, high-poverty areas because of landlord refusal, payment standards, discrimination, transportation, social ties, or limited mobility assistance.

Management and Governance

Housing Authority Transformation

HOPE VI encouraged public housing agencies to become developers, asset managers, partnership participants, and coordinators of private capital rather than only owners and direct property managers.

Private Management

Many developments used private or nonprofit property management. Management standards commonly addressed rent collection, maintenance, landscaping, lease enforcement, resident relations, security, and financial performance.

Ownership Complexity

Different phases could be owned by separate limited partnerships, limited-liability companies, housing authority affiliates, nonprofit entities, or private developers. Public housing declarations and operating agreements preserved federal interests in replacement units.

Resident Governance

Resident councils, advisory committees, community associations, and homeowners associations participated in varying degrees. Mixed-income developments often replaced traditional public housing resident governance with broader neighborhood or property structures.

Federal Oversight

HUD approved grant agreements, development proposals, budgets, amendments, demolition, replacement units, mixed-finance transactions, relocation, and closeout. Oversight challenges included staff capacity, inconsistent data, slow expenditure, changing plans, and multi-year implementation.

Grant Amendments

Projects frequently revised unit mix, financing, schedules, service plans, partners, locations, or budgets. Material changes required HUD approval and sometimes renewed resident consultation.

Program Outcomes

Physical Transformation

HOPE VI removed many of the nation’s most deteriorated public housing buildings and replaced them with new housing, infrastructure, streets, parks, and community facilities.

Unit Production

By September 2009, HUD reported that grantees had demolished 94,367 public housing units, completed 80,130 total housing units, and occupied 78,347 units. Total completed units included public housing, other affordable housing, market-rate rentals, and homeownership rather than only one-for-one public housing replacement.

Private and Public Leverage

The $6.2 billion revitalization portfolio leveraged nearly $11 billion in additional financing. The program established mixed-finance development as a standard public-housing tool.

Crime and Safety

Many redeveloped sites experienced lower crime, improved resident perceptions of safety, lower vacancy, and stronger management. Some crime reduction resulted from physical design and management; some also reflected substantial population reduction and resident relocation.

Housing Quality

Residents living in new or relocated housing frequently reported better physical conditions than in the former development. Outcomes depended on the condition and location of replacement housing and private-market units.

Poverty Concentration

Mixed-income redevelopment reduced the measured concentration of extremely poor households on original sites. This occurred through addition of higher-income households, reduction of public housing units, relocation of former residents, and changes in admissions and tenure.

Resident Well-Being

Studies found improvements in safety and housing quality for many residents but less consistent gains in employment, earnings, health, and self-sufficiency. Relocation outcomes varied by household characteristics and destination neighborhood.

Long-Term Child Outcomes

A 2026 Census Bureau working paper reports positive adult outcomes for children who entered revitalized HOPE VI housing after redevelopment, especially where redevelopment increased connection to higher-income neighbors and surrounding communities. The findings concern post-redevelopment exposure and do not erase concerns about original-resident displacement or selection into the new developments.

Institutional Legacy

HOPE VI reshaped public housing agencies, design practice, finance, relocation policy, resident services, and federal expectations concerning neighborhood integration.

Major Controversies

Demolition Without One-for-One Replacement

Many sites demolished more public housing units than they replaced. Supporters argued that obsolete density and vacancy made one-for-one replacement inappropriate. Critics argued that reduced replacement accelerated the national loss of deeply affordable housing.

Low Resident Return

Only a portion of former residents returned to many redeveloped sites. Disagreement concerns whether nonreturn reflected resident choice, inadequate tracking, changed needs, long delays, screening, reduced units, exclusion, or relocation failures.

Displacement

Relocation could improve housing and neighborhood conditions, but it also disrupted social networks and sometimes moved households to other high-poverty neighborhoods.

Mixed-Income Assumptions

HOPE VI assumed that mixing incomes could improve social networks, norms, safety, services, and opportunity. Evidence of direct interaction and economic benefit to the poorest adult residents has been mixed.

Resident Participation

Housing agencies were required to consult residents, but consultation did not always provide control over whether demolition occurred, how many units returned, who owned the property, or what screening standards applied.

Privatization

Critics describe mixed-finance ownership and private management as privatization of public housing land and governance. Supporters argue that private capital and management were necessary to rebuild and preserve distressed assets.

Gentrification

HOPE VI could contribute to neighborhood reinvestment and rising property values. In stronger markets, nearby low-income residents and businesses could face displacement pressures without broader preservation strategies.

Cost and Delay

Projects often required many years and extensive financing. Delays increased costs, prolonged relocation, and weakened trust among residents.

Selective Success

Competitive funding favored housing agencies with stronger plans, partners, land markets, financing capacity, and local support. Some of the most distressed agencies and communities had difficulty assembling viable applications.

Key Distinctions

HOPE VI and the Federal Public Housing Program

The Federal Public Housing Program is the continuing national system of publicly owned or controlled rental housing. HOPE VI was a competitive redevelopment grant program targeting selected severely distressed developments within that system.

HOPE VI and Urban Renewal

Urban renewal principally acquired and cleared designated areas for redevelopment. HOPE VI focused on public housing sites and included replacement housing, mixed-income development, relocation, management reform, and resident services. Both could involve demolition and displacement.

HOPE VI and Choice Neighborhoods

HOPE VI centred on severely distressed public housing. Choice Neighborhoods can address public housing and certain privately owned HUD-assisted housing while formally integrating housing, neighborhood, education, services, and local institutional transformation.

HOPE VI and Section 18 Demolition or Disposition

HOPE VI provided competitive redevelopment funding. Section 18 is a statutory approval process through which public housing agencies can demolish or dispose of public housing under qualifying conditions, with separate replacement and relocation rules.

HOPE VI and the Rental Assistance Demonstration

HOPE VI commonly demolished and redeveloped distressed sites with competitive grants. RAD converts public housing subsidy to long-term project-based Section 8 assistance to support preservation or redevelopment, generally without a HOPE VI grant.

HOPE VI and Moving to Work

Moving to Work provides selected housing agencies flexibility over funding and statutory requirements. A Moving to Work agency could also receive HOPE VI grants, but the programs had distinct purposes and authorities.

HOPE VI and Low-Income Housing Tax Credits

HOPE VI provided federal grant capital. LIHTC provided investor equity through federal tax credits. Many HOPE VI phases combined both resources.

Revitalization Grant and Demolition Grant

A revitalization grant supported comprehensive redevelopment and replacement housing. A demolition grant primarily funded removal of distressed units and resident relocation without financing a complete mixed-income redevelopment.

Public Housing Replacement and Total Housing Production

Total HOPE VI housing production included public housing, tax-credit housing, other affordable units, market-rate rentals, and homeownership. A count of total units should not be interpreted as a count of replacement public housing units.

Geographic Scope

HOPE VI revitalization grants were awarded to public housing agencies across the United States, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. The 262-grant portfolio covered 132 public housing agencies and developments in major metropolitan centres, midsized cities, smaller cities, and selected specialized sites.

Grant locations included Atlanta, Baltimore, Boston, Chicago, Cleveland, Denver, Detroit, Louisville, Memphis, Milwaukee, New Orleans, Oakland, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, Portland, San Antonio, San Francisco, Seattle, St. Louis, Washington, D.C., and many other communities.

The largest concentrations occurred in regions with extensive older public housing inventories, high-rise or superblock developments, industrial decline, racial segregation, and strong local redevelopment capacity.

Site conditions varied. Some developments occupied valuable central-city land near downtown districts, universities, hospitals, transit, or waterfronts. Others were isolated by highways, industrial uses, vacant land, or neighborhood disinvestment.

Geographic outcomes depended on both the original site and off-site relocation. A physically successful redevelopment could coexist with weak outcomes for former residents who relocated to distant or similarly disadvantaged neighborhoods.

HOPE VI Main Street grants had a different geographic focus, assisting smaller communities with affordable housing connected to historic or traditional downtown revitalization.

Legal Context

Departments of Veterans Affairs and Housing and Urban Development, and Independent Agencies Appropriations Act, 1993

Public Law 102-389 created the Urban Revitalization Demonstration under the HOPE-grants heading and appropriated $300 million for fiscal year 1993. Annual appropriations continued the program before permanent statutory authorization.

United States Housing Act of 1937

HOPE VI redevelopment operated within the broader federal public housing system governing development, operation, rents, eligibility, demolition, resident rights, and public housing agency responsibilities.

Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998

QHWRA added Section 24 to the United States Housing Act, formally establishing purposes, eligible activities, selection criteria, resident services, relocation, replacement housing, reporting, and program authority. It also advanced deconcentration, mixed-income occupancy, local flexibility, and broader public-housing reform.

HOPE VI Program Reauthorization and Small Community Mainstreet Rejuvenation and Housing Act of 2003

Enacted within Public Law 108-186, this law extended program authority, revised selection and reporting, and established grants for affordable housing connected to Main Street revitalization in smaller communities.

Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970

The Uniform Relocation Act established federal requirements for advisory services, moving expenses, replacement-housing payments, property acquisition, and treatment of people displaced by covered federally assisted projects. Applicability depended on the specific action, funding, and legal framework.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

The Fair Housing Act prohibited specified forms of discrimination in housing and applied to site selection, relocation, marketing, tenant selection, accessibility, and redevelopment activity.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibited discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in HUD-funded activities and required housing authorities to administer redevelopment without unlawful discrimination.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 required nondiscrimination, accessibility, reasonable accommodation, and program access for people with disabilities in covered federally assisted activities.

Americans with Disabilities Act

Title II applied to public housing agencies and local governments as public entities. Other provisions governed covered public accommodations and services.

National Environmental Policy Act

HOPE VI development required environmental review addressing potential effects, alternatives, hazards, and mitigation under applicable HUD procedures.

National Historic Preservation Act

Section 106 review applied when redevelopment affected properties listed or eligible for listing in the National Register of Historic Places. Many public housing developments had become old enough to require historic evaluation.

Section 18 of the United States Housing Act

Section 18 governs demolition and disposition of public housing. HOPE VI provided separate grant and redevelopment authority, but demolition, land transfers, and replacement plans interacted with broader public housing law and HUD approval.

Section 3 of the Housing and Urban Development Act of 1968

Section 3 directed covered employment, training, and contracting opportunities toward low-income persons and businesses connected to HUD-assisted communities.

Low-Income Housing Tax Credit Law

Internal Revenue Code Section 42 governed tax-credit phases within HOPE VI projects, including investor ownership, income and rent restrictions, compliance, extended use, and state allocation.

Grant Agreements and Funding Notices

Each project was governed by its grant agreement, approved revitalization plan, annual funding notice, amendments, mixed-finance documents, relocation plan, declarations of trust, regulatory agreements, and other project-specific requirements.

Summary

The HOPE VI Program was the central federal public-housing redevelopment initiative of the 1990s and 2000s. Created in 1992 after the National Commission on Severely Distressed Public Housing identified approximately 86,000 severely distressed units, it funded demolition, rehabilitation, replacement housing, mixed-income redevelopment, resident relocation, management reform, and supportive services.

HOPE VI transformed the physical and financial model of public housing. Housing authorities used federal grants to leverage tax-credit equity, loans, bonds, local resources, and private development capacity. Large superblocks and high-rise developments were frequently replaced with lower-density streets, townhouses, apartments, parks, public facilities, affordable housing, market-rate rentals, and homeownership.

The program’s 262-grant revitalization portfolio received approximately $6.2 billion in federal funding and leveraged nearly $11 billion in additional resources. Many redeveloped sites achieved higher-quality housing, improved management, lower vacancy, reduced crime, stronger physical integration, and renewed private and public investment.

These achievements carried substantial trade-offs. Demolition commonly exceeded on-site public housing replacement. Tens of thousands of households relocated, and only a portion returned. Long redevelopment periods, reduced density, screening, changing family circumstances, and limited replacement units disrupted established resident communities and reduced the availability of deeply subsidized housing at many sites.

HOPE VI therefore remains both a major public-housing achievement and a major displacement controversy. Its record depends on which outcome is measured: the quality of the redeveloped site, the number and type of units produced, the experience of returning residents, the destinations of displaced households, the preservation of public housing, or the long-term trajectory of the surrounding neighborhood.

Choice Neighborhoods succeeded HOPE VI and retained many of its core strategies while broadening attention to neighborhood institutions, education, services, and privately owned assisted housing. HOPE VI continues to shape contemporary policy through its mixed-income model, financing structures, resident-relocation lessons, design principles, preservation needs, and unresolved questions about who benefits from public redevelopment.
