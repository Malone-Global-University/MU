Title I Education Program

The principal federal elementary and secondary education formula-grant program providing supplemental resources to school districts and schools serving concentrations of children from low-income families.

Overview

The Title I Education Program is the common name for Title I, Part A of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965. Its formal current title is Improving Basic Programs Operated by Local Educational Agencies. It is the largest federal program specifically supporting elementary and secondary schools serving concentrations of children from low-income families.

This fact sheet uses “Title I” to mean Title I, Part A unless another part is expressly identified. The broader Title I of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act also includes separate authorities involving state assessments, migratory children, and children and youths who are neglected, delinquent, or at risk.

Congress created Title I through the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 as part of President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society and War on Poverty. The program represented a major expansion of the federal role in public education while preserving state and local control over schools, curriculum, staffing, and ordinary school finance.

The program’s statutory purpose is to provide all children a significant opportunity to receive a fair, equitable, and high-quality education and to close educational achievement gaps. It seeks to advance that purpose by directing federal funds toward local educational agencies and schools with substantial concentrations of children from low-income families.

Title I is not a scholarship, voucher, or direct cash benefit to an individual family. It is a federal formula grant flowing principally from the United States Department of Education to state educational agencies, then to local educational agencies, and finally to eligible public schools and services for eligible private-school students.

Federal allocations are calculated through four formulas: Basic Grants, Concentration Grants, Targeted Grants, and Education Finance Incentive Grants. The formulas use annual Census Bureau estimates of children ages five through seventeen living in families below the federal poverty threshold, supplemented by smaller state-reported counts of children in specified categories.

Poverty determines where Title I funds are concentrated; it does not ordinarily determine which individual student may receive an instructional service. Within a targeted assistance school, students are selected according to educational need. In a schoolwide program, Title I funds can improve the entire educational program for all students, with particular attention to students who are lowest achieving or most at risk.

A school generally becomes eligible to operate a schoolwide program when at least 40 percent of enrolled children or children residing in its attendance area are from low-income families. A state educational agency can approve a schoolwide program below the 40-percent threshold when the school demonstrates that a schoolwide approach would best serve students.

Schools that receive Title I funds but do not operate schoolwide programs use the targeted assistance model. Those schools identify children who are failing or most at risk of failing to meet state academic standards and provide supplemental services to the selected students.

Title I can support teachers, paraprofessionals, interventionists, instructional coaches, evidence-based curricula, tutoring, extended learning time, summer programs, early childhood education, educational technology, professional development, school climate, attendance initiatives, counseling, family engagement, and other activities aligned with a comprehensive needs assessment and federal requirements.

The program is integrated with the state standards, assessment, accountability, and school-improvement framework established by the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015. States identify schools for comprehensive, targeted, or additional targeted support and improvement. Title I school-improvement funds help local educational agencies implement evidence-based strategies.

Title I is supplemental. It does not replace the constitutional and statutory responsibility of states and local governments to finance public education. The federal supplement-not-supplant, maintenance-of-effort, comparability, and fiscal-accountability requirements are intended to prevent Title I schools from receiving fewer ordinary state and local resources because federal aid is available.

The program includes extensive parent and family engagement requirements. Districts and schools must develop written policies with participating families, hold annual meetings, provide understandable notices, establish school-parent compacts, communicate academic information, and create meaningful opportunities for families to participate in program planning and evaluation.

Local educational agencies must also provide equitable Title I services to eligible children attending nonprofit private schools. The public agency retains control of funds and program administration; private schools do not receive a direct allocation.

The most recently posted enacted federal funding total is approximately $18.41 billion for fiscal year 2025. The Department of Education reported approximately $18.19 billion in new state awards after required national set-asides and adjustments. The program remains active in 2026.

Title I has major racial and civil-rights significance because poverty, residential segregation, school segregation, unequal property wealth, and state and local finance systems have concentrated African American, Latino, Native American, and other historically underserved students in under-resourced schools. Title I directs additional federal resources toward those communities but does not equalize total school funding or eliminate segregation.

Definitions

Title I
A title of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act addressing the education of disadvantaged students. In ordinary school usage, “Title I” usually means Title I, Part A.
Title I, Part A
The federal formula-grant program formally titled Improving Basic Programs Operated by Local Educational Agencies.
Elementary and Secondary Education Act
The primary federal statute governing elementary and secondary education programs. The abbreviation ESEA is commonly used.
Every Student Succeeds Act
The 2015 law that amended and reauthorized the ESEA. The abbreviation ESSA is commonly used.
State educational agency
The state board, department, or other agency primarily responsible for state supervision of public elementary and secondary schools. The abbreviation SEA is commonly used.
Local educational agency
A public board of education or other public authority legally responsible for administrative control or direction of public elementary or secondary schools. The abbreviation LEA is commonly used.
School attendance area
The geographic area in which children normally served by a particular school reside.
Title I school
A public school receiving and operating a program with Title I, Part A funds.
Title I-eligible school
A school or attendance area eligible for Title I funding under poverty-ranking and statutory allocation rules. An eligible school does not necessarily receive funds.
Formula child
A child counted for federal Title I allocation purposes, principally a child age five through seventeen living in a family below the Census Bureau poverty threshold, plus specified smaller categories.
Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates
The Census Bureau program producing annual school-district estimates of children ages five through seventeen in poverty used in Title I allocations. The abbreviation SAIPE is commonly used.
State per-pupil expenditure
A state expenditure measure used within the federal grant formulas and subject to statutory minimum and maximum percentages of the national average. The abbreviation SPPE is commonly used.
Basic Grant
The original and broadest Title I allocation formula, serving eligible local educational agencies with at least ten formula children exceeding 2 percent of the district’s school-age population.
Concentration Grant
A formula directing additional funds to local educational agencies with more than 6,500 formula children or a formula-child rate exceeding 15 percent.
Targeted Grant
A formula using number and percentage weights to provide larger per-child allocations to local educational agencies with higher concentrations of formula children.
Education Finance Incentive Grant
A formula allocating funds according to state-level measures of effort and equity and local district poverty weights. The abbreviation EFIG is commonly used.
Hold-harmless provision
A statutory rule limiting the annual reduction in an eligible local educational agency’s allocation relative to an earlier year, subject to poverty-level and formula conditions.
State minimum
A formula provision guaranteeing qualifying states a minimum share of national Title I funds, subject to statutory caps and available appropriations.
School poverty measure
A uniform district-selected measure used to rank school attendance areas and schools, such as Census poverty, free and reduced-price meals, TANF, Medicaid, or a composite measure.
Schoolwide program
A Title I model allowing a school to use federal funds to improve the entire educational program for all students, particularly the lowest-achieving students.
Targeted assistance program
A Title I model in which supplemental services are directed to identified students who are failing or most at risk of failing to meet state standards.
Comprehensive needs assessment
A systematic examination of academic, demographic, attendance, school-climate, instructional, staffing, family, and resource data used to identify priorities and design a schoolwide program.
Evidence-based intervention
An activity, strategy, or intervention supported by strong, moderate, promising, or rationale-based evidence under ESSA definitions and applicable program requirements.
Comprehensive support and improvement
State identification and support for at least the lowest-performing 5 percent of Title I schools, high schools with very low graduation rates, and certain schools with chronically underperforming subgroups. The abbreviation CSI is commonly used.
Targeted support and improvement
Support and improvement for schools with one or more consistently underperforming student subgroups. The abbreviation TSI is commonly used.
Additional targeted support and improvement
Support for schools in which a subgroup performs at a level comparable to the lowest-performing schools statewide. The abbreviation ATSI is commonly used.
Supplement, not supplant
The fiscal principle requiring Title I funds to add to, rather than replace, state and local resources that would otherwise support participating students and schools.
Comparability
The requirement that Title I schools receive state and local services comparable to those provided in non-Title I schools before federal funds are added.
Maintenance of effort
The requirement that a local educational agency generally maintain at least 90 percent of its prior state and local education effort under the statutory comparison. The abbreviation MOE is commonly used.
Carryover
Unspent Title I funds remaining available after the initial federal fiscal period, subject to period-of-availability and percentage limitations.
Reservation
A portion of an SEA or LEA allocation set aside before the remaining funds are distributed to schools, such as funds for school improvement, administration, homeless students, or parent engagement.
Equitable services
Secular, neutral, and nonideological Title I services provided by a public agency to eligible children attending nonprofit private schools, their teachers, and their families.
School-parent compact
A written agreement describing how families, school staff, and students share responsibility for improved academic achievement.
Parent right to know
Title I notice rights concerning teacher qualifications, student assessment results, language instruction, school status, and other program information.
Skipped school
An otherwise eligible school not funded with Title I because it receives equivalent or greater supplemental state and local resources meeting statutory conditions.
Nonprofit private school
A nonpublic elementary or secondary school eligible to participate in Title I equitable services through an LEA when statutory requirements are satisfied.

Historical Origins

Federal Aid Debate

Before 1965, proposals for broad federal school aid repeatedly failed because of disputes concerning religion, race, segregation, federal control, regional politics, and whether aid should flow to public or private schools. Title I overcame part of this conflict by targeting aid according to child poverty rather than distributing unrestricted general school funds.

War on Poverty

President Johnson regarded education as a principal instrument for reducing poverty across generations. Title I linked federal anti-poverty policy to schools serving communities with limited household income and weaker local fiscal capacity.

Civil Rights

The ESEA followed Brown v. Board of Education and the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Federal education funds increased national leverage over school systems that continued to discriminate or resist desegregation.

Children Rather Than Institutions

The program was framed as aid to children with educational need rather than permanent federal operating support for particular school systems. This framing also supported services for eligible children attending nonprofit private schools without direct grants to religious institutions.

Fiscal Federalism

Title I established a continuing federal role while leaving states and localities responsible for most education funding. Federal money was intended to be supplemental and concentrated rather than sufficient to operate the public school system.

Early Implementation Problems

Early evaluations found that districts sometimes spread funds thinly, used money for ordinary expenses, selected weakly targeted activities, or lacked reliable evaluation. Congress and federal agencies gradually added fiscal controls, student targeting, program standards, parent involvement, and accountability.

Program Structure and Administration

United States Department of Education

The Department calculates state and district-level formula allocations, issues regulations and guidance, monitors state administration, approves state plans, collects data, provides technical assistance, resolves complaints, and enforces federal requirements.

State Educational Agencies

SEAs receive federal awards and are responsible for:

  • Administering the state ESEA plan
  • Adjusting federal LEA estimates when authorized
  • Reserving required and optional state-level funds
  • Reviewing LEA plans and applications
  • Monitoring fiscal and program compliance
  • Identifying schools for support and improvement
  • Providing technical assistance
  • Approving schoolwide waivers below 40 percent poverty
  • Administering complaints and corrective action
  • Reporting performance and expenditure data

Local Educational Agencies

LEAs develop local plans, consult stakeholders, reserve required funds, calculate equitable services, rank schools by poverty, allocate funds, approve school programs, employ staff, oversee purchases, monitor results, maintain records, and submit claims or expenditure reports.

Schools

Title I schools conduct needs assessments, plan instructional and support strategies, engage parents, coordinate staff, deliver services, monitor student progress, evaluate implementation, and revise programs.

Federal–State–Local Relationship

The federal government establishes conditions for receiving funds but does not ordinarily prescribe a national curriculum. States establish standards and assessments, and local systems make many program and spending decisions within federal and state requirements.

Consolidated Plans

States and LEAs can submit consolidated ESEA plans covering multiple federal programs. Consolidation reduces separate paperwork but does not erase the statutory purposes and fiscal requirements of each program.

Funding Flow

Congressional Appropriation

Congress appropriates annual discretionary funding for Title I, Part A. The program can continue even when an authorization-of-appropriations period has expired, provided Congress enacts funding.

National Set-Asides

Before state awards are calculated, federal law generally reserves:

  • 0.7 percent for the Secretary of the Interior and Bureau of Indian Education schools
  • 0.4 percent for specified outlying areas
  • Funding to obtain annual Census Bureau poverty estimates
  • Amounts required for federal bypass services where applicable

Four Formula Pools

Remaining appropriations are divided among Basic, Concentration, Targeted, and Education Finance Incentive Grants according to congressional appropriations and statutory rules.

State Awards

The Department calculates estimated LEA amounts and totals them into state awards. State totals are not identical to the amount ultimately available for ordinary school allocations because states make required reservations and adjustments.

State Reservations

States reserve funds for school improvement, administration, specified programs for children in institutions, and optional direct student services before distributing final LEA awards.

LEA Reservations

Local districts reserve funds for required activities such as services for homeless children, equitable private-school services, parent and family engagement when applicable, and other districtwide obligations.

School Allocations

The remaining amount is distributed to eligible schools according to poverty ranking and per-pupil allocation rules. Funds are not allocated to schools by counting only the students who will directly receive services.

Four Federal Allocation Formulas

Basic Grants

Basic Grants are the oldest and broadest Title I formula. An LEA generally qualifies when it contains at least ten formula children and those children exceed 2 percent of its population ages five through seventeen.

The initial grant calculation multiplies formula children by an expenditure factor derived from state per-pupil expenditure. State minimums, hold-harmless rules, appropriations, and ratable reductions affect the final award.

Concentration Grants

Concentration Grants provide additional funds to districts with relatively large numbers or shares of formula children. An LEA generally qualifies when it has more than 6,500 formula children or a formula-child rate above 15 percent.

Concentration eligibility can continue temporarily under statutory hold-harmless rules when a district falls below the ordinary threshold.

Targeted Grants

Targeted Grants generally require at least ten formula children constituting at least 5 percent of the district’s school-age population. Formula children are weighted through separate number and percentage scales.

The weights rise with the number or percentage of formula children, concentrating more money per formula child in districts with greater poverty concentration.

Education Finance Incentive Grants

EFIG uses a two-stage structure. First, funds are allocated among states according to the state’s share of formula children, state education effort, and the equity of state and local school spending. Second, each state distributes its award among eligible LEAs using weighted formula-child counts and statutory rules.

Like Targeted Grants, EFIG generally requires at least ten formula children constituting at least 5 percent of the district’s school-age population.

Why Four Formulas Exist

The four formulas reflect different congressional priorities:

  • Broad national coverage
  • Concentration in high-poverty districts
  • Protection from abrupt annual funding loss
  • State minimum allocations
  • Recognition of differences in education costs
  • Incentives for state fiscal effort and funding equity
  • Balance among large cities, suburbs, small districts, and rural areas

Complexity

The formulas interact with current poverty data, prior-year allocations, state expenditure measures, statutory caps, weights, minimums, and appropriations. As a result, changes in child poverty do not translate directly or immediately into proportional changes in final allocations.

Formula Children and Poverty Data

Census Poverty Estimates

Approximately 97 percent of formula children are children ages five through seventeen living in families below the Census Bureau poverty threshold. SAIPE produces annual estimates for more than 13,000 school districts.

Other Formula Categories

Smaller state-reported categories include specified children in institutions for neglected or delinquent children, children in publicly supported foster homes, and children in families receiving TANF payments above the Census poverty income level.

Poverty Threshold and Poverty Guidelines

The Census poverty threshold used for statistical allocation purposes is not the same as the Department of Health and Human Services poverty guidelines used for many public-benefit eligibility decisions.

Formula Child and Title I Participant

A formula child is used to calculate district funding. The child is not automatically an identified Title I participant, and students receiving Title I services are not limited to those counted in the formula.

Annual Change

Allocations can shift when poverty estimates, district boundaries, population, state spending, or institutional counts change.

2024 SAIPE Context

The Census Bureau reported that the median estimated poverty rate for children ages five through seventeen across school districts was 12.5 percent in 2024.

State Reservations and Adjustments

School Improvement

Each state generally reserves 7 percent of its Title I allocation for school improvement under ESEA Section 1003. At least 95 percent of the reserved amount is generally distributed to LEAs to support schools identified for comprehensive or targeted improvement.

State Administration

States reserve a limited amount for administration, monitoring, technical assistance, reporting, and implementation of federal programs.

Direct Student Services

A state may reserve up to 3 percent for direct student services under Section 1003A. Authorized services can include advanced coursework, career and technical education, personalized learning, transportation for public school choice, and academic tutoring.

Neglected and Delinquent Children

States and LEAs must identify and distribute funds generated by children in qualifying institutions in accordance with Title I, Part D and related Part A allocation rules.

LEA Boundary Adjustments

States can adjust federal estimates among LEAs in specified circumstances, including inaccurate boundaries, special local educational agencies, and charter-school structures, while preserving the total state award and statutory allocation principles.

Small Districts

States can calculate final allocations for small LEAs under statutory procedures rather than relying exclusively on direct federal district calculations.

Within-District School Allocations

Step One: Select a Poverty Measure

An LEA selects a consistent measure for comparing school poverty. Permissible measures can include:

  • Census poverty data
  • Eligibility for free and reduced-price school meals
  • Temporary Assistance for Needy Families
  • Medicaid eligibility
  • A composite of authorized measures

Step Two: Calculate School Poverty

The district determines the number and percentage of children from low-income families in each attendance area or school using the selected measure.

Step Three: Rank Schools

The LEA ranks school attendance areas and schools from highest to lowest poverty.

Step Four: Serve Schools Above 75 Percent

The LEA generally must allocate funds to all eligible school attendance areas and schools with poverty rates above 75 percent before serving lower-poverty schools.

Step Five: Allocate Remaining Funds

After serving schools above 75 percent, an LEA can continue districtwide ranking or rank eligible schools within grade-span groupings.

Per-Pupil Amount

The per-pupil amount allocated to a lower-ranked school cannot ordinarily exceed the amount allocated to a higher-ranked school within the applicable ranking structure.

Schoolwide Status Does Not Determine Allocation

A school does not receive a larger allocation merely because its Title I program serves all students. Poverty ranking—not the number of directly served students—controls the school allocation.

Skipped Schools

An LEA can skip an otherwise eligible school when it receives supplemental state and local funds equal to or greater than the Title I amount and those funds meet statutory conditions.

One-Year Continuation

An LEA can continue serving a school for one additional year when the school becomes ineligible because of changing poverty data, subject to federal rules.

Charter Schools

New and expanding charter schools receive Title I allocations under federal charter-school allocation requirements and state procedures intended to provide timely and equitable funding.

Schoolwide Programs

Eligibility

A school may ordinarily operate a schoolwide program when at least 40 percent of enrolled children or children residing in the attendance area are from low-income families. The SEA may waive the threshold.

Purpose

A schoolwide program improves the entire educational program so that all students—particularly the lowest-achieving students—can meet challenging state academic standards.

Comprehensive Needs Assessment

The school examines data concerning achievement, growth, attendance, discipline, climate, staffing, curriculum, family engagement, student groups, resources, and other local conditions.

Schoolwide Plan

The written plan describes strategies, evidence, resource use, parent participation, coordination, monitoring, and evaluation. It is developed with teachers, school leaders, paraprofessionals, families, and other stakeholders.

Whole-School Flexibility

Schoolwide funds can support activities benefiting any student when the activity is designed to improve the school’s entire program and address identified needs.

Lowest-Achieving Students

Whole-school flexibility does not permit a school to disregard students experiencing the greatest academic difficulty. Strategies must particularly address the lowest-achieving students and historically underserved groups.

Consolidation of Funds

A schoolwide program can consolidate Title I with other federal, state, and local funds when statutory conditions are met. Consolidation can reduce separate cost accounting and support an integrated educational strategy.

Annual Review

The school regularly monitors implementation and student outcomes and revises the plan when strategies do not produce the intended results.

2026 Federal Flexibility Emphasis

In January 2026, the Department encouraged states to use their authority to approve schoolwide programs below 40 percent poverty when the model would reduce unnecessary compliance burden and improve student success.

Targeted Assistance Programs

Student Selection

A targeted assistance school identifies students who are failing or most at risk of failing to meet state academic standards. The school uses multiple, educationally related, objective criteria established by the LEA and supplemented by school criteria.

Not an Income Test

Individual student poverty does not determine participation. A student can receive targeted assistance based on educational need regardless of household income.

Statutorily Eligible Groups

Federal law recognizes eligibility for certain children, including qualifying children who recently participated in Head Start, Title I preschool, or Migrant Education; children experiencing homelessness; and children in local institutions for neglected or delinquent children.

Supplemental Services

Services may include tutoring, intervention, additional instructional time, small-group instruction, counseling, technology, summer learning, family engagement, and transition support.

Coordination with Regular Instruction

Title I staff coordinate with classroom teachers so that supplemental services reinforce rather than isolate students from the regular educational program.

Avoiding Stigma

Schools should provide services in a manner that does not publicly identify, segregate, or stigmatize participating students.

Records

The school must document student eligibility, services, staff time, expenditures, and program effectiveness more specifically than a schoolwide program.

Allowable Uses of Funds

Title I expenditures must be reasonable, necessary, allocable, documented, consistent with the program plan, and supplemental to ordinary state and local resources.

Instructional Personnel

  • Teachers and interventionists
  • Instructional coaches
  • Paraprofessionals
  • Reading and mathematics specialists
  • Counselors and student-support staff
  • Preschool staff
  • Program coordinators

Instruction and Intervention

  • Evidence-based reading and mathematics intervention
  • High-quality tutoring
  • Extended-day and extended-year learning
  • Summer programs
  • Credit recovery
  • Advanced coursework access
  • Transition programs
  • Dropout prevention

Curriculum and Materials

Funds can support instructional materials, software, books, assessments, adaptive resources, licenses, and supplies necessary to implement the approved program.

Educational Technology

Technology can be funded when connected to an identified educational need and supported by devices, connectivity, training, accessibility, maintenance, and instructional implementation.

Professional Development

Title I can support sustained professional learning for teachers, paraprofessionals, administrators, counselors, and other staff serving participating students.

Student Supports

Allowable activities can include attendance intervention, behavioral supports, school climate, mental and behavioral health services, social work, mentoring, and coordination with community services when they advance the Title I plan.

Parent and Family Engagement

Funds can support family liaisons, workshops, translation, transportation, childcare, home-school communication, family resource centers, and materials helping families support learning.

Preschool

LEAs and schools can use Title I to serve eligible preschool children through districtwide, schoolwide, targeted, or coordinated early-learning programs.

Capital and Equipment

Equipment, limited remodeling, and facility-related costs can be allowable when necessary for program implementation and compliant with federal property, procurement, accessibility, and fiscal requirements.

Preschool and Early Learning

Districtwide Preschool

An LEA can reserve Title I funds to operate or support a districtwide preschool program for eligible children.

Schoolwide Preschool

A Title I schoolwide program can serve preschool children as part of its comprehensive plan when state law and program structure permit.

Targeted Preschool

A targeted assistance program uses multiple educationally related criteria to identify preschool children most at risk of failing to meet state academic standards.

Head Start Standards

Title I-funded preschool programs generally must comply with applicable Head Start education performance standards established through federal law and joint Education–Health and Human Services guidance.

Coordination

Programs can coordinate with Head Start, state prekindergarten, IDEA preschool services, childcare, community-based organizations, and local early-learning systems.

Transition to Kindergarten

Title I can support transition activities, family engagement, records transfer, screening, professional collaboration, and continuity between preschool and elementary school.

School Improvement and Accountability

State Standards and Assessments

States receiving Title I funds maintain challenging academic standards and administer annual assessments in specified grades and subjects, including separate reporting for student subgroups.

State Accountability Systems

Each state establishes long-term goals, indicators, annual differentiation, subgroup measures, and methods for identifying schools needing support.

Comprehensive Support and Improvement

States identify at least the lowest-performing 5 percent of Title I schools, public high schools with graduation rates at or below the statutory threshold, and certain schools with persistent subgroup underperformance.

Targeted Support and Improvement

Schools with consistently underperforming student subgroups develop targeted plans addressing the causes of underperformance.

Evidence-Based Strategies

Improvement plans select strategies supported by the level of evidence required by the ESEA and appropriate to identified needs.

Resource Inequities

Comprehensive improvement plans examine resource inequities, which can include access to effective educators, advanced coursework, instructional materials, funding, facilities, technology, and student supports.

State Set-Aside

States use the 7-percent school-improvement reservation to make formula or competitive subgrants to LEAs and provide technical assistance.

Exit Criteria

States establish exit criteria and more rigorous intervention when a school fails to improve within the state-defined period.

Report Cards

State and local report cards publicly present assessment results, graduation, accountability status, educator data, expenditure information, and outcomes by subgroup.

Parent and Family Engagement

LEA Policy

Every LEA receiving Title I funds develops a written parent and family engagement policy jointly with participating families.

School Policy

Each Title I school develops and periodically updates a school-level policy describing how families will participate in planning, review, communication, capacity building, and program improvement.

Annual Meeting

Each Title I school holds an annual meeting to explain the program, requirements, funding model, family rights, and opportunities for involvement.

School-Parent Compact

Families and school staff jointly develop a compact describing shared responsibility for academic achievement and effective communication.

One-Percent Reservation

An LEA receiving more than $500,000 must reserve at least 1 percent of its Title I allocation for parent and family engagement. At least 90 percent of the required public-school share is generally distributed to participating schools.

Family Input

Parents participate in deciding how reserved engagement funds are allotted and used.

Right-to-Know Notices

Families can request information about teacher and paraprofessional qualifications and receive notice when a child has been taught for four or more consecutive weeks by a teacher who does not meet applicable state certification or licensure requirements.

Language and Accessibility

Notices must be understandable and, to the extent practicable, provided in a language families can understand and in accessible alternative formats.

Evaluation

LEAs annually evaluate barriers to family participation, including barriers affecting families experiencing economic disadvantage, disability, limited English proficiency, limited literacy, or racial and ethnic marginalization.

Equitable Services for Private-School Students

Eligible Children

Eligible children attending nonprofit private schools can receive Title I services when they reside in participating public school attendance areas and meet educational-need criteria.

Proportional Share

The LEA calculates a proportional share based principally on the number of children from low-income families who reside in participating public school attendance areas and attend nonprofit private schools.

Public Control

The LEA or another public agency retains title to materials, controls funds, approves services, employs or contracts with providers, and maintains records.

No Direct Grant

Federal funds do not become the property of the private school. Services must be secular, neutral, and nonideological.

Consultation

The LEA engages in timely and meaningful consultation with private-school officials concerning student identification, services, providers, location, assessment, family engagement, timing, and fund calculation.

Comparable and Equitable

Services must be equitable in comparison with those provided to public-school participants, although they need not be identical.

Bypass

The Secretary of Education can arrange bypass services when an LEA is prohibited from providing equitable services or has substantially failed to do so.

Students Experiencing Homelessness, Foster Care, and Other Instability

Homeless Students

LEAs reserve Title I funds as necessary to provide comparable services to children and youths experiencing homelessness, including students attending non-Title I schools.

Allowable activities can include tutoring, supplies, transportation connected to education, counseling, identification, outreach, and other services not ordinarily available from other sources.

Foster Care

State and local plans include procedures intended to maintain school stability for children in foster care. LEAs and child-welfare agencies coordinate best-interest determinations, immediate enrollment, records, points of contact, and transportation.

Migratory Children

Title I, Part C operates the separate Migrant Education Program. Migratory children can also participate in Part A programs when they meet schoolwide or targeted assistance requirements.

English Learners

English learners can receive Title I services in addition to language-instruction services under Title III. Title I cannot replace the core language services required by civil-rights law and state obligations.

Children with Disabilities

Students with disabilities can participate in Title I. Title I services supplement and do not replace services required under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act or Section 504.

Neglected and Delinquent Children

Separate Title I, Part D programs support children in state institutions, local institutions, detention settings, and community day programs. Part A and Part D funding rules interact but remain legally distinct.

Fiscal Requirements

Supplement, Not Supplant

Under ESSA, an LEA generally demonstrates compliance by showing that it uses a neutral methodology to allocate state and local funds to schools without considering Title I status. The test focuses on the allocation methodology rather than a presumption against every individual Title I cost.

Comparability

Before receiving Title I funds, an LEA must use state and local resources to provide services in Title I schools that are comparable to those in non-Title I schools. LEAs maintain records and generally demonstrate comparability annually.

Maintenance of Effort

An LEA generally must maintain combined state and local education expenditures at not less than 90 percent of the applicable prior-year comparison. Failure can reduce the federal award unless the Department grants a statutory waiver.

Carryover Limitation

An LEA receiving at least $50,000 generally may not carry over more than 15 percent of its Title I allocation into the next fiscal period. The SEA can grant a waiver under specified circumstances.

Period of Availability

Federal education funds are generally available for the initial fiscal year plus an additional carryover period under the Tydings Amendment, producing an ordinary period of approximately twenty-seven months.

Reasonable and Necessary Costs

Every expense must be necessary for the program, reasonable in amount, allocable to the benefiting activity, consistently treated, and adequately documented.

Time and Effort

LEAs maintain records supporting salaries and wages charged to federal awards, particularly when employees work on multiple programs or cost objectives.

Procurement

Purchases follow federal Uniform Guidance and state and local procurement rules, including competition, conflict-of-interest, contract oversight, and documentation.

Equipment and Property

Equipment purchased with Title I funds is inventoried, safeguarded, used for authorized purposes, and disposed of under federal property rules.

Audits and Monitoring

Title I funds are subject to state monitoring, Single Audit requirements, federal review, corrective action, questioned costs, repayment, and enforcement.

School Eligibility

NCES reported that in school year 2021–2022 approximately 63 percent of traditional public schools and 62 percent of public charter schools were Title I eligible.

Program Models

In the same year, approximately 42 percent of traditional public schools and 50 percent of charter schools operated schoolwide Title I programs. Approximately 9 percent of each operated targeted assistance programs.

Eligible but Unfunded Schools

Some eligible schools receive no Title I allocation because districts must concentrate funds, serve higher-poverty schools first, and maintain programs of sufficient size and quality.

Share of Education Spending

NCES reported that Title I expenditures represented approximately 2 percent of current public elementary and secondary education expenditures in fiscal year 2022. Title I is nationally important but remains a small share of total school funding.

Per-Pupil Variation

Federal allocations per formula child and expenditures per public-school pupil vary substantially among states and districts because of formula weights, poverty concentration, state expenditure factors, minimums, hold harmless, and local program decisions.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Civil-Rights Context

Title I was enacted one year after the Civil Rights Act and eleven years after Brown v. Board of Education. Federal education aid and federal nondiscrimination enforcement developed together during a period of widespread resistance to school desegregation.

Concentrated Poverty

African American children are disproportionately represented in high-poverty schools because of historic and continuing discrimination in housing, employment, lending, wealth accumulation, land use, municipal boundaries, and school attendance patterns.

School Finance

State and local school funding systems can reproduce inequalities associated with property wealth, tax capacity, student need, political boundaries, and state formulas. Title I adds federal support but does not replace or fully equalize those systems.

Segregation

Many Title I schools are racially and economically isolated. Additional resources can improve instruction within segregated schools while leaving the underlying segregation unchanged.

Resource Inequity

Black students in high-poverty schools can face inexperienced teachers, staff turnover, limited advanced coursework, aging facilities, larger student needs, weaker technology, and fewer enrichment opportunities. Title I can address some but not all of these conditions.

Standards and Accountability

Subgroup reporting makes racial disparities visible and prevents statewide averages from concealing low performance among Black students and other groups. Accountability systems can also narrow instruction or impose sanctions without providing sufficient resources.

Schoolwide Programs

Schoolwide authority permits comprehensive investment in schools serving predominantly Black communities, including staffing, curriculum, climate, attendance, family engagement, and student supports.

Parent Power

Federal parent-engagement requirements create formal rights to information and participation. Meaningful influence depends on language access, scheduling, trust, representation, transparency, and whether school leaders share decision-making power.

Private-School Services

Equitable services can reach eligible Black children attending nonprofit private schools, but the public agency retains fiscal control and must ensure that services do not undermine civil-rights obligations.

Equity Standard

Racial-equity evaluation should examine whether Title I improves access to effective educators, rigorous coursework, early learning, safe facilities, student support, family influence, and measurable opportunity—not only whether federal funds were spent.

Benefits and Documented Outcomes

Concentrated Federal Support

Title I directs substantial federal funds toward school districts and schools serving greater concentrations of children in poverty.

Instructional Capacity

The program finances teachers, interventionists, specialists, coaches, tutoring, curriculum, technology, professional development, and additional learning time that many local systems could not otherwise provide at the same level.

Whole-School Improvement

Schoolwide programs allow schools to align federal resources with a comprehensive strategy rather than operate isolated pull-out services.

Transparency

Title I requires public reporting of academic results and other indicators by race, income, disability, English-learner status, and other subgroups.

Family Rights

The program establishes parent notice, participation, policy, meeting, compact, and funding requirements exceeding ordinary local practice in many districts.

Preschool and Transition

Title I can extend federal support into early childhood and help children transition into kindergarten and elementary school.

School Improvement

The 7-percent state reservation provides a continuing funding stream for evidence-based improvement in low-performing schools.

Evaluation Limits

Title I operates in schools facing concentrated and interconnected educational, economic, health, housing, and social challenges. Changes in test scores or graduation cannot be attributed to Title I alone without careful research design.

Scale Relative to Need

The program can materially improve local services while remaining too small to offset the full effects of poverty or unequal state and local finance.

Program Challenges

Formula Complexity

Four formulas, state minimums, expenditure factors, weights, hold harmless, district boundaries, and annual data changes make allocations difficult for the public to understand.

Imperfect Poverty Measures

Census estimates are essential but statistically uncertain for small districts. School meal eligibility and other local poverty measures can change because of program participation or universal-meal policies rather than underlying household conditions.

Funding Dilution

Title I reaches a large share of public schools. Broad coverage can reduce the amount available per school and limit the intensity of services.

Uneven Local Capacity

Districts differ in grant management, needs assessment, procurement, evidence use, data analysis, staffing, and program evaluation.

Staffing Shortages

Federal funds do not guarantee that qualified teachers, interventionists, counselors, and specialists are available for hire.

Program Fragmentation

Schools can operate separate federal, state, and local initiatives with different plans, budgets, staff, reporting, and eligibility rules.

Compliance Burden

Fiscal controls protect public funds but can consume administrative capacity and encourage risk-avoidant spending.

Short-Term Spending

Annual allocations and carryover limits can make long-term staffing or multiyear reform difficult, especially when district poverty counts fluctuate.

Measuring Effectiveness

Schools commonly combine Title I with state and local resources. This integration benefits students but complicates evaluation of the federal program’s independent effect.

Major Policy Debates

Concentration or Broad Coverage

One approach would concentrate funds in fewer, higher-poverty districts and schools. Another emphasizes national reach and protection of small, rural, suburban, and moderately poor communities.

Formula Reform

Critics argue that state minimums, expenditure factors, and hold-harmless provisions can weaken poverty targeting. Defenders emphasize stability, federalism, cost differences, and the needs of small states and districts.

Funding Level

Advocates argue that Congress funds Title I below the amount authorized and below the scale needed to equalize opportunity. Fiscal critics question federal spending growth, program effectiveness, and national control.

Federal Accountability or State Control

No Child Left Behind emphasized federal testing and consequences. ESSA shifted greater design authority to states. Debate continues over whether state flexibility protects innovation or permits weak accountability.

Schoolwide Flexibility or Targeted Protection

Schoolwide programs allow integrated reform and reduce administrative separation. Targeted approaches make it easier to identify exactly which students and services receive federal support.

Testing

Annual assessments provide comparable subgroup information and accountability. Critics argue that testing can narrow curriculum, consume time, distort incentives, and inadequately measure learning.

Public School Choice

Federal policy has alternated between improvement within neighborhood schools and options allowing students to transfer from low-performing schools. ESSA gives states greater discretion than No Child Left Behind.

Equitable Services

Supporters describe private-school services as aid to eligible children. Critics raise concerns about public accountability, religious-school participation, administrative cost, and diversion of public capacity.

Universal Poverty Measures

Expansion of Community Eligibility in school meals has reduced the availability of individual free and reduced-price lunch applications. Districts must use approved alternative poverty measures for Title I allocation and reporting.

Key Distinctions

Title I and Title I, Part A

Title I is the broader statutory title. Part A is the large formula-grant program ordinarily meant when educators say “Title I.”

Title I Eligibility and Title I Participation

A school can be eligible because of poverty but receive no allocation. A funded school can operate either a schoolwide or targeted assistance program.

Poverty and Student Selection

Poverty determines district and school funding concentration. Educational need determines student selection in a targeted assistance program.

Schoolwide and Targeted Assistance

A schoolwide program improves the entire school. A targeted assistance program directs Title I services to selected students.

Title I and IDEA

Title I addresses educational disadvantage and school poverty. IDEA establishes special education rights and services for eligible children with disabilities.

Title I and Title III

Title I supports academic improvement in high-poverty schools. Title III specifically supports English language acquisition and immigrant students.

Title I, Part A and Part C

Part A is the high-poverty school formula program. Part C is the Migrant Education Program serving migratory children.

Title I, Part A and Part D

Part A supports local educational agencies and schools. Part D supports children and youths who are neglected, delinquent, or at risk in institutional and community settings.

Title I and School Lunch Eligibility

School meal data are one permitted local measure of school poverty. Title I is an education program and does not determine meal eligibility.

Federal Allocation and School Allocation

Federal formulas allocate funds to districts through states. LEAs use separate statutory rules to rank and fund schools.

Supplemental Funding and General School Aid

Title I adds targeted federal resources. State and local systems remain responsible for the ordinary cost of public education.

Geographic Scope

Title I operates throughout the United States. State awards cover the fifty states, District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. Separate statutory provisions support American Samoa, Guam, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, the United States Virgin Islands, and Bureau of Indian Education schools.

The formulas serve large urban districts, suburban systems, towns, rural districts, charter-school LEAs, county systems, and geographically isolated communities.

Large cities often receive high total allocations because they contain large numbers and percentages of children in poverty. Remote rural districts can receive high allocations per formula child because of small-state minimums, expenditure factors, district size, and formula design.

Within districts, Title I resources are concentrated in schools with the highest poverty rates. Neighborhood segregation, district boundaries, school choice, magnet programs, charter enrollment, and attendance policies influence which children and schools are included.

Geographic distribution changes annually as Census poverty estimates, student populations, district boundaries, and state finance measures change.

Summary

The Title I Education Program—formally Title I, Part A of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act—is the principal federal formula grant supporting districts and schools serving concentrations of children from low-income families.

Congress created Title I in 1965 as part of the Great Society, War on Poverty, and expanding federal civil-rights role. The program sought to direct national resources toward children whose educational opportunities were limited by poverty and unequal school systems.

Funding flows from Congress through the Department of Education, state educational agencies, local educational agencies, and eligible schools. Four formulas—Basic, Concentration, Targeted, and Education Finance Incentive Grants—use Census poverty estimates and other statutory factors to distribute funds.

Local districts rank schools by poverty, serve the highest-poverty schools first, make required reservations, and allocate funds sufficient to operate meaningful programs. Poverty determines funding concentration; educational need determines individual participation in targeted assistance schools.

Schoolwide programs can improve the entire educational program for all students, particularly those who are lowest achieving. Targeted assistance programs provide supplemental services to selected students at greatest risk of failing to meet state standards.

Title I can support instruction, tutoring, intervention, extended learning, preschool, technology, professional development, counseling, attendance, school climate, family engagement, and other evidence-based activities. Programs must align with identified need and comply with federal cost and fiscal rules.

The program is integrated with ESSA accountability and school improvement. States identify low-performing schools and reserve Title I funds for comprehensive and targeted improvement strategies.

Fiscal safeguards require Title I to supplement state and local resources. Comparability, maintenance of effort, carryover, procurement, documentation, audit, and monitoring protect the federal investment.

Parent and family engagement is a statutory program component. Families have rights to information, participation, school policies, annual meetings, compacts, and input concerning reserved funds.

Title I has major racial-equity significance because African American and other historically underserved students remain disproportionately concentrated in high-poverty and segregated schools. Federal funds can improve services but cannot by themselves equalize school finance, reverse residential segregation, or eliminate the effects of poverty.

The fiscal year 2025 appropriation was approximately $18.41 billion. Despite its size among federal education programs, Title I represents only a small share of total elementary and secondary education spending.

The program’s continuing effectiveness depends on adequate funding, accurate poverty data, strong state and local capacity, meaningful concentration of resources, evidence-based instruction, transparent fiscal administration, civil-rights enforcement, family participation, and reform of the larger state and local systems that determine educational opportunity.
