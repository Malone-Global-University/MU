United States Housing Authority Program

The New Deal federal–local public-housing system created in 1937 to finance slum clearance and decent, safe, sanitary low-rent dwellings through local public housing authorities.

Terminology Note

The United States Housing Authority, commonly abbreviated USHA, was the federal body created by the United States Housing Act of 1937. It should not be confused with a local housing authority, which was a state- or locally created public body that developed, owned, and managed housing within a specific jurisdiction.

The program was commonly associated with the Wagner–Steagall Act, named for Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York and Representative Henry B. Steagall of Alabama. The statute’s formal title was the United States Housing Act of 1937.

The term low-rent housing was the original statutory language. The law defined it as decent, safe, and sanitary housing within the financial reach of low-income families. The modern statutory term is generally public housing.

The USHA existed under that name from 1937 until February 24, 1942, when it entered the National Housing Agency and was replaced by the Federal Public Housing Authority. Its federal–local financing model continued through successor institutions and the repeatedly amended United States Housing Act of 1937.

Historical documents use period racial and social terminology. This fact sheet uses modern language except where an official title, archival term, or direct description of historical policy requires otherwise.

Overview

The United States Housing Authority program was created to replace temporary federal experimentation with a permanent federal–local system of low-rent public housing.

Before 1937, the Public Works Administration had financed or directly developed a limited number of demonstration housing projects. Constitutional, administrative, political, and land-acquisition problems made direct federal construction difficult.

The Wagner–Steagall system decentralised ownership and daily management. States authorised local public housing authorities. Those local bodies proposed projects, acquired sites, cleared structures, issued obligations, selected tenants, collected rents, and maintained the developments.

The federal USHA supplied long-term loans, annual subsidies, technical review, design and cost controls, project agreements, bond support, research, and supervision.

The financing system was designed so that tenant rents could cover operating and maintenance costs and part of project debt, while federal annual contributions reduced the remaining cost enough to preserve low rents.

Public housing was tied closely to slum clearance. New units generally required the elimination of a comparable number of unsafe or insanitary dwellings in the same locality or metropolitan area.

The program produced modern dwellings with indoor plumbing, electricity, light, ventilation, open space, playgrounds, and planned community facilities. It also displaced residents, reinforced racial segregation, and often failed to rehouse the poorest families removed from clearance areas.

Classification and Scope

This page treats the United States Housing Authority as a government program because the primary subject is the operating system it administered: federal loans, annual contributions, local authority formation, project approval, slum clearance, cost limits, tenant eligibility, rents, labour standards, project transfer, racial policy, and defence conversion.

The United States Housing Act of 1937 belongs primarily under Laws and Legislation. The USHA itself may also be cross-referenced under Institutions.

The broader Federal Public Housing Program includes the PWA Housing Division, USHA, Federal Public Housing Authority, Public Housing Administration, HUD, and later capital and operating systems. This page focuses on the USHA phase from 1937 through 1942.

Individual developments belong under Geography, Institutions, or Historical Events where their local history warrants a separate page.

The USHA should not be confused with the Federal Housing Administration. FHA insured privately originated mortgages; USHA financed publicly owned low-rent projects through local housing agencies.

Historical Context

Industrialisation, immigration, internal migration, racial segregation, low wages, weak building regulation, and rapid urban growth had produced severe housing problems before the Great Depression.

Families lived in overcrowded tenements, converted houses, alleys, shacks, basements, and dwellings lacking adequate sanitation, light, ventilation, water, heating, or structural safety.

Private builders generally did not construct new housing at rents affordable to the poorest families because land, materials, finance, taxes, and operating costs exceeded those households’ rent-paying capacity.

The Great Depression intensified the crisis. Unemployment reduced rent payments and household formation, while residential construction collapsed and buildings deteriorated.

Housing reformers argued that slum conditions imposed public costs through disease, fire, crime, delinquency, educational disruption, and municipal services.

Property owners and real-estate organisations often opposed public construction as unfair competition. Congressional compromise therefore limited the program to low-income families and linked new construction to removal of substandard housing.

Public Works Administration Housing Predecessor

The National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 authorised the Public Works Administration to support low-cost housing and slum clearance.

Early PWA policy attempted loans to limited-dividend corporations and local housing bodies. Where those arrangements proved inadequate, the federal government directly acquired land and built projects.

Demonstration developments included Techwood Homes and University Homes in Atlanta, First Houses and Harlem River Houses in New York, Langston Terrace in Washington, D.C., and other projects.

The PWA projects established important precedents in architecture, construction, tenant selection, management, racial policy, labour standards, and community facilities.

Direct federal ownership was expensive and politically difficult. The 1937 act instead made local public housing authorities the principal long-term owners and operators.

Executive Order 7732 of October 27, 1937, initiated transfer of federal housing projects, records, personnel, funds, and related property to the USHA. The remaining PWA Housing Division responsibilities were completed or absorbed during the 1939 reorganisation.

Legislative Development

Senator Robert F. Wagner, Representative Henry B. Steagall, housing reformers, organised labour, municipal officials, architects, social workers, and public-housing advocates supported permanent legislation.

Catherine Bauer’s research and advocacy helped translate European and American housing experience into a federal–local financing proposal.

Opposition centred on cost, federal power, competition with private real estate, local control, race, tenant selection, and the possibility that public housing would become a permanent entitlement.

Compromise preserved a narrow target population, imposed low construction-cost ceilings, required local participation, and tied construction to slum elimination.

Congress passed the act in August 1937, and Roosevelt signed it on September 1.

Declaration of Policy

The original act declared that federal funds and credit should assist states and political subdivisions in:

  • alleviating present and recurring unemployment;
  • remedying unsafe and insanitary housing conditions;
  • addressing the shortage of decent, safe, and sanitary dwellings;
  • serving low-income families in rural and urban communities;
  • protecting national health, safety, and morals;
  • clearing slums;
  • stimulating construction and business activity; and
  • establishing a continuing federal housing institution.

The declaration treated inadequate housing as a national welfare issue while assigning implementation chiefly to state and local public bodies.

Federal Institutional Structure

The act created the USHA within the Department of the Interior under Secretary Harold L. Ickes’s general supervision.

A single administrator held the authority’s statutory powers rather than a multi-member board.

Nathan Straus Jr. was appointed administrator on November 1, 1937. The first interim report stated that the agency organised its principal divisions immediately and drew personnel and records from the PWA housing program.

Federal units addressed project planning, architecture, engineering, finance, law, management, tenant policy, research, statistics, information, race relations, labour, and field administration.

Loans, contributions, grants, and major contracts required presidential approval under the original act.

The General Accounting Office audited the authority’s financial transactions.

Local Public Housing Authorities

A community could participate only where state law authorised a public body to develop or administer low-rent housing or slum clearance.

State enabling laws established the powers, territory, board appointments, borrowing authority, tax status, eminent-domain authority, and operating duties of local housing authorities.

Local boards were commonly appointed by mayors, governors, or other officials, depending on state law.

Local agencies generally:

  • documented local housing need;
  • selected and acquired sites;
  • negotiated with municipal departments;
  • issued local bonds or notes;
  • prepared project applications;
  • commissioned architects and engineers;
  • cleared unsafe structures;
  • contracted for construction;
  • selected tenants and established waiting lists;
  • set rent schedules within federal requirements;
  • operated and maintained developments;
  • enforced leases and occupancy rules; and
  • submitted financial, statistical, and management reports.

Local ownership made the program politically acceptable to many members of Congress but allowed local segregation, exclusion, site politics, and unequal management to shape federal benefits.

Project Application and Development Process

  1. Create or designate a public housing agency. State and local law had to authorise the participating body.
  2. Document housing need. Surveys identified slum conditions, overcrowding, low-income households, rents, vacancies, and available sites.
  3. Select a project area. The local authority evaluated acquisition cost, transportation, schools, employment, utilities, race, land use, and clearance requirements.
  4. Prepare a preliminary application. The proposal included unit count, tenant group, rent plan, cost, local contribution, slum elimination, and legal authority.
  5. Obtain federal review. USHA staff examined need, feasibility, design, cost, financing, labour standards, and low-rent character.
  6. Negotiate federal contracts. Loan and annual-contribution agreements established project obligations.
  7. Acquire land and relocate occupants. Purchase, condemnation, title work, demolition, and relocation proceeded under local law and federal agreements.
  8. Complete plans and construction. Architects, engineers, contractors, labourers, inspectors, and public agencies developed the project.
  9. Select tenants. The local authority verified family composition, income, need, race under local segregation practices, and ability to pay the scheduled rent.
  10. Operate the project. Rents and subsidies financed debt, management, maintenance, utilities, reserves, and local payments.

Federal Development Loans

Section 9 authorised USHA loans to public housing agencies for development, acquisition, or administration of low-rent and slum-clearance projects.

The federal loan could cover no more than 90 percent of development or acquisition cost.

Local authorities therefore had to provide or borrow the remaining share, commonly through local obligations purchased by banks, pension funds, insurance companies, or other investors.

Loans carried interest of at least the going federal rate plus one-half of one percentage point.

Repayment could extend for no more than sixty years.

The long term and federal rate allowed projects to carry lower debt service than ordinary private financing.

USHA retained contractual remedies if a project lost its low-rent character or passed to an unauthorised third party.

Annual Contributions

A low-rent project could not ordinarily pay its full development debt, operating expense, maintenance, reserves, and local charges solely from rents affordable to the target population.

Section 10 therefore authorised fixed annual federal contributions under long-term contracts.

The contribution was limited to the amount and period considered necessary to create and preserve low rents.

The original maximum annual amount for a project was tied to the project cost and the going federal interest rate plus one percentage point.

Contributions were applied first toward interest or principal owed on a USHA loan.

Contracts could last as long as sixty years, with reexamination after ten years and at five-year intervals where the agreement exceeded twenty years.

The United States pledged its faith to payment of annual contributions contracted under the statute.

Local Contributions and Tax Exemption

A state or local government had to contribute at least 20 percent of the federal annual contribution.

The contribution could take the form of cash, general or special tax remission, or tax exemption.

Public-housing property was commonly exempt from ordinary local real-estate taxation.

Local authorities could make payments in lieu of taxes for municipal services under contracts or state law.

Tax exemption reduced project operating costs but also limited local revenue. Municipalities therefore debated the value of clearance, improved health, federal construction, employment, and housing against foregone taxes and service costs.

Capital-Grant Alternative

Section 11 authorised capital grants as an alternative where a public housing agency demonstrated that an upfront subsidy would better achieve and maintain low rents.

The original federal capital grant could not exceed 25 percent of development or acquisition cost.

The local agency also had to receive a contribution from state, local, or other sources equal to at least 20 percent of cost through cash, land, services, facilities, or tax relief.

The president could allocate additional unemployment-relief funds for project labour, limited to 15 percent of development cost.

Most of the lasting public-housing model came to rely on long-term annual contributions rather than a single capital grant.

Federal Obligations and Financing Authority

The USHA issued notes, bonds, or other obligations to obtain funds for project loans.

The original act authorised $100 million immediately, an additional $200 million after July 1, 1938, and another $200 million after July 1, 1939.

The obligations could mature over periods not exceeding sixty years and carry interest not exceeding 4 percent.

Principal and interest were fully and unconditionally guaranteed by the United States.

The Treasury could purchase, market, hold, and sell the obligations as public-debt transactions.

Amendments in 1938 expanded available loan resources to approximately $800 million and accelerated the first nationwide construction program.

Equivalent Elimination and Slum Clearance

Congress attempted to prevent public housing from adding subsidised units that competed with adequate private rental housing.

A locality receiving annual contributions or capital grants for new dwellings generally had to eliminate unsafe or insanitary housing in approximately the same number as the new units.

Elimination could occur through:

  • demolition;
  • condemnation;
  • effective closing;
  • compulsory repair;
  • compulsory improvement; or
  • an approved combination of these actions.

Removal could occur outside the exact project site but within the same locality or metropolitan area.

USHA could defer elimination where housing shortage was so acute that immediate clearance would create dangerous overcrowding.

Equivalent elimination was a housing-supply rule, not a personal rehousing guarantee. The family displaced from a demolished dwelling did not automatically receive the new unit.

Displacement and Relocation

Local authorities acquired occupied tenements, houses, businesses, churches, and other property for some projects.

Residents received notice and varying degrees of relocation assistance under local practice. Federal relocation protections were much less developed than those adopted in later decades.

Owners could receive compensation for condemned property, but tenants did not own the real estate and could lose housing, neighbourhood networks, nearby employment, schools, churches, and community institutions without equivalent compensation.

New projects contained modern dwellings but often fewer people than the dense areas they replaced because overcrowding was reduced and open space increased.

Some former residents failed income, family-size, rent-paying, race, or tenant-selection standards.

Many of the poorest slum residents therefore moved into other low-cost private housing rather than the new development.

Tenant Eligibility

The act reserved low-rent housing for families in the lowest income group who could not afford rents sufficient to cause private enterprise to produce adequate housing for them.

At admission, net annual family income generally could not exceed five times annual rent including the value or cost of heat, light, water, and cooking fuel.

Families with three or more minor dependents could have income up to six times annual rent.

Local authorities verified income, family size, household composition, present housing, ability to pay, and other selection factors.

The income-to-rent rule established an upper limit but did not guarantee admission to the neediest family.

A household with almost no income could be unable to pay even the low project rent, while a steadily employed low-wage family could appear financially more suitable.

Tenant Selection

Local housing authorities developed applications, waiting lists, investigations, interviews, references, and occupancy rules under federal guidance.

Selection commonly considered:

  • income and employment;
  • family size and bedroom needs;
  • current housing condition;
  • overcrowding;
  • local residency or employment;
  • displacement by the project;
  • ability to pay the assigned rent;
  • housekeeping and tenancy references;
  • race under segregated local assignment systems;
  • citizenship or legal status under applicable policy;
  • family composition and dependency; and
  • project-specific preferences.

The selection process could exclude households described by administrators as unstable, undesirable, unable to pay, overcrowded beyond available unit size, or otherwise inconsistent with project policy.

The concept of the “deserving” working family often shaped admission more strongly than a strict ranking by poverty.

Rent Policy

Original USHA rents were not based on the later uniform rule requiring a fixed percentage of household income.

Local authorities established rent schedules by unit type, project cost, utilities, local income conditions, and the federal annual-contribution contract.

Rents were designed to remain within the reach of eligible low-income families while supporting:

  • management;
  • routine maintenance;
  • utilities supplied by the project;
  • repairs and replacements;
  • insurance;
  • reserves;
  • payments in lieu of taxes;
  • local debt; and
  • federal loan obligations after subsidy.

Projects could use graded rent schedules to serve several low-income groups.

Even subsidised rents could remain above the means of households with irregular work, public relief, disability, or extremely low income.

Income Reexamination and Continued Occupancy

Housing authorities periodically reviewed tenant income and family composition.

Families whose income rose beyond project limits could face rent adjustment, transfer, or termination so that units remained reserved for low-income households.

Turnover policy created tension. Removing upwardly mobile households preserved income targeting but could weaken community stability and punish economic progress.

During wartime housing shortages, federal authorities sometimes suspended or modified ordinary income-based removal because vacancies would interfere with defence labour needs.

Later federal law repeatedly revised income limits, rent formulas, preferences, and continued-occupancy rules.

Project Design and Planning

USHA projects were expected to be economical, durable, sanitary, and free of elaborate or expensive materials.

Common designs used low-rise brick rowhouses or garden apartments arranged around open courts, lawns, pedestrian walks, and playgrounds.

Planning addressed:

  • sunlight and ventilation;
  • indoor plumbing and sanitation;
  • electricity and heating;
  • family-size unit layouts;
  • fire safety;
  • open space and play areas;
  • laundry and storage;
  • community rooms and management offices;
  • schools and child facilities;
  • streets, utilities, drainage, and transportation;
  • maintenance efficiency; and
  • construction cost limits.

Superblock layouts separated pedestrians from through traffic and removed commercial or industrial uses from residential interiors.

The same planning could isolate residents from existing street networks, stores, neighbourhood institutions, and city life.

Cost Controls

Congress imposed strict cost ceilings to limit federal subsidy and protect private housing from competition.

Projects generally could not exceed $4,000 per family dwelling or $1,000 per room, excluding land, demolition, and nondwelling facilities.

In cities with more than 500,000 residents, the authority could approve up to $5,000 per dwelling or $1,250 per room where labour and material costs justified the difference.

Average dwelling construction cost also could not exceed comparable private construction under the same legal building and labour requirements.

Cost limits encouraged standard plans, repetitive units, simple finishes, efficient site work, and competitive bidding.

They also limited architectural variety, unit size, materials, amenities, and the ability to adapt to unusually expensive sites.

Labor Standards and Construction Employment

Public-housing construction was intended to reduce unemployment and stimulate building trades and material production.

Contracts required prevailing local wages or fees for architects, technical engineers, draftsmen, technicians, labourers, and mechanics.

Federal wage, hour, performance-bond, payment-bond, employee-compensation, and contractor-reporting rules applied through the statute and incorporated laws.

Contractors reported payroll employment, wages, labour hours, subcontractors, and material expenditures.

Construction created jobs in demolition, excavation, masonry, carpentry, plumbing, electrical work, roofing, painting, landscaping, engineering, architecture, inspection, clerical work, and materials supply.

Union exclusion and discriminatory craft practices limited Black workers’ access to skilled and supervisory positions.

African American Housing Benefits

Black urban families faced overcrowding, discriminatory rents, poor housing, restrictive covenants, segregated neighbourhoods, mortgage exclusion, and limited municipal investment.

USHA-financed projects provided some Black families with modern plumbing, electricity, heating, open space, playgrounds, management, and lower rents than comparable standard private housing.

Developments built for African American occupancy included projects such as Richard Allen Homes in Philadelphia, Ida B. Wells Homes in Chicago, and other locally designated Black projects.

Black architects, professionals, managers, and workers also gained limited federal and local housing employment.

Robert C. Weaver served as a race adviser and special assistant in the Interior and USHA system, pressing for fair participation in federally funded jobs and benefits.

These gains were meaningful but operated within segregated policy rather than an integrated right to housing throughout the city.

Racial Segregation

The original statute did not require racial integration.

Local authorities commonly designated developments as white, Black, Mexican American, or otherwise segregated according to local racial systems.

The USHA generally observed local racial customs and did not use its financial power to impose national integration.

Federal policy often sought to avoid changing the existing racial composition of a neighbourhood. This approach protected segregation while presenting it as local stability.

Black projects could receive fewer units, inferior sites, delayed approval, lower local political support, or unequal access to services.

White authorities sometimes used public housing to remove Black residents from valuable central areas and relocate them into separate developments.

Housing segregation shaped schools, parks, transportation, employment access, policing, municipal services, and long-term neighbourhood investment.

African American Construction Employment

Federal and local officials collected reports concerning Black participation in public-housing construction.

Local housing boards were asked to document employment and efforts to allocate work in relation to the African American population of the locality.

Nondiscrimination language alone did not overcome exclusion by unions, apprenticeship systems, contractors, and local political networks.

Black labourers obtained many unskilled and semiskilled jobs but remained underrepresented in licensed trades, skilled classifications, foreman positions, architecture, engineering, and contracting.

Robert Weaver and other Black New Deal officials argued for measurable standards rather than general assurances of fairness.

Latino, Immigrant, and Other Communities

Public housing operated within local racial and ethnic hierarchies extending beyond the Black–white division.

Alazán–Apache Courts in San Antonio was among the early USHA-financed projects built for Mexican American residents within a segregated city system.

Immigrant and ethnic neighbourhoods were also affected by clearance, site selection, tenant screening, and the preference for simplified residential land use.

Local authorities could classify communities according to prevailing racial, national-origin, and social categories.

Federal financing modernised selected housing without guaranteeing equal access across racial and ethnic lines.

Women, Children, and Family Policy

Women managed household budgets, applied for housing, maintained dwellings, organised tenant life, and raised children within developments.

Widows, separated women, and women supporting children could qualify as family heads, depending on local policy and income.

Projects reduced some burdens through indoor water, sanitation, electricity, safer play space, laundry facilities, and improved heating.

Tenant selection could favour conventional married-family structures and scrutinise women’s relationships, housekeeping, employment, and perceived morality.

Clearance disrupted women’s nearby childcare, kin networks, neighbourhood commerce, churches, schools, and informal support.

Management rules governing children, visitors, household composition, domestic activity, and use of public space could be intrusive.

Project Management and Maintenance

Local authorities employed managers, rent collectors, maintenance workers, clerks, security personnel, social-service staff, and grounds employees.

Management enforced leases, collected rent, certified income, assigned units, inspected dwellings, maintained grounds, repaired systems, and administered waiting lists.

Preventive maintenance was essential because projects had to preserve housing quality over long debt and contribution periods.

Tenant organisations, councils, clubs, recreation, and community programs developed in many projects, although management often retained substantial control.

Low rents limited operating revenue, making federal subsidy, local support, competent management, and adequate reserves central to long-term quality.

Relationship to Private Housing

The statute defined the target group as families unable to pay rents high enough to cause private enterprise to build an adequate supply for them.

Cost ceilings, income limits, equivalent elimination, and low-rent covenants were designed to prevent direct competition with ordinary private housing.

Real-estate and landlord groups still opposed the program, arguing that subsidised public projects reduced private rents, removed taxable property, and expanded government ownership.

Supporters argued that the private market had failed to house the lowest-income population at decent standards and that slum conditions imposed public costs.

The program remained much smaller than the private rental market and never approached universal eligibility.

Program Scale and Production

The original act made $500 million available through staged federally guaranteed obligations. The 1938 amendments expanded loan authority to approximately $800 million.

Local housing agencies across the country organised, submitted applications, and entered federal contracts.

Historic American Buildings Survey documentation credits the USHA with sponsoring approximately 130,000 new dwelling units in roughly 300 public-housing projects between 1937 and 1941.

Other historical counts may differ because they include or exclude transferred PWA projects, defence housing, projects under construction, permanent units, temporary units, acquisitions, or later successor-agency completions.

The program’s scale was nationally significant but small relative to the number of households living in substandard housing.

United States Housing Act Amendments of 1938

Congress amended the new program in 1938 before most projects were complete.

The amendments expanded loan authority, refined financing, and increased the resources available for local projects.

By 1939, federal publications described approximately $800 million as available for loans to public housing agencies.

The enlarged authority accelerated project commitments while preserving annual-contribution limits, local participation, cost controls, and low-rent requirements.

Transfer to the Federal Works Agency

Reorganization Plan No. I of 1939 created the Federal Works Agency and transferred the USHA from the Department of the Interior effective July 1, 1939.

The transfer grouped public housing with public buildings, roads, public works, and the Works Progress Administration.

Functions of the remaining PWA Housing Division were absorbed into the USHA structure.

Nathan Straus continued as administrator during most of the USHA period.

Federal Works Administrator John M. Carmody exercised general supervisory authority over the relocated agency.

Shift toward National Defense Housing

European war and American defence mobilisation created acute housing shortages around shipyards, aircraft plants, arsenals, military bases, and industrial centres.

Housing agencies redirected staff, design experience, local authorities, land powers, construction methods, and management capacity toward defence housing.

The Lanham Act of 1940 created separate emergency authority for defence housing and community facilities.

Some local housing authorities constructed or managed projects serving defence workers, while federal agencies also developed temporary and permanent units directly.

The ordinary slum-clearance and low-rent program slowed as labour, materials, and federal priorities shifted toward war production.

Defence housing was frequently segregated, and Black workers faced unequal unit supply, location, quality, and access.

Replacement by the Federal Public Housing Authority

Executive Order 9070 of February 24, 1942, consolidated federal housing institutions into the National Housing Agency.

The USHA became part of the new Federal Public Housing Authority.

The FPHA also absorbed public and defence housing functions from the Federal Works Agency, War and Navy Departments, Farm Security Administration, and other agencies.

The reorganisation ended the USHA as a separately named federal institution but preserved its local-authority contracts, projects, loans, annual contributions, and statutory foundation.

In 1947, the Public Housing Administration succeeded the FPHA within the Housing and Home Finance Agency.

HUD assumed federal public-housing functions in 1965.

Program Achievements

  • It established the first permanent nationwide federal framework for locally owned low-rent public housing.
  • It replaced direct federal project development with a federal–local institutional partnership.
  • It enabled local authorities to borrow at federally supported long-term rates.
  • It created the annual-contributions system for maintaining low rents.
  • It financed approximately 130,000 new units in about 300 projects between 1937 and 1941 under commonly cited historical counts.
  • It replaced many unsafe dwellings with housing containing plumbing, electricity, ventilation, open space, and modern sanitation.
  • It created construction employment during the Great Depression.
  • It established federal labour, wage, cost, design, accounting, and management requirements.
  • It expanded municipal capacity for housing surveys, land acquisition, project planning, tenant selection, and property management.
  • It created housing for some African American, Mexican American, immigrant, and low-income white families excluded from adequate private housing.
  • It transferred PWA demonstration projects into a continuing public-housing system.
  • It supplied institutional experience used during World War II defence housing.
  • It created the statutory and contractual foundation of later public housing, operating subsidies, capital funds, and rental assistance.

Limitations and Failures

  • The program served only a small fraction of households living in substandard or unaffordable housing.
  • Equivalent elimination reduced the net addition of low-cost dwellings.
  • Slum clearance displaced residents before or without guaranteed replacement housing.
  • New projects often housed fewer people than the dense neighbourhoods they replaced.
  • The poorest families could not always afford even subsidised rents.
  • Tenant screening favoured households considered stable and able to pay.
  • Local control enabled segregation, exclusion, political patronage, and unequal site selection.
  • The USHA generally accommodated Jim Crow and racially separate housing.
  • Black projects and workers frequently received unequal treatment.
  • Strict cost limits could produce small units, standardised designs, limited amenities, and construction compromises.
  • Tax exemption created conflict with local governments and public services.
  • Public-housing sites could be isolated from commerce, jobs, transit, and existing street networks.
  • The program did not directly solve low wages, unemployment, land inequality, mortgage discrimination, or the shortage of affordable private rentals.
  • World War II interrupted the ordinary low-rent and slum-clearance program.

Major Controversies

Public versus Private Housing

Landlords, builders, and real-estate interests argued that subsidised public construction competed with private enterprise. Supporters argued that the target families could not generate rents sufficient for private construction.

Federal versus Local Control

Federal funds and standards shaped projects, while local authorities controlled sites, race, tenants, management, and municipal relationships.

Slum Clearance

Clearance removed dangerous housing but also destroyed communities and displaced residents who did not necessarily enter the replacement project.

Racial Segregation

Federal financing expanded housing benefits while preserving or strengthening local racial separation.

Eligibility

The program was intended for low-income families but often excluded the very poorest because rents and management required stable payment.

Design and Cost

Reformers sought healthy planned communities, while Congress demanded austere construction that would not appear more desirable than private housing.

Tax Exemption

Local governments debated whether project benefits justified reduced ordinary property-tax revenue.

Tenant Governance

Public ownership created improved housing but also exposed residents to administrative scrutiny and rules not imposed on many private tenants.

Documented Outcomes

The program created durable local housing authorities across the country.

Projects provided modern housing to tens of thousands of low-income families and established a national public-housing profession.

Long-term federal loans and annual contributions made rents possible below the amount required for ordinary private new construction.

Slum-clearance requirements removed substantial substandard housing but often shifted displaced families into other low-rent neighbourhoods.

Public-housing construction supported building trades and material industries during recovery.

Racial segregation distributed new housing by local political decisions rather than equal metropolitan need.

The programme’s small scale prevented it from transforming the national low-income housing market.

Its financial contracts and local institutions nevertheless survived every subsequent federal reorganisation.

Historical Legacy

The USHA established the federal–local structure that still defines American public housing.

Local public housing agencies own or administer projects and rental assistance, while the federal government supplies capital, operating subsidy, regulation, oversight, and contractual authority.

The 1937 act became the statutory foundation for later public housing, Section 8, Housing Choice Vouchers, operating funds, capital funds, resident programs, demolition, redevelopment, and mixed-finance policy.

The annual-contributions contract became an enduring method for committing future federal subsidy to local housing.

The history also demonstrates how decentralised administration can allow federal programs to reproduce local racial and political inequality.

Later housing policy repeatedly confronted problems visible in the USHA era: insufficient supply, tenant displacement, segregation, concentrated poverty, maintenance, local opposition, project isolation, and unstable federal appropriations.

Genealogical and Local-History Value

Tenant files may identify applicants, spouses, children, relatives, addresses, employers, occupations, income, former housing, rent, admission, transfer, and departure.

Clearance and acquisition files may identify owners, tenants, businesses, churches, property descriptions, compensation, demolition, and relocation.

Construction records may identify contractors, architects, engineers, unions, workers, wages, and minority employment.

Project photographs, maps, and plans document neighbourhoods before clearance and the developments that replaced them.

Local housing-authority minutes, newspapers, city council files, deeds, condemnation cases, school records, churches, and oral histories can supplement federal records.

Privacy restrictions, archival sampling, local retention, and incomplete transfer may limit access to individual tenant records.

Research Cautions

  • USHA and a local housing authority were different institutions. The federal authority financed and supervised; local bodies ordinarily owned and operated.
  • USHA and FHA were different. USHA financed public housing; FHA insured private mortgages.
  • USHA and the PWA Housing Division were related but not identical. PWA began direct federal demonstration projects; USHA established the local-authority system and absorbed transferred projects.
  • Federal financing did not mean federal day-to-day management. Tenant selection, rent collection, maintenance, and local policy were usually administered locally.
  • Equivalent elimination did not guarantee equivalent rehousing. Demolished units and new units could involve different families, locations, rents, sizes, and eligibility rules.
  • Income limits were not the modern area-median-income system. The original act used a relationship between net income and project rent.
  • Original rents were not uniformly 30 percent of income. Modern income-based formulas developed later.
  • Unit counts vary by scope. Sources may include authorised, contracted, started, completed, transferred, permanent, temporary, defence, or successor-agency units.
  • Public housing was generally segregated. A federal project for Black residents was not evidence of integrated or equal access.
  • Modern statutory language should not be projected backward. The United States Housing Act has been extensively amended since 1937.
  • Agency names changed. USHA became part of the FPHA in 1942, PHA in 1947, and HUD in 1965.

Definitions

Annual contribution
A recurring federal subsidy paid under contract to help a public housing agency achieve and maintain low rents.
Capital grant
An upfront federal contribution toward development or acquisition cost used as an alternative to annual contributions under the original act.
Development cost
Approved expenses required for planning, financing, land acquisition, demolition, construction, equipment, and physical completion of a project.
Equivalent elimination
The requirement that a locality eliminate unsafe or insanitary dwellings in a number approximately equal to newly constructed public-housing units.
Federal project
A low-rent housing or slum-clearance project owned or administered directly by USHA, principally through transfer from an earlier federal program.
Going federal rate of interest
The rate stated in the most recently issued long-term federal obligations of ten years or more, used as a benchmark for USHA financing.
Low-income family
Under the original act, a family in the lowest income group unable to pay enough rent to induce adequate private construction in the locality.
Low-rent housing
Decent, safe, sanitary dwellings within the financial reach of low-income families and developed for economy and stable operation.
Payment in lieu of taxes
A negotiated project payment to local government in place of ordinary property taxation.
Public housing agency
A state, county, municipality, governmental entity, or public body authorised to develop or administer low-rent housing or slum clearance.
Slum
Under the original law, an area dominated by dwellings whose deterioration, overcrowding, design, or lack of sanitation, light, or ventilation harmed safety, health, or morals.
Slum clearance
Demolition and removal of buildings from a designated slum area.
United States Housing Authority
The federal body created in 1937 to finance and supervise low-rent housing and slum clearance.
Wagner–Steagall Act
Common name for the United States Housing Act of 1937.

Geographic Scope

The act defined states broadly to include the states, District of Columbia, territories, dependencies, and possessions.

Large Industrial Cities
Major slum-clearance and low-rent developments, high land cost, dense displacement, strong labour demand, and intense racial and real-estate politics.
Southern Cities
Segregated white and Black projects, low wages, high Black housing need, union exclusion, and strong local control.
Northern and Midwestern Cities
Tenement and industrial housing, racial covenants, growing Black migration, immigrant neighbourhoods, and public–private conflict.
Western Cities
Rapid growth, Mexican American and Asian American segregation, land availability, and later defence housing.
Small Cities and Towns
Smaller garden-style projects, limited local administrative capacity, and close relationships between authority boards and municipal government.
Territories and Possessions
Program authority existed, but project scale and implementation depended on local law, federal approval, land, construction cost, and territorial administration.

Summary

The United States Housing Authority program was created by the Wagner–Steagall Housing Act on September 1, 1937.

It established a permanent federal–local system under which local public housing authorities developed, owned, selected tenants for, and managed low-rent housing while USHA supplied loans, annual contributions, standards, technical assistance, and oversight.

Federal loans could cover up to 90 percent of project development or acquisition cost, carry terms as long as sixty years, and bear interest tied to the federal borrowing rate. Local agencies supplied the remaining capital share.

Long-term annual contributions covered the financial gap necessary to preserve low rents. Local governments contributed through cash, tax remission, or tax exemption.

New construction generally required equivalent elimination of unsafe or insanitary housing, but the family displaced by clearance was not guaranteed admission to the replacement project.

Eligibility was limited to low-income families, with original income ceilings tied to project rent. Tenant selection frequently favoured steadily employed households able to pay the scheduled rent, leaving some of the poorest slum residents unserved.

The program sponsored approximately 130,000 new units in about 300 projects between 1937 and 1941 under commonly cited historical counts.

Public housing provided modern sanitation, utilities, open space, and safer buildings but also displaced residents and operated through segregated local systems. The USHA generally accommodated local racial customs rather than imposing integration.

The authority transferred from the Interior Department to the Federal Works Agency in 1939. In 1942, it became part of the Federal Public Housing Authority as federal housing policy shifted toward wartime needs.

The USHA’s statutory, contractual, and local-authority model became the institutional foundation of later American public housing and federal rental assistance.
