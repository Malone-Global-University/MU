Child Tax Credit

The federal family tax credit that reduces income-tax liability for qualifying children and provides a partially refundable earnings-based benefit through the Additional Child Tax Credit.

What Is the Child Tax Credit?

The Child Tax Credit is a federal income-tax credit calculated for each qualifying child.

The credit has two connected components:

Child Tax Credit
The nonrefundable portion that reduces federal income-tax liability.
Additional Child Tax Credit
The refundable portion that can generate a payment after eligible tax liability has been reduced to zero.

A family does not automatically receive the full $2,200 per child as cash. The amount depends on modified adjusted gross income, tax liability, earned income, filing status, number of qualifying children, Social Security numbers, and the ACTC formula.

The same Schedule 8812 also calculates the separate Credit for Other Dependents.

Tax-year 2026 rules generally apply to income and family circumstances during 2026 and to federal returns filed in 2027.

Historical Context

Before 1997, federal tax policy recognised children principally through personal exemptions, filing status, dependent-care provisions, and the Earned Income Tax Credit.

Personal exemptions reduced taxable income but provided greater value to taxpayers in higher tax brackets and little benefit to families with no income-tax liability.

During the 1990s, policymakers proposed a per-child credit that would reduce tax liability more directly.

Debate centred on whether the credit should be refundable, whether it should reward work, which ages should qualify, how it should interact with EITC, and how broadly it should extend into middle-income families.

Creation in 1997

President Bill Clinton signed the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 on August 5, 1997.

The original credit was generally:

  • $400 per qualifying child for tax year 1998;
  • $500 per qualifying child beginning in 1999;
  • nonrefundable for most families;
  • limited to children under age seventeen; and
  • phased out at specified income thresholds.

Limited refundability was initially available to selected families with three or more children through coordination with payroll taxes and EITC.

The original structure placed the credit primarily within tax reduction rather than universal child allowance policy.

Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001

The 2001 tax law scheduled the credit to rise gradually to $1,000 per child.

It established broader refundability based on a percentage of earned income above a threshold.

The refundable component became known as the Additional Child Tax Credit.

Congress later accelerated and extended the $1,000 amount through additional legislation.

The shift transformed CTC from a largely nonrefundable middle-income tax reduction into a more substantial work-based income supplement.

2003 and 2004 Extensions

The Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003 accelerated the $1,000 maximum.

The Working Families Tax Relief Act of 2004 extended the larger credit and coordinated family tax definitions.

Refundability remained tied to earnings, so families with little or no earned income could still receive less than the maximum or no refundable credit.

Great Recession Changes

Congress lowered the earned-income threshold used to calculate ACTC during the financial crisis.

The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 lowered the threshold temporarily.

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 lowered it further to $3,000.

Lowering the threshold increased refundable benefits for workers with modest earnings.

The $3,000 threshold was repeatedly extended and eventually made permanent before later being reduced to $2,500 by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act.

Permanence of the $1,000 Credit

The American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 made the $1,000 maximum permanent.

The Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes Act of 2015 made the lower refundable-credit earnings threshold permanent and imposed the mid-February refund delay for ACTC and EITC returns.

By the middle of the 2010s, CTC had become a permanent but only partly refundable family credit.

Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act temporarily changed the credit for tax years 2018 through 2025.

It:

  • increased the maximum from $1,000 to $2,000 per qualifying child;
  • created a refundable maximum initially set at $1,400 and indexed for inflation;
  • lowered the earned-income threshold from $3,000 to $2,500;
  • raised phaseout thresholds to $200,000 for most filers and $400,000 for joint filers;
  • required a qualifying child to have a Social Security number valid for employment;
  • created the $500 Credit for Other Dependents; and
  • coordinated the credit with the temporary suspension of personal exemptions.

American Rescue Plan Expansion of 2021

The American Rescue Plan temporarily transformed CTC for tax year 2021.

It:

  • increased the maximum to $3,600 for children under age six;
  • increased the maximum to $3,000 for children ages six through seventeen;
  • included seventeen-year-old children;
  • made the credit fully refundable for qualifying taxpayers with a principal United States home or Puerto Rico residence;
  • removed the earned-income requirement for the expanded refundable amount;
  • created separate phaseout rules for the temporary increase; and
  • authorised advance monthly payments of up to one-half of the expected annual credit.

The changes expired after 2021.

Advance Monthly Payments in 2021

The IRS issued six monthly payments from July through December 2021.

Maximum monthly amounts were:

  • $300 for each child under age six; and
  • $250 for each child ages six through seventeen.

Treasury and the IRS used prior-year tax returns, information-return data, and an online portal to estimate eligibility.

More than thirty-six million families containing approximately sixty-one million children received more than $92 billion in advance payments.

Families reconciled advance payments with the final 2021 credit on Schedule 8812 when filing in 2022.

Advance-Payment Reconciliation

Advance payments were based on estimated rather than final tax-year information.

Changes involving income, marital status, residence, custody, birth, death, or a child moving between households could produce an overpayment or additional credit.

Congress created repayment protection for lower- and moderate-income families whose advance payments exceeded final eligibility because of changes in qualifying children.

Higher-income taxpayers generally had to repay excess advances through the tax return.

Reconciliation demonstrated both the potential and administrative challenge of paying a family credit before final annual eligibility is known.

Child Poverty in 2021

The expanded CTC was a major component of the extraordinary decline in Supplemental Poverty Measure child poverty during 2021.

Census Bureau data showed the child SPM rate falling from 9.7 percent in 2020 to 5.2 percent in 2021, a 46-percent decline.

The fully refundable structure reached children in families whose earnings and tax liability were too low to receive the full pre-2021 credit.

Poverty rose after the temporary expansion and pandemic-era assistance expired, renewing debate over refundability and monthly payments.

Return to the Pre-2021 Structure

Beginning with tax year 2022, the one-year ARPA changes expired.

The maximum returned to $2,000, the qualifying age returned to under seventeen, monthly payments ended, and full refundability ended.

ACTC again depended on earned income above $2,500 and the annual refundable cap.

The abrupt change reduced benefits most sharply for children in families with the lowest earnings.

Permanent Expansion Under Public Law 119-21

Public Law 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025, permanently extended the expanded post-2017 Child Tax Credit structure.

The law:

  • removed the scheduled expiration after 2025;
  • increased the maximum credit from $2,000 to $2,200 beginning in tax year 2025;
  • indexed the $2,200 maximum for inflation beginning after 2025;
  • continued inflation adjustment of the refundable maximum;
  • made the $200,000 and $400,000 phaseout thresholds permanent;
  • continued the $2,500 earned-income threshold;
  • continued the $500 Credit for Other Dependents;
  • required the taxpayer or at least one spouse on a joint return to have a work-authorised Social Security number; and
  • continued the child Social Security number requirement.

Nonrefundable Child Tax Credit

The nonrefundable CTC reduces eligible federal income-tax liability.

A family with enough liability can use up to the full credit after phaseout and other limitations.

When tax liability is lower than the available CTC, the unused amount does not automatically become a refund.

The taxpayer must qualify separately for ACTC and complete its earnings-based calculation.

Other nonrefundable credits can affect how much tax liability remains available for CTC, and Schedule 8812 includes credit-limit worksheets.

Additional Child Tax Credit

ACTC is the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit.

It can provide a refund when the family’s eligible CTC exceeds tax liability.

ACTC is limited by:

  • unused CTC after nonrefundable calculation;
  • the annual refundable maximum multiplied by qualifying children;
  • earned income above $2,500;
  • the 15-percent formula or alternative three-child formula;
  • Social Security number rules;
  • modified AGI phaseout;
  • taxpayer and child eligibility; and
  • the Form 2555 restriction.

Basic ACTC Formula

The standard calculation generally begins with earned income above $2,500.

The taxpayer multiplies the excess by 15 percent.

The refundable amount is generally the smallest of:

  1. the unused Child Tax Credit;
  2. 15 percent of earned income above $2,500; or
  3. $1,700 multiplied by the number of qualifying children for 2026.

A family can therefore receive less than $1,700 per child when earnings are low or when little unused CTC remains.

Illustrative ACTC Calculation

Assume a qualifying family has one child, no phaseout, and $12,500 of earned income.

Earned income above the threshold is $10,000:

$12,500 − $2,500 = $10,000.

Fifteen percent of $10,000 is $1,500.

Even though the 2026 refundable maximum is $1,700 for one child, the earnings formula would ordinarily limit ACTC to $1,500, further limited by unused CTC and other tax-return calculations.

This example is illustrative and does not calculate all return provisions.

Alternative Formula for Three or More Children

A taxpayer with three or more qualifying children can compare the basic 15-percent calculation with an alternative payroll-tax formula.

The alternative generally considers specified employee and self-employment Social Security and Medicare taxes, reduced by EITC.

The taxpayer can use the larger eligible amount, subject to unused CTC and the refundable maximum.

The formula reflects the original policy of offsetting payroll-tax burdens for larger working families.

Schedule 8812 and its worksheets should be used because the calculation can involve Additional Medicare Tax, railroad retirement tax, self-employment tax, and combat pay.

Income Phaseout

The maximum combined CTC and Credit for Other Dependents begins to decline when modified adjusted gross income exceeds:

  • $400,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly; or
  • $200,000 for all other eligible filing statuses.

The reduction is generally $50 for each $1,000, or fraction of $1,000, above the threshold.

The phaseout applies to the combined amount for qualifying children and other dependents.

Higher-income families can receive a partial credit until the phaseout eliminates the full amount.

Modified Adjusted Gross Income

Modified AGI for CTC begins with adjusted gross income.

It adds back selected income excluded under federal provisions for:

  • foreign earned income and housing;
  • Puerto Rico income; and
  • American Samoa income.

The modified calculation prevents taxpayers from avoiding the phaseout by excluding qualifying territorial or foreign income from ordinary AGI.

Qualifying-Child Tests

A child must satisfy all applicable requirements:

  1. relationship;
  2. age;
  3. residence;
  4. support;
  5. dependency;
  6. citizenship or residency;
  7. joint-return limitations;
  8. Social Security number; and
  9. competing-claim or tiebreaker rules.

The CTC qualifying-child definition overlaps with, but is not identical to, EITC, dependency, head-of-household, and Child and Dependent Care Credit rules.

Relationship Test

A qualifying child can be the taxpayer’s:

  • son or daughter;
  • stepchild;
  • adopted child or child lawfully placed for adoption;
  • eligible foster child;
  • brother or sister;
  • half-brother or half-sister;
  • stepbrother or stepsister;
  • grandchild;
  • niece or nephew; or
  • another descendant of a qualifying child or sibling.

An eligible foster child must be placed by an authorised placement agency or court order under federal tax rules.

Age Test

The child must be younger than seventeen at the end of the tax year.

A child who turns seventeen during 2026 is not a qualifying child for the 2026 CTC.

The permanent and totally disabled exception that removes age limits for some EITC and dependency purposes does not eliminate CTC’s under-seventeen requirement.

An older dependent can potentially qualify for the $500 Credit for Other Dependents.

Residence Test

The child generally must live with the taxpayer for more than one-half of the year.

Temporary absences for school, illness, medical care, vacation, military service, juvenile placement, or similar reasons can count as time living together.

Special rules apply to:

  • birth or death during the year;
  • kidnapped children;
  • divorced or separated parents;
  • foster placements;
  • military deployment;
  • homeless families; and
  • children moving between households.

Support Test

The child cannot provide more than one-half of the child’s own support during the year.

Support includes food, housing, clothing, education, medical care, recreation, transportation, and similar necessities.

Scholarships received by a full-time student generally are not treated as support provided by the student for dependency purposes.

The test asks who provided the child’s support, not whether the taxpayer alone provided more than half.

Dependency and Joint-Return Tests

The taxpayer generally must claim the child as a dependent.

The child generally cannot file a joint return with a spouse unless the return is filed solely to claim a refund of withholding or estimated tax and neither spouse would otherwise have tax liability.

Dependency rules include special treatment for divorced or separated parents, multiple-support arrangements, kidnapped children, and citizens or residents living abroad.

A taxpayer should not assume that paying child support automatically creates the right to claim CTC.

Citizenship and Residency Requirement

The child generally must be a:

  • United States citizen;
  • United States national; or
  • United States resident alien.

A child living abroad can qualify in limited circumstances when other dependency and tax-residency rules are satisfied.

Federal tax citizenship rules differ from eligibility rules for Medicaid, SNAP, housing, or immigration benefits.

Qualifying Child Social Security Number

A child used for CTC or ACTC must have a Social Security number valid for employment.

The number must be issued by the return due date, including extensions.

A child with only an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number or Adoption Taxpayer Identification Number cannot qualify for CTC or ACTC under current law.

The same child can potentially qualify the taxpayer for the $500 Credit for Other Dependents when the child has another eligible taxpayer identification number issued on time and all other requirements are met.

Taxpayer Social Security Number

Beginning with tax year 2025, an individual claiming CTC or ACTC must have a Social Security number valid for employment.

On a joint return, at least one spouse must have the qualifying Social Security number.

The other spouse generally must have an SSN or ITIN issued by the return due date.

This rule excludes many mixed-status families from the federal credit even when children are United States citizens with valid Social Security numbers.

Divorced and Separated Parents

Special dependency rules can allow a noncustodial parent to claim a child for CTC when the custodial parent signs Form 8332 or a substantially similar release and all statutory conditions are met.

The custodial parent is generally the parent with whom the child lived for the greater number of nights.

Releasing the child for CTC does not ordinarily transfer:

  • Earned Income Tax Credit;
  • head-of-household filing status;
  • Child and Dependent Care Credit; or
  • the dependent-care exclusion.

Divorce decrees alone may be insufficient for post-2008 returns when Form 8332 is required.

Competing Claims and Tiebreaker Rules

A child can be the qualifying child of more than one person, but only one tax return can claim the child for the same CTC.

Tax-law tiebreaker rules consider:

  • whether the claimant is a parent;
  • which parent had the child for more nights;
  • adjusted gross income when parents have equal residence time;
  • whether an eligible parent declines to claim the child; and
  • the adjusted gross income of qualifying nonparents.

Electronic filing can reject a second return claiming the same child even when the later filer is legally entitled. The eligible filer may need to file by paper and provide documentation.

Adopted Children

An adopted child is treated as the taxpayer’s child for federal tax purposes.

A child lawfully placed for legal adoption can satisfy the relationship test before finalisation.

An Adoption Taxpayer Identification Number can support dependency and other tax administration while adoption is pending, but it does not satisfy the current CTC child-SSN requirement.

Adoptive families can separately qualify for the federal Adoption Credit when applicable.

Foster Children

An eligible foster child must be placed with the taxpayer by an authorised placement agency, a court, or another qualifying governmental or tax-exempt institution.

Informal care of a child who is not otherwise related can fail the relationship test even when the taxpayer provides substantial support.

Foster-care records, court orders, agency placement letters, school records, and residence documents can establish eligibility.

Children with Disabilities

Disability does not extend the CTC age limit beyond sixteen.

A dependent age seventeen or older who is permanently and totally disabled can potentially qualify for the Credit for Other Dependents, EITC qualifying-child treatment, medical-expense provisions, or other tax benefits.

Disability-related living arrangements, hospitalisation, education, respite care, and residential placement can affect residence and support documentation.

Families should examine each tax provision separately rather than assuming one qualifying-child definition controls every credit.

Birth and Death During the Year

A child born alive during the tax year can satisfy the more-than-half-year residence requirement when the child lives with the taxpayer for the child’s entire life during that year.

A child who dies during the year can also satisfy the residence requirement under special rules.

The taxpayer must obtain and report the required Social Security number unless a narrow deceased-child filing exception applies under current return instructions.

Birth certificates, hospital records, death certificates, and Social Security records can be necessary.

Military Families

Military deployments can be treated as temporary absences for residence purposes.

Nontaxable combat pay can be included in earned income for ACTC calculation when the statutory election applies.

The election can increase refundable credit when a family has limited taxable earnings.

Military family care plans, overseas assignments, custody arrangements, and dependent residence require careful documentation.

Self-Employment and Earned Income

Legitimate net self-employment earnings can count in the ACTC earned-income calculation.

Taxpayers must report gross receipts and ordinary and necessary business expenses accurately.

Understating expenses can improperly inflate ACTC, while overstating expenses can understate tax and distort other credits.

Gig workers, household workers, childcare providers, construction workers, farmers, hair-care providers, delivery workers, and other cash businesses should retain reliable records.

Fabricated self-employment cannot create refundable-credit eligibility.

Clergy and Special Earned-Income Rules

Ministers can have wages, housing allowances, parsonage value, and self-employment income treated differently across federal tax provisions.

Schedule 8812 includes specialised earned-income worksheets for clergy and self-employed taxpayers.

A minister’s nontaxable housing allowance can affect earned-income computations through special instructions even when it is excluded from ordinary income tax.

Foreign Earned Income and Form 2555

A taxpayer filing Form 2555 generally cannot claim ACTC.

The restriction applies even when the taxpayer has qualifying children and earned income.

The nonrefundable CTC can remain available when all requirements are satisfied and sufficient tax liability exists.

Foreign earned income and housing exclusions are added back when calculating modified AGI for phaseout purposes.

Puerto Rico

Congress expanded refundable CTC access for bona fide residents of Puerto Rico under the American Rescue Plan.

Puerto Rico residents can claim ACTC through an applicable federal return even when they have little or no federal income-tax liability.

Current rules generally use the $2,200 CTC amount and $1,700 refundable maximum per qualifying child, subject to Social Security numbers, earned-income or payroll-tax formulas, and other requirements.

Forms 1040-SS, 1040, or other applicable returns can be used depending on the taxpayer’s income and federal filing status.

Puerto Rico wages, self-employment tax, Social Security and Medicare taxes, and territorial income exclusions require specialised calculations.

Other United States Territories

The tax treatment of CTC in American Samoa, Guam, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, and the United States Virgin Islands differs according to territorial tax systems, mirror codes, federal payments, and statutory rules.

Residents should use territory-specific revenue-agency and IRS instructions.

Territorial families can be affected by delayed federal reimbursement, local administrative capacity, identification requirements, and different filing forms.

Credit for Other Dependents

The Credit for Other Dependents is a separate nonrefundable credit of up to $500 per eligible dependent.

It can apply to:

  • children age seventeen or older;
  • college students;
  • adult children with disabilities;
  • dependent parents;
  • other qualifying relatives;
  • unrelated household members meeting qualifying-relative requirements; and
  • some dependents with an ITIN or ATIN who cannot satisfy the CTC child-SSN rule.

The credit is not refundable and shares the $200,000 and $400,000 phaseout structure with CTC.

Filing Status

CTC can be claimed on returns using:

  • single;
  • head of household;
  • married filing jointly;
  • married filing separately; or
  • qualifying surviving spouse.

Filing status affects tax liability and the phaseout threshold.

Married filing separately generally uses the $200,000 threshold, not one-half of the joint threshold by a separate rule.

A person can qualify for CTC while failing to qualify for head-of-household status because those provisions have different tests.

How to Claim the Credit

  1. Identify each dependent on Form 1040, Form 1040-SR, or the applicable return.
  2. Confirm relationship, age, residence, support, dependency, citizenship, and joint-return requirements.
  3. Confirm taxpayer, spouse, and child identification-number requirements.
  4. Check the Child Tax Credit or Credit for Other Dependents box for each dependent.
  5. Complete Schedule 8812.
  6. Calculate modified AGI phaseout.
  7. Calculate nonrefundable CTC and ODC using available tax liability.
  8. Calculate ACTC when unused CTC remains.
  9. Complete the three-child or Puerto Rico worksheets when applicable.
  10. Retain evidence supporting the claim.

Documentation

Useful records include:

  • birth certificates;
  • Social Security cards;
  • adoption or foster-placement records;
  • school and childcare records;
  • medical records;
  • leases and utility bills;
  • landlord or shelter statements;
  • court orders;
  • Form 8332;
  • military orders;
  • insurance and benefit records;
  • wage forms;
  • self-employment records; and
  • evidence explaining temporary absences.

Documentation must establish the particular disputed test rather than merely showing that the taxpayer knows or supports the child.

Refund Timing

The IRS cannot issue a refund on a return claiming ACTC before mid-February.

The hold applies to the taxpayer’s entire refund, not only the ACTC portion.

The delay permits wage matching and fraud screening but can create hardship for families expecting refunds for rent, utilities, vehicles, childcare, debt, or emergency expenses.

Duplicate child claims, income mismatches, identity verification, audits, offsets, or paper filing can delay payment further.

Free Filing and Tax Preparation

Eligible families can use:

  • Volunteer Income Tax Assistance;
  • Tax Counseling for the Elderly;
  • IRS Free File;
  • community tax clinics;
  • tribal tax-preparation programmes;
  • military tax assistance; and
  • Low Income Taxpayer Clinics for disputes.

Free preparation protects the value of the credit from commercial fees and can improve accuracy.

Paid-Preparer Due Diligence

Paid return preparers claiming CTC, ACTC, ODC, EITC, American Opportunity Credit, or head-of-household status must satisfy enhanced due-diligence requirements.

Preparers generally must:

  • complete Form 8867;
  • compute the credit accurately;
  • ask reasonable follow-up questions;
  • avoid relying on information that appears incorrect, inconsistent, or incomplete;
  • retain required records; and
  • submit the due-diligence form with the return.

Penalties can apply for each failure and each covered tax benefit.

Audits and Examinations

CTC and ACTC issues can be examined through correspondence, office, or field audits.

Common issues include:

  • duplicate child claims;
  • residence;
  • custodial-parent status;
  • Form 8332;
  • relationship;
  • child age;
  • support;
  • Social Security numbers;
  • self-employment income;
  • ACTC earned-income calculations;
  • Puerto Rico residency; and
  • identity theft.

Families can challenge proposed changes through IRS appeals and, when applicable, the United States Tax Court.

Mathematical or Clerical Error Authority

Congress permits the IRS to correct certain identification-number and credit errors without first using ordinary deficiency procedures.

The IRS can deny or adjust a claim when required Social Security numbers are missing, invalid, or issued too late.

A math-error notice provides a shorter response period than a conventional examination, making prompt review essential.

Taxpayers can request abatement within the statutory period and preserve ordinary dispute rights.

Improper Payments

An improper payment can be an overpayment, underpayment, unsupported payment, payment to an ineligible person, or payment in an incorrect amount.

The estimated ACTC improper-payment rate declined from 14.5 percent in fiscal year 2023 to 10.75 percent in fiscal year 2024.

Common causes include:

  • qualifying-child errors;
  • residency disputes;
  • duplicate claims;
  • self-employment misreporting;
  • incorrect refundable-credit calculations;
  • preparer misconduct;
  • identity theft;
  • Social Security number errors; and
  • insufficient documentation.

Improper-payment estimates do not mean every error is intentional fraud.

Underclaiming and the Credit Gap

Payment integrity includes ensuring that eligible families receive the correct amount, not only preventing overpayments.

IRS tax-year 2022 research estimated:

  • $76 billion in CTC claimed by filers;
  • a $133 million CTC gap among approximately 110,000 filing taxpayers;
  • $31 billion in ACTC claimed by filers; and
  • a $175 million ACTC gap among approximately 135,000 filing taxpayers.

The estimates excluded some nonfilers and therefore did not measure every unclaimed benefit.

Underclaiming can result from incorrect dependency decisions, missing Schedule 8812, preparer error, fear of audits, lack of filing requirement, or failure to obtain valid identification numbers on time.

Identity Theft and Duplicate Dependents

A stolen Social Security number can be used to file a fraudulent return or claim a child.

When a child’s number appears on an earlier electronic return, a later eligible return can be rejected.

The eligible taxpayer can file a paper return and may need to provide residence, relationship, identity, and custody evidence.

Identity Protection PINs, secure records, reputable preparers, prompt filing, and IRS fraud reporting can reduce harm.

Effect on Other Public Benefits

Federal law generally excludes refundable federal tax credits from income for many means-tested programmes.

Refunds are also excluded from resources for specified federal benefit purposes for a limited period after receipt.

Treatment can differ by programme, state, territory, tribe, timing, and whether funds remain saved beyond the statutory exclusion.

Families should verify treatment with the relevant benefit agency before assuming a tax refund has no later asset effect.

Family Policy

CTC recognises the cost of raising children through the federal tax system.

It can support food, housing, clothing, transportation, childcare, education, healthcare, recreation, and savings.

The credit is broader than programmes limited to poverty-level income because the phaseout begins at relatively high income.

Its partly refundable structure creates two policy roles:

  • tax relief for middle-income families; and
  • income support for lower-income working families.

Poverty Reduction

Refundable CTC and ACTC raise after-tax family resources.

The Supplemental Poverty Measure counts refundable tax credits, while the official poverty measure does not.

The one-year fully refundable 2021 expansion demonstrated that benefit design strongly affects child-poverty outcomes.

Under current law, families with no earnings generally receive no ACTC, and families with very low earnings receive only part of the refundable maximum.

Partial refundability therefore creates the “upside-down” result in which some of the poorest children receive smaller credits than children in families with higher earnings and tax liability.

Work and Earnings

ACTC increases with earnings above $2,500, creating a work-conditioned refundable benefit.

Supporters argue that the formula rewards employment and avoids paying a large refundable credit without work.

Critics argue that conditioning a child benefit on parental earnings excludes children during unemployment, disability, caregiving, illness, recession, education, domestic violence, or unstable work.

The phaseout at higher incomes creates a small additional marginal tax rate but begins far above the income range affecting most ACTC households.

Significance for African American Families

Black families are affected by racial wage gaps, unemployment, occupational segregation, wealth inequality, unstable scheduling, caregiving burdens, housing cost, and unequal access to employer benefits.

CTC and ACTC provide substantial tax relief and refundable income to eligible Black families.

Partial refundability reduces benefits for families with the lowest earnings, which can disproportionately affect Black children because of labour-market inequality.

The 2021 fully refundable expansion contributed to record-low Black child poverty under the Supplemental Poverty Measure.

The current taxpayer Social Security number requirement can exclude some citizen children in mixed-status families and intersects with racial and immigration inequality.

Equitable administration requires free preparation, fair audits, accessible documentation, language support, and protection from commercial refund-product fees.

Immigrant and Mixed-Status Families

Current law requires the child and the taxpayer—or at least one spouse on a joint return—to have Social Security numbers valid for employment.

A citizen child with a valid SSN can still be excluded when neither tax-filing parent has the required SSN.

The other spouse on a joint return can generally use a timely issued ITIN when one spouse meets the work-authorised SSN rule.

Families with ITIN dependents can sometimes claim the Credit for Other Dependents but not CTC or ACTC for those dependents.

Language access and trustworthy tax assistance are critical because immigration fear and preparer fraud can suppress legitimate filing.

Women and Single-Parent Families

Women are more likely to be custodial parents and primary caregivers while working in lower-paid occupations.

CTC can reduce taxes and ACTC can provide a major annual refund.

Single parents can face:

  • custody disputes;
  • duplicate child claims;
  • childcare costs;
  • unstable earnings;
  • domestic violence;
  • lack of documents held by a former partner; and
  • refund interception or preparer exploitation.

The credit’s annual structure does not necessarily provide support when childcare or housing bills arise each month.

Families Affected by Disability

Parents with disabilities can qualify when they have tax liability or earned income under applicable rules.

Children with disabilities remain subject to the CTC under-seventeen age limit, although older dependent children can qualify for ODC and sometimes EITC treatment.

Families can experience reduced earnings because of caregiving, medical appointments, service shortages, or inability to obtain accessible childcare.

A fully refundable child benefit reaches nonworking caregivers more effectively than the current earnings-based ACTC formula.

Rural Families

Rural families can experience seasonal employment, low wages, farm income volatility, self-employment, long transportation distances, limited childcare, and fewer free tax-preparation sites.

Refunds support household needs and circulate through local economies.

Broadband gaps, mail delays, limited IRS offices, and shortages of credentialed preparers can make filing and audit response more difficult.

Homelessness and Unstable Housing

A family does not need a conventional lease or permanent address for a child to meet the residence test.

Shelters, motels, vehicles, transitional housing, encampments, and temporary arrangements can constitute a principal home.

Homeless families can have difficulty obtaining Social Security cards, secure mail, bank accounts, school records, and identity documents.

Community filing programmes and shelters can provide residence letters and safe refund access.

State Child Tax Credits

A growing number of states provide child tax credits or young-child tax credits.

State designs differ in:

  • credit amount;
  • child age;
  • income phaseout;
  • refundability;
  • earned-income requirements;
  • ITIN eligibility;
  • inflation adjustment;
  • advance payment; and
  • coordination with state EITC.

Some state credits reach families excluded from federal CTC because of earnings or Social Security number restrictions.

State law changes frequently and must be verified for the relevant year.

Relationship to the Earned Income Tax Credit

CTC and EITC are separate credits that can be claimed on the same return.

EITC depends on earned income, adjusted gross income, filing status, and number of qualifying children.

CTC depends on qualifying children, tax liability, modified AGI, and identification requirements, while ACTC adds an earned-income calculation.

The same child can satisfy one credit and fail another because age, residency, dependency, Social Security number, and release rules differ.

The three-child ACTC formula also subtracts EITC from specified payroll taxes.

Relationship to the Child and Dependent Care Credit

CTC is based on having a qualifying child and does not require payment of childcare expenses.

The Child and Dependent Care Credit is based on qualifying care expenses paid so the taxpayer and spouse can work or look for work.

A family can potentially claim both credits for the same child when all separate requirements are satisfied.

The age limit and definition of a qualifying person differ between the two provisions.

Monthly Payment Debate

Supporters of monthly payment argue that child expenses occur throughout the year and that periodic benefits reduce food hardship, rent instability, utility arrears, and debt.

Opponents raise concerns about:

  • overpayments when income or custody changes;
  • IRS capacity;
  • tax-return reconciliation;
  • fraud and identity theft;
  • effects on annual refund expectations;
  • work incentives; and
  • cost.

A permanent advance system would require current household data, accessible opt-out mechanisms, strong nonfiler outreach, and protection from repayment hardship.

Refundability and Universality Debate

Current ACTC requires earnings above $2,500 and rises at fifteen cents per additional earned dollar.

Full refundability would make the entire credit available regardless of tax liability or parental earnings, subject to income phaseout and other rules.

Supporters describe full refundability as equal treatment of children and a direct anti-poverty policy.

Opponents argue that work-conditioned refundability preserves labour incentives and limits federal cost.

The 2021 experience provides evidence for both administrative feasibility and reconciliation challenges.

Federal Budget Treatment

The nonrefundable CTC reduces federal income-tax receipts.

Refundable ACTC exceeding tax liability is recorded as federal outlay spending in budget accounting.

The programme is therefore both a tax expenditure and a spending programme.

Budget estimates depend on credit amount, refundability, income thresholds, demographic change, employment, inflation, and filing compliance.

Major Achievements

  • Created a broad per-child federal tax credit.
  • Reduced federal income-tax liability for millions of families.
  • Developed a refundable component for working families with limited liability.
  • Increased from $400 in 1998 to $2,200 in current law.
  • Provides up to $1,700 per child in refundable ACTC for 2026.
  • Coordinates with EITC and payroll-tax relief.
  • Supports children across a broad income range.
  • Provided approximately $107 billion in combined CTC and ACTC claims by tax-year 2022 filers.
  • Demonstrated capacity for monthly payments to more than thirty-six million families in 2021.
  • Contributed to a historic 2021 decline in child poverty.
  • Expanded refundable access in Puerto Rico.
  • Created a $500 credit for older children and other dependents.
  • Indexes the maximum credit and refundable amount for inflation under current law.
  • Provides durable family tax relief through permanent authorization.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • The full $2,200 is not automatically refundable.
  • Families with no earnings generally receive no ACTC.
  • Very low earnings produce only a partial refundable credit.
  • Children age seventeen are excluded from CTC.
  • The taxpayer and child Social Security number rules exclude some mixed-status families.
  • The credit requires annual tax filing.
  • Families can miss benefits because they are not otherwise required to file.
  • Custody and residence rules are complex.
  • Duplicate claims can delay legitimate refunds.
  • Paid-preparer fees reduce household benefits.
  • The mid-February refund hold can create hardship.
  • ACTC improper-payment rates remain above ten percent.
  • Correspondence audits can burden low-income households.
  • Monthly payments ended after 2021.
  • State and territorial administration varies.
  • The credit does not address childcare cost directly.
  • The credit can be reduced by limited tax liability and competing nonrefundable credits.
  • Permanent law provides less poverty reduction than the fully refundable 2021 design.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Implementation of the 2025 Law

The IRS is implementing the permanent $2,200 credit, taxpayer Social Security number requirement, inflation adjustments, and revised forms and guidance.

2026 Forms

Draft 2026 Schedule 8812 retains the $1,700 refundable maximum and ACTC worksheets while incorporating current identification requirements.

Full Refundability

Congress continues debating whether to restore full refundability or preserve the earnings-based ACTC formula.

Monthly Payments

Advocates propose periodic payments based on the 2021 model, while policymakers debate reconciliation and administrative controls.

Child Age

Proposals would include seventeen-year-olds permanently, as the 2021 expansion did.

Social Security Number Restrictions

The new taxpayer-SSN requirement has intensified debate over benefits for citizen children in mixed-status families.

Improper Payments

The IRS continues using wage matching, preparer due diligence, identity verification, and audits to reduce ACTC errors while preserving access.

Underclaiming

IRS research increasingly treats missed credits and underpayments as an administrative problem alongside overclaims.

State Child Credits

States continue experimenting with fully refundable, young-child, ITIN-inclusive, and periodic credits.

Poverty and Work Policy

Policymakers remain divided over whether CTC should function primarily as tax relief, a work-conditioned wage supplement, or a universal child benefit.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Child Tax Credit
The nonrefundable federal credit for qualifying children under age seventeen.
Additional Child Tax Credit
The refundable portion of unused CTC, subject to earned-income and other limits.
Credit for Other Dependents
A nonrefundable credit of up to $500 for qualifying dependents ineligible for CTC.
Qualifying child
A child meeting relationship, age, residence, support, dependency, citizenship, identification, and other requirements.
Refundable credit
A credit that can exceed income-tax liability and produce a refund.
Nonrefundable credit
A credit limited by eligible income-tax liability.
Modified adjusted gross income
AGI increased by selected excluded foreign and territorial income for phaseout purposes.
Phaseout
Reduction of the credit after modified AGI exceeds the statutory threshold.
Earned income
Qualifying work compensation used in the ACTC calculation.
Custodial parent
Generally the parent with whom the child lived for the greater number of nights during the year.
Form 8332
The form used by a custodial parent to release a dependency claim for specified tax benefits to a noncustodial parent.
Social Security number valid for employment
An SSN issued under qualifying Social Security Act authority and on time for the return.
Advance Child Tax Credit
The temporary 2021 system paying up to half of expected CTC before annual return filing.
Reconciliation
Comparison of advance payments with final tax-year eligibility.
Improper payment
A payment made in the wrong amount, to the wrong recipient, or without sufficient support.
Credit gap
Tax benefits for which taxpayers appear eligible but do not claim fully.

Summary

The Child Tax Credit is a federal family tax benefit created in 1997 and permanently expanded in 2025.

For tax year 2026, the maximum credit is $2,200 for each qualifying child under age seventeen.

The credit is only partly refundable. The Additional Child Tax Credit can provide up to $1,700 per qualifying child, generally based on fifteen percent of earned income above $2,500 and limited by unused CTC.

Families with three or more qualifying children can use an alternative formula based on specified payroll taxes when it produces a larger allowable refundable amount.

A qualifying child must meet relationship, age, residence, support, dependency, citizenship, joint-return, identification, and competing-claim requirements.

The child and the claiming taxpayer—or at least one spouse on a joint return—must have Social Security numbers valid for employment and issued by the return due date.

The full credit begins phasing out above $200,000 of modified AGI for most taxpayers and $400,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly.

The 2021 American Rescue Plan temporarily increased the credit, included seventeen-year-olds, made it fully refundable, and delivered approximately $93 billion in monthly advances to families of about sixty-one million children.

That expansion contributed to a 46-percent decline in Supplemental Poverty Measure child poverty in 2021, but it expired after one year.

Current law provides broad family tax relief while giving the smallest refundable benefits to children in families with little or no earnings.

The central policy debate is whether CTC should remain a partly refundable work-conditioned tax credit or become a fully refundable periodic child benefit.
