Pell Grant Program

The principal federal need-based grant program helping students with limited financial resources pay for undergraduate education, approved prison education, postbaccalaureate teacher certification, and qualifying short-term workforce programs.

Overview

The Federal Pell Grant Program is the largest source of federal grant aid for undergraduate postsecondary education in the United States. It provides need-based assistance to eligible students attending participating colleges, universities, community colleges, career schools, prison education programs, and—beginning July 1, 2026—approved short-term workforce programs.

A Pell Grant is student-based aid. Eligibility follows the student to an eligible institution rather than being awarded competitively to a college for a limited number of recipients. Institutions calculate the award, draw federal funds, credit the student’s account, pay any authorized remaining balance, and report disbursements to the United States Department of Education.

Pell Grants are generally considered gift aid and ordinarily do not have to be repaid. Repayment can be required when a student withdraws before earning all disbursed aid, receives an overpayment, loses eligibility, receives an incorrect award, or fails to begin attendance in courses used to calculate the grant.

The program was created by the Education Amendments of 1972 as the Basic Educational Opportunity Grant Program. The first awards were made for the 1973–1974 academic year. Congress renamed the grants in honor of Senator Claiborne Pell through the Education Amendments of 1980.

Pell Grants are intended to form the foundation of a student’s federal need-based aid package. They can be combined with state grants, institutional grants, scholarships, work-study, veterans’ benefits, and federal student loans, subject to cost-of-attendance, overaward, and program-specific rules.

Students apply through the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, commonly abbreviated FAFSA. The FAFSA Processing System uses student and contributor information to calculate a Student Aid Index and determine whether the applicant qualifies for a maximum, calculated, or minimum Pell Grant.

For the 2026–2027 award year, the maximum Scheduled Award is $7,395 and the minimum Scheduled Award is $740. The award year runs from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027.

A student whose Student Aid Index is equal to or greater than twice the maximum Pell Grant is generally ineligible. For 2026–2027, that ceiling is $14,790. The ceiling does not apply to qualifying children of certain deceased servicemembers and public safety officers receiving a maximum award under the statutory Special Rule.

Maximum and minimum Pell eligibility can be established directly through household adjusted gross income, family size, family composition, state of residence, tax-filing requirements, and federal poverty guidelines. Students who do not qualify automatically can receive a calculated award generally equal to the maximum Pell Grant minus the Student Aid Index, rounded to the nearest five dollars and limited by cost of attendance.

The Scheduled Award assumes full-time attendance for a full academic year. The actual annual and payment-period amounts depend on enrollment intensity, academic calendar, program length, cost of attendance, remaining lifetime eligibility, and whether the student attends for the entire award year.

Students can receive up to 150 percent of their Scheduled Award during one award year through Year-Round Pell when they remain otherwise eligible and attend additional payment periods. Beginning with 2024–2025, a student no longer must be enrolled at least half time to receive additional Pell beyond 100 percent.

Lifetime Pell eligibility is generally limited to the equivalent of twelve full-time semesters, expressed as 600 percent Lifetime Eligibility Used. A full-time Scheduled Award received for one award year ordinarily counts as 100 percent. Part-time or partial-year use consumes a proportional percentage.

Most recipients must be undergraduates who have not earned a bachelor’s, graduate, or professional degree. Limited exceptions exist for qualifying postbaccalaureate teacher certification programs and, beginning in 2026, eligible workforce programs. Workforce Pell can serve some students who already possess a bachelor’s degree, but not students enrolled in or holding a graduate credential under the applicable rules.

Confined or incarcerated individuals can receive Pell Grants only through an approved Prison Education Program, subject to special institutional, correctional-agency, cost-of-attendance, reporting, and student-protection requirements.

Public Law 119-21 made several changes effective for 2026–2027. It established Workforce Pell, imposed the $14,790 SAI ceiling for the current year, changed how foreign earned income exclusions affect maximum and minimum Pell eligibility, and made students ineligible when nonfederal grant and scholarship aid equals or exceeds their cost of attendance.

The program is funded through a combination of annual discretionary appropriations and mandatory budget authority. Although eligible students generally receive grants without a fixed institutional allotment, Pell is not a traditional permanently funded entitlement. Congress determines award levels and provides funding through appropriations and other legislation.

In school year 2024–2025, approximately seven million undergraduate students received nearly $40 billion in Pell Grant awards. The program has a major effect on community colleges, minority-serving institutions, regional public universities, career schools, adult learners, independent students, first-generation students, and households with limited income or wealth.

Definitions

Federal Pell Grant
A federal need-based grant helping eligible students meet the cost of approved postsecondary education or workforce training.
Basic Educational Opportunity Grant
The original name of the Pell Grant when Congress created the program in 1972. The abbreviation BEOG was commonly used.
Free Application for Federal Student Aid
The application used to determine eligibility for Pell Grants and other federal student aid. The abbreviation FAFSA is commonly used.
Student Aid Index
An index calculated from FAFSA information and used to measure relative financial need and determine aid eligibility. It is not a bill or the amount a family must pay. The abbreviation SAI is commonly used.
Expected Family Contribution
The former federal need-analysis number replaced by the Student Aid Index beginning with the 2024–2025 award year. The abbreviation EFC was commonly used.
Maximum Pell Grant
The full published Scheduled Award for an applicant meeting automatic maximum eligibility criteria or the Special Rule.
Minimum Pell Grant
The published minimum Scheduled Award, equal to 10 percent of the maximum and rounded to the nearest five dollars.
Calculated Pell Grant
An award ordinarily calculated by subtracting the student’s SAI from the published maximum, rounding to the nearest five dollars, and applying minimum-award and cost-of-attendance limits.
Scheduled Award
The maximum Pell amount a student would receive for full-time attendance for a full academic year before adjustment for enrollment intensity and payment periods.
Annual Award
The Pell amount a student is eligible to receive for a full academic year based on enrollment intensity, cost of attendance, and other limits.
Award year
The federal student-aid period beginning July 1 and ending June 30 of the following calendar year.
Payment period
The academic term or defined portion of a nonterm program for which an institution calculates and disburses part of the annual award.
Enrollment intensity
The percentage of a full-time course load in which a student is enrolled for Pell purposes. Seven credits when twelve credits is full time ordinarily produces 58 percent enrollment intensity after rounding.
Cost of attendance
The institutionally determined student budget for tuition, fees, books, supplies, housing, food, transportation, dependent care, disability-related costs, and other authorized expenses. The abbreviation COA is commonly used.
Year-Round Pell
Authority allowing an otherwise eligible student to receive up to 150 percent of the Scheduled Award during one award year.
Lifetime Eligibility Used
The cumulative percentage of a student’s Pell lifetime limit already consumed. The abbreviation LEU is commonly used.
600-percent lifetime limit
The statutory maximum Pell use equivalent to approximately twelve full-time semesters or six full-time academic years.
Federal School Code
An identifier used on the FAFSA to send student information to selected colleges or career schools participating in federal student aid.
Institutional Student Information Record
The electronic FAFSA result sent to an eligible institution and used by financial-aid administrators. The abbreviation ISIR is commonly used.
FAFSA Submission Summary
The student-facing summary of processed FAFSA information, SAI, federal aid eligibility estimates, and required corrections or next steps.
Professional judgment
Authority allowing a financial-aid administrator to adjust specified FAFSA data or cost-of-attendance components when documented special circumstances justify an individualized determination.
Satisfactory academic progress
The institution’s federally compliant standards for grades, pace, and maximum time frame that students must meet to continue receiving Title IV aid. The abbreviation SAP is commonly used.
Eligible program
An educational or workforce program meeting federal length, credential, institutional, and program-participation requirements for Title IV aid.
Eligible workforce program
A short-term postsecondary program approved under the Workforce Pell requirements and certified through applicable state and federal processes.
Prison Education Program
An approved educational program offered by an eligible institution to confined or incarcerated individuals under federal student-aid rules. The abbreviation PEP is commonly used.
Special Rule
The statutory pathway providing a maximum Pell Grant to certain students whose parent or guardian died in the line of duty as a servicemember or public safety officer and who meet the age and documentation requirements.
Credit balance
Title IV funds remaining after an institution applies authorized aid to tuition, fees, and other institutional charges. The institution ordinarily pays the balance to the student or parent under federal cash-management rules.
Overaward
An aid package exceeding a student’s financial need or cost of attendance under applicable rules.
Overpayment
Federal student aid received beyond the amount for which a student was eligible and potentially subject to return or repayment.
Return of Title IV Funds
The federal calculation determining how much aid a student earned before withdrawing and how much unearned aid must be returned. The abbreviation R2T4 is commonly used.

Historical Development

Higher Education Act of 1965

The Higher Education Act created a broad federal structure of student loans, work-study, institutional support, teacher programs, and college-access initiatives. It did not initially contain the modern portable Pell Grant.

Student-Based Grant Design

During debate over the 1972 amendments, lawmakers considered how to make aid follow students while preserving institutional diversity. The resulting Basic Grant was designed as a foundation that students could use at eligible public, nonprofit, or proprietary institutions.

First Awards

The first award year began in 1973. Limited appropriations required the government to phase in eligibility and restrict the earliest awards.

Claiborne Pell

Senator Claiborne Pell championed broad access to education and helped create the Basic Educational Opportunity Grant. Congress renamed the program in his honor in 1980.

Expansion and Retrenchment

Over time, Congress expanded eligibility, raised maximum awards, permitted less-than-half-time attendance, changed need analysis, and added mandatory funding. It also imposed lifetime limits, reduced or froze award values in some periods, and changed eligibility for specific populations.

FAFSA Simplification

The 2020 FAFSA Simplification Act restructured Pell eligibility around three paths: maximum Pell, calculated Pell, and minimum Pell. It replaced EFC with SAI and increased reliance on secure federal tax information.

Workforce and Prison Education

The modern program extends beyond conventional campus-based undergraduate degrees. Approved prison education became generally eligible in 2023, and approved short-term workforce programs became eligible in 2026.

Administration and Funding Structure

Department of Education

Federal Student Aid administers FAFSA processing, eligibility systems, institutional participation, grant origination, disbursement records, reconciliation, program reviews, data systems, and guidance.

Participating Institutions

Colleges and career schools determine final eligibility, resolve conflicting information, establish cost of attendance, calculate awards, monitor enrollment, disburse funds, return unearned aid, and maintain records.

Common Origination and Disbursement

Institutions originate and report Pell awards through the Common Origination and Disbursement system, commonly abbreviated COD.

National Student Loan Data System

Schools and students use federal records to review prior aid, Pell lifetime use, enrollment, and other information relevant to eligibility.

Discretionary Funding

Congress provides a large annual discretionary appropriation supporting the maximum award and current recipients.

Mandatory Funding

Federal law also provides mandatory budget authority that supplements annual appropriations and supports award levels and program costs.

Shortfall and Surplus

Because recipient numbers and average awards change with the economy, FAFSA filings, enrollment, and eligibility law, actual program costs can differ from forecasts. The Pell program can accumulate a surplus or face a projected funding shortfall.

Quasi-Entitlement Operation

Eligible students ordinarily receive awards without an institutional quota. However, Congress can change the maximum award, eligibility, or funding, making Pell different from a permanently funded entitlement such as Social Security.

General Student Eligibility

A student generally must satisfy both Pell-specific and general federal student-aid requirements.

Ordinary Requirements

  • Complete a valid FAFSA for the award year
  • Demonstrate Pell eligibility under maximum, calculated, minimum, or Special Rule criteria
  • Be a United States citizen or eligible noncitizen
  • Be enrolled or accepted for enrollment in an eligible program at an eligible domestic institution
  • Have a qualifying high school diploma, recognized equivalent, homeschool completion, or another authorized ability-to-benefit pathway
  • Maintain satisfactory academic progress
  • Not exceed the 600-percent lifetime limit
  • Not receive concurrent Pell payments from more than one institution
  • Resolve federal aid defaults, overpayments, identity issues, conflicting information, and other eligibility conditions when applicable

Undergraduate Requirement

Most recipients must be undergraduate students who have not completed the requirements for a first bachelor’s degree.

Associate and Certificate Graduates

A student who has earned an associate degree or sub-baccalaureate certificate can remain eligible while enrolled in another undergraduate program, subject to the lifetime limit and other rules.

Graduate Students

Students enrolled as graduate or professional students and students who have earned a graduate or professional degree are generally ineligible.

Foreign Institutions

Ordinary Pell Grants are available only at eligible nonforeign institutions. A student studying abroad can receive Pell through an eligible home institution when the program satisfies federal rules.

Enrollment Level

Pell does not generally require half-time enrollment. A student enrolled in one qualifying course can receive a prorated amount when all other requirements are met.

FAFSA and Student Aid Index

Annual Application

A student submits a FAFSA for each award year. The form collects identity, dependency, family-size, income, asset, tax, benefit, education, and school-selection information.

Contributors

Depending on dependency and marital status, contributors can include the student, spouse, parent, or parent’s spouse. Contributors provide required information and consent to federal tax-data exchange but do not become financially liable for the student’s education merely by contributing to the FAFSA.

Prior-Prior Tax Year

The 2026–2027 FAFSA generally uses 2024 federal tax information. Current changes in income or family circumstances can be considered through professional judgment.

SAI Range

The SAI can be negative, beginning at –1500, or a positive number. A lower SAI generally indicates greater measured financial need.

SAI Is Not a Bill

The SAI does not state how much a family must pay. It is an index used in federal need analysis and aid packaging.

Assets

Some applicants are exempt from reporting assets. When required, reportable assets can include cash and investments, while the primary residence and qualified retirement accounts are generally excluded.

Family Members in College

The current SAI formula does not divide the index by the number of family members attending college, unlike the former EFC formula.

Professional Judgment

A financial-aid administrator can consider documented job loss, income reduction, unusual medical costs, divorce, death, dependency circumstances, or other qualifying changes. Professional judgment is case specific and cannot be applied through an automatic blanket policy.

Maximum, Minimum, and Calculated Pell

Maximum Pell

A dependent or independent applicant can qualify automatically for maximum Pell when the relevant person is not required to file a federal tax return or when adjusted gross income falls within specified poverty-guideline percentages.

For both dependent and independent students, the principal maximum-Pell income limits are:

  • Up to 225 percent of the applicable poverty guideline for a single-parent family
  • Up to 175 percent for a family not headed by a single parent

Beginning in 2026–2027, the foreign earned income exclusion reported on the FAFSA is added to adjusted gross income when applying maximum- and minimum-Pell income tests.

Calculated Pell

A student not automatically eligible for maximum or minimum Pell can qualify through the SAI calculation.

The basic calculation is:

Published Maximum Pell Grant − Student Aid Index = Calculated Pell Grant

The result is rounded to the nearest five dollars. It must be at least the published minimum and cannot exceed the student’s Pell cost of attendance.

Minimum Pell

A student whose calculated Pell amount is too small can still qualify for the published minimum through separate income thresholds.

For dependent students, minimum-Pell thresholds are generally:

  • Up to 325 percent of the poverty guideline for a single-parent family
  • Up to 275 percent for a family not headed by a single parent

For independent students, thresholds vary according to whether the student is a single parent, another parent, or has no dependents other than a spouse.

SAI Ceiling

An applicant with an SAI of $14,790 or higher is generally ineligible for 2026–2027 even when another eligibility pathway might otherwise appear available. The statutory Special Rule is exempt.

Published Amount Versus Actual Payment

The maximum is not automatically paid to every recipient. Actual payment depends on eligibility pathway, enrollment intensity, payment periods, attendance, cost of attendance, prior disbursements, and lifetime use.

Cost-of-Attendance Cap

A Scheduled Award cannot exceed the Pell cost of attendance used under federal rules.

Rounding

Calculated awards are generally rounded to the nearest five dollars. Payment-period calculations can involve additional rounding rules.

Appropriations

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 confirmed the $7,395 maximum and $740 minimum for the award year.

Enrollment Intensity

Term-Based Programs

For a standard term-based credit-hour program, enrollment intensity is calculated by dividing enrolled credits by the institution’s full-time standard and rounding to the nearest whole percentage.

Part-Time Eligibility

A student can receive Pell while enrolled less than half time. The award is reduced according to enrollment intensity and can be further limited by cost of attendance.

Nonterm Programs

Clock-hour and nonterm credit-hour programs use payment formulas based on hours and weeks rather than ordinary term enrollment percentages.

Enrollment Changes

Institutions establish a Pell recalculation policy and adjust awards when enrollment changes before or on the applicable recalculation date.

Year-Round Pell

Additional Eligibility

An eligible student can receive up to 150 percent of the Scheduled Award during one award year.

Purpose

Year-Round Pell supports students who attend summer or additional terms, accelerate completion, or enroll continuously in nontraditional academic calendars.

No Larger Payment Per Ordinary Term

The provision does not automatically increase each semester payment. It permits additional qualifying payment periods after the student has used or is expected to use the ordinary 100-percent Scheduled Award.

No Half-Time Requirement

Beginning with the 2024–2025 award year, a student no longer must be at least half time to receive additional Pell beyond 100 percent.

Lifetime Effect

Every additional Pell dollar counts toward the 600-percent lifetime limit. A student receiving 150 percent in one year uses one and one-half years of lifetime eligibility.

Transfer Students

When a student transfers during an award year, the new institution reviews prior-year disbursements and remaining annual eligibility to prevent the student from receiving more than 150 percent.

Lifetime Eligibility Used

Six Full-Time Years

The lifetime cap is 600 percent, equivalent to six full-time Scheduled Awards or approximately twelve full-time semesters.

Percentage Accounting

A student receiving half of a Scheduled Award uses 50 percent. A student receiving the full award uses 100 percent. Year-Round Pell can increase annual use to 150 percent.

All Institutions Combined

Lifetime use follows the student across institutions. Transferring or changing programs does not reset the limit.

Prior Awards Count

Pell and earlier Basic Educational Opportunity Grant awards received before enactment of the current lifetime rule can count toward the 600-percent total.

Restored Eligibility

When an institution returns Pell funds because of withdrawal, overpayment correction, or another adjustment, the student’s lifetime percentage can be reduced accordingly.

Student Access

Students can review federal aid history through StudentAid.gov. Institutions receive federal records and warnings for students approaching the limit.

No General Appeal

The lifetime limit is statutory. A school cannot waive it merely because a student changes majors, transfers, experiences hardship, or needs additional semesters.

Eligible Institutions

Public Institutions

Eligible public universities, community colleges, technical colleges, and other public postsecondary institutions can participate.

Private Nonprofit Institutions

Eligible nonprofit colleges, universities, religious institutions, and career schools can participate.

Proprietary Institutions

Eligible for-profit career schools and colleges can participate when they satisfy federal institutional and program requirements.

Accreditation and State Authorization

An institution generally must be legally authorized, accredited or preaccredited by a recognized agency, and certified by the Department for Title IV participation.

Program Participation Agreement

The institution signs an agreement accepting federal administrative, fiscal, consumer-information, reporting, cash-management, and program-integrity duties.

Foreign Schools

Foreign institutions can participate in certain federal loan programs but are not ordinary Pell-eligible institutions.

Institutional Choice

A student can use Pell at any institution and program for which both the student and program are eligible. The grant does not guarantee admission or that the award will cover the full price.

Eligible Educational Programs

Degrees

Associate and bachelor’s degree programs at eligible institutions can qualify.

Certificates and Diplomas

Career and technical programs leading to recognized credentials can qualify when they meet federal length and institutional requirements.

Clock-Hour Programs

Cosmetology, welding, aviation maintenance, practical nursing, automotive technology, and other clock-hour programs can qualify when program rules are met.

Distance Education

Online and hybrid programs can qualify when the institution, program, state authorization, regular and substantive interaction, and other federal requirements are satisfied.

Study Abroad

A Pell-eligible student can use aid for an approved study-abroad program through an eligible domestic home institution.

Remedial Coursework

Limited remedial coursework can count toward Pell eligibility under federal rules.

English as a Second Language

Qualifying English-language coursework can be eligible when it forms part of an eligible program or meets authorized preparatory conditions.

Ineligible Examples

Ordinary noncredit personal-enrichment courses, avocational programs, graduate programs, unapproved short-term training, and programs at ineligible institutions do not qualify.

Workforce Pell

Effective Date

Students became eligible for Pell Grants in approved workforce programs beginning July 1, 2026.

Purpose

Workforce Pell extends federal grants to high-quality short-term programs preparing students for high-skill, high-wage, or in-demand employment.

Program Length

Eligible programs can be substantially shorter than traditional Title IV programs and can prepare students for employment in as little as eight weeks, subject to statutory and regulatory hour, week, credential, and performance requirements.

State Role

Governors and state workforce boards identify high-demand industries and participate in certifying programs under the federal framework.

Federal Approval

An institution must obtain Department approval before disbursing Workforce Pell for a program.

Performance Requirements

Programs are subject to completion, employment, earnings, cost, value, and data requirements intended to connect federal aid with labor-market outcomes.

Bachelor’s Degree Holders

A student can receive Workforce Pell even after earning a bachelor’s degree. The student cannot be enrolled in a graduate-credential program and cannot already hold a graduate credential.

Concurrent Enrollment

A student cannot receive simultaneous Pell awards from multiple schools or combine concurrent enrollment in a workforce program with another program to create multiple Pell payments.

Lifetime Limit

Workforce Pell counts toward the same 600-percent lifetime limit as traditional Pell.

Consumer Protection

The program’s central policy question is whether outcome standards, state certification, federal approval, disclosures, and tuition limits will prevent low-value short-term programs from capturing student aid.

Prison Education Programs

Restored Eligibility

General Pell eligibility for incarcerated students was restored effective July 1, 2023 after nearly three decades of statutory exclusion.

Approved Program Requirement

A confined or incarcerated student can receive Pell only through an eligible Prison Education Program approved under federal rules.

Correctional Oversight Entity

The responsible correctional agency or other oversight entity evaluates whether the program operates in the student’s best interest.

Institutional Requirements

Participating colleges must satisfy accreditation, authorization, transferability, instructor, advising, data, disclosure, and program-review requirements.

Cost of Attendance

The incarcerated-student COA can include tuition, fees, books, supplies, equipment, and licensing or credential costs. Housing, food, and transportation are generally excluded because the correctional institution provides them.

No Credit Balance

An incarcerated student cannot receive a Pell credit balance. Excess funds must be returned and restored to remaining eligibility.

Second Chance Pell

The experimental Second Chance Pell initiative preceded full restoration. Most experimental authority ended June 30, 2026, with limited extensions through December 31, 2026 for specified participants.

Reentry

Effective programs connect coursework with credentials, transfer, employment, advising, identification documents, technology access, and continuation after release.

Postbaccalaureate Teacher Certification

Limited Exception

A student who already holds a bachelor’s degree can receive Pell while enrolled at least half time in a qualifying postbaccalaureate teacher certification or licensure program.

Program Conditions

  • The program does not lead to a graduate degree
  • The institution does not offer a bachelor’s degree in education
  • The student is seeking an initial state teaching credential
  • The coursework is required by the state for professional certification or licensure

Student Classification

The institution treats the student as an undergraduate for specified federal aid purposes.

Half-Time Requirement

Unlike ordinary Pell eligibility, this exception requires at least half-time enrollment.

Children of Certain Deceased Servicemembers and Public Safety Officers

Maximum Award

A qualifying student receives the maximum Scheduled Award regardless of calculated SAI.

Parent or Guardian

The student’s parent or guardian must have died in the line of duty while serving on active duty in the United States Armed Forces on or after September 11, 2001, or while actively performing the duties of a qualifying public safety officer.

Age

For 2026–2027, the student must have been younger than thirty-three on January 1, 2026.

Documentation

The institution reviews military casualty records, public-safety benefit determinations, death records, government attestations, or other credible documentation.

SAI Ceiling Exception

The general $14,790 SAI ceiling does not disqualify an otherwise eligible Special Rule recipient.

Lifetime and Enrollment Limits

The award remains subject to cost of attendance, enrollment intensity, lifetime use, and other ordinary Pell conditions.

Cost of Attendance

Purpose

COA provides a standardized student budget for determining financial need, packaging aid, and limiting awards.

Common Components

  • Tuition and required fees
  • Books, course materials, supplies, and equipment
  • Housing and food
  • Transportation
  • Miscellaneous personal expenses
  • Federal student-loan fees
  • Dependent-care expenses
  • Disability-related expenses
  • Study-abroad costs
  • Cooperative-education employment costs
  • Professional licensure or certification expenses

Full-Time Full-Year Pell COA

For determining the Scheduled Award, Pell COA is based on full-time costs for a full academic year even when the student enrolls part time or for a shorter period. Payment calculations then account for enrollment intensity and program structure.

Living Arrangement

Institutions establish different housing and food allowances for students living on campus, off campus, or with parents, using reasonable data and federal requirements.

Individual Adjustment

A financial-aid administrator can adjust COA for documented special circumstances such as unusually high dependent care, disability, transportation, or computer costs.

Pell Cannot Exceed COA

The student’s Pell Scheduled Award is capped when the calculated amount exceeds Pell COA.

Awarding and Disbursement

Financial-Aid Offer

After admission, FAFSA processing, and institutional review, the college communicates the estimated or final Pell amount within the student’s financial-aid offer.

Payment Periods

The annual award is divided among semesters, quarters, trimesters, modules, or nonterm payment periods.

Institutional Charges

The institution can apply Pell to tuition, fees, and authorized institutional charges.

Credit Balance

Remaining funds are generally paid to the student for authorized educational expenses within federal time limits. The student can authorize certain institutional uses but retains federal protections.

Books and Supplies

When a student will have a Title IV credit balance and meets applicable conditions, the institution must provide a way to obtain required books and supplies by the seventh day of the payment period.

Attendance

An institution cannot retain Pell for a class the student never began. Attendance and academic activity documentation are especially important in online, modular, clock-hour, and nonterm programs.

Late Disbursement

A former student can receive a late Pell disbursement for a completed payment period when federal eligibility and timing requirements are met.

Withdrawal, Overpayments, and Repayment

Withdrawal

When a student withdraws before completing more than 60 percent of a payment period, the institution generally calculates the portion of Title IV aid earned through the withdrawal date.

Unearned Aid

The institution and student can be responsible for returning unearned aid according to the Return of Title IV Funds formula.

Failure to Begin Attendance

Pell must be recalculated when a student never begins one or more classes used to determine enrollment intensity.

Overpayment

A student can owe repayment when an incorrect FAFSA, institutional mistake, simultaneous payment, unresolved eligibility issue, or enrollment change causes excess receipt.

Fraud

Intentional false statements, identity theft, forged records, or schemes involving nonexistent students can result in administrative, civil, and criminal enforcement.

Grant Versus Loan

The possibility of returning unearned or incorrectly paid funds does not make Pell an ordinary loan. Properly awarded and earned Pell aid has no repayment obligation.

Interaction with Other Financial Aid

Foundation of the Package

Institutions generally package Pell before campus-based grants, work-study, and federal loans because Pell does not require repayment and represents the primary federal need-based grant.

Federal Supplemental Educational Opportunity Grant

Schools give priority for FSEOG to students with exceptional financial need, especially Pell recipients. FSEOG has a limited institutional allocation, unlike Pell.

State and Institutional Grants

Pell can be combined with state grants, tuition waivers, institutional scholarships, and private aid.

Federal Student Loans

Pell does not prevent an eligible student from borrowing Direct Loans. The institution must observe need and cost-of-attendance limits.

Veterans’ Education Benefits

Many veterans and dependents can receive both Pell and qualifying Department of Veterans Affairs education benefits, subject to program rules and institutional packaging.

Nonfederal Aid Exclusion Beginning 2026–2027

A student is ineligible for Pell when nonfederal grants and scholarships equal or exceed cost of attendance under the new law and regulations. The rule applies to award years beginning on or after July 1, 2026.

Order of Adjustments

When total aid exceeds limits, institutions may reduce loans, work-study, or other aid before Pell when permitted. The new outside-aid exclusion can require removal of Pell eligibility rather than ordinary packaging adjustment.

Satisfactory Academic Progress

Grade Standard

The student must meet the institution’s qualitative measure, commonly expressed through cumulative grade-point average.

Pace

The student must complete a sufficient percentage of attempted coursework to finish within the maximum time frame.

Maximum Time Frame

For undergraduate credit-hour programs, the maximum ordinarily cannot exceed 150 percent of the published program length.

All Applicable Coursework

Transfer credits, withdrawals, repeats, incompletes, and changes of major are treated according to the institution’s federally compliant policy.

Warning and Probation

Institutions can use federal financial-aid warning in qualifying review systems and can place a student on probation after a successful appeal.

Appeal

A student can appeal when illness, injury, death of a relative, or another special circumstance affected academic progress and the institution’s policy permits an appeal.

Lifetime Limit Is Separate

A student can meet SAP yet be ineligible because of 600-percent lifetime use, or have remaining lifetime eligibility yet fail SAP.

Program Integrity and Oversight

Institutional Eligibility

The Department certifies institutions and can impose provisional participation, heightened cash monitoring, reimbursement payment methods, fines, limitations, suspension, or termination.

Accrediting Agencies

Recognized accreditors review educational quality, institutional capacity, student outcomes, governance, and compliance within their scope.

State Authorization

States authorize institutions to operate and can investigate consumer protection, licensing, closure, and academic misconduct.

Program Reviews and Audits

Institutions are subject to Department program reviews, annual compliance audits, financial-statement audits, and reconciliation.

Verification

Selected applicants and institutions document specified FAFSA information. Schools must also resolve conflicting information regardless of formal verification selection.

Reconciliation

Institutions compare internal student records, disbursements, federal COD records, and cash received to ensure that federal funds match eligible awards.

Identity Fraud

Online enrollment and stolen identities have increased concern about fraudulent applications, organized aid schemes, false academic activity, and improper disbursement.

Institutional Incentives

Because Pell follows the student, institutions have a financial incentive to recruit eligible students. Oversight seeks to distinguish legitimate access from deceptive recruitment and low-quality programs.

African American Students and Educational Equity

Income and Wealth

Racial disparities in household income, unemployment, homeownership, inheritance, and family wealth increase the importance of need-based grants for many African American students.

College Access

Pell can reduce the immediate price barrier preventing a student from enrolling in community college, a four-year institution, or career training.

Historically Black Colleges and Universities

HBCUs enroll substantial numbers of Pell recipients and first-generation students. Pell funds flow through students and form an important source of tuition revenue for institutions serving communities with limited private wealth.

Community Colleges

Many Black students begin at community colleges, where Pell can cover a larger share of tuition than at higher-priced institutions but may still fall short of housing, transportation, childcare, and lost wages.

Independent and Adult Students

Independent students often have work, family, childcare, and transportation obligations. Pell eligibility can be high, but the grant does not replace earnings lost while attending school.

Completion Gap

Access without sufficient academic, financial, housing, health, and advising support may not produce completion. Students can use lifetime eligibility while making limited progress or transferring credits inefficiently.

Debt

Pell reduces borrowing but rarely covers the entire cost of attendance. Black students can still borrow heavily because of lower family wealth, institutional price, unmet need, and graduate-school financing.

Institutional Sector

African American students have historically been recruited disproportionately by some high-cost, low-completion proprietary institutions. Strong consumer information and program oversight are therefore central to racial equity.

Prison Education

Restored Pell eligibility has particular racial significance because Black Americans are disproportionately represented in prisons and face severe barriers to education and employment after release.

Workforce Pell

Short-term grants can expand rapid access to credentials, but equity depends on whether programs lead to transferable credit, recognized credentials, stable employment, wage growth, and protection from low-value training.

Program Scale and Recipient Characteristics

School Year 2024–2025

Approximately seven million undergraduate students received nearly $40 billion in Pell Grant awards.

FAFSA Eligibility

GAO found that among students completing the FAFSA, 9.87 million were Pell eligible in 2024–2025 and 7.91 million were eligible for maximum Pell. Eligibility exceeds actual receipt because some eligible applicants do not enroll, attend an eligible program, or receive a disbursement.

Maximum-Pell Expansion

GAO reported that 80 percent of Pell-eligible FAFSA applicants qualified for maximum Pell in 2024–2025, compared with 65 percent in 2023–2024.

Income

Most Pell-eligible applicants had household incomes below $60,001 in GAO’s 2024–2025 analysis, although the simplified rules expanded eligibility in some higher income ranges.

Independent Students

More than three-quarters of independent FAFSA applicants were Pell eligible in 2024–2025, and most Pell-eligible independent students qualified for the maximum.

Vulnerable Populations

More than 90 percent of FAFSA applicants in several vulnerable categories—including students reporting homelessness or foster care—were Pell eligible.

Institutional Distribution

Recipients attend public two-year and four-year colleges, private nonprofit institutions, proprietary schools, and specialized career institutions. The share of students receiving Pell varies widely by institution.

Benefits and Public Purposes

College Access

Pell lowers the price a financially needy student must cover through earnings, family contributions, loans, or unmet need.

Student Choice

Portability allows recipients to choose among thousands of eligible institutions and programs.

No Ordinary Repayment

The grant does not create the long-term repayment obligation associated with student loans.

Part-Time Access

Students can receive prorated aid while attending less than half time, supporting workers, caregivers, and adult learners.

Acceleration

Year-Round Pell can help students attend summer terms and complete credentials more quickly.

Institutional Revenue

Pell helps public, nonprofit, and proprietary institutions serve students unable to pay the full price from family resources.

Workforce Development

Traditional career programs and Workforce Pell can connect federal aid with occupational credentials and labor-market entry.

Reentry and Rehabilitation

Prison education can strengthen literacy, credentials, employment opportunity, family stability, and successful reentry.

Limitations and Risks

Award Adequacy

The maximum grant often covers only a portion of tuition and living costs, especially at four-year institutions and in high-cost regions.

Living Expenses

Housing, food, transportation, childcare, and lost work time can exceed tuition and create barriers that the grant does not fully address.

Lifetime Limit

Students who attend part time, change programs, repeat courses, lose credits in transfer, or require remediation can exhaust eligibility before completing a credential.

Administrative Complexity

FAFSA, verification, tax-data problems, dependency rules, identity checks, institutional paperwork, and award recalculation can delay access.

Price Response

Debate continues over whether institutions capture some aid through higher prices. Research varies by sector, program, market power, and period.

Institutional Quality

The federal government can approve aid at programs with weak completion, employment, transfer, or earnings outcomes. Students can lose time and lifetime eligibility even when a program provides limited value.

Budget Volatility

The maximum award and program rules depend on Congress. A projected shortfall can produce proposals to reduce awards or narrow eligibility.

Outside-Aid Exclusion

The 2026 rule can make a student ineligible for Pell when outside grants and scholarships fully cover COA, potentially changing how institutions and scholarship providers coordinate aid.

Major Policy Debates

Maximum Award

Supporters of expansion seek to double the maximum or restore a larger share of college cost. Fiscal critics emphasize federal spending and question whether larger subsidies improve completion or encourage price growth.

Automatic Inflation Adjustment

An automatic formula could preserve purchasing power. Annual congressional control allows budget flexibility but creates uncertainty.

Entitlement Status

Making Pell a true mandatory entitlement could stabilize funding. Opponents argue that Congress should retain annual appropriations control.

Short-Term Programs

Workforce Pell can broaden access to rapid credentials. Critics warn that short programs can have weak transferability, narrow occupational value, and high risk of aggressive recruitment.

Institutional Accountability

Debate concerns whether Pell eligibility should depend more heavily on completion, earnings, debt, price, transfer, licensure, or other outcome measures.

College Completion

A grant can support enrollment, but completion may require advising, tutoring, emergency aid, childcare, housing, food, mental health care, and academic reform.

Lifetime Limit

The six-year equivalent limits cost and encourages progress, but can disadvantage part-time students, adult learners, and students harmed by poor institutional transfer practices.

Prison Education

Supporters emphasize reentry, employment, safety, and reduced recidivism. Opponents question federal aid for incarcerated people when many nonincarcerated students have unmet need.

State and Institutional Supplanting

Federal grants can encourage states and colleges to reduce their own aid unless maintenance, pricing, and transparency policies preserve additional support.

Key Distinctions

Pell Grant and Student Loan

Pell is gift aid that normally does not require repayment. A student loan creates a debt obligation with repayment terms and interest rules.

Pell Grant and Scholarship

Pell is a federal need-based grant determined through statutory eligibility. Scholarships can be awarded by governments, institutions, foundations, employers, or private organizations using need, merit, service, identity, or other criteria.

Pell Grant and FSEOG

Pell is available to all eligible students at participating institutions without a fixed institutional allotment. FSEOG funding is limited and allocated to institutions, which select recipients under federal priority rules.

Maximum Pell and Maximum Payment

Maximum-Pell eligibility establishes the Scheduled Award. Actual payment can be lower because of part-time attendance, partial-year enrollment, COA, withdrawal, or lifetime use.

SAI and Family Payment

SAI is an aid index. It is not an invoice, expected bill, or guarantee of what a family will contribute.

Scheduled Award and Year-Round Pell

The Scheduled Award represents 100 percent. Year-Round Pell permits total annual receipt up to 150 percent.

Pell Eligibility and Institutional Admission

A Pell-eligible student must still be admitted to and enrolled in an eligible program. Financial eligibility does not guarantee admission.

Traditional Pell and Workforce Pell

Traditional Pell supports eligible undergraduate degree and certificate programs. Workforce Pell supports approved high-quality short-term programs under separate requirements.

Prison Education and Ordinary Campus Programs

Prison Education Programs require correctional oversight, specialized approval, student protections, and restricted COA and credit-balance rules.

Pell Eligibility and Receipt

A FAFSA applicant can be eligible but never receive a grant because the student does not enroll, chooses an ineligible school or program, fails another requirement, or does not complete institutional processing.

Geographic Scope

Pell Grants are available to eligible students attending participating domestic institutions across the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and United States territories.

Students from rural communities, Tribal lands, cities, suburbs, and United States territories use Pell at eligible public, nonprofit, and proprietary institutions.

Eligibility calculations account for state of residence when applying poverty-guideline thresholds. Alaska and Hawaii use poverty guidelines different from the contiguous states.

Ordinary Pell cannot be used to enroll directly at a foreign institution. An eligible domestic institution can include an approved study-abroad period in the student’s program and award Pell through the home school.

Workforce Pell depends partly on state identification of high-demand fields and state certification processes, creating geographic differences in eligible short-term programs.

Prison Education Program availability depends on partnerships among colleges, accrediting agencies, correctional oversight entities, and federal approval. Access therefore varies widely by state and correctional institution.

Summary

The Federal Pell Grant Program is the central federal grant supporting students with financial need in postsecondary education. Congress created it in 1972 as the Basic Educational Opportunity Grant and renamed it for Senator Claiborne Pell in 1980.

Students apply through the FAFSA. Eligibility is determined through maximum, calculated, minimum, or Special Rule pathways using tax filing, adjusted gross income, poverty guidelines, family circumstances, Student Aid Index, and other federal requirements.

For 2026–2027, the maximum Scheduled Award is $7,395 and the minimum is $740. An SAI of $14,790 or more generally makes an applicant ineligible, except under the Special Rule.

Actual awards depend on enrollment intensity, cost of attendance, academic calendar, program length, payment periods, attendance, prior disbursements, and remaining lifetime eligibility.

Students can receive up to 150 percent of the Scheduled Award through Year-Round Pell. All receipt counts toward the 600-percent lifetime limit, approximately twelve full-time semesters.

Most recipients are undergraduates without a bachelor’s degree. Limited exceptions exist for qualifying postbaccalaureate teacher certification, approved Workforce Pell programs, and eligible Prison Education Programs.

Workforce Pell became effective July 1, 2026 and extends grants to approved short-term programs tied to high-demand occupations and performance standards. Prison education eligibility was restored in 2023 after a long statutory ban.

Pell is generally gift aid and does not require repayment. A student can nevertheless owe funds after withdrawal, failure to begin attendance, loss of eligibility, fraud, or overpayment.

The program reaches approximately seven million students and distributed nearly $40 billion in school year 2024–2025. It is especially important to community colleges, HBCUs, adult learners, independent students, first-generation students, and households with limited income and wealth.

Pell expands access but rarely covers the full cost of tuition, housing, food, transportation, and childcare. Many recipients still work substantial hours, borrow, attend part time, or leave college with unmet need.

The program’s continuing effectiveness depends on award adequacy, simple and reliable FAFSA administration, institutional quality, accurate disbursement, consumer protection, successful completion, transferability, workforce value, congressional funding, and coordination with state and institutional aid.
