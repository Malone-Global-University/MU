Project Safe Neighborhoods

The nationwide Justice Department strategy launched in 2001 to reduce gun, gang, and violent crime through U.S. Attorney–led partnerships, data-driven targeting, federal and local prosecution, prevention, community engagement, research, and district formula grants.

What Is Project Safe Neighborhoods?

Project Safe Neighborhoods is a national strategy for reducing violent crime through locally tailored partnerships led by United States Attorneys.

Each federal district identifies its most serious violent-crime problems, develops a district plan, selects targeted areas or offenders, coordinates federal and nonfederal agencies, and directs grant resources toward the chosen strategy.

PSN is not one police unit, one prosecution statute, or one mandatory intervention. It is an organizing framework combining enforcement, prosecution, research, community engagement, prevention, intervention, reentry, communication, and accountability.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s launch, intellectual foundations, federal administration, district organization, historical and current design elements, statutory authorization, allocation formula, fiscal agents, district plans, enforcement strategies, prevention and intervention, grant management, evaluation evidence, racial and civil-rights concerns, policy shifts, and current 2026 status.

It distinguishes PSN from Project Exile, Operation Ceasefire, Weed and Seed, Project Guardian, Project Safe Childhood, Project Safe Neighborhoods prosecutions generally, and other Justice Department initiatives.

Geographic Scope

PSN operates through the ninety-four federal judicial districts covering the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, United States Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

A district can be statewide or cover only part of a state. Some states contain two, three, or four federal districts.

District plans can focus on selected cities, neighborhoods, corridors, rural regions, reservations, criminal networks, or offense patterns rather than the entire district.

Historical Context

PSN emerged after violent crime had declined from early-1990s peaks but firearm homicide, illegal gun markets, gang violence, and repeat offending remained serious concerns.

Federal policymakers sought to combine successful local gun-violence strategies with increased federal firearms prosecution and a nationwide structure led by United States Attorneys.

The program drew from focused deterrence, problem-oriented policing, federal-state case screening, data analysis, community outreach, and strategic prosecution.

Boston Operation Ceasefire

Boston’s Operation Ceasefire used a problem-solving partnership to identify youth gun violence, focus attention on violent groups, communicate consequences, coordinate enforcement, and connect people with services.

Its focused-deterrence model influenced PSN’s partnerships, offender-notification meetings, strategic targeting, and communication components.

Richmond Project Exile

Project Exile emphasized federal prosecution of prohibited firearm possession and firearm crime in Richmond, Virginia.

It used police-prosecutor case screening, federal penalties, public advertising, and coordination between local and federal authorities.

PSN incorporated elements of this prosecution-focused model while allowing broader locally tailored strategies.

Strategic Approaches to Community Safety Initiative

The Strategic Approaches to Community Safety Initiative operated in ten cities during the late 1990s.

SACSI emphasized research partnerships, local problem analysis, multidisciplinary teams, targeted strategy, and evaluation.

These features became important parts of PSN strategic planning and accountability.

Launch on May 14, 2001

President George W. Bush and Attorney General John Ashcroft announced Project Safe Neighborhoods in Philadelphia on May 14, 2001.

The administration proposed approximately $558.8 million over two years, including funding for federal and state prosecutors, investigators, training, and community outreach.

Every United States Attorney was directed to establish a district partnership against gun crime.

Original Gun-Crime Focus

Early PSN concentrated on unlawful firearm possession, firearm use in violent or drug crimes, firearm trafficking, armed career criminals, domestic-violence firearm prohibitions, and prosecution under federal or state law.

Districts selected the jurisdiction and charge offering the strongest lawful and appropriate response.

Five Original Core Elements

  1. Partnerships.
  2. Strategic planning.
  3. Training.
  4. Community outreach and public awareness.
  5. Accountability.

The elements were intended to support local variation rather than impose one national enforcement tactic.

Original Partnership Element

United States Attorneys convened federal, state, local, and tribal law enforcement; prosecutors; probation and parole; researchers; service providers; and community organizations.

Partnerships enabled coordinated case screening, intelligence sharing, targeted enforcement, prevention, and public communication.

Original Strategic-Planning Element

Districts were expected to define the local gun-crime problem using data rather than assume that the same offenders, locations, or markets drove violence everywhere.

Strategic plans identified target areas, priority offenders, legal tools, resources, partners, communication, and expected outcomes.

Original Training Element

Training supported prosecutors, investigators, patrol officers, analysts, probation officers, task-force members, and community partners.

Topics included federal firearm law, case preparation, tracing, intelligence, gang investigation, officer safety, evidence, prosecution, and strategic implementation.

Original Outreach Element

Public-awareness campaigns communicated firearm laws, consequences, safety, and program priorities.

Outreach included advertising, school and community presentations, offender-notification meetings, victim engagement, gun-lock distribution, and partnerships with faith and community institutions.

Original Accountability Element

Districts were expected to track implementation, prosecutions, crime trends, partnerships, outreach, and results.

Research partners helped identify whether strategies were implemented as planned and whether target-area violence changed.

Federal Firearms Prosecution

PSN increased emphasis on federal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. 922 and 924.

Federal cases could provide mandatory penalties, limited parole, broader jurisdiction, investigative resources, and transfer away from local criminal networks.

Federal prosecution also raised questions about sentence severity, racial disparity, forum selection, federalism, and duplication of state authority.

State and Local Prosecution

Not every PSN case was prosecuted federally.

District teams screened cases and could select state or local prosecution when charges, evidence, sentencing, victim needs, speed, or local policy made that forum more appropriate.

Case Screening

Prosecutors and law-enforcement partners reviewed firearm arrests and violent-crime investigations to identify the most appropriate charge and jurisdiction.

Screening considered criminal history, conduct, firearm use, gang role, trafficking, evidence, victim risk, state law, federal law, and district priorities.

Role of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives

ATF provides firearm tracing, ballistics intelligence, trafficking investigations, federal firearm-law expertise, task-force participation, National Integrated Ballistic Information Network support, and investigative resources.

ATF has been one of PSN’s central federal partners since the program’s creation.

Role of the Federal Bureau of Investigation

The FBI participates in violent-crime, gang, organized-crime, trafficking, intelligence, and task-force investigations.

Its role varies according to the district’s priority networks, offenses, and existing federal task forces.

Role of the United States Marshals Service

The Marshals Service supports fugitive investigations, violent-offender apprehension, witness security, task forces, and federal custody.

Fugitive operations can be integrated with PSN strategies targeting people wanted for serious violence or firearm offenses.

Role of the Drug Enforcement Administration

DEA participates when violent crime is connected to drug-trafficking organizations, armed distribution networks, overdose markets, gangs, or transnational groups.

Current PSN priorities place greater emphasis on dismantling drug-trafficking networks linked to violence.

Probation, Parole, and Community Supervision

Federal and state supervision agencies can identify high-risk conduct, enforce conditions, support notification meetings, share lawful information, and connect people with services.

Intensive supervision can deter violence but can also increase technical violations and reincarceration when not paired with proportionality and support.

Research Partners

PSN encourages partnerships with universities, analysts, research organizations, crime centers, and public-health institutions.

Researchers help diagnose violence, map networks, identify hot spots, design strategy, measure implementation, and evaluate results.

Research independence and data quality are essential when enforcement agencies are also responsible for defining success.

Data Analysis

District teams use crime reports, shootings, homicides, firearm recoveries, ballistic links, criminal histories, social networks, victimization, probation data, intelligence, and community information.

Analysis should distinguish people at high risk from broad demographic categories and avoid treating association, location, or prior contact as proof of criminal conduct.

Hot-Spot Strategies

Hot-spot policing concentrates resources at small places where violence is highly clustered.

PSN grants can support patrol, investigation, outreach, environmental change, and problem solving in targeted locations.

Sites should monitor displacement, community burden, and whether enforcement is narrowly connected to violent crime.

Focused Deterrence

Focused deterrence identifies people or groups at highest risk of serious violence, communicates legal consequences, coordinates enforcement, and offers assistance or alternatives.

The approach is associated with group-violence intervention, offender-notification meetings, call-ins, custom notifications, and service referrals.

Offender-Notification Meetings

Notification meetings bring selected people under supervision or identified as high risk together with law enforcement, prosecutors, community representatives, service providers, and sometimes people affected by violence.

Participants hear a direct message about consequences, community norms, available help, and the desire to prevent future harm.

Selection and communication require due-process, accuracy, voluntariness, and nondiscrimination safeguards.

Custom Notifications

Custom notifications deliver an individualized message to a person believed to face elevated risk of shooting, being shot, or participating in violent retaliation.

They can include legal information, service offers, family engagement, conflict mediation, and warning of focused enforcement.

Gang Focus

DOJ expanded PSN in 2006 to include enhanced anti-gang strategies.

Gang-related projects can involve investigations, intelligence, prosecution, prevention, intervention, reentry, outreach, witness support, and task forces.

Definitions of gang membership require care because unreliable designations can produce stigma, surveillance, enhanced charges, and unequal treatment.

The 2006 Anti-Gang Expansion

The 2006 expansion used PSN partnerships to address violent street gangs as well as firearm offenses.

Additional grants, training, technical assistance, and anti-gang coordination broadened the initiative beyond its original gun-prosecution identity.

Project ChildSafe and Firearm Safety

Early PSN included firearm-safety outreach and distribution of gun-lock kits through Project ChildSafe.

Safe-storage education addressed unintentional shootings, youth access, theft, and firearm diversion.

Safety distribution was a related prevention component rather than the main PSN enforcement strategy.

Reentry

Reentry assistance can reduce future violence by addressing housing, employment, identification, treatment, family connection, supervision, and community support.

Later PSN models more explicitly included reentry alongside enforcement and prevention.

Victims and Witnesses

Victim advocates and service providers can participate in district teams and grant projects.

Relevant needs include safety planning, trauma services, compensation referral, witness support, relocation, language access, family assistance, and communication concerning cases.

Community Outreach

Outreach seeks to explain the strategy, obtain local information, build legitimacy, communicate consequences, connect people with services, and involve residents in prevention.

One-way public messaging does not constitute meaningful engagement if residents cannot influence priorities or raise concerns about enforcement.

Faith and Community Organizations

Faith-based and community organizations can provide credibility, violence interruption, mentoring, victim support, reentry, youth programs, conflict mediation, and service referral.

Federal assistance remains subject to constitutional and civil-rights rules concerning religion and nondiscrimination.

Prevention and Intervention

PSN prevention and intervention can include youth development, street outreach, hospital-based violence intervention, cognitive-behavioral programs, mentoring, treatment, victim services, conflict mediation, community violence intervention, and support for people at highest risk.

The 2018 statute expressly recognizes evidence-based and data-driven intervention and prevention.

Community Violence Intervention

Community violence intervention uses trusted messengers, outreach workers, violence interrupters, case managers, hospitals, and community organizations to prevent shootings and retaliation.

CVI can complement PSN enforcement but requires clear confidentiality, referral, data-sharing, and role boundaries.

Hospital-Based Violence Intervention

Hospital programs engage survivors of violent injury during a period when risk, trauma, and willingness to accept help can be high.

Services can include safety planning, counselling, housing, employment, legal support, relocation, and conflict mediation.

Youth Prevention

Youth projects can address firearm access, gang recruitment, retaliation, school disengagement, trauma, family support, recreation, mentoring, and employment.

Prevention should avoid criminalizing young people based on neighborhood, friendships, clothing, or broad risk categories.

Public-Awareness Campaigns

PSN campaigns have used billboards, radio, television, social media, school programs, community meetings, posters, and direct notifications.

Early slogans emphasized severe firearm penalties. Later outreach more often combined deterrence with services, legitimacy, prevention, and community safety.

The 2017 Reinvigoration

Attorney General Jeff Sessions announced a reinvigoration of PSN on October 5, 2017.

The program became the centerpiece of DOJ’s violent-crime reduction strategy and required each United States Attorney to develop a localized plan using data, technology, partnerships, targeted enforcement, prevention, and accountability.

Five 2017 Design Features

  1. Leadership by the United States Attorney.
  2. Partnerships with law enforcement and community stakeholders.
  3. Targeted and prioritized enforcement.
  4. Prevention.
  5. Accountability for results.

United States Attorney Leadership

The United States Attorney directs the district strategy, convenes the PSN team, establishes priorities, coordinates prosecutors, certifies the fiscal agent, and ensures alignment with current DOJ policy.

Central leadership can improve coordination but can also cause the program to shift rapidly when national administrations or individual U.S. Attorneys change.

Targeted and Prioritized Enforcement

Targeted enforcement seeks to focus limited resources on the people, networks, locations, and conduct most connected to serious violence.

The model rejects indiscriminate enforcement in theory, but targeting methods require validation, oversight, and periodic removal of outdated information.

Project Safe Neighborhoods Grant Program Authorization Act of 2018

President Donald Trump signed Public Law 115-185 on June 18, 2018.

The act established the Project Safe Neighborhoods Block Grant Program within OJP and codified its purposes at 34 U.S.C. 60701–60705.

Before 2018, PSN operated through administrative direction and annual appropriations without a dedicated permanent statutory chapter.

Statutory Purpose

The statute seeks sustained reductions in violent crime by fostering partnerships among federal, state, and local agencies, United States Attorneys, affected communities, victim advocates, and researchers.

It authorizes comprehensive strategic plans, evidence-based prevention and intervention, and collection of outcome data including violent-crime rates, incarceration, and recidivism.

Comprehensive Strategic Plans

Statutory plans can include enforcement of firearm laws and prioritization of individuals or organizations identified as responsible for increased violence in a geographic area.

Plans should connect enforcement to locally diagnosed violence rather than rely only on general firearm prosecution.

Statutory Prevention and Intervention

The 2018 law recognizes juvenile-justice activity, street outreach, conflict mediation, treatment, social services, and changing community norms as violence-reduction tools.

This language confirms that PSN is legally broader than prosecution.

Additional Statutory Purpose Areas

The Attorney General may use authorized funds for:

  • competitive and evidence-based gun- and gang-violence reduction programs;
  • the Byrne Criminal Justice Innovation Program;
  • community-based violence prevention; and
  • gang and youth violence education, prevention, intervention, and related activity.

Local Control

The statute directs that grant funds be locally controlled to the greatest extent practicable.

Local control operates within federal priorities, district leadership, grant conditions, statutory set-asides, and appropriations restrictions.

Thirty-Percent Gang Task Force Set-Aside

Thirty percent of annual PSN grant funds must support gang task forces in regions experiencing significant or increased criminal or transnational organizations engaged in high levels of violent crime, firearm offenses, human trafficking, and drug trafficking.

Current applicants must explain how the district will satisfy the set-aside even when the local violence problem is not primarily described as gang-driven.

Aggravating or Leadership Role Priority

The statute directs priority toward investigation and prosecution of people with aggravating or leadership roles in qualifying criminal or transnational organizations.

This priority aims to focus on people directing or intensifying violence rather than treating every associated person equally.

Expiration of the Authorization Period

Public Law 115-185 authorized $50 million for each of fiscal years 2019 through 2021.

The authorization period expired after fiscal year 2021.

Congress continued to appropriate funds, so the program remained operational despite the expired authorization period.

Reauthorization Efforts

Bipartisan reauthorization bills were introduced in later Congresses to extend authorization, add technology and task-force support, and require additional reporting.

A Senate bill passed that chamber in 2023 but was not enacted, and new House and Senate bills were introduced in 2025.

As of August 2, 2026, the 2018 act remained the current enacted statutory authorization.

The 2021 Violent-Crime Strategy

On May 26, 2021, DOJ issued a comprehensive violent-crime strategy that strengthened PSN and made district PSN plans the overarching federal violent-crime framework.

The strategy emphasized legitimacy, community-based prevention, focused enforcement, and measurement of results.

It stated that the goal was violent-crime reduction rather than arrests or prosecutions as ends in themselves.

Trust and Legitimacy

The 2021 framework identified community trust, procedural justice, transparency, and accountability as foundations of effective violence reduction.

Trust affects witness cooperation, information sharing, service participation, compliance, and perceived fairness.

Community-Based Prevention

DOJ directed districts to support organizations capable of intervening before violence occurs and to incorporate community resources into PSN plans.

Prevention funding expanded beyond traditional law-enforcement recipients to community-based partners.

Focused Enforcement Priorities

The 2021 model required districts to identify the people, groups, places, and conduct driving violence rather than maximize enforcement volume.

Firearm traffickers, violent groups, repeat shooters, and networks connected to serious violence could receive priority.

Measuring Results

Districts were directed to measure reductions in violent crime, shootings, victimization, and other outcomes rather than count only arrests, charges, or convictions.

Measurement should account for implementation, displacement, community effects, fairness, and sustainability.

The 2025 Policy Shift

In 2025, DOJ aligned PSN with Operation Take Back America.

Current policy places greater emphasis on rapid charging, enhanced penalties where appropriate, dangerous offenders, criminal and transnational organizations, drug trafficking, human trafficking, immigration enforcement, and accountability through district plans and reporting.

The 2021 language remains important to the program’s history, but the fiscal year 2025–2026 funding opportunity uses a different three-principle framework.

Operation Take Back America

Operation Take Back America is the current Justice Department policy framework with which PSN district plans must align.

PSN serves as a district-level mechanism for coordinating violent-crime, gang, trafficking, organized-crime, and related enforcement priorities.

Current Three Core Principles

  1. Rapid response to violent crime through prompt charging of serious, readily provable offenses and enhanced penalties where appropriate.
  2. Strategic partnerships and information sharing among federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial public-safety partners.
  3. Accountability and deterrence through district plans, reporting, measurement, and assessment of progress.

PSN District Plan

The PSN District Plan replaced the term “PSN Strategic Plan” in current guidance.

It identifies the district’s violence problems, target areas, priority networks or offenders, enforcement approach, prevention resources, partners, grant priorities, and measures.

The plan guides how grant funds are used but is not necessarily published in full because it can contain law-enforcement-sensitive information.

PSN Team

Each district maintains a PSN Team under the direction of the United States Attorney’s Office.

The team can include federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial law enforcement; prosecutors; analysts; probation; community organizations; service providers; victims’ representatives; researchers; and local officials.

PSN Coordinator

The PSN Coordinator serves as the principal liaison among the United States Attorney’s Office, team members, fiscal agent, selection committee, subrecipients, and BJA.

The coordinator supports meetings, planning, application development, communication, implementation, and reporting.

Selection Committee

Current grant-funding decisions must be made by a nonconflicted, nonfederally employed selection committee.

The committee translates district priorities into subawards or contracts while avoiding federal employees’ direct selection of grant recipients.

Certified Fiscal Agent

BJA awards formula funds to a fiscal agent certified by the relevant United States Attorney.

Eligible agents can include states, local governments, educational institutions, faith-based or community organizations, nonprofit organizations, tribal nonprofits, and federally recognized tribes.

The fiscal agent must have the capacity to manage federal money and subawards.

Fiscal Agent Responsibilities

Responsibilities include:

  • submitting the district application;
  • developing subaward notices or contracts;
  • making payments;
  • maintaining financial records;
  • monitoring subrecipients;
  • submitting federal reports;
  • processing grant modifications;
  • cooperating with audits;
  • ensuring compliance with award conditions; and
  • keeping the PSN Team informed about fiscal and administrative issues.

Subawards and Contracts

The fiscal agent generally distributes funds to agencies or organizations carrying out the district strategy.

Subrecipients can include law-enforcement agencies, prosecutors, local governments, research partners, community organizations, service providers, and training institutions.

The federal fiscal agent remains responsible for monitoring and compliance.

Formula Grant Structure

Since the 2018 authorization, PSN has operated as a district formula grant rather than a national open competition among all local applicants.

Formula amounts are assigned to federal judicial districts, while local competition or selection occurs through the district process.

District Minimum Allocation

Each district receives an initial $50,000 amount under the current formula.

The minimum ensures that small districts can maintain a grant-supported strategy but reduces the share distributed strictly by population and violent crime.

Population Factor

Fifty percent of funds remaining after district minimums is allocated according to each district’s population.

Population recognizes the scale of the jurisdiction but does not by itself measure violent-crime concentration or fiscal need.

Violent-Crime Factor

The other fifty percent is allocated according to each district’s share of the latest three-year average of Part 1 violent crimes.

Reported crime data can be affected by agency participation, reporting systems, classification, and changes in public reporting.

Part 1 Violent Crime

The allocation formula uses reported murder and nonnegligent manslaughter, rape, robbery, and aggravated assault.

The formula does not directly measure shootings, firearm trafficking, gang activity, victimization not reported to police, or community fiscal capacity.

Combined Territorial Allocation

Guam, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands share one allocation.

This produces ninety-three formula awards for ninety-four federal judicial districts.

Fiscal Year 2025–2026 Funding

BJA combined fiscal year 2025 and fiscal year 2026 appropriations into one funding opportunity.

The expected total is $27,392,795, with published district allocations totalling $27,344,103.

The combined structure is intended to support sustained multiyear implementation rather than separate short annual projects.

These amounts are combined with fiscal year 2026 allocations in the current application.

Small District Allocations

Minimum allocations preserve nationwide participation but can limit the number or scale of local subawards.

Award Period

Current awards have a forty-eight-month project period beginning October 1, 2026.

Multiyear periods allow planning, subaward competition, implementation, investigation, prevention, and evaluation, but can contribute to slow spending when administration is weak.

No Match Requirement

The fiscal year 2025–2026 PSN formula grant does not require nonfederal cost sharing.

Districts are encouraged to leverage other law-enforcement, service, research, and community resources.

Administrative Costs

A fiscal agent can retain up to 10 percent of the grant allocation for administration.

Additional money can be retained for substantive program activity when the selection committee determines that the fiscal agent is the appropriate implementing entity and documents the decision.

Application Process

The certified fiscal agent submits a Standard Form 424 through Grants.gov and the full application through JustGrants.

The application must reflect the district plan, PSN Team input, selection-committee decisions, budget, implementation timeline, subawards, outcomes, and United States Attorney certification.

Current Fiscal Year 2026 Deadlines

The combined fiscal year 2025–2026 opportunity was posted July 13, 2026.

The Grants.gov deadline is August 20, 2026, and the JustGrants deadline is August 27, 2026.

Awards are expected to be announced by September 30, 2026.

Description of Need

Applications must identify the violent-crime issue, drivers, targeted geographic areas, relevant data, agencies involved, stakeholder input, and community resources.

Current guidance also asks applicants to describe criminal organizations, gangs, transnational organizations, trafficking, and immigration-related public-safety concerns where applicable.

Goals and Objectives

Goals describe the broad public-safety change sought, while objectives identify measurable results.

Current examples include reduced shootings, increased arrests, increased prosecution rates, dismantled networks, improved coordination, and implementation of district priorities.

Project Design and Implementation

Applications describe project activity, partners, subrecipients, budgets, data collection, research involvement, and frequency of analysis.

Design should explain why selected interventions are appropriate for the diagnosed violence problem.

Research Independence

Research partners should have access to necessary data and freedom to report credible results.

Current applications require a research and evaluation independence and integrity statement when applicable.

Grant Management

Recipients manage budgets, drawdowns, subawards, contracts, procurement, performance reporting, financial reporting, monitoring, property, records, modifications, and closeout.

A district’s strategic value does not excuse weak financial controls.

Financial-Management Training

Current fiscal-agent award administrators and financial managers must complete DOJ Grants Financial Management training within 120 days unless they have already satisfied the requirement.

Procurement

Purchases and contracts must follow federal standards and applicable state or local rules.

Competition, conflicts of interest, cost reasonableness, vendor oversight, suspension-and-debarment review, cybersecurity, and documentation can apply.

Performance Reporting

Recipients must submit regular progress and performance reports.

Measures can include shootings, homicides, arrests, prosecutions, convictions, investigations, networks disrupted, services, referrals, outreach, reentry, implementation, and grant activity.

Current Accountability Framework

Current policy uses PSN District Plans and annual reporting of accomplishments to assess progress and guide strategy.

Accountability should distinguish enforcement volume from sustained reductions in violence and victimization.

Civil-Rights Compliance

PSN recipients and participating agencies must comply with the Constitution, federal civil-rights laws, DOJ regulations, and award conditions.

Federal funding does not authorize discriminatory targeting, unlawful searches, excessive force, coercive community engagement, retaliation, or denial of due process.

Current Nondiscrimination Condition

The fiscal year 2025–2026 funding notice excludes activity that violates federal civil-rights or nondiscrimination law.

It also excludes programs that unlawfully favour or disadvantage people on the basis of race or another protected classification.

Current Immigration-Related Conditions

The current funding notice treats programs that violate or impede federal immigration law as outside the grant’s scope.

It identifies 8 U.S.C. 1373, access by federal immigration officers, detainer-related cooperation, and immigration-enforcement coordination.

Immigration grant conditions have generated substantial litigation under prior DOJ programs, and recipients must review current law and award terms carefully.

Legal-Services Restriction

Current guidance generally treats federal grant support for legal services to removable or unlawfully present noncitizens as unallowable, subject to stated exceptions for crime-victim protection orders and services expressly authorized or required by law or judicial ruling.

Race and Disparate Impact

PSN frequently focuses on neighborhoods and groups already experiencing high levels of police contact, firearm victimization, poverty, segregation, and incarceration.

Federal firearm prosecutions can produce severe sentences and racial disparities even when targeting is described as data driven.

Evaluation should examine who is selected, who receives services, who is prosecuted, sentence differences, geographic burden, and community safety outcomes.

Gang Databases and Network Analysis

Gang and social-network data can help identify patterns of violence, but inaccurate or broad criteria can label people who have not committed violent offenses.

Safeguards should include documented criteria, source reliability, periodic review, correction procedures, limits on dissemination, and removal of outdated designations.

Federalization of Local Crime

PSN permits conduct traditionally prosecuted by states to be charged federally when federal firearm or other jurisdiction exists.

Supporters argue that federal prosecution adds resources, consistency, and incapacitation.

Critics argue that forum selection can increase sentences, bypass local reform policy, and expand federal power over local crime.

Mandatory and Enhanced Penalties

Federal firearm statutes can impose mandatory minimums, consecutive terms, enhanced penalties for prior convictions, and long sentences for firearm use in crimes of violence or drug trafficking.

Current policy encourages the most serious readily provable charges and enhanced penalties where appropriate.

Domestic Violence and Firearm Prohibitions

PSN districts can prioritize illegal firearm possession by people subject to qualifying domestic-violence convictions or protection orders.

Enforcement should coordinate with victim safety planning, advocates, firearm surrender procedures, and state domestic-violence systems.

Firearm Trafficking

Firearm trafficking supplies weapons to prohibited possessors, gangs, drug networks, domestic abusers, and people planning violence.

PSN can coordinate tracing, straw-purchase investigations, interstate cases, dealers, ballistic intelligence, and prosecution.

Ballistic Intelligence and NIBIN

The National Integrated Ballistic Information Network compares cartridge-case evidence and can connect shootings, recovered firearms, and crime scenes.

Ballistic leads require timely collection, laboratory processing, investigative follow-up, quality control, and interpretation.

Crime Gun Intelligence Centers

Crime Gun Intelligence Centers integrate firearm tracing, NIBIN, analysis, investigators, prosecutors, and partner agencies.

PSN funding and strategy can support CGIC development and use.

Technology

PSN projects can support data systems, intelligence platforms, digital evidence, ballistics, cameras, communications, analytical software, and other tools connected to violent-crime reduction.

Technology requires privacy, cybersecurity, accuracy, procurement, retention, transparency, and bias safeguards.

Multijurisdictional Task Forces

Task forces combine personnel and authority from multiple agencies to investigate gangs, firearm trafficking, organized crime, fugitives, drugs, and violent networks.

Shared operations require clear command, use-of-force policy, evidence procedures, disclosure, civil-rights oversight, fiscal controls, and public accountability.

Tribal Participation

Tribal governments and law-enforcement agencies can participate in district teams, receive subawards, serve as certified fiscal agents, and coordinate cases involving Indian country.

Tribal involvement must respect sovereignty, jurisdiction, treaty rights, federal trust responsibilities, and local community priorities.

Territorial Participation

United States territories participate through their federal judicial arrangements and local law-enforcement institutions.

Geographic isolation, limited staffing, shipping cost, small populations, tourism, trafficking routes, and local legal structures shape territorial strategies.

Rural Districts

Rural PSN strategies can address dispersed violence, drug trafficking, domestic violence, limited forensic capacity, tribal jurisdiction, long travel distances, and small-agency resource constraints.

A city-centered model cannot be transferred automatically to rural districts.

Urban Districts

Urban districts often focus on small geographic concentrations of shootings, gang or crew networks, firearm markets, carjackings, robbery, and retaliation.

Dense data and institutional capacity can support precise targeting but also create intensive surveillance and enforcement burdens.

Crime Trends and Attribution

PSN has operated during periods of national violent-crime decline, increase, pandemic disruption, local reform, economic change, and shifting firearm markets.

Changes in one district cannot be attributed to PSN solely because cases were described as PSN prosecutions.

The 2009 National Evaluation

An NIJ-sponsored evaluation compared violent-crime trends in PSN target cities and other cities and examined implementation across districts.

Researchers found larger violent-crime reductions in PSN target cities and stronger results where federal gun prosecution and implementation were more intensive.

The study also emphasized variation among districts and cautioned that implementation quality mattered.

The 4.1-Percent Finding

The national evaluation reported an average 4.1-percent violent-crime decline in eighty-two PSN target cities compared with a 0.9-percent decline in 170 non-target cities.

This association supported PSN’s evidence base but did not prove that every local strategy was effective.

Federal Prosecution Intensity

Target cities in districts with high federal gun-prosecution intensity experienced larger estimated reductions than cities in low-prosecution districts.

The finding can reflect prosecution, partnership strength, strategic focus, implementation quality, or unmeasured district differences.

Nine-City Analysis

In-depth evaluation of nine cities found declines in gun-related violence after implementation, with local variation in strategy and effect.

Case studies illustrated that PSN was not one treatment and that local implementation shaped outcomes.

Detroit Evaluation

An NIJ evaluation of a Detroit gun-violence initiative found a small but statistically significant reduction during the intensive intervention period.

The effect weakened after concentrated activity subsided, illustrating the challenge of sustaining reductions.

Chicago Evidence

Chicago PSN combined federal prosecution, offender-notification forums, police activity, and community partners.

Research has reported reductions associated with the strategy while also examining whether effects persisted and whether federal prosecution or focused-deterrence communication drove change.

Multilevel Evaluation of the 2018 Model

NIJ funded a multilevel evaluation examining PSN implementation, violent crime, arrests, prosecutions, partnerships, and the five-feature 2018 model.

The research found substantial variation in district priorities, partnership composition, enforcement, prevention, and use of analysis.

National Portrait of PSN Implementation

A 2024 NIJ publication reported that districts focused on varied violent crimes, with felon-in-possession cases common across PSN strategies.

The study reinforced that PSN implementation differs substantially among federal districts.

Evaluation Limitations

Evaluation is difficult because districts select their own strategies, target areas, partners, offenses, and measures.

Areas receiving PSN often have unusually serious violence and concurrent interventions.

Arrests, prosecutions, crime reports, shootings, and community perceptions can move in different directions.

Sustainability

Durable violence reduction requires continued analysis, community trust, prevention capacity, prosecution coordination, staffing, and funding after a grant period.

A temporary surge in enforcement can reduce violence briefly without changing the conditions producing future conflict.

Displacement

Focused operations can move violence, firearm markets, or criminal networks to nearby locations, different times, or new methods.

Evaluation should examine surrounding areas and possible diffusion of benefits as well as displacement.

Community-Trust Risks

Trust can decline when PSN is experienced primarily as raids, surveillance, severe federal sentencing, broad gang designation, or publicity surrounding prosecutions.

Meaningful engagement requires residents to influence priorities and receive transparent information about goals, safeguards, and results.

Balance Between Enforcement and Prevention

Enforcement agencies have established personnel, authority, and data, while community organizations often receive smaller, shorter, and administratively burdensome grants.

A formally comprehensive strategy can therefore become enforcement dominated unless prevention funding and community participation are protected.

Arrests and Prosecutions Are Not Ends in Themselves

The 2021 DOJ strategy explicitly stated that the objective was reducing violent crime, not increasing arrests or prosecutions as ends in themselves.

Current policy gives greater attention to arrests and prosecution rates, but sustained public safety remains the program’s statutory purpose.

Appropriate Metrics

Useful measures include:

  • homicides and nonfatal shootings;
  • violent victimization;
  • retaliatory incidents;
  • firearm recoveries and trafficking disruption;
  • case processing and outcomes;
  • services and successful referrals;
  • community trust and perceived safety;
  • racial and geographic disparities;
  • complaints and civil-rights incidents;
  • recidivism and reentry;
  • implementation fidelity;
  • cost; and
  • sustainability after intensive activity ends.

Funding History

Early PSN resources exceeded the amount shown in modern PSN formula grants because the initiative included prosecutors, federal agents, training, technology, firearm-safety programs, research, and related appropriations.

The Bush administration described more than $1 billion committed during the program’s first years.

Modern formula grants are a smaller portion of the broader federal personnel and operational resources supporting district PSN activity.

How Funding Figures Should Be Interpreted

Historical statements concerning billions of dollars can include federal salaries, prosecutors, task forces, grants, training, gun locks, research, and associated programs.

The current $27.4 million funding opportunity refers specifically to combined fiscal year 2025–2026 OJP formula grants.

These amounts should not be presented as directly comparable without explaining their scope.

Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Proposal

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 budget proposed $20 million for PSN, an increase of approximately $7 million over the proposed fiscal year 2026 program level used in the budget comparison.

A budget proposal does not determine final appropriations and should be distinguished from the open fiscal year 2025–2026 award.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

PSN remains active in all federal judicial districts.

The combined fiscal year 2025–2026 formula opportunity is open, with applications due in August 2026.

Current district plans are aligned with Operation Take Back America, use three core principles, and operate under Public Law 115-185 and annual appropriations.

Major Achievements

  • Created a nationwide violent-crime partnership in every federal judicial district.
  • Made United States Attorneys responsible for locally tailored violent-crime strategies.
  • Integrated federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial enforcement and prosecution.
  • Expanded firearm tracing, ballistic intelligence, case screening, and task-force coordination.
  • Promoted research partnerships and data-driven targeting.
  • Incorporated prevention, intervention, outreach, victims, and reentry into a prosecution-led framework.
  • Produced measurable violent-crime reductions in several national and local evaluations.
  • Provided formula funding to every district after 2018.
  • Created a statutory role for affected communities, victim advocates, and researchers.
  • Adapted across multiple administrations and changing violence problems.

Limitations and Failures

  • PSN implementation varies greatly by district.
  • Federal prosecution can dominate prevention and community engagement.
  • Severe federal sentences can increase incarceration and racial disparity.
  • Gang and network data can be inaccurate or overly broad.
  • Reported arrests and prosecutions do not establish sustained crime reduction.
  • Community organizations often possess less authority and administrative capacity than enforcement partners.
  • Program identity changes substantially between administrations.
  • Formula allocations are modest for large districts and complex strategies.
  • Targeted enforcement can displace violence.
  • Research findings do not prove that every district strategy is effective.
  • The authorization period expired after fiscal year 2021.
  • Public access to complete district plans and local grant decisions can be limited.

Major Controversies

Federal sentencing severity
Federal firearm charges can produce longer sentences than state prosecution for similar conduct.
Federalization
The program expands federal influence over violent crime traditionally handled by states and localities.
Race
Targeting and prosecution frequently affect Black and Latino communities already burdened by gun violence and incarceration.
Gang designation
Gang lists and network analysis can create consequences for association rather than proven violent conduct.
Enforcement-prevention balance
Prevention is formally included but often receives fewer resources and less institutional authority.
Immigration alignment
The current grant framework links violent-crime funding to immigration-enforcement cooperation and criminal-alien priorities.
Outcome claims
DOJ announcements often identify cases as PSN activity without proving that the program caused broader crime trends.
Administrative change
Districts must repeatedly revise plans when national policy changes.

How Project Safe Neighborhoods Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the federal district, policy period, target area, PSN plan, partners, fiscal agent, subrecipients, allocation, offense priorities, enforcement tactics, prevention activity, data sources, and measured outcomes.

Important distinctions include:

  • the national PSN initiative versus the PSN grant;
  • federal district activity versus one city project;
  • the 2001 model versus later revisions;
  • firearm prosecution versus comprehensive violence reduction;
  • federal prosecution versus state prosecution;
  • historical five elements versus current three principles;
  • the 2021 strategy versus the 2025–2026 strategy;
  • formula allocation versus subaward;
  • arrests and prosecutions versus violent-crime outcomes;
  • association data versus proven conduct;
  • target-area reduction versus displacement; and
  • authorization of appropriations versus enacted appropriations.

Why Project Safe Neighborhoods Matters

PSN transformed federal violent-crime policy by making each United States Attorney responsible for a locally tailored strategy rather than one national tactic.

It connected federal prosecution and investigative resources to local police, prosecutors, analysts, community organizations, prevention programs, victims, and researchers.

The initiative helped institutionalize firearm case screening, ballistic intelligence, focused deterrence, research partnerships, district violent-crime plans, and nationwide accountability.

Its central strength is adaptability: districts can respond to different violent-crime problems and resources.

Its central weakness is the same adaptability: PSN can range from a balanced community violence strategy to a prosecution-dominated label applied to individual federal cases.

The program’s history also demonstrates how federal violent-crime policy changes between administrations while retaining the same institutional name and district infrastructure.

Summary

Project Safe Neighborhoods is a nationwide violent-crime reduction initiative coordinated by United States Attorneys in all ninety-four federal judicial districts.

President George W. Bush and Attorney General John Ashcroft launched PSN on May 14, 2001 as “America’s Network Against Gun Violence.”

The original model combined partnerships, strategic planning, training, outreach, accountability, and increased federal firearm prosecution. DOJ expanded the initiative to violent street gangs in 2006.

Attorney General Jeff Sessions reinvigorated PSN in 2017 around U.S. Attorney leadership, partnerships, targeted enforcement, prevention, and accountability.

Public Law 115-185 established the Project Safe Neighborhoods Block Grant Program in 2018 and codified it at 34 U.S.C. 60701–60705. The statute recognizes strategic enforcement, community partnerships, prevention, treatment, social services, research, and outcome measurement.

DOJ’s 2021 violent-crime strategy emphasized trust and legitimacy, community-based prevention, focused enforcement, and results rather than arrests as ends in themselves.

Current 2025–2026 policy aligns PSN with Operation Take Back America and emphasizes rapid response, strategic partnerships, and accountability and deterrence.

BJA awards formula funds to United States Attorney–certified fiscal agents. Each district receives $50,000 before the remaining funds are divided equally by population and three-year Part 1 violent-crime share.

The combined fiscal year 2025–2026 funding opportunity expects $27,392,795 through ninety-three awards with forty-eight-month periods and no match requirement. Published district allocations total $27,344,103.

Thirty percent of grant funds must support qualifying gang-task-force activity, and fiscal agents can retain up to 10 percent for administration.

Research has associated strong PSN implementation with reductions in violent and gun crime, but effects vary substantially by district and cannot be inferred from prosecution counts alone.

PSN’s adaptability is both its strength and weakness. It can integrate research, prevention, community engagement, and focused enforcement, but can also become dominated by federal prosecution, severe sentencing, gang designation, and administration-specific priorities.

As of August 2, 2026, PSN remained active nationwide. The 2018 authorization period had expired, but annual appropriations continued to support the program.
