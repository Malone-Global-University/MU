Affordable Care Act Marketplace Program

The federal-state health-insurance exchange system created by the Affordable Care Act to organise the individual insurance market, compare qualified health plans, determine eligibility for financial assistance, and enrol eligible consumers in private coverage.

What Is the Affordable Care Act Marketplace Program?

The Affordable Care Act Marketplace Program is the system through which eligible individuals and families compare and enroll in regulated private health-insurance plans and apply for federal financial assistance.

The Marketplace is not itself an insurance company. It is a purchasing, eligibility, certification, enrollment, data, and consumer-assistance framework. Private insurers issue the health plans, collect premiums, establish provider networks, process claims, and administer covered benefits.

A Marketplace application evaluates potential eligibility for qualified health plans, advance premium tax credits, cost-sharing reductions, Medicaid, the Children’s Health Insurance Program, and—where applicable—the Basic Health Program.

The Marketplace serves primarily the individual health-insurance market. It is commonly used by self-employed people, workers without affordable job-based insurance, early retirees not yet eligible for Medicare, people between jobs, small-business owners, and families whose employer coverage does not satisfy federal affordability or minimum-value standards.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the individual-market exchanges created by the Affordable Care Act. It covers federal and state Marketplace structures, qualified health plans, essential health benefits, plan categories, premium tax credits, cost-sharing reductions, enrollment periods, eligibility, Medicaid coordination, insurance protections, risk adjustment, consumer assistance, civil-rights significance, major litigation, limitations, and policy conditions through August 2, 2026.

It discusses the Small Business Health Options Program only briefly because the modern individual Marketplace is the principal focus.

It is not an individual eligibility determination, insurance recommendation, tax opinion, or guarantee that a particular provider, drug, or service is covered. Consumers must review current Marketplace eligibility results, plan documents, provider directories, formularies, and tax guidance.

Historical Context

Before the Affordable Care Act, the individual insurance market operated under state law with limited federal standards. Insurers in many states could deny applications, exclude preexisting conditions, charge substantially different premiums based on health status or sex, impose annual or lifetime limits, and offer policies with significant benefit exclusions.

People receiving insurance through large employers generally benefited from group purchasing and federal employment-benefit protections. People buying coverage independently faced greater underwriting, administrative cost, price variation, and risk of rejection.

Federal health-reform proposals repeatedly considered insurance exchanges or purchasing cooperatives. The Clinton administration’s 1993–1994 reform plan included regional health alliances. Later proposals drew from state reforms, regulated purchasing pools, tax-credit models, and Massachusetts health reform.

Massachusetts enacted a state health-insurance exchange, individual coverage requirement, subsidies, Medicaid expansion, and insurance reforms in 2006. That model influenced national debate, though the Affordable Care Act differed in significant respects.

Creation of the Marketplaces

President Barack Obama signed the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act on March 23, 2010. The Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act made related amendments on March 30, 2010.

Section 1311 directed each state to establish an American Health Benefit Exchange. Section 1321 authorised HHS to establish and operate an exchange when a state did not do so or did not meet federal requirements.

The law created premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions, qualified health-plan certification, essential health benefits, risk-adjustment systems, insurance-market reforms, and a coordinated application for health coverage.

The statutory design combined federal regulation and financing with state insurance authority. States retained substantial responsibility for insurer licensing, rate review, benefit mandates, network rules, and enforcement, while federal law established nationwide minimum standards.

Marketplace Launch in 2013–2014

Open enrollment began on October 1, 2013, for coverage effective in 2014.

HealthCare.gov experienced severe technical failures involving account creation, identity management, application processing, insurer data, and system capacity. State exchanges had mixed performance; some launched successfully, while others experienced failures or later transferred functions to the federal platform.

Federal contractors and CMS rebuilt major system components during the enrollment period. By the end of the first period, millions of consumers had selected Marketplace plans.

The difficult launch became a major public-administration case study concerning procurement, systems integration, executive oversight, testing, transparency, and digital service delivery.

Federal-State Structure

The Marketplace system operates through several administrative models.

Federally facilitated Marketplace
CMS performs central eligibility, enrollment, plan display, consumer assistance, and related functions through HealthCare.gov, while states retain ordinary insurance-regulatory authority and may perform selected plan-management functions.
State-based Exchange on the federal platform
A state maintains legal responsibility for its Marketplace and selected functions but uses HealthCare.gov for eligibility and enrollment.
State-based Exchange with its own platform
The state operates its own eligibility and enrollment system, plan certification, consumer assistance, outreach, and Marketplace administration within federal standards.

States can change administrative models after federal approval and operational preparation. Transitions require coordination of accounts, applications, insurer data, tax-credit information, and consumer communication.

Marketplace Structure in 2026

For the 2026 plan year, thirty states used the HealthCare.gov eligibility and enrollment platform.

Twenty states and the District of Columbia operated state-based exchanges using their own eligibility and enrollment platforms.

State-based marketplaces can establish enrollment deadlines and administrative procedures that differ from the federal platform, provided they comply with federal law.

The division demonstrates that the Marketplace is a national statutory system rather than one website.

Eligibility to Enroll

Marketplace enrollment is generally available to people who:

  • reside in the United States;
  • are United States citizens, nationals, or lawfully present noncitizens under applicable federal rules;
  • are not incarcerated, subject to statutory exceptions; and
  • seek eligible individual-market coverage.

A person can buy Marketplace coverage without qualifying for financial assistance. Eligibility for a premium tax credit involves additional tax, income, coverage, filing-status, and household requirements.

Marketplace eligibility is not limited to people with low incomes. Consumers above the subsidy range can purchase full-price qualified health plans.

Qualified Health Plans

A qualified health plan, commonly abbreviated QHP, is a private insurance plan certified by a Marketplace.

A QHP must:

  • cover essential health benefits;
  • follow federal limits on cost sharing;
  • comply with actuarial-value requirements;
  • meet issuer, licensing, marketing, and enrollment standards;
  • maintain an adequate provider network under applicable federal and state rules;
  • include essential community providers under applicable standards;
  • provide required consumer information;
  • participate in quality reporting and improvement where required;
  • comply with nondiscrimination rules; and
  • receive annual Marketplace certification.

Certification does not mean every plan has the same providers, drugs, deductible, referral rules, or service-management policies.

Essential Health Benefits

Non-grandfathered individual and small-group plans must cover ten broad essential health-benefit categories.

  1. ambulatory patient services;
  2. emergency services;
  3. hospitalisation;
  4. pregnancy, maternity, and newborn care;
  5. mental-health and substance-use-disorder services, including behavioural-health treatment;
  6. prescription drugs;
  7. rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices;
  8. laboratory services;
  9. preventive and wellness services and chronic-disease management; and
  10. paediatric services, including oral and vision care.

States define detailed benefit benchmarks within federal rules. Covered services can therefore vary by state.

Adult dental and vision services are not universally required essential health benefits, though some plans or states provide them.

Metal Categories

Marketplace plans are grouped according to actuarial value—the expected share of covered medical spending paid by the plan for a standard population.

Bronze
Lower actuarial value, often lower premium, and generally higher deductibles or cost sharing.
Silver
Intermediate actuarial value and the only metal category through which eligible consumers receive cost-sharing reductions.
Gold
Higher actuarial value, often higher premiums, and generally lower cost sharing.
Platinum
Highest standard metal value, high premiums, and lower expected cost sharing; availability is limited in many markets.
Catastrophic
High-cost-sharing coverage available principally to people under age thirty and people qualifying for hardship or affordability exemptions.

Metal level does not measure medical quality. It describes how costs are divided between the insurer and a standard enrolled population.

Plan Types and Provider Networks

Marketplace insurers can offer health maintenance organisations, preferred provider organisations, exclusive provider organisations, point-of-service plans, and other lawful designs.

Plan type affects specialist referral, out-of-network coverage, primary-care requirements, and provider choice.

Consumers should verify physicians, hospitals, laboratories, pharmacies, behavioural-health providers, and other facilities directly with both the plan and provider before enrollment and during the year.

Provider directories can become inaccurate as contracts change. A listed provider may not accept new patients or every product offered by the same insurer.

Guaranteed Availability and Preexisting Conditions

Insurers in the regulated individual market cannot deny coverage, exclude a condition, or charge a higher premium because of health status, medical history, disability, genetic information, or a preexisting condition.

Coverage ordinarily begins only through open enrollment, a special enrollment period, or another authorised process. Guaranteed availability does not create unrestricted day-to-day enrollment.

Plans also cannot impose lifetime dollar limits on essential health benefits, and annual dollar limits on essential health benefits are generally prohibited.

How Premiums Are Rated

Within the ACA-regulated individual market, premiums can generally vary based on:

  • age, within a federally limited ratio;
  • geographic rating area;
  • family composition;
  • tobacco use, where permitted; and
  • the plan selected.

Premiums cannot vary because of sex, health status, claims history, disability, occupation, or genetic information.

State law can impose stricter rating limits, prohibit tobacco rating, or establish additional market rules.

Premium Tax Credit

The premium tax credit is a refundable federal income-tax credit under Internal Revenue Code Section 36B.

The credit is generally calculated using:

  • household income;
  • tax-family size;
  • the premium of the applicable second-lowest-cost Silver plan;
  • the statutory expected household contribution; and
  • the premium of the plan actually selected.

The credit cannot exceed the enrollment premium and does not ordinarily include the portion of a premium attributable to nonessential benefits or tobacco surcharges for benchmark calculation.

It is refundable, meaning an eligible taxpayer can receive its full value even when the credit exceeds ordinary federal income-tax liability.

Advance Premium Tax Credit

A consumer can choose to have all, some, or none of the estimated premium tax credit paid in advance directly to the insurer.

Advance payments reduce the monthly premium due during the coverage year. The Marketplace estimates eligibility using projected annual household income, tax-family information, benchmark premiums, and access to other coverage.

Choosing less than the maximum advance amount can reduce the risk of owing money at tax filing when income is uncertain.

Consumers should report changes in income, household, marriage, divorce, address, employer coverage, Medicaid eligibility, or dependents promptly.

Premium Tax Credit Rules in 2026

In general, a tax household must have annual income of at least 100 percent and no more than 400 percent of the federal poverty line to qualify for the premium tax credit in 2026.

Additional conditions include:

  • enrollment in non-catastrophic Marketplace coverage;
  • ineligibility for affordable employer-sponsored coverage providing minimum value;
  • ineligibility for qualifying government coverage such as Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, or TRICARE;
  • filing a federal tax return;
  • generally filing jointly when married, subject to domestic-abuse and spousal-abandonment exceptions; and
  • not being claimed as another taxpayer’s dependent.

Lawfully present immigrants with income below 100 percent of the poverty line can qualify under specialised statutory rules when ineligible for Medicaid because of immigration status, subject to current eligibility restrictions.

Temporary Enhanced Credits, 2021–2025

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 temporarily increased premium tax credits and removed the 400-percent income ceiling for 2021 and 2022.

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 extended those provisions through December 31, 2025.

The enhanced formula lowered expected household contributions and allowed households above 400 percent of the poverty line to qualify when benchmark premiums exceeded the statutory percentage of income.

Congress did not extend the enhanced formula beyond 2025. Beginning with 2026 coverage, the original 100-to-400-percent income range and less generous contribution schedule returned.

The expiration increased net premiums for many subsidised consumers even when the gross insurer premium changed little.

Tax Reconciliation

The Marketplace sends Form 1095-A showing enrollment premiums, benchmark premiums, and advance credit payments.

A taxpayer uses Form 8962 to compare advance payments with the premium tax credit allowed under actual annual income and tax-family circumstances.

When the allowed credit exceeds advance payments, the taxpayer can claim the difference. When advance payments exceed the allowed credit, the excess generally reduces the refund or increases tax due.

For tax years after 2025, there is no statutory repayment cap limiting repayment of excess advance premium tax credits. Consumers can therefore owe the full excess amount.

Failure to file and reconcile can affect eligibility for advance credits in later years.

Cost-Sharing Reductions

Cost-sharing reductions, commonly called CSRs or extra savings, lower deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and maximum out-of-pocket exposure for eligible consumers.

A consumer must select a Silver Marketplace plan to receive ordinary income-based CSR.

The amount of assistance varies by income. Lower-income eligible consumers receive plan variations with higher actuarial value.

American Indians and Alaska Natives meeting statutory requirements can qualify for specialised zero-cost-sharing or limited-cost-sharing plan variations.

CSR is distinct from the premium tax credit. A person can apply a premium tax credit to plans in other metal categories but loses ordinary CSR by selecting a non-Silver plan.

Federal CSR Payment Litigation and Silver Loading

The ACA directed insurers to provide cost-sharing reductions and provided for federal reimbursement. Congress did not enact a permanent explicit annual appropriation accepted by all branches and courts for those payments.

The federal government stopped direct CSR reimbursement to insurers in October 2017.

Insurers remained legally obligated to provide reduced cost sharing. Many states permitted insurers to increase Silver-plan premiums to account for the unpaid federal reimbursements, a practice called silver loading.

Because premium tax credits are tied to Silver benchmark premiums, silver loading can increase tax credits and create unusually low net premiums for some Bronze or Gold plans.

Employer-Sponsored Coverage

A person offered affordable employer-sponsored insurance providing minimum value generally cannot receive a premium tax credit for Marketplace coverage.

Affordability is evaluated under annual federal percentages and rules concerning the employee and family members.

Beginning in 2023, affordability for an employee’s family members is generally evaluated using the employee contribution required for family coverage rather than employee-only coverage. This change addressed the condition commonly called the family glitch.

A person can decline COBRA or retiree coverage and may qualify for a premium tax credit if other requirements are met. Special rules apply to health reimbursement arrangements.

Coordination with Medicaid and CHIP

The Marketplace application screens applicants for Medicaid and CHIP and transfers information to the appropriate state agency when eligibility is possible.

People eligible for qualifying Medicaid or CHIP coverage generally cannot receive Marketplace premium tax credits for the same months.

In Medicaid-expansion states, most adults with income below the effective 138-percent-of-poverty threshold qualify for Medicaid rather than subsidised Marketplace coverage.

In non-expansion states, some adults with income below the poverty line fall into the coverage gap: too little income for ordinary Marketplace credits under the original ACA structure but ineligible for their state’s traditional Medicaid categories.

Eligibility transitions can create overlapping coverage, delayed terminations, premium debt, or tax consequences when state and Marketplace data are not updated promptly.

Basic Health Program

Section 1331 of the Affordable Care Act permits a state to establish a Basic Health Program for eligible residents with income generally above Medicaid limits and through 200 percent of the federal poverty line.

The federal government provides the state with a percentage of the premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions that eligible residents otherwise would have received through the Marketplace.

Basic Health Program coverage can offer lower premiums and cost sharing than ordinary Marketplace plans and can reduce movement between Medicaid and Marketplace coverage.

The program is legally distinct from qualified health-plan coverage even though eligibility is coordinated with the Marketplace.

Open Enrollment

Open enrollment is the annual period during which eligible consumers can enroll in, renew, or change Marketplace plans without a separate qualifying event.

HealthCare.gov open enrollment for 2026 coverage ran from November 1, 2025, through January 15, 2026.

Selection by an earlier deadline may be necessary for January 1 coverage. State-based exchanges can establish different final deadlines.

Consumers should return actively rather than rely solely on automatic reenrollment because plan availability, premiums, tax credits, providers, formularies, and household circumstances change.

Special Enrollment Periods

Outside open enrollment, a consumer can enroll after specified qualifying events or exceptional circumstances.

  • loss of qualifying health coverage;
  • marriage;
  • birth, adoption, placement for foster care, or court order;
  • permanent move with required prior coverage in applicable cases;
  • change in household composition;
  • gain of lawful immigration status;
  • release from incarceration;
  • Marketplace or plan error;
  • exceptional circumstances; and
  • other events recognised by federal or state rules.

Documentation and pre-enrollment verification can be required. Coverage-effective dates differ according to the event and application timing.

Enrollment and Verification Rules in 2026

CMS adopted temporary 2026 rules intended to reduce unauthorised enrollment and improper advance tax-credit payments on the federal platform.

  • pre-enrollment verification for at least 75 percent of new special-enrollment-period enrollments on the federal platform;
  • elimination for 2026 of the monthly low-income special enrollment period previously available to certain consumers at or below 150 percent of the poverty line;
  • additional income verification when federal tax data are unavailable or materially inconsistent;
  • loss of advance tax-credit eligibility after failure to file and reconcile for the applicable prior year under the temporary 2026 rule;
  • a $5 monthly premium for certain automatically reenrolled federal-platform consumers who otherwise would have a zero-dollar premium and do not confirm eligibility information; and
  • revised automatic-reenrollment priorities.

Several of these policies were designed to sunset after the 2026 plan year, while later legislation and the 2027 payment rule created additional permanent or future requirements.

Automatic Reenrollment

Current enrollees can be automatically reenrolled when they do not make an active plan selection, subject to federal and state rules.

The system generally attempts to preserve the current product or place the enrollee into a comparable replacement plan when the old plan is unavailable.

Automatic reenrollment protects continuity but can create problems when premiums, tax credits, networks, benefits, or household circumstances change.

Active review is especially important after the 2025 expiration of enhanced premium tax credits.

Coverage Effectuation and Premium Payment

Selecting a plan does not always mean coverage has taken effect.

The consumer ordinarily must pay the required first premium, commonly called the binder payment, according to the insurer’s deadline.

Ongoing coverage requires timely payment of the enrollee’s share. Grace-period protections can apply to people receiving advance premium tax credits who have paid at least one full month.

Claims can be held or denied during portions of a grace period, and coverage can terminate retroactively under applicable rules.

Eligibility Appeals

Applicants and enrollees can appeal Marketplace determinations involving:

  • eligibility to enroll;
  • premium tax credits;
  • cost-sharing reductions;
  • special enrollment periods;
  • exemptions;
  • Medicaid or CHIP assessment and transfer issues; and
  • other appealable eligibility decisions.

The Marketplace provides notices explaining the decision, legal basis, effective date, and appeal method.

Insurance claims, prior authorisation, network, and benefit disputes are generally handled through plan appeals, external review, state insurance departments, and other processes rather than Marketplace eligibility appeals.

Consumer Assistance

Consumers can receive enrollment help from:

  • Marketplace call centres;
  • Navigators;
  • certified application counsellors;
  • state consumer-assistance programs;
  • licensed agents and brokers;
  • approved web-brokers and direct-enrollment entities;
  • health centres, hospitals, and community organisations; and
  • tax professionals for reconciliation questions.

Navigators and counsellors provide impartial enrollment assistance and do not receive insurer commissions for plan selection.

Agents and brokers can receive commissions and may not represent every issuer. Consumers should understand the assister’s role and verify that enrollment changes are authorised.

Agents, Brokers, and Unauthorized Enrollment

Agents and brokers play a major role in Marketplace enrollment, especially for consumers needing assistance with plans, tax credits, and documentation.

CMS has documented unauthorised enrollments, plan switching, misleading marketing, and improper use of consumer information.

Federal actions in 2025 and 2026 strengthened consent, identity, marketing, audit, termination, and verification requirements.

Consumers should review Marketplace and insurer notices, protect account credentials, report unauthorised changes promptly, and avoid offers claiming that everyone automatically qualifies for free insurance or cash payments.

Quality Ratings and Plan Information

The Marketplace Quality Rating System compares qualified health plans using clinical quality, enrollee experience, and plan-administration measures.

Plans can receive star ratings displayed during shopping when sufficient data are available.

A Summary of Benefits and Coverage presents standardised information regarding deductible, cost sharing, exclusions, and example medical scenarios.

Quality ratings and summaries support comparison but do not predict whether a specific physician, hospital, drug, or treatment will be accessible.

Risk Adjustment and Market Stability

The ACA established a permanent risk-adjustment program for the individual and small-group markets.

Risk adjustment transfers funds among insurers according to the relative health risk of enrolled populations. Plans with lower-risk enrollment generally pay into the system, while plans with higher-risk enrollment receive transfers.

The program is intended to reduce incentives to avoid people with significant medical needs and to support competition based on cost and quality rather than risk selection.

The ACA also created temporary reinsurance and risk-corridor programs for the initial market transition. Only risk adjustment remains a permanent nationwide federal program.

Section 1332 State Innovation Waivers

Section 1332 permits states to waive selected ACA Marketplace provisions beginning in 2017 when a proposed alternative satisfies federal guardrails.

A waiver must provide coverage at least as comprehensive and affordable, cover a comparable number of residents, and avoid increasing the federal deficit.

Many states use waiver authority to operate reinsurance programs that reduce individual-market premiums.

Federal pass-through funding reflects federal savings produced by the state program, particularly reduced premium tax-credit expenditure.

Small Business Health Options Program

The Affordable Care Act also created the Small Business Health Options Program, known as SHOP.

SHOP was intended to organise small-employer plan choices and facilitate a small-business tax credit.

Federal SHOP enrollment later shifted away from a central online enrollment system toward insurer, agent, and broker channels.

SHOP remains legally distinct from the individual Marketplace and has had a much smaller practical enrollment role.

Individual Coverage Requirement

The Affordable Care Act required most individuals to maintain minimum essential coverage, qualify for an exemption, or make a shared-responsibility payment.

In National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), the Supreme Court upheld the payment as a constitutional exercise of Congress’s taxing power.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 reduced the federal payment to zero beginning in 2019.

Some states and the District of Columbia established their own coverage requirements and penalties.

Major Supreme Court Cases

National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012)
Upheld the individual shared-responsibility payment under the taxing power but made the ACA Medicaid expansion effectively optional for states.
King v. Burwell (2015)
Held that premium tax credits are available through federally facilitated exchanges as well as state-established exchanges.
California v. Texas (2021)
Rejected a challenge to the ACA because the plaintiffs lacked standing, leaving the law in place after the federal mandate payment had been reduced to zero.

Numerous other cases have addressed contraceptive coverage, religious objections, risk corridors, cost-sharing reductions, nondiscrimination, abortion-related provisions, and state waivers.

King v. Burwell and Federal Exchange Subsidies

Challengers argued that statutory language limited premium tax credits to an exchange “established by the State.”

In 2015, the Supreme Court held that the ACA makes credits available to eligible consumers in every state, including states using the federal Marketplace.

The Court interpreted the statute as an integrated reform designed to combine guaranteed insurance access, tax credits, and market-participation rules.

A contrary decision would have removed subsidies from federal-platform states and destabilised their individual insurance markets.

Significance for African American Communities

The Marketplace expanded access to individual health insurance for Black adults and families affected by employment instability, jobs without benefits, lower household wealth, insurer underwriting, preexisting conditions, and unequal access to employer-sponsored coverage.

Premium tax credits made private coverage more accessible to many households, while the ACA’s prohibition on health-status discrimination protected people with chronic disease and prior medical conditions.

The Marketplace’s effect is intertwined with Medicaid expansion. Black residents in expansion states gained access through both Medicaid and subsidised private coverage. Black residents in non-expansion states remain disproportionately affected by the coverage gap, especially in parts of the South.

Coverage does not guarantee equal care. Narrow networks, hospital closures, specialist shortages, maternal-health disparities, prescription formularies, deductibles, transportation, and medical discrimination continue to shape outcomes.

Marketplace administration should therefore be evaluated through enrollment, retention, affordability, provider access, claim outcomes, language, geography, and racial disparities—not merely the number of plan selections.

Immigration and Marketplace Eligibility

Lawfully present noncitizens can enroll in Marketplace coverage when they meet applicable federal conditions.

Immigration rules affect premium tax-credit eligibility, Medicaid coordination, verification, and the special rule for some lawfully present people with income below the federal poverty line.

Public Law 119-21, enacted in 2025, narrowed eligibility for federal health programs and premium tax credits for several noncitizen categories, with provisions taking effect on statutory schedules.

Marketplace applications require immigration information only for household members seeking coverage, although tax-household and income information can still be needed to determine financial assistance.

American Indian and Alaska Native Provisions

Members of federally recognised tribes and Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act corporation shareholders can qualify for specialised Marketplace protections.

These can include monthly enrollment opportunities, zero-cost-sharing plans for qualifying income groups, limited-cost-sharing plans, and protection from cost sharing when receiving covered services through Indian health providers under applicable conditions.

Marketplace coverage does not replace Indian Health Service, tribal, or urban Indian health programs or the federal trust responsibility.

Rural Significance

The Marketplace provides an insurance option for rural residents without employer coverage, including farmers, ranchers, self-employed workers, and people employed by small businesses.

Rural markets can have few insurers, narrow hospital networks, high premiums, limited specialists, and long travel distances.

A county can have Marketplace plans while lacking practical access to covered clinicians or nearby hospitals.

State reinsurance, network standards, telehealth, rural payment policy, and hospital stability influence whether insurance produces usable access.

Maternal and Reproductive Health

Marketplace plans cover pregnancy, maternity, and newborn care as essential health benefits.

Preventive-service rules also affect contraception, cancer screening, prenatal services, and other reproductive-health care, subject to federal law, litigation, exemptions, and plan administration.

Plan networks and state law determine practical access to obstetric, midwifery, maternity-hospital, abortion, infertility, and related services.

A person becoming eligible for Medicaid during pregnancy can transition from Marketplace coverage, requiring careful coordination to avoid duplicate premiums or tax-credit problems.

Mental Health and Substance-Use Coverage

Mental-health and substance-use-disorder services are essential health benefits.

Plans must also comply with applicable federal mental-health parity requirements.

Practical access can remain limited by narrow networks, provider shortages, reimbursement, prior authorisation, residential-treatment standards, and inaccurate directories.

Consumers should review behavioural-health networks and drug formularies rather than assuming every clinician or treatment setting participates.

Abortion Coverage

The Affordable Care Act permits states to prohibit abortion coverage in Marketplace plans and establishes specialised rules for plans covering abortion services beyond federal funding exceptions.

Federal premium tax credits cannot be used to pay for prohibited abortion coverage. Issuers offering such coverage must follow segregation and payment requirements under federal law.

Plan availability and coverage vary substantially according to state law and issuer decisions.

Civil-Rights and Nondiscrimination Requirements

Marketplace operations, issuers, plans, assisters, and related entities can be subject to federal civil-rights laws.

  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
  • section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973;
  • the Age Discrimination Act of 1975;
  • the Americans with Disabilities Act;
  • section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act;
  • federal language-access requirements;
  • ACA prohibitions on discriminatory benefit design and marketing; and
  • state insurance and civil-rights law.

Nondiscrimination concerns include application access, disability accommodation, language services, network composition, benefit design, drug formularies, marketing, gender identity, race, age, disability, and treatment of chronic conditions.

Data, Identity, and Privacy

Marketplace eligibility requires exchange of information among applicants, CMS, state agencies, the Internal Revenue Service, Social Security Administration, Department of Homeland Security, insurers, and other authorised sources.

Information can include identity, citizenship or immigration status, tax-family relationships, income, employer coverage, Medicaid eligibility, address, incarceration status, and plan selection.

Federal systems must protect data while permitting verification and enrollment.

Identity theft, unauthorised agent activity, false applications, account takeover, and inaccurate data matching create risks for consumers and public funds.

Program Financing

Marketplace administration is financed through federal appropriations, user fees paid by participating insurers, state assessments, state appropriations, and other authorised revenue.

Premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions are federal subsidies connected to individual enrollment.

Marketplace establishment grants helped states develop exchanges before and during implementation, but ordinary establishment funding was time limited.

State-based exchanges often impose insurer assessments to finance continuing operations.

Enrollment Trends

Marketplace plan selections grew from the difficult first enrollment period to more than twenty million annually in the mid-2020s.

Enrollment increased sharply after the American Rescue Plan expanded financial assistance and federal policy increased outreach and enrollment support.

The 2025 open-enrollment period produced more than twenty-four million plan selections.

After enhanced premium tax credits expired, approximately 23.1 million consumers selected or were automatically reenrolled for 2026—below the 2025 record but near historically high levels.

Plan selections exceed effectuated enrollment because some consumers do not pay the first premium, terminate coverage, obtain other insurance, or are later found ineligible.

2026 Plan-Selection Patterns

CMS reported approximately 23.1 million Marketplace plan selections for 2026.

Approximately 40 percent selected Bronze plans, 43 percent selected Silver plans, and 17 percent selected Gold plans.

Compared with 2025, enrollment shifted away from Silver and toward Bronze and Gold.

The shift reflected changes in premium tax credits, relative plan pricing, silver loading, consumer preferences, and issuer offerings.

Premium Conditions in 2026

CMS projected that the average lowest-cost HealthCare.gov premium after tax credits would be approximately $50 per month for eligible enrollees in 2026.

This average does not describe every consumer. Net premiums vary by age, location, household income, family size, benchmark plan, tobacco rating, and plan selection.

The expiration of enhanced tax credits increased expected consumer contributions. CMS estimated that a fifty-year-old at twice the poverty line would have 81 percent of the benchmark premium covered by tax credits in 2026, compared with 93 percent in 2025.

Gross premiums can rise or fall independently of the net premium because tax credits adjust with benchmark prices.

Program Integrity

Marketplace program integrity addresses improper enrollment, inaccurate income, duplicate coverage, tax-credit overpayment, agent misconduct, identity fraud, insurer noncompliance, and false documentation.

CMS reported that enforcement actions during 2025 ended advance tax credits or coverage for nearly 1.5 million people found ineligible for assistance or enrolled without authorisation on the federal platform.

Integrity controls must protect public funds without creating unreasonable barriers for eligible consumers experiencing income volatility, document loss, language barriers, homelessness, disaster, or agency error.

Verification systems should distinguish fraud from ordinary mistake and should provide clear notice and meaningful appeal.

Major Achievements

  • Created a national regulated purchasing framework for individual health insurance.
  • Made income-based federal premium assistance available through advance payments.
  • Reduced deductibles and other out-of-pocket expenses for qualifying Silver-plan enrollees.
  • Protected people with preexisting conditions from denial and health-status rating.
  • Standardised essential health-benefit categories.
  • Ended lifetime dollar limits on essential health benefits.
  • Created annual plan comparison and standardised consumer information.
  • Coordinated screening for Marketplace, Medicaid, CHIP, and Basic Health Program eligibility.
  • Expanded insurance options for self-employed people and workers without job-based benefits.
  • Established quality ratings and insurer certification.
  • Created permanent risk adjustment for individual and small-group markets.
  • Supported state innovation and reinsurance through Section 1332 waivers.
  • Reached record or near-record enrollment in the mid-2020s.
  • Created a federal fallback preventing state refusal from eliminating access to an exchange.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Premiums can remain unaffordable for people outside the subsidy range.
  • High deductibles can make insured care difficult to use.
  • Cost-sharing reductions require selection of a Silver plan.
  • Narrow networks can exclude preferred hospitals, specialists, and clinicians.
  • Insurer participation varies by county and year.
  • Income estimation can produce tax debt when advance credits exceed final eligibility.
  • After 2025, there is no repayment cap for excess advance credits.
  • The original tax-credit structure excludes most households above 400 percent of poverty.
  • Some low-income adults remain in the Medicaid coverage gap.
  • Complex eligibility rules create administrative burden.
  • Automatic reenrollment can place consumers in unsuitable or unexpectedly expensive plans.
  • Agent and broker misconduct can produce unauthorised enrollment or plan switching.
  • Provider directories and formularies can be inaccurate or change during the year.
  • State variation produces unequal access and consumer protection.
  • Coverage selection does not guarantee premium payment or continued enrollment.
  • Political and regulatory changes create instability for consumers and insurers.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Expiration of Enhanced Premium Tax Credits

The American Rescue Plan and Inflation Reduction Act subsidy enhancements ended after 2025. Consumers face higher expected contributions and the return of the 400-percent income ceiling.

Full Repayment of Excess Advance Credits

For tax years after 2025, consumers generally must repay the full amount by which advance tax credits exceed final allowable credits. Accurate income updates are therefore more important.

Implementation of Public Law 119-21

CMS is implementing eligibility-verification, immigration, tax-credit, and program-integrity provisions enacted in July 2025.

2027 Payment Rule

The 2027 final rule, effective July 20, 2026, strengthened agent and broker marketing standards, implemented additional program-integrity provisions, modified qualified health-plan certification, expanded hardship-exemption policy, and changed plan-design and state-flexibility rules for 2027.

Unauthorized Enrollment

CMS continues to investigate agent, broker, and web-broker activity, consumer consent, account changes, plan switching, and misleading zero-premium marketing.

Marketplace Affordability

Policymakers continue debating whether to restore enhanced tax credits, revise the benchmark formula, appropriate direct CSR payments, expand reinsurance, or create other affordability mechanisms.

Medicaid Transitions

Medicaid eligibility changes scheduled for 2027 could increase Marketplace transitions and demand for coordinated enrollment assistance.

State-Based Exchange Expansion

States continue evaluating whether operating an independent platform offers greater policy control, data access, outreach capacity, and financial flexibility.

Network Adequacy and Rural Access

Insurance availability remains distinct from practical access to hospitals, behavioural-health clinicians, maternity care, specialists, and pharmacies.

Future Congressional Policy

Congress can alter subsidy levels, eligibility, cost sharing, abortion provisions, market rules, appropriations, and Marketplace administration through legislation.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Marketplace or Exchange
The government-regulated system for comparing and enrolling in qualified health plans and determining financial assistance.
Qualified health plan
A private insurance plan certified by a Marketplace and meeting ACA standards.
Premium
The amount charged periodically to maintain insurance coverage.
Premium tax credit
A refundable federal tax credit reducing the cost of Marketplace premiums for eligible tax households.
Advance premium tax credit
Estimated premium tax-credit payments sent during the coverage year directly to an insurer.
Cost-sharing reduction
A subsidy reducing deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket limits for eligible Silver-plan enrollees.
Second-lowest-cost Silver plan
The benchmark plan used in calculating the premium tax credit for an eligible coverage family.
Modified adjusted gross income
The income methodology used for Marketplace financial assistance and many Medicaid categories.
Essential health benefits
Ten broad categories that non-grandfathered individual and small-group plans must cover.
Actuarial value
The percentage of covered medical spending a plan is expected to pay for a standard population.
Metal level
A Bronze, Silver, Gold, or Platinum category based on actuarial value.
Open enrollment
The annual period when eligible consumers can enroll or change plans without a separate qualifying event.
Special enrollment period
A limited enrollment opportunity triggered by specified life events or exceptional circumstances.
Binder payment
The first premium payment required to effectuate coverage.
Minimum essential coverage
A statutory category of health coverage relevant to ACA tax and eligibility rules.
Minimum value
An employer-plan standard relevant to premium tax-credit eligibility.
Silver loading
The practice of incorporating unpaid insurer CSR costs principally into Silver-plan premiums.
Risk adjustment
Transfers among insurers reflecting the relative health risk of enrolled populations.
Section 1332 waiver
Authority allowing a state to modify selected ACA requirements while satisfying statutory coverage guardrails.
Coverage gap
The condition affecting some poor adults in non-expansion states who qualify for neither Medicaid nor ordinary Marketplace premium credits.

Summary

The Affordable Care Act Marketplace Program is the federal-state system created in 2010 to organise individual health-insurance purchasing, certify private plans, determine financial assistance, and coordinate enrollment with Medicaid and CHIP.

Marketplace qualified health plans cover essential health benefits and operate under federal and state rules concerning guaranteed availability, preexisting conditions, premiums, cost sharing, networks, quality, consumer information, and nondiscrimination.

Eligible tax households can receive a refundable premium tax credit, paid in advance if requested. Qualifying consumers selecting Silver plans can also receive cost-sharing reductions.

The American Rescue Plan and Inflation Reduction Act temporarily expanded tax credits through 2025. Those enhancements expired for 2026, restoring the general 100-to-400-percent poverty-level range and increasing net premiums for many consumers.

Approximately 23.1 million consumers selected or were automatically reenrolled in 2026 Marketplace coverage—15.8 million through HealthCare.gov and 7.2 million through independent state platforms.

Marketplace coverage has expanded insurance access and protected people with preexisting conditions, but affordability, tax reconciliation, high deductibles, provider networks, state variation, the Medicaid coverage gap, and regulatory instability remain major limitations.
