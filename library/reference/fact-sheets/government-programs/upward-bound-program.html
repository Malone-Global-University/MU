Upward Bound Program

The intensive Federal TRIO college-preparation program helping eligible secondary-school students develop the academic skills, knowledge, motivation, and support needed to complete high school and enter and succeed in postsecondary education.

Overview

The Upward Bound Program is a federal college-access and academic-preparation program serving secondary-school students who face economic and educational barriers to postsecondary education. It provides sustained instruction, tutoring, counseling, mentoring, financial-aid assistance, college exposure, summer programming, and other support intended to help participants complete high school and enter and succeed in college.

Upward Bound was the first program later included within the Federal TRIO Programs. It began under the authority of the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 as part of President Lyndon B. Johnson’s War on Poverty. The program was later transferred into the Higher Education Act of 1965, which remains its statutory home.

The program’s formal federal purpose is to generate the skills and motivation necessary for success in education beyond secondary school. Modern law and regulations further define its objectives as secondary-school completion, postsecondary enrollment, persistence, and completion.

Regular Upward Bound is one of three project types administered under the federal Upward Bound statutory and regulatory framework. The other two are Upward Bound Math-Science and Veterans Upward Bound. Regular Upward Bound provides broad academic and college preparation to secondary-school students. Upward Bound Math-Science concentrates on science, technology, engineering, mathematics, laboratory work, and research. Veterans Upward Bound serves eligible veterans preparing to enter or resume postsecondary education.

This fact sheet uses “Upward Bound” to mean the regular Upward Bound Program unless another project type is expressly identified.

Upward Bound is not a scholarship, student loan, admissions preference, or cash-assistance entitlement. The Department of Education awards competitive grants to eligible institutions and organizations. Each funded project recruits and selects a limited number of participants from approved target schools or geographic areas.

A student does not apply directly to the federal government for an Upward Bound grant. The student applies to a local funded project operated by a college, university, public agency, nonprofit organization, secondary school, or consortium. Meeting the federal eligibility definition does not guarantee selection because each project has limited space, target-school boundaries, staffing, service capacity, and local selection procedures.

Participants generally must have completed the eighth grade, be between thirteen and nineteen years of age, demonstrate a need for academic support, and be low income, a potential first-generation college student, or at high risk for academic failure under the applicable participant category.

At least two-thirds of each regular Upward Bound project’s participants generally must be both low income and potential first-generation college students. The remaining one-third may be low income, potential first generation, or at high risk for academic failure.

A low-income individual generally comes from a family whose taxable income for the preceding year did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level. The Department publishes annual TRIO income levels. For 2026, the low-income ceiling for a family of four is $49,500 in the contiguous states and District of Columbia, $61,875 in Alaska, and $56,925 in Hawaii.

A potential first-generation college student generally is a student whose natural or adoptive parents did not complete a four-year bachelor’s degree. When a student regularly lived with and received support from only one parent, the definition ordinarily focuses on whether that parent completed a bachelor’s degree.

Projects commonly recruit students from middle schools and high schools with low college-going rates, low adult educational attainment, high poverty, high student-to-counselor ratios, significant dropout risk, or limited access to advanced coursework and college-planning resources.

Regular Upward Bound ordinarily operates two connected program components. The academic-year component provides recurring services while students attend their regular schools. The summer component provides intensive instruction, campus exposure, cultural activities, and often a residential college-going experience lasting approximately six weeks.

Projects that have received federal funds for at least two years must include a core curriculum providing instruction in mathematics through pre-calculus, laboratory science, foreign language, composition, and literature. Tutoring and supplemental instruction can also address reading, writing, study skills, mathematics, science, and other subjects.

Required services include academic tutoring; assistance selecting secondary and postsecondary courses; college entrance examination and admissions assistance; information concerning federal student aid and scholarships; assistance with secondary-school reentry or alternative education when needed; and financial and economic literacy.

Projects may also provide mentoring, cultural activities, career exploration, residential experiences, work-study, support for students with disabilities or limited English proficiency, foster youth, homeless students, and other disconnected students, and activities designed around local educational needs.

Upward Bound can pay certain educational expenses on behalf of participants, including transportation, application fees when waivers are unavailable, residential summer room and board, educational materials, computer equipment, summer bridge tuition, and limited tuition for rigorous secondary coursework not otherwise available.

Projects may provide modest participation stipends. Under current regulations, regular Upward Bound stipends may not exceed $40 per month during the academic-year component or $60 per month during the summer recess for no more than three months. A participant in an approved summer work-study position may receive up to $300 per month. Stipends can be prorated and require satisfactory participation.

The stipend is not the program’s primary benefit. The principal federal investment is the instructional, advising, residential, transportation, staffing, technological, and institutional structure surrounding each participant.

Regular Upward Bound grants ordinarily operate in five-year award cycles and do not require institutional cost sharing or matching. The Department states that competitions are generally held every four years. The last regular Upward Bound competition was held in fiscal year 2022, and the current principal grant cycle extends from 2022 through 2027.

In fiscal year 2024, regular Upward Bound received approximately $379.88 million for 1,026 projects funded to serve 73,519 participants. The average project award was approximately $370,249, the average funded project size was seventy-two participants, and the federal cost per funded participant was approximately $5,167.

Fiscal year 2025 continuation funding for regular Upward Bound was approximately $386.919 million. This amount excludes the separate funding lines for Upward Bound Math-Science and Veterans Upward Bound.

The Department reported that 78.6 percent of the defined 2024 Upward Bound outcome cohort enrolled in postsecondary education. The measure covered participants with a regular secondary-school diploma who subsequently enrolled in postsecondary education and included regular Upward Bound and Math-Science projects in the federal performance framework.

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 budget request proposed eliminating funding for all Federal TRIO Programs, including Upward Bound. A presidential budget request is not enacted law. As of August 2, 2026, Upward Bound remained authorized, current grants remained active, annual performance reporting continued, and the Department still listed the program as an operating federal grant program.

Upward Bound has major civil-rights and racial-equity significance. It developed during the Civil Rights and Great Society eras when African American and other marginalized students faced segregation, unequal school funding, limited counseling, discriminatory admissions, poverty, and exclusion from college-preparatory institutions and networks.

Most eligibility rules are based on income, parental education, academic need, and risk rather than race. Nevertheless, racial disparities in wealth, school quality, advanced coursework, counselor access, neighborhood resources, and college knowledge make African American, Latino, Native American, and other historically underserved students central participant populations.

Definitions

Upward Bound
The Federal TRIO program providing intensive academic preparation and motivation to eligible secondary-school students seeking postsecondary education.
Regular Upward Bound
The broad secondary-school college-preparation project type distinguished from Upward Bound Math-Science and Veterans Upward Bound.
Federal TRIO Programs
The federal outreach and student-support programs serving low-income, first-generation, disabled, veteran, and other eligible participants across the educational pipeline.
Low-income individual
An individual whose family taxable income for the preceding year did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level under the applicable annual TRIO table.
Potential first-generation college student
A student whose natural or adoptive parents did not complete a bachelor’s degree, subject to the federal rule for a student supported by one parent.
High risk for academic failure
A participant category involving specified academic, educational, environmental, or personal conditions placing a student at substantial risk of not completing secondary school or entering postsecondary education.
Need for academic support
A project determination that a student requires academic instruction, tutoring, counseling, preparation, or related services to pursue education beyond secondary school successfully.
Participant
An eligible student formally selected by a funded project and receiving documented Upward Bound services.
Potential participant
A student recruited, screened, referred, or considered by a project who has not necessarily been admitted into the funded participant group.
Target area
The approved geographic area containing the schools and communities from which a project recruits participants.
Target school
A middle or secondary school specifically identified in the funded application as a source of Upward Bound participants.
Academic-year component
The recurring tutoring, counseling, instruction, advising, mentoring, and enrichment provided while participants attend their regular schools.
Summer component
An intensive instructional program, ordinarily lasting approximately six weeks, designed to simulate a college-going experience.
Residential summer component
A summer component in which participants live on a college campus or other approved residential site under project supervision.
Summer bridge
A transitional program for recent secondary-school graduates that can include postsecondary credit courses and preparation before fall college enrollment.
Core curriculum
The required instructional sequence in mathematics through pre-calculus, laboratory science, foreign language, composition, and literature for projects funded for at least two years.
Rigorous secondary school program of study
A challenging secondary course sequence intended to prepare students for postsecondary education, potentially including advanced placement, honors, dual-enrollment, or equivalent coursework.
Stipend
A limited payment supporting satisfactory full-time participation in project activities rather than wages for ordinary employment.
Work-study position
An approved summer employment experience exposing a participant to a career requiring postsecondary education and subject to the Upward Bound stipend and project rules.
Prior experience
Competitive points available to an existing grantee based on documented performance in designated prior project years.
Standard objectives
The federally approved project commitments concerning academic performance, secondary retention and graduation, rigorous coursework, postsecondary enrollment, and postsecondary completion.
Annual Performance Report
The federal report documenting participant eligibility, services, progress, graduation, college enrollment, project outcomes, and prior-experience performance. The abbreviation APR is commonly used.
Postsecondary education
Education beyond secondary school, including eligible colleges, universities, community colleges, and qualifying career or technical institutions.
College undermatching
Enrollment at an institution less selective than a student’s academic preparation and options might support.
Disconnected student
A broad program category that can include students experiencing homelessness, foster care, limited English proficiency, disability, educational interruption, or other barriers to postsecondary opportunity.

Historical Origins

War on Poverty

Upward Bound reflected the Johnson administration’s belief that poverty could not be addressed solely through income support. Educational preparation, institutional access, employment, health, community organization, and civil rights were treated as interconnected.

Beyond Tuition Assistance

Federal policymakers recognized that grants and loans alone would not ensure college access. Students from under-resourced schools often lacked rigorous coursework, counselors, application knowledge, test preparation, campus familiarity, and family experience navigating postsecondary institutions.

Residential College Experience

Early projects brought students to college campuses during the summer. The residential model allowed participants to experience dormitories, classrooms, laboratories, libraries, faculty interaction, cultural events, and greater independence.

Civil Rights

The program emerged as African American students and civil-rights organizations challenged segregated schools, exclusionary admissions, unequal curriculum, and discriminatory public institutions. Federal college-access policy became part of the broader national response to educational inequality.

Community Action

Early Upward Bound projects operated within the Economic Opportunity Act’s community-action framework, connecting colleges with low-income neighborhoods and local organizations.

Transfer to the Education System

Placing Upward Bound under the Higher Education Act shifted it from a temporary anti-poverty initiative toward a continuing federal college-access institution.

Formation of TRIO

Talent Search followed in 1965, and Special Services for Disadvantaged Students followed in 1968. Together, the three programs became known as TRIO.

Program Purpose and Theory

Secondary Completion

Participants must first remain in school, complete required coursework, and earn a regular diploma or recognized equivalent.

Academic Preparation

Instruction and tutoring address gaps in mathematics, science, reading, writing, languages, study skills, and other college-preparatory subjects.

College Knowledge

Projects explain admissions, financial aid, academic majors, credentials, transfer, campus systems, deadlines, and institutional expectations.

Motivation and Identity

Repeated exposure to campuses, faculty, peers, professionals, and successful alumni can strengthen the participant’s confidence and expectation of college attendance.

Application Completion

Staff help students translate aspiration into completed tests, applications, essays, financial-aid forms, scholarship materials, and enrollment decisions.

Institutional Navigation

Projects teach students how to communicate with admissions, financial aid, housing, disability, advising, and academic offices.

Persistence Beyond Entry

Federal performance does not stop at college admission. Projects track postsecondary enrollment and, to the extent possible, persistence and completion.

Multiyear Relationship

Upward Bound is designed as a sustained relationship rather than a one-time workshop. Many participants remain in the project across several secondary-school years and through the transition to college.

Relationship to the Federal TRIO Programs

First TRIO Program

Upward Bound predates the TRIO name and established the intensive precollege model around which later outreach and support programs developed.

Talent Search

Talent Search commonly serves larger numbers of students through counseling, outreach, financial-aid information, and application assistance. Upward Bound generally provides more intensive instruction, repeated contact, and summer programming.

Student Support Services

Student Support Services begins after college enrollment. Upward Bound projects can refer graduating participants to SSS projects operating at the institutions they attend.

Upward Bound Math-Science

UBMS uses a similar eligibility and project framework but focuses specifically on mathematics, science, engineering, technology, laboratory skills, and research.

Veterans Upward Bound

VUB adapts academic preparation and educational navigation for veterans, without the ordinary secondary-school age structure.

Educational Opportunity Centers

EOC primarily assists adults and individuals reentering education. Upward Bound primarily serves secondary-school students.

McNair

McNair operates later in the pipeline, preparing eligible undergraduates for graduate education and doctoral study.

Participant Coordination

Projects document, to the extent practicable, services a participant receives from other federal programs to reduce unnecessary duplication and strengthen continuity.

Eligible Grant Applicants

The Department can award regular Upward Bound grants to:

  • Institutions of higher education
  • Public agencies
  • Private nonprofit agencies and organizations
  • Community-based organizations experienced in serving disadvantaged youth
  • Combinations or consortia of eligible institutions and organizations
  • Secondary schools when appropriate to the program’s purpose

Colleges and Universities

Most projects are hosted by public or private colleges, universities, and community colleges that can provide classrooms, laboratories, dormitories, libraries, faculty, admissions expertise, and campus exposure.

Community Organizations

Nonprofit organizations can operate projects with strong neighborhood relationships, culturally responsive recruitment, social-service partnerships, and experience serving disconnected students.

Public Agencies

State, local, Tribal, educational, and other public agencies can apply when they possess appropriate capacity and authority.

No Individual Grant

Students and families cannot submit a federal grant application. They seek admission to a local funded project.

No Matching Requirement

The program does not require a formal nonfederal cost share. Applicants nevertheless describe institutional commitment, facilities, partnerships, and resources supporting effective implementation.

Participant Eligibility

Educational Stage

A participant generally must have completed the eighth grade. Projects often begin recruiting students during the spring or summer before ninth grade.

Age

Regular Upward Bound generally serves students between thirteen and nineteen years of age. Regulatory exceptions can apply when age limits would defeat the purpose of serving a student who otherwise qualifies.

Academic Need

The project must document a need for academic support in order to pursue successfully education beyond secondary school.

Low-Income or First-Generation Requirement

Every participant must fit an authorized category. Most participants fall within the low-income and first-generation group required by the two-thirds rule.

Two-Thirds Group

At least two-thirds of the participants served each project year generally must be both:

  • Low income; and
  • Potential first-generation college students.

Remaining One-Third

The remaining participants may be:

  • Low income but not first generation
  • First generation but not low income
  • At high risk for academic failure

Citizenship or Qualifying Status

Participants generally must be United States citizens, permanent residents, or meet another status recognized by the program regulations.

No Race Requirement

Race alone does not establish eligibility. Projects must apply the statutory income, parental-education, academic-need, age, educational-stage, and status rules.

Continuing Participants

Once properly admitted, participants can remain in the project while making satisfactory progress and benefiting from services, subject to local policies and federal reporting rules.

Determining Academic Need

Projects use documented, educationally relevant information to determine whether a student needs support to pursue postsecondary education.

Potential Indicators

  • Low grades or uneven academic performance
  • Low standardized test scores
  • Limited access to advanced courses
  • Weak preparation in mathematics, science, reading, or writing
  • High student-to-counselor ratios
  • Attendance problems
  • Risk of dropping out
  • Limited English proficiency
  • Disability-related barriers
  • Homelessness or foster-care experience
  • Frequent school changes
  • Family or economic circumstances affecting study
  • Lack of information concerning college and financial aid

Potential Versus Current Performance

Upward Bound is not limited to high-achieving students. A participant can possess postsecondary potential while needing substantial academic development.

Individual Review

Projects avoid reducing academic need to one test score. Interviews, transcripts, counselor recommendations, school data, family information, and educational history can be considered.

Documentation

The grantee maintains records explaining the basis for each participant’s academic-need determination.

Taxable Income

The program uses family taxable income, not gross income or adjusted gross income alone. Projects follow federal instructions when reviewing tax returns, nonfiling status, dependency, family size, and documentation.

Annual Change

The income ceilings are updated annually. A project uses the table applicable when it makes the eligibility determination.

Income Is Not the Only Requirement

A low-income student must also meet the program’s age, educational-stage, citizenship or residency, and academic-need requirements.

Target Schools and Communities

Project Need

Grant applicants document why the proposed target area needs Upward Bound. Federal reviewers examine:

  • Low family income
  • Low adult educational attainment
  • High dropout rates
  • Low college-going rates
  • High student-to-counselor ratios
  • Limited access to college-preparatory services

School Agreements

Projects commonly enter partnerships or memoranda of understanding with target schools concerning recruitment, student records, facilities, counselor coordination, tutoring, transportation, and scheduling.

Geographic Boundaries

A student attending a school outside the project’s approved target structure may be ineligible for that local project even when meeting national income and first-generation standards.

Multiple Projects

Some metropolitan areas contain several Upward Bound projects serving different schools. Other high-need areas have no nearby project.

Changing Conditions

School closures, district boundaries, demographic shifts, charter growth, and transportation can affect recruitment and project design during a multiyear grant.

Recruitment and Participant Selection

Recruitment

Projects recruit through school counselors, teachers, family meetings, community organizations, social media, classroom presentations, alumni, college fairs, and direct outreach.

Application

A local application can request:

  • Student and parent information
  • Tax or income documentation
  • Parental education
  • Transcript and test information
  • Academic interests and goals
  • Counselor or teacher recommendations
  • Household circumstances
  • Consent for records and program participation

Interview

Projects may interview students and families to assess motivation, academic need, availability, transportation, and understanding of participation expectations.

Selection Is Not Based Solely on Grades

The program is designed for students who need support. A low grade point average can demonstrate need rather than disqualify a student, provided the project determines that the student can benefit.

Limited Capacity

Many projects are funded to serve approximately fifty to one hundred participants. Eligible applicants can be placed on waiting lists or referred to Talent Search, GEAR UP, school counseling, or other programs.

Family Commitment

Projects can require attendance, participation, academic effort, respectful conduct, communication, and summer availability as conditions of continuing service.

Equal Opportunity

Recruitment and selection must comply with federal civil-rights and nondiscrimination requirements.

Academic-Year Component

Tutoring

Projects provide individual, small-group, online, peer, or subject-specific tutoring in core academic areas.

Saturday or After-School Academies

Many projects organize recurring weekend or after-school sessions combining instruction, workshops, test preparation, advising, and enrichment.

Academic Advising

Staff review grades, attendance, graduation requirements, course sequences, college readiness, and intervention needs.

Course Selection

Participants receive advice concerning algebra, geometry, advanced mathematics, laboratory science, foreign language, honors, dual enrollment, advanced placement, career education, and graduation requirements.

College and Career Counseling

Students explore majors, credentials, occupations, labor-market information, educational requirements, and different types of institutions.

Application Assistance

Seniors receive structured help with entrance examinations, application forms, essays, recommendations, transcripts, financial aid, scholarships, and enrollment decisions.

Parent Engagement

Projects explain college cost, admissions, aid, academic expectations, safety, housing, and family roles in postsecondary transition.

Progress Monitoring

Staff review school records and communicate with participants, families, counselors, and teachers to address emerging problems.

Summer Instructional Component

College-Going Experience

Regular Upward Bound must provide a summer instructional component designed to simulate a college-going experience.

Duration

Projects commonly operate approximately six weeks, although exact schedules vary according to the funded design, institution, and local school calendar.

Instruction

Summer courses can preview the next academic year, strengthen deficient skills, provide advanced content, and build study habits.

Residential Experience

Participants may live in campus housing under supervision, follow a structured daily schedule, eat in dining facilities, use libraries and laboratories, and experience greater independence.

Commuter Programs

Not every project is residential. Day and hybrid programs can provide intensive instruction while participants commute.

Cultural and Educational Activities

Field trips, museums, performances, lectures, science centers, historical sites, career visits, and recreational activities broaden exposure.

Social Development

Living and learning with a cohort develops communication, conflict resolution, time management, teamwork, and peer support.

Health and Safety

Projects establish supervision, transportation, emergency, medication, conduct, disability, insurance, and residential safety procedures.

Cost to Participants

Federal grant funds can cover eligible instruction, transportation, activities, room, board, materials, insurance, and other approved costs. Projects generally seek to prevent family income from becoming a barrier to participation.

Core Curriculum and Academic Instruction

Mathematics Through Pre-Calculus

Projects build arithmetic, algebra, geometry, data, functions, trigonometry, and pre-calculus skills needed for postsecondary coursework.

Laboratory Science

Instruction emphasizes scientific reasoning, experiments, measurement, analysis, reporting, and exposure to biology, chemistry, physics, environmental science, and related fields.

Foreign Language

Language instruction helps participants satisfy secondary and college-admission expectations and develop communication and cultural understanding.

Composition

Students practice grammar, organization, argument, evidence, research, revision, academic writing, and college-application essays.

Literature

Reading and discussion develop comprehension, interpretation, vocabulary, critical analysis, historical understanding, and written response.

Study Skills

Projects teach note taking, time management, reading strategies, exam preparation, organization, research, technology, and self-advocacy.

Rigorous Course Access

Federal funds can support limited tuition for qualifying rigorous secondary courses when the course is unavailable within the school district, the school accepts it toward graduation, a waiver is unavailable, and the regulatory conditions are met.

Individualization

Instruction is adapted to participant grade level, academic preparation, disability, language proficiency, college goals, and target-school curriculum.

Required Services

Every regular Upward Bound project must provide the following categories of assistance:

Academic Tutoring

Tutoring supports completion of secondary and postsecondary courses and can include reading, writing, study skills, mathematics, science, and other subjects.

Course-Selection Assistance

Staff help participants choose appropriate secondary and postsecondary courses aligned with graduation, admissions, majors, transfer, and career goals.

Entrance Examination and Admissions Assistance

Projects help students prepare for authorized admissions tests and complete college applications.

Financial-Aid Information

Participants learn about Pell Grants, Federal Work-Study, loans, scholarships, veterans’ benefits, state aid, institutional aid, loan forgiveness, and other resources.

Secondary Reentry and Alternative Education

Projects assist participants who left school or face interruption with reentry, alternative programs, diploma pathways, high-school equivalency, or postsecondary entry.

Financial and Economic Literacy

Students and families receive instruction concerning college costs, budgeting, banking, credit, debt, aid offers, borrowing, taxes, and financial planning.

Core Curriculum

After at least two years of federal funding, projects provide the required core subjects in the academic-year or summer instructional structure.

Additional Authorized Services

Cultural Exposure

Projects can provide access to events, institutions, travel, performances, and academic experiences not ordinarily available to participants.

Career Exploration

Students learn about occupations, educational requirements, workplace expectations, salaries, and career pathways.

Mentoring

Teachers, counselors, faculty, college students, alumni, professionals, and community leaders can serve as mentors.

On-Campus Residential Programs

Federal funds can support structured residential programs at colleges and other approved sites.

Work-Study

Projects may operate summer work-study positions exposing participants to careers requiring postsecondary education.

Specially Designed Services

Projects can tailor services for:

  • Students with limited English proficiency
  • Students with disabilities
  • Students experiencing homelessness
  • Students in or aging out of foster care
  • Students traditionally underrepresented in postsecondary education
  • Other disconnected students

Technology

Projects can purchase or provide computers, software, internet-supported instruction, and other technology needed for participant services and administration.

Other Purpose-Aligned Activities

The project can implement additional evidence-informed activities consistent with the approved application and federal program purpose.

College Admissions and Financial Aid

College Search

Participants compare institution type, program offerings, admissions, cost, location, campus environment, graduation, transfer, accreditation, and career outcomes.

College Visits

Projects arrange campus tours, classroom visits, student panels, residence-hall exposure, and meetings with admissions and financial-aid staff.

Testing

Projects can provide test preparation, registration support, transportation, and fees when waivers are unavailable and payment satisfies federal rules.

Applications

Staff assist with forms, essays, activity lists, recommendations, transcripts, fee waivers, portfolios, auditions, and deadlines.

FAFSA

Projects help students and contributors create accounts, gather information, consent to tax-data exchange, submit the FAFSA, correct errors, and interpret the FAFSA Submission Summary.

Scholarships

Participants identify and apply for public, institutional, foundation, employer, community, military, and private scholarships.

Aid-Offer Comparison

Students compare grants, loans, work-study, net price, unmet need, renewable conditions, housing, deposits, and indirect costs.

Enrollment Transition

Projects help participants complete orientation, housing, placement, registration, health, disability, transcript, and deposit requirements.

Stipends and Work-Study

Purpose of Stipends

Stipends recognize full-time participation and can offset transportation, meals, personal expenses, or income the student might otherwise earn during project activity.

Academic-Year Ceiling

A regular Upward Bound stipend may not exceed $40 per month during the academic-year component.

Summer Ceiling

A summer stipend may not exceed $60 per month for no more than three months.

Summer Work-Study Ceiling

A youth participating in an approved summer work-study position may receive up to $300 per month.

Satisfactory Participation

The participant must demonstrate regular attendance and performance under standards described in the funded application.

Proration

The project can prorate a stipend according to the number of scheduled sessions attended.

Not a Scholarship

The limited stipend is separate from college financial aid and does not substitute for Pell Grants, institutional aid, or scholarships.

Work-Study Distinction

Upward Bound summer work-study is a project activity for secondary-school youth. It is different from the Federal Work-Study Program administered as postsecondary student aid.

Summer Bridge

Eligible Participants

Recent secondary-school graduates transitioning into postsecondary education can participate when the project includes an approved bridge component.

College Credit

Grant funds can pay tuition for postsecondary credit courses at the host institution under the applicable rules.

Transition Skills

Bridge activities can include registration, placement, writing, mathematics, study skills, time management, financial aid, campus navigation, housing, and academic advising.

Early Momentum

Earning credit and becoming familiar with the institution before the fall can reduce uncertainty and strengthen the first-year transition.

Limits

Summer bridge is not a separate degree program and does not guarantee continued enrollment or completion.

Allowable Program Costs

Upward Bound funds must be reasonable, necessary, allocable, documented, and consistent with the approved project and federal grant rules.

Instruction and Personnel

Allowable costs can include project directors, counselors, instructors, tutors, residential staff, administrative support, training, and qualifying fringe benefits.

Residential Costs

Projects can pay participant room and board during an approved residential summer component, subject to the host institution’s regular weekly rate.

Transportation

Projects can pay participant transportation for regularly scheduled activities and staff travel necessary to supervise participants.

Educational Activities

Admissions fees, museums, cultural events, field trips, educational travel, activity fees, and related costs can be allowable.

Technology and Materials

Projects can purchase or lease computers, software, equipment, service agreements, supplies, books, and instructional materials supporting participant services and project administration.

Admissions and Examination Fees

The project can pay a fee directly to a third party when a waiver is unavailable.

Rigorous Coursework

Grant funds can pay limited tuition for qualifying secondary courses unavailable in the district when all regulatory conditions are met. A project can pay for no more than the equivalent of two such courses per participant in a school year.

Insurance and Health Costs

Accident insurance, medical insurance, and health-service fees can be allowable for full-time summer participants.

Unallowable Examples

Construction, renovation, unrelated research, and unauthorized staff tuition or financial aid cannot be charged to the grant.

Grant Competition and Selection

Competitive Application

Eligible organizations submit a detailed proposal during a federal notice inviting applications.

Need for the Project

Regular Upward Bound applicants document poverty, adult educational attainment, dropout, college-going rates, student-to-counselor ratios, and unmet service need within the target area.

Project Design

Reviewers examine recruitment, services, academic instruction, summer programming, staffing, institutional commitment, partnerships, evaluation, and budget.

Objectives

The applicant commits to measurable outcomes concerning academic performance, secondary retention, graduation, rigorous coursework, postsecondary enrollment, and completion.

Plan of Operation

The application explains management, participant selection, service delivery, records, coordination, staff authority, and procedures for addressing participant needs.

Institutional Commitment

Although no formal match is required, reviewers consider facilities, administrative support, faculty involvement, campus resources, and the applicant’s capacity to sustain high-quality service.

Peer Review

Nonfederal reviewers score applications using published federal criteria. The Department resolves eligibility, technical, budget, prior-experience, and funding-band questions.

Current Cycle

The last competition was held in fiscal year 2022. Current projects generally operate within the five-year 2022–2027 award cycle.

Prior-Experience Points

Purpose

Prior-experience points recognize an existing grantee’s documented success in operating the same TRIO program.

Maximum Points

Federal regulations authorize up to fifteen prior-experience points.

Performance Areas

Points can reflect attainment of approved objectives involving:

  • Academic performance
  • Secondary-school retention
  • Graduation
  • Completion of rigorous coursework
  • Postsecondary enrollment
  • Postsecondary completion

Designated Years

The Department identifies the project years used for the next competition. Current 2022–2027 APR procedures preserve participant and performance data needed for future review.

Continuity

Prior-experience points help preserve effective projects and relationships built over time.

Barrier to New Applicants

New applicants cannot receive prior-experience points. A strong new proposal can therefore compete against an incumbent with up to fifteen additional points.

Policy Debate

Supporters describe prior experience as performance accountability. Critics argue that it can entrench the existing geographic distribution and make it difficult to establish projects in newly underserved areas.

Project Administration

Project Director

A grantee generally employs a full-time project director unless a regulatory exception or waiver applies. The director must possess sufficient authority to administer the project effectively.

Project Staff

Staff can include counselors, academic coordinators, instructors, tutors, residential supervisors, family-engagement specialists, data personnel, drivers, and administrative support.

Participant Number

Each grantee must serve at least the number of participants identified in the competition notice and funded application.

Recordkeeping

For each participant, the grantee documents:

  • Eligibility
  • Academic need
  • Services received
  • Secondary-school progress
  • Postsecondary progress when available
  • Services from similar federal programs when practicable

Annual Performance Report

The grantee submits an APR documenting progress toward project objectives and supplying data used in federal performance and prior-experience calculations.

Financial Management

Projects maintain internal controls, allowable-cost documentation, procurement records, payroll, travel, equipment, participant payments, and federal cash-management compliance.

Evaluation

The grantee implements the evaluation plan described in its funded application and uses results to improve service delivery.

Audit

Federal grant expenditures are subject to Uniform Guidance, Single Audit, Department monitoring, corrective action, questioned costs, and other enforcement.

Performance Measures

Academic Performance

Projects track grade-point average or another approved measure of secondary academic performance.

Secondary Retention

Projects measure whether participants continue to the next grade level and remain enrolled.

Graduation

Participants are expected to earn a regular secondary-school diploma within the applicable period.

Rigorous Coursework

Projects report whether participants complete a rigorous secondary-school program of study.

Postsecondary Enrollment

The Department measures the share of college-ready participants enrolling in postsecondary education.

Postsecondary Completion

Projects track degree and credential completion to the extent possible after participants leave high school.

2024 Federal Outcome

The Department reported a 78.6-percent postsecondary enrollment rate for the defined Upward Bound outcome cohort in 2024, compared with an 84-percent federal target.

Cost per Successful Outcome

The Department reported an average federal cost of approximately $5,450 per defined successful Upward Bound outcome in 2024. A successful outcome can include grade persistence, secondary graduation, or postsecondary enrollment depending on the participant stage.

Evaluation and Research

National Evaluation

Federal evaluations have examined participant characteristics, service intensity, high-school completion, college enrollment, institution choice, persistence, and degree attainment.

Mixed Average Effects

Past experimental and quasi-experimental studies have produced mixed overall findings. Some analyses found stronger effects for students with lower educational expectations, participants receiving more intensive service, or particular program designs.

Find the Fit

The Institute of Education Sciences tested an advising approach using college information, text reminders, and staff training to reduce undermatching.

Find the Fit Results

The 2021 report found that the intervention shifted some enrollment choices toward more selective colleges but did not significantly change whether students undermatched or persisted through the third year after high school.

Interpretive Limits

An evaluation of one advising strategy is not identical to an evaluation of every Upward Bound service. Projects combine instruction, counseling, summer experience, mentoring, family engagement, and local partnerships in different ways.

Selection and Comparison

Participants are selected because they need and can benefit from services. Many nonparticipants receive Talent Search, school counseling, nonprofit support, or other college-access assistance, complicating simple comparisons.

Current Federal Study

The Department continues to study TRIO implementation and the feasibility of future outcome evaluations. Project APR data remain the principal annual performance source.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Civil Rights Era Context

Upward Bound developed during the struggle to desegregate schools and colleges and to remove legal and institutional barriers limiting Black educational opportunity.

School Inequality

African American students remain disproportionately represented in schools with concentrated poverty, limited advanced coursework, high counselor caseloads, teacher turnover, aging facilities, and fewer college-preparatory resources.

Income and Wealth

Racial disparities in household income, homeownership, inheritance, savings, and family wealth affect the ability to pay for test preparation, travel, applications, summer programs, and unpaid educational experiences.

First-Generation Status

Historical exclusion from higher education shapes whether parents possess direct experience with admissions, financial aid, majors, transfer, graduate education, and campus institutions.

Historically Black Colleges and Universities

HBCUs operate Upward Bound projects and serve as college destinations, summer campuses, sources of faculty and mentors, and institutional anchors in Black communities.

Curriculum and Identity

Projects can expose participants to African American history, literature, science, art, institutions, and professional achievement while maintaining rigorous college preparation.

Mentorship

Black faculty, college students, alumni, professionals, and community leaders can provide role models and networks that are limited in some target schools.

College Choice

Advising can help students compare HBCUs, predominantly White institutions, community colleges, regional universities, selective institutions, and career programs according to academic fit, cost, support, safety, and goals.

Equity Standard

Racial-equity evaluation should examine who is recruited, who is admitted, service intensity, discipline, residential safety, advanced-course access, financial-aid outcomes, college enrollment, persistence, and completion—not only total participant counts.

Family and Community Role

Family Information

Projects explain college options, costs, financial aid, safety, academic expectations, transportation, housing, and the meaning of first-generation transition.

Consent and Documentation

Parents or guardians commonly provide consent for records, travel, residential participation, medical care, photographs, technology, and emergency procedures.

Family Financial Literacy

Workshops can address FAFSA contributor duties, scholarships, borrowing, parent loans, scams, budgeting, and comparing aid offers.

Community Partnerships

Projects partner with schools, libraries, youth organizations, churches, cultural institutions, businesses, public agencies, and alumni.

Transportation

Reliable transportation is essential for after-school, weekend, campus, and summer participation, especially in rural and geographically dispersed communities.

Community Trust

Longstanding staff, culturally responsive outreach, clear communication, and visible alumni success strengthen recruitment and retention.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Academic Preparation

Instruction and tutoring strengthen skills needed for high-school completion and entry-level college coursework.

College Exposure

Campus visits and residential summer experiences make postsecondary institutions more familiar and understandable.

Application Support

Staff help students complete complex admissions, testing, financial-aid, and enrollment requirements.

Financial Literacy

Participants and families make more informed decisions concerning price, grants, loans, work, scholarships, and unmet need.

Mentoring and Networks

Students build relationships with peers, educators, faculty, professionals, alumni, and college staff.

Summer Learning

Intensive summer instruction can reduce learning loss, preview advanced coursework, and provide time unavailable during the school year.

Family Support

Projects create a structured source of information for families without prior college experience.

Institutional Capacity

Grants connect colleges with schools and communities and create staff expertise in low-income and first-generation college access.

Long-Term Aspiration

Repeated participation can affect course choices, educational expectations, persistence, and the participant’s sense of belonging in higher education.

Limitations and Risks

Limited Reach

The number of eligible students greatly exceeds funded spaces. Many schools and communities have no Upward Bound project.

Geographic Restrictions

A student can meet federal eligibility standards but live outside a project’s target area or attend a non-target school.

Competitive Instability

A project can lose funding after a competition, disrupting staff, target-school relationships, transportation, summer programming, and participant continuity.

Staff Turnover

Low salaries, temporary appointments, grant uncertainty, and demanding schedules can undermine relationship-based service.

Transportation

Students may be unable to attend after-school, weekend, or summer activities because of distance, employment, family duties, or inadequate transportation.

Participation Burden

Intensive schedules can conflict with jobs, athletics, family responsibilities, caregiving, religious activity, and other commitments.

Uneven Program Quality

Projects vary in curriculum, faculty involvement, residential experience, mentoring, technology, counseling, family engagement, and college-transition support.

Evaluation Complexity

Participants receive services over multiple years from multiple institutions. Average national estimates can conceal strong and weak project models.

College Completion

Preparing students for college does not eliminate tuition, housing, food, transportation, health, academic, or institutional barriers encountered after enrollment.

Major Policy Debates

Expansion or Elimination

Supporters seek increased funding and broader geographic coverage. The fiscal year 2027 administration request proposes ending federal TRIO funding and shifting responsibility to states and institutions.

Evidence of Effectiveness

Critics cite missed performance targets and mixed national evaluations. Advocates emphasize multiyear services, participant disadvantage, local variation, benefits for subgroups, and limitations of studying one program model or intervention.

Prior-Experience Points

Prior experience rewards successful incumbent grantees and protects continuity. It can also disadvantage new applicants and reinforce the current map of projects.

Depth or Breadth

Intensive residential and instructional services cost more per participant. Broader counseling models can serve more students but provide less sustained support.

College Versus Workforce Preparation

Upward Bound’s statutory mission centers on postsecondary education. Current policy debates ask how projects should incorporate career exploration, technical education, apprenticeships, and short-term credentials without weakening college preparation.

Residential Programs

Residential summer experiences can be transformative but costly. Commuter and virtual models can expand access while providing less immersion.

Income Definition

The taxable-income test is administratively clear but does not fully measure wealth, regional cost, family instability, or current hardship.

Federal or Local Responsibility

Supporters describe national college access as a civil-rights and economic-mobility responsibility. Critics argue that schools, states, colleges, and communities should design and finance local support.

Key Distinctions

Upward Bound and TRIO

Upward Bound is one program within the broader Federal TRIO system.

Regular Upward Bound and Upward Bound Math-Science

Regular Upward Bound provides broad academic and college preparation. UBMS concentrates on mathematics, science, technology, engineering, research, and related careers.

Regular Upward Bound and Veterans Upward Bound

Regular Upward Bound serves secondary-school students. Veterans Upward Bound serves veterans preparing to enter or return to postsecondary education.

Upward Bound and Talent Search

Upward Bound generally provides more intensive instruction and summer programming to smaller cohorts. Talent Search generally offers broader outreach and counseling to larger populations.

Upward Bound and GEAR UP

Upward Bound selects eligible individual participants through funded projects. GEAR UP commonly serves entire cohorts or priority students in high-poverty schools beginning no later than middle school.

Upward Bound and Student Support Services

Upward Bound prepares students before college entry. Student Support Services assists eligible students after enrollment at a participating institution.

Eligibility and Admission

Federal eligibility allows a student to be considered by a local project. It does not guarantee selection.

Stipend and Scholarship

An Upward Bound stipend is a small participation payment. A scholarship pays postsecondary educational costs under separate criteria.

Summer Work-Study and Federal Work-Study

Upward Bound work-study is a precollege project activity. Federal Work-Study is a separate need-based postsecondary student-employment program.

College Preparation and College Admission

Upward Bound strengthens preparation and applications but does not control institutional admissions decisions.

Geographic Scope

Upward Bound operates throughout the United States, including states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and participating territories.

Projects serve urban neighborhoods, rural communities, Tribal areas, border regions, migrant communities, suburbs, small towns, and geographically isolated schools.

Project availability is not universal. Federal grant competition produces a map of funded target schools rather than a nationwide entitlement available at every secondary school.

Rural projects face distance, transportation, broadband, staffing, and residential-program challenges. Urban projects can face large school populations, fragmented systems, violence, housing instability, and extensive demand exceeding funded capacity.

Public four-year institutions operate the largest share of the broader Upward Bound family’s funding, followed by public two-year colleges, private institutions, nonprofits, agencies, and other organizations.

Alaska and Hawaii use separate income levels reflecting federal poverty-guideline differences.

Summary

Upward Bound is the first Federal TRIO Program and one of the nation’s principal intensive college-preparation initiatives for low-income and potential first-generation secondary-school students.

The program began under the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 as part of the War on Poverty and later became part of the Higher Education Act.

Regular Upward Bound provides sustained academic-year and summer services. It is separate from Upward Bound Math-Science and Veterans Upward Bound, although all three share a statutory and regulatory framework.

Participants generally must have completed the eighth grade, be thirteen through nineteen years old, need academic support, and meet an authorized low-income, first-generation, or academic-risk category.

At least two-thirds of participants generally must be both low income and potential first-generation college students. The current low-income standard equals 150 percent of the federal poverty guideline.

Projects provide tutoring, course-selection advice, college entrance and application assistance, financial-aid information, reentry support, financial literacy, and a required core curriculum after two years of funding.

Projects may also provide mentoring, cultural exposure, residential summer experiences, work-study, technology, career exploration, and specialized services for students with disabilities, limited English proficiency, homelessness, foster-care experience, or other disconnection.

The summer component is intended to simulate a college-going experience. Many projects combine intensive instruction with campus residence, laboratories, libraries, cultural activities, social development, and structured independence.

Upward Bound is a competitive project grant, not an individual entitlement. Students apply to local funded projects, and eligible applicants can be denied because of limited capacity or target-school boundaries.

Regular grants ordinarily operate for five years and require no formal cost match. The most recent competition was held in fiscal year 2022, producing the current principal 2022–2027 grant cycle.

In fiscal year 2024, regular Upward Bound received approximately $379.88 million for 1,026 projects funded to serve 73,519 participants. Fiscal year 2025 continuation funding was approximately $386.919 million.

The Department reported a 78.6-percent postsecondary enrollment rate for the defined 2024 Upward Bound outcome cohort.

Upward Bound’s public purpose extends beyond college applications. It seeks to overcome unequal access to rigorous coursework, counseling, financial knowledge, campuses, mentors, and institutional networks.

The program has particular significance for African American communities and HBCUs because it developed during the Civil Rights era and addresses barriers produced by poverty, school inequality, segregation, limited wealth, and exclusion from college-preparatory resources.

The administration has proposed eliminating TRIO funding in fiscal year 2027, but Congress has not enacted that proposal. As of August 2026, Upward Bound remains authorized and active.

The program’s continuing effectiveness depends on adequate funding, stable staff, high-quality instruction, transportation, strong school partnerships, meaningful summer experiences, accurate participant selection, family engagement, college affordability, and support after participants enter postsecondary education.
