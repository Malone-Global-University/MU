Disadvantaged Business Enterprise Program

The United States Department of Transportation nondiscrimination and small-business participation program governing federally assisted highway, transit, and airport contracts administered by state and local recipients.

Terminology Note

The Disadvantaged Business Enterprise Program, commonly abbreviated DBE Program, is administered by the United States Department of Transportation under 49 C.F.R. Part 26.

A DBE is a certified for-profit small business that is at least 51 percent owned by one or more individuals who establish social and economic disadvantage and whose management and daily operations are controlled by qualifying owners.

DBE certification does not ordinarily create a federal prime-contract preference in direct United States Department of Transportation purchasing. Part 26 principally governs contracts awarded by state and local agencies, airport sponsors, transit agencies, and other recipients using federal DOT financial assistance.

A DBE contract goal is not a quota or a contract set-aside. A bidder may satisfy a contract goal through enforceable DBE commitments or qualify for award by proving adequate good-faith efforts when the goal is not met.

The separate Airport Concession Disadvantaged Business Enterprise Program, abbreviated ACDBE, governs concessions serving the travelling public at qualifying airports under 49 C.F.R. Part 23. Many certification standards overlap, but airport concessions and airport construction contracts are not the same program activity.

The program entered a major legal and administrative transition on October 3, 2025. An interim final rule removed race- and sex-based presumptions of disadvantage, required individualized evidence from every qualifying owner, and directed every state Unified Certification Program to reevaluate its existing certified firms.

As of August 2, 2026, reevaluation status varied by jurisdiction. A recipient covered by a Unified Certification Program that had not completed reevaluation could not set DBE contract goals or count participation toward DBE overall goals. Researchers and contractors must therefore verify the current status of the relevant state or territorial certification program.

Overview

The DBE Program governs participation by eligible disadvantaged small businesses in transportation contracts financed through federal grants, cooperative agreements, loan guarantees, and related DOT financial assistance.

It does not ordinarily require a state transportation department or transit authority to award a fixed percentage of every contract to DBEs.

Each covered recipient develops an overall goal based on the availability of firms in its transportation contracting market. The recipient estimates how much participation will occur through neutral measures available to all small businesses and uses DBE-conscious contract goals only for the remaining portion.

When a contract goal applies, the apparent successful bidder must identify certified DBEs, work items, and dollar commitments sufficient to meet the goal or document intensive and reasonable efforts to obtain participation.

The recipient must verify that committed firms are certified for the relevant work, monitor their actual performance and payment, prevent improper termination, and count only commercially useful work.

Certification is conducted through state Unified Certification Programs rather than by every recipient separately. The system is intended to provide one-stop certification and a public directory of firms and approved work codes.

The October 2025 interim final rule removed presumptive eligibility based on race, ethnicity, or sex. Every individual owner now must demonstrate personal social and economic disadvantage through individualized evidence and financial information.

The same rule required every UCP to reevaluate its existing directory. This created a transitional patchwork in which some jurisdictions completed reevaluation and resumed goal administration while others remained unable to use contract goals or count participation.

Classification and Scope

This page classifies DBE as a government program because it is a continuing federal civil-rights and contracting system administered through DOT financial-assistance recipients.

The Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982, later surface-transportation authorization acts, airport statutes, and related provisions belong primarily under Laws and Legislation.

Unified Certification Programs, the Federal Highway Administration, Federal Transit Administration, Federal Aviation Administration, state transportation departments, and individual transit or airport recipients may receive separate institutional pages.

The Airport Concession DBE Program should receive a separate program page because it concerns airport concessions, management contracts, franchises, car rentals, goods, and services rather than ordinary construction and professional-service contracts.

State minority-business, women-business, small-business, and local disadvantaged-business certifications are not automatically federal DBE certifications. A firm must appear in the relevant Part 26 UCP directory.

Historical Context

Highway construction, airport development, public transit, engineering, materials supply, and heavy construction historically relied on established networks of prime contractors, unions, financial institutions, sureties, suppliers, and government officials.

Minority-owned and women-owned firms encountered exclusion from trade associations, unions, apprenticeship systems, credit, bonding, equipment finance, public bidding, professional licensing, and subcontracting networks.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination in federally assisted programs, but formal nondiscrimination alone did not produce equal access to transportation contracts.

DOT developed minority- and women-business participation requirements under earlier regulations before Congress enacted a specific statutory disadvantaged-business provision.

The Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982 required, unless the Secretary determined otherwise, that at least 10 percent of specified highway and transit funds be expended with small businesses owned and controlled by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals.

Congress and DOT continued and amended the program through later highway, transit, airport, and infrastructure laws.

Earlier Minority- and Women-Business Programs

Before the modern DBE statute, DOT and its operating administrations used minority-business and women-business participation policies under civil-rights, procurement, and financial-assistance authority.

Early programs were often described through separate Minority Business Enterprise and Women Business Enterprise categories.

Administrative rules evolved in response to litigation, congressional direction, constitutional standards, and the need for uniform certification.

The modern term DBE combined eligible disadvantaged ownership under a single transportation framework and eventually replaced the principal older categories in Part 26.

Surface Transportation Assistance Act of 1982

Section 105(f) of the Surface Transportation Assistance Act established the first major statutory DBE provision for federal-aid highway and transit funds.

The act directed DOT, except to the extent the Secretary determined otherwise, to ensure expenditure of at least 10 percent of specified funds with small firms owned and controlled by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals.

The statute borrowed disadvantage concepts from the Small Business Act.

DOT amended its regulations in 1983 to implement the new statutory requirement.

The national percentage established a federal policy objective but did not permanently settle how recipients should set local goals or prove that race-conscious measures were constitutionally justified.

Surface Transportation and Uniform Relocation Assistance Act of 1987

Congress renewed the disadvantaged-business requirement in the 1987 surface-transportation law.

DOT revised its rules and continued use of overall and contract participation goals.

The program increasingly integrated women-owned firms and specified racial and ethnic groups through statutory and regulatory presumptions that remained central until 2025.

Later authorization statutes continued the national 10-percent objective for highway, transit, and related programs.

Airport Development DBE Requirements

Federal airport-development law requires disadvantaged-business participation in qualifying airport planning and development funds.

Airport sponsors use Part 26 for construction, architecture, engineering, professional services, equipment, and other contracts financed with qualifying Federal Aviation Administration assistance.

Federal airport statutes also contain a national 10-percent expenditure objective and require uniform certification criteria.

Airport concession activity is administered separately under Part 23.

Adarand Constructors v. Peña

In 1995, the Supreme Court held that federal racial classifications are subject to strict scrutiny.

A racial classification must serve a compelling governmental interest and be narrowly tailored to achieve that interest.

The case arose from a federal highway subcontracting incentive involving a certified disadvantaged firm.

Adarand did not automatically invalidate every disadvantaged-business program. It required DOT and Congress to justify and tailor any race-conscious components.

The 1999 Part 26 rule responded by emphasising evidence-based local goals, race-neutral measures, flexible contract goals, good-faith efforts, waivers, and the prohibition of quotas.

1999 Part 26 Framework

DOT published the comprehensive Part 26 final rule on February 2, 1999, effective March 4, 1999.

The rule:

  • created uniform program objectives;
  • required evidence-based recipient overall goals;
  • required maximum feasible use of race-neutral measures;
  • limited contract goals to the portion not expected through neutral means;
  • prohibited quotas and ordinary DBE set-asides;
  • established Unified Certification Programs;
  • standardised ownership, control, size, and disadvantage rules;
  • required commercially useful functions;
  • created bidder good-faith-effort protections;
  • regulated substitution and termination;
  • created administrative certification appeals; and
  • required monitoring, reporting, and enforcement.

Western States Paving Co. v. Washington State Department of Transportation

In 2005, the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held that race-conscious DBE measures must be supported by evidence of discrimination in the relevant state or local transportation market.

The court accepted Congress’s compelling interest in addressing discrimination but required local narrow tailoring.

The decision increased emphasis on disparity studies, local market data, neutral measures, and limits on contract goals in jurisdictions within the Ninth Circuit.

Other courts generally upheld the federal framework while applying strict-scrutiny principles to particular state implementation.

2014 Implementation Modifications

DOT revised Part 26 in 2014 to strengthen certification, goal implementation, prompt payment, good-faith efforts, contract monitoring, and reporting.

The rule required bidders to provide quotes when a non-DBE was selected over a DBE for goal work and strengthened recipient review of substitutions and continuing efforts.

It also clarified annual declarations, interstate certification, and protections against arbitrary removal of committed DBEs.

2024 DBE and ACDBE Modernization

DOT published a major final rule on April 9, 2024, effective May 9, with technical corrections on July 3.

Major changes included:

  • raising the personal-net-worth threshold to $2,047,000;
  • updating the statutory business-size cap;
  • modernising personal-net-worth calculations;
  • simplifying ownership-investment rules;
  • revising control and governance analysis;
  • requiring immediate interstate certification reciprocity;
  • strengthening data and directory standards;
  • creating separate regular-dealer and distributor counting rules;
  • requiring written supplier affirmations;
  • expanding design-build open-ended performance plans;
  • strengthening proactive monitoring and running tallies;
  • updating reporting and electronic systems;
  • creating more flexible interviews and reviews; and
  • revising ACDBE rules in parallel.

Mid-America Milling Litigation

Contractors challenged race- and sex-based presumptions and contract-goal requirements in federal court.

On September 23, 2024, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky issued a preliminary injunction protecting the plaintiffs from DOT-mandated use of the presumptions on contracts for which they competed.

In 2025, DOT and the Department of Justice concluded that the presumptions were unconstitutional and sought a consent order.

DOT then issued a nationwide interim final rule changing the certification system rather than limiting relief to the original plaintiffs.

October 2025 Interim Final Rule

The interim final rule became effective immediately on October 3, 2025.

It:

  • removed race- and sex-based presumptions from the program regulations;
  • required all applicants to prove social and economic disadvantage individually;
  • required a personal narrative describing specific barriers, hardship, denied opportunities, and resulting economic harm;
  • required a current personal-net-worth statement and relevant financial evidence;
  • required every UCP to reevaluate every currently certified DBE and ACDBE;
  • required written recertification or decertification decisions;
  • excluded ordinary Part 26 decertification procedures from the special reevaluation process;
  • prohibited new DBE contract goals before jurisdiction-wide reevaluation completion;
  • prohibited counting DBE participation toward goals before completion;
  • suspended recipient goal-shortfall compliance consequences during transition; and
  • preserved the remainder of the certification, contracting, counting, monitoring, and enforcement system.

Current Jurisdiction-by-Jurisdiction Transition

DOT maintains a public list identifying whether each state or territorial UCP has completed reevaluation.

Jurisdictions completing reevaluation may resume contract-goal administration and counting using firms recertified under the individualized standard.

Jurisdictions that have not completed reevaluation remain unable to set DBE contract goals or count participation toward overall goals.

The transition does not necessarily prohibit every DBE-neutral small-business activity, technical-assistance measure, or continued performance of an existing contract.

Contractors must review the current UCP status, solicitation language, recipient guidance, and firm directory before relying on DBE credit.

Regulatory Objectives

Part 26 seeks to:

  • ensure nondiscrimination in DOT-assisted contracting;
  • create a level playing field for certified firms;
  • ensure that the program is narrowly tailored;
  • ensure that only eligible firms participate;
  • remove barriers to DBE participation;
  • assist development so firms can compete outside the program;
  • provide appropriate flexibility to recipients; and
  • ensure compliance with federal civil-rights and financial-assistance requirements.

Recipients Covered by Part 26

Part 26 applies to recipients of specified FHWA, FTA, and FAA financial assistance.

FHWA recipients include state transportation departments and other agencies expending federal-aid highway funds.

FTA Tier I recipients expect to award more than $670,000 in FTA-funded prime contracts, excluding transit-vehicle purchases, during a federal fiscal year.

FTA Tier II recipients expect to award no more than $670,000 and may operate a neutral program under the current regulatory structure.

FAA recipients receiving planning or development grants must maintain a program when expected FAA-funded prime contracts exceed $250,000 during a fiscal year.

Lower-volume recipients remain subject to applicable nondiscrimination, reporting, certification-use, and grant conditions even where particular goal requirements differ.

DOT-Assisted Contract

A DOT-assisted contract is a legally binding prime contract or subcontract funded wholly or partly through DOT financial assistance.

Assistance can include grants, letters of credit, and loan guarantees.

Construction, architecture, engineering, planning, consulting, equipment, supplies, professional services, trucking, materials, and leases may fall within the program.

A contract solely for land purchase is excluded from the Part 26 definition.

A project can contain both federally assisted and non-federal contracts. The funding and solicitation documents determine whether Part 26 applies.

Recipient DBE Program

A covered recipient adopts a written DBE program approved by its principal DOT operating administration.

The program describes:

  • the recipient’s nondiscrimination commitment;
  • DBE liaison officer and organisational responsibilities;
  • financial and staffing resources;
  • Unified Certification Program participation;
  • directory use;
  • goal-setting methods;
  • neutral and conscious measures;
  • bidder good-faith-effort procedures;
  • contract monitoring;
  • prompt payment and retainage;
  • commercially useful function review;
  • reporting and data collection;
  • small-business elements;
  • complaint and enforcement procedures; and
  • public consultation and notice.

DBE Liaison Officer

Each recipient designates a DBE liaison officer with direct and independent access to the chief executive concerning program matters.

The officer coordinates goals, outreach, contract review, monitoring, data, complaints, corrective action, and communication with DOT.

The position requires adequate staff, authority, training, and resources.

Assigning the title without sufficient independence or capacity does not satisfy effective administration.

Unified Certification Program

Every state has a Unified Certification Program, commonly abbreviated UCP.

The UCP may be operated by one lead agency, a committee, several certifying partners, or a regional structure approved by DOT.

A UCP makes Part 26 certification decisions on behalf of all DOT recipients within its jurisdiction.

Certification by the UCP must be honored by every recipient in that state.

The UCP must maintain a public directory identifying each DBE and the specific work codes for which it is certified.

UCPs cooperate with DOT audits, oversight, training, database, reevaluation, and certification directives.

Jurisdiction of Original Certification

The jurisdiction of original certification is ordinarily the state where the firm maintains its principal place of business when it first applies.

The firm must obtain certification there before seeking interstate certification elsewhere.

The original UCP maintains the principal certification file and annual declaration anniversary.

Loss of certification in the original jurisdiction now produces coordinated effects in other jurisdictions under the interstate system.

Interstate Certification

The 2024 rule replaced the former document-heavy interstate process with immediate reciprocity.

A firm certified in its original jurisdiction submits to another UCP:

  • a cover letter identifying the request and all jurisdictions where it is certified;
  • an electronic image or equivalent evidence from the original UCP directory; and
  • a current Declaration of Eligibility.

The additional UCP must confirm certification within ten business days and certify the firm immediately without repeating the full application process.

After certification, the additional UCP may obtain the full unredacted file and may initiate coordinated decertification for legitimate reasons.

DBE Directory

The public directory identifies certified firms, contact information, work descriptions, and NAICS or other classification codes.

Prime contractors use the directory to identify potential subcontractors, suppliers, truckers, consultants, and joint-venture partners.

Certification credit is limited to work for which the DBE is listed and qualified.

Directory listing does not establish that the firm is available, responsible, licensed, bonded, experienced, or capable of every project described by a broad NAICS code.

During the 2025–2026 reevaluation, firms may be removed, recertified, or have work codes changed.

Certification Application Process

  1. Identify the original jurisdiction. The firm applies through the UCP where it maintains its principal place of business.
  2. Submit the uniform application. The applicant provides ownership, control, financial, business, affiliate, work-history, and other required information.
  3. Provide individualized disadvantage evidence. Each qualifying owner submits a personal narrative and economic evidence under the October 2025 standard.
  4. Submit a personal-net-worth statement. The certifier reviews assets, liabilities, exclusions, transfers, and financial access.
  5. Document ownership and investment. Organisational records, stock, capital contributions, purchase agreements, loans, and gifts establish real ownership.
  6. Document control. The applicant supplies governance, licences, résumés, management authority, contracts, bank authority, and operational records.
  7. Undergo interview and site review. Certifiers investigate business operations, equipment, employees, offices, and the owner’s knowledge and authority.
  8. Receive a certification decision. Approval includes work codes; denial must state reasons and appeal rights.
  9. File annual declarations. The firm reports continuing eligibility and gross receipts each year.
  10. Seek interstate reciprocity where needed. Additional UCPs must follow the current acceptance procedure.

Burden of Proof

The applicant bears the burden of proving every certification requirement by a preponderance of the evidence.

The certifier considers the entire record rather than treating one document or threshold as conclusive.

During an ordinary decertification proceeding, the certifier bears the burden of proving that the firm is no longer eligible.

The special 2025 reevaluation process follows its own rule and does not apply the ordinary Part 26 decertification procedure.

Individualized Social Disadvantage

Every qualifying owner must now establish social and economic disadvantage affirmatively without regard to race or sex.

The required personal narrative must describe specific experiences and circumstances in American society.

Relevant evidence can concern:

  • economic hardship;
  • systemic barriers;
  • denied educational opportunity;
  • employment barriers;
  • business exclusion;
  • unequal access to contracts or professional networks;
  • financing on worse terms than similarly situated persons;
  • bonding, licensing, or supplier barriers;
  • loss of customers or opportunities; and
  • other measurable impediments to progress or success.

The narrative must connect the barriers to actual economic harm and compare the owner’s circumstances with similarly situated non-disadvantaged persons.

Economic Disadvantage

Economic disadvantage is not established by the personal narrative alone.

The qualifying owner must provide a current personal-net-worth statement and other relevant financial evidence.

Certifiers examine wealth, liquidity, income, assets, liabilities, transfers, ownership resources, capital access, and comparative financing circumstances.

An owner whose personal net worth exceeds $2,047,000 does not satisfy the current financial threshold.

The cap is scheduled for a future inflation-related adjustment using federal household-net-worth data.

Personal-Net-Worth Calculation

Personal net worth is the net value of reportable assets minus liabilities under Part 26.

The owner must use the official form and provide required supporting documents.

Qualified retirement accounts are excluded from net worth but must be disclosed.

Ownership interests, real estate, investments, cash, businesses, trusts, and other assets are evaluated according to the rule’s inclusions, exclusions, and transfer provisions.

A certifier may request additional information only where it has a legitimate and demonstrable need.

Manipulative transfers, understated values, undisclosed beneficial interests, or inconsistent tax and financial information can support denial or fraud referral.

Business Size

A DBE must qualify as a small business under the SBA industry size standard corresponding to the work it performs.

Affiliated businesses are included under SBA size rules.

For FHWA- and FTA-assisted work, Part 26 also imposes a special statutory cap of $30.72 million in average annual gross receipts, calculated over the prior three fiscal years under the current rule.

A firm may be certified for several work codes but can count only where it is small and qualified for the relevant activity.

Size standards and statutory caps are periodically adjusted.

Affiliation

Affiliation can arise when one firm controls or has the power to control another.

Certifiers may examine:

  • common ownership;
  • common management;
  • identity of interest;
  • economic dependence;
  • shared employees;
  • shared equipment or facilities;
  • exclusive customer or subcontract relationships;
  • options and convertible interests;
  • franchise or licence arrangements;
  • joint ventures; and
  • control through contracts or financing.

Affiliation can make the combined enterprise too large and can also show that the qualifying owner lacks independent control.

Ownership

One or more qualifying owners must hold at least 51 percent of the firm.

Ownership must be real, substantial, continuing, and not subject to conditions that allow another person to reclaim or control the interest.

The owner must bear genuine risk and receive benefits proportional to ownership.

Capital contributions may be made through qualifying cash, tangible property, real estate, purchase, or permitted gifts.

Contributions of labour, services, promises, or guarantees do not constitute ownership investment.

Debt-financed ownership can qualify where the transaction preserves the owner’s risk, independence, and proportional benefits.

Control

Qualifying owners must possess the power to direct long-term policy and daily operations.

The owner must understand the business, direct employees, control finances, make contract decisions, and exercise real managerial authority.

A qualifying owner does not need to perform every trade task but must have the experience and authority necessary to control the firm.

State licensing requirements can affect eligibility when a required licence gives a non-qualifying individual indispensable control.

Supermajority provisions, veto rights, board arrangements, financing covenants, employment agreements, family relationships, and dependence on a former employer can defeat control.

Indian Tribe, Native Hawaiian Organization, and Alaska Native Corporation Firms

Part 26 contains special ownership provisions for firms owned by Indian Tribes and Native Hawaiian Organizations.

An eligible Tribe- or NHO-owned firm may qualify if it meets the remaining certification requirements.

A qualifying Alaska Native Corporation subsidiary, joint venture, or partnership may be certified through special criteria where Native shareholders retain majority equity and voting power and the entity has qualifying SBA certification.

These provisions recognise collective Native ownership structures that do not fit ordinary individual ownership.

Principal Place of Business

The principal place of business determines the firm’s original UCP jurisdiction.

It is the location where the firm’s business is directed and controlled, not merely a registered-agent address or temporary project office.

Interstate applicants must first be certified in this jurisdiction.

Changes in principal place of business must be reported and can affect which UCP leads future annual and decertification activity.

Site Review and Owner Interview

Certification includes investigation rather than paper review alone.

A certifier may inspect offices, yards, shops, vehicles, equipment, records, and current work.

The qualifying owner is interviewed concerning estimating, finances, contracts, employees, customers, suppliers, equipment, licences, and management decisions.

Modern rules permit procedural flexibility, including remote tools where appropriate, but certifiers retain responsibility for an adequate factual record.

Annual Declaration of Eligibility

A certified firm submits a new Declaration of Eligibility every year on the anniversary of original certification.

The firm also provides gross-receipts documentation for its most recently completed fiscal year.

The declaration confirms that the firm continues to meet certification requirements and identifies material changes.

Failure to file can result in suspension or decertification.

Annual filing is not the same as the special one-time UCP-wide reevaluation required by the 2025 interim rule.

Reporting Material Changes

A DBE must promptly report changes affecting eligibility rather than waiting for the annual declaration.

Changes can include:

  • ownership transfers;
  • new shareholders or members;
  • changes in officers or managers;
  • marriage, divorce, inheritance, or trust arrangements affecting ownership;
  • new affiliates;
  • merger or acquisition;
  • loss of required licence;
  • major changes in equipment or employees;
  • changes in principal place of business;
  • gross receipts exceeding size limits;
  • personal net worth above the cap; and
  • control by a non-qualifying person.

Certification Denial

A UCP denial must explain each reason and identify the evidence supporting the decision.

Common grounds include failure to establish individual disadvantage, excessive personal net worth, insufficient ownership investment, lack of control, business size, affiliation, incomplete records, or inability to verify operations.

The firm may appeal a final denial to DOT under Part 26.

An appeal reviews the administrative record and does not ordinarily function as a new application with previously omitted evidence.

Ordinary Decertification

Outside the special 2025 reevaluation, a certifier must provide due process before removing certification.

The UCP issues a notice of intent explaining the grounds and provides a hearing or other required opportunity to respond.

The certifier bears the burden of proving ineligibility.

A final notice of decision removes the firm in all jurisdictions under the coordinated interstate rules.

The firm may appeal to DOT.

Special 2025–2026 Reevaluation

Every UCP must identify every currently certified DBE, give each firm an opportunity to submit evidence under the new standard, and issue a written recertification or decertification decision.

The reevaluation requires a personal narrative and personal-net-worth evidence from qualifying individual owners.

The ordinary Part 26 decertification procedure does not govern these transitional decisions.

A UCP must complete the entire directory as quickly as practicable and notify DOT when finished.

Only then may covered recipients resume contract goals and count DBE participation.

National Ten-Percent Goal

Transportation statutes state that, unless the Secretary determines otherwise, at least 10 percent of specified authorised funds should be expended with disadvantaged firms.

Part 26 calls this an aspirational national goal.

It does not require every recipient or contract to use a 10-percent goal.

A recipient cannot simply copy 10 percent without analysing the local availability of DBEs.

Recipient goals may be above or below 10 percent depending on market evidence.

Recipient Overall Goals

An overall goal estimates the DBE participation a recipient would expect absent the effects of social and economic disadvantage.

The recipient begins with a base figure reflecting ready, willing, and able DBEs relative to all relevant firms in the market.

Evidence can include:

  • UCP directories;
  • Census and business data;
  • bidders lists;
  • disparity studies;
  • industry directories;
  • past participation;
  • geographic market analysis;
  • contracting opportunities;
  • capacity and work codes; and
  • other local evidence.

The recipient may adjust the base figure for capacity, discrimination, financing, bonding, education, employment, and other relevant market conditions.

Three-Year Goal Cycle

Most recipients submit overall goals every three years according to operating-administration schedules.

The goal applies annually during the three-year period.

A recipient may revise a goal when circumstances change.

Large or unusual projects can receive separate project goals covering the entire development period.

Timely goal submission and implementation are conditions of continued DOT assistance, subject to the current UCP reevaluation transition.

Consultation and Public Notice

Goal setting requires direct consultation with stakeholders possessing information about DBE availability and market barriers.

Participants can include contractors, DBEs, trade groups, community organisations, professional associations, unions, chambers, and public agencies.

The recipient publishes its proposed goal and methodology on its website before submission.

Public consultation should influence the analysis rather than serve as a purely formal hearing after the result is fixed.

DBE-Neutral and Small-Business Measures

Recipients must maximise measures that can assist DBEs without restricting benefits solely to DBEs.

Measures include:

  • unbundling large contracts;
  • small-business set-asides where independently authorised;
  • simplified bidding and qualification;
  • prompt-payment enforcement;
  • bonding and insurance assistance;
  • financing and working-capital support;
  • technical and management assistance;
  • bid-matching and outreach;
  • mentor–protégé programs;
  • training in estimating and compliance;
  • reduced retainage or alternative security;
  • support for equipment and materials; and
  • information concerning future contracts.

Current regulations use the terms DBE-neutral and DBE-conscious, but the October 2025 rule requires all certification decisions to be race and sex neutral.

Contract Goals

Contract goals are used only where the recipient does not expect to meet its overall goal through neutral participation.

Goals are placed on contracts with realistic subcontracting opportunities.

A specific contract goal may be above or below the recipient’s overall percentage based on work type, location, timing, and DBE availability.

The goal must be open to all certified DBEs rather than divided into separate racial, ethnic, or sex categories.

A recipient must reduce goals when it is exceeding the overall objective and increase or adjust measures when participation falls short.

No contract goal may currently be used in a jurisdiction whose UCP has not completed reevaluation.

No Quotas or Ordinary DBE Set-Asides

Part 26 prohibits DBE quotas.

A recipient cannot refuse to consider adequate good-faith efforts because the bidder failed to reach a numerical goal.

Part 26 also prohibits ordinary DBE contract set-asides.

A DBE-only set-aside may be used only in limited and extreme circumstances when no other reasonable method could address egregious discrimination.

Separate small-business programs or state-law preferences may operate under different authority but must be distinguished from federal DBE credit.

Bidder Compliance Process

When a solicitation contains a DBE goal, the bidder must submit required participation information at the time specified by the recipient.

The submission identifies:

  • each committed certified DBE;
  • the work, materials, or services to be performed;
  • the dollar amount or percentage;
  • signed commitment and confirmation documents;
  • supplier status and anticipated counting percentage;
  • joint-venture participation where applicable; and
  • good-faith-effort evidence if the numerical goal is not met.

The recipient must verify completeness, accuracy, certification, work codes, supplier counting, and the adequacy of good-faith efforts before award.

Good-Faith Efforts

A bidder can qualify for award without meeting the numerical goal if its efforts were sufficiently intensive, timely, and appropriate.

The test asks whether the bidder actively and aggressively tried to obtain enough DBE participation.

Relevant efforts include:

  • early and broad solicitation of certified firms;
  • direct follow-up with firms expressing interest;
  • breaking work into economically feasible units;
  • providing plans, specifications, and adequate response time;
  • good-faith negotiation;
  • explaining rejection of DBE quotes;
  • assisting with bonding, insurance, credit, equipment, and materials;
  • using business-assistance organisations;
  • considering qualified DBEs rather than relying solely on habitual subcontractors; and
  • documenting all contacts and decisions.

Pro forma emails or a promise to seek DBEs after award are generally insufficient.

DBE and Non-DBE Quote Comparison

When a bidder selects a non-DBE over a DBE for goal work, it must provide copies of the relevant DBE and non-DBE quotes.

A DBE may not be rejected merely because its quote is not the lowest.

The prime is not required to accept an unreasonable or excessive price.

The recipient reviews whether price, scope, qualifications, schedule, or other reasons genuinely explain the choice.

Other bidders’ success in obtaining DBE participation can inform whether the apparent successful bidder’s efforts were adequate.

Administrative Reconsideration

A bidder found not to have met the goal or made adequate good-faith efforts must receive an opportunity for reconsideration before award is denied on that ground.

The reconsideration official must not have participated in the original adverse decision.

The bidder may submit written argument and evidence and meet with the official.

The recipient issues a written decision explaining the result.

The decision is not administratively appealable to DOT, though other procurement remedies may be available under applicable law.

Design-Build and Open-Ended Performance Plans

A design-build solicitation may occur before detailed subcontracting opportunities are fully known.

Part 26 permits an Open-Ended DBE Performance Plan in place of final pre-award DBE commitments.

The proposer commits to the goal, identifies anticipated types and values of DBE work, and provides a schedule for executing subcontracts.

The recipient monitors continuing good-faith efforts as design develops.

Written revisions may adjust work packages and timing while preserving the commitment and documented efforts.

Termination, Reduction, and Substitution

A prime contractor cannot remove a committed DBE or reduce its work without prior written recipient consent unless the recipient itself caused the change.

Good cause may include failure or refusal to perform, bankruptcy, ineligibility, loss of licence, inability to obtain required insurance, or other documented circumstances.

The prime must give the DBE notice and an opportunity to respond.

When committed work is lost, the prime must make good-faith efforts to obtain replacement participation sufficient to meet the goal.

A prime’s preference to self-perform the work or inability to find a replacement at the original price does not alone establish good faith.

Prompt Payment and Retainage

Recipients must include contract provisions requiring prime contractors to pay subcontractors promptly after receiving payment for satisfactory work.

Retainage attributable to a subcontractor’s work must also be returned promptly under the applicable method.

The recipient must monitor compliance and provide appropriate remedies for late or withheld payment.

Prompt-payment protection applies to DBE and non-DBE subcontractors.

Delayed payment can undermine small firms that have limited working capital and still must pay employees, suppliers, taxes, equipment, and insurance.

Contract Monitoring

Recipients must monitor actual performance rather than count promised participation automatically.

Monitoring includes:

  • executed subcontract review;
  • work-site observation;
  • employee and equipment verification;
  • invoice and payment tracking;
  • supplier and trucking review;
  • joint-venture oversight;
  • change orders and underruns;
  • termination and substitution approval;
  • commercially useful function determinations;
  • running tallies of commitments and payments;
  • final reconciliation; and
  • fraud or noncompliance referrals.

Counting DBE Participation

A recipient counts only the value of work actually performed by a currently countable DBE under the applicable transition rules.

The work must fall within the firm’s certified work codes.

Credit is based on payment for a commercially useful function rather than the face amount of a subcontract.

Joint-venture credit is limited to the distinct portion performed by the DBE.

Materials, trucking, brokerage, and regular-dealer activities receive different counting treatment.

During the 2025–2026 transition, no DBE participation may be counted in a jurisdiction until its UCP finishes the required reevaluation.

Commercially Useful Function

A DBE performs a commercially useful function when it is responsible for executing, managing, and supervising its work.

For materials, it ordinarily negotiates price, determines quality and quantity, orders, pays for, and where applicable installs the supplies.

The recipient considers subcontracting, industry practice, payment, staffing, equipment, supervision, financial responsibility, and whether the role is commensurate with the credit claimed.

A DBE is not performing a commercially useful function when it is an unnecessary extra participant used to create the appearance of participation.

If the DBE performs less than 30 percent of its subcontract with its own workforce or subcontracts an unusually large portion, it is presumed not to perform a commercially useful function but may rebut that presumption.

DBE Prime Contractors

A certified firm can serve as a prime contractor rather than only as a subcontractor.

DBE credit for a prime award is limited to work the DBE performs with its own forces and eligible DBE subcontracts or supplier participation.

Money passed through a DBE prime to non-DBE subcontractors does not automatically count.

A DBE prime must meet the same responsibility, licensing, bonding, technical, safety, and contract-performance requirements as other prime contractors.

Joint Ventures

A DBE may join one or more other firms for a single for-profit business enterprise.

DBE credit is limited to the distinct, clearly defined portion of work that the DBE performs with its own forces.

The DBE’s ownership, control, management, risk, capital contribution, and profit must be commensurate with its joint-venture participation.

A joint venture cannot be used to place a DBE name on a contract while a non-DBE controls and performs the DBE’s claimed work.

DBE Trucking

A DBE trucking company must manage and supervise the trucking operation for which it claims credit.

It must own and operate at least one licensed, insured, and operational truck used on the contract.

Full credit is available for transportation performed with trucks owned, insured, and operated by the DBE using its employees.

Credit also applies to qualifying trucks leased from other certified DBEs.

Trucks leased from non-DBEs receive more limited treatment under the current counting formula.

Contrived leases, dispatching by another firm, lack of control, or use of a nominal DBE can defeat credit.

Manufacturers

A certified manufacturer produces goods from raw materials or substantially alters materials into the required product.

The recipient may count 100 percent of the cost of materials or supplies obtained from a DBE manufacturer when the regulatory conditions are met.

Mere relabelling, minor assembly, brokering, or temporary possession does not establish manufacturing.

Regular Dealers

A regular dealer is an established business that owns, operates, or maintains a store, warehouse, or other establishment in which the relevant materials are bought, stocked, and regularly sold or leased.

Current rules generally allow credit for 60 percent of the cost of eligible materials or supplies purchased from a DBE regular dealer.

The firm must have the capacity and intent to act as a regular dealer on the particular contract.

The bidder provides a written supplier affirmation, and the recipient makes a preliminary counting determination before award.

Distributors

The 2024 rule created a distinct distributor category.

A distributor may arrange delivery and assume contractual and financial responsibility without maintaining the inventory structure required of a regular dealer.

Current rules generally permit 40-percent credit for eligible materials and supplies obtained from a qualifying DBE distributor.

The firm must satisfy the regulatory definition and perform the represented function rather than act as an unnecessary pass-through.

Brokers and Transaction Arrangers

When a DBE acts solely as a broker or transaction arranger, the recipient counts only the reasonable fee or commission for the DBE’s work.

The entire value of materials, equipment, insurance, bonds, or services acquired from others is not counted.

A broker cannot be treated as a manufacturer, regular dealer, or distributor without meeting the applicable definition and performing that role.

Professional and Technical Services

DBEs can perform architecture, engineering, surveying, planning, environmental, information-technology, legal, accounting, inspection, design, and other professional services.

The qualifying owner must control the professional practice and required licences.

Credit is based on services actually performed, managed, and supervised by the DBE.

Staff supplied, directed, or paid by another firm can create control and commercially useful function concerns.

Transit Vehicle Manufacturers

Transit vehicle procurements use specialised DBE requirements.

Eligible manufacturers establish DBE goals and submit required information to the Federal Transit Administration.

Transit recipients ordinarily purchase vehicles only from manufacturers meeting FTA’s DBE program requirements or obtain an approved waiver.

Transit-vehicle purchases are excluded from the ordinary FTA recipient contract-volume calculation because the manufacturer program applies.

Business Development and Supportive Services

Part 26 encourages recipients to help DBEs become stronger and compete outside the program.

Federal Highway Administration supportive-services funding can finance training, accounting, estimating, bonding, technical assistance, and capacity development.

Appendix C provides guidelines for recipient DBE business-development elements.

Assistance may help firms enter nontraditional work, obtain equipment, improve financial systems, build management capacity, and pursue prime contracts.

Technical assistance is neutral or developmental; it does not replace certification or guarantee work.

Mentor–Protégé Programs

Recipients may establish mentor–protégé programs to develop DBE capacity.

An experienced firm may provide management, technical, safety, accounting, estimating, equipment, bonding, or project assistance.

The relationship must be structured to develop the DBE rather than create dependency or allow the mentor to control DBE operations.

Mentor assistance and joint ventures are monitored separately from ordinary DBE subcontract credit.

Recipient Compliance and Enforcement

A recipient is not automatically penalised merely because actual participation falls below its overall goal.

It must administer the program in good faith, analyse shortfalls, identify causes, and implement corrective steps.

Failure to adopt an approved program, set lawful goals, monitor contracts, enforce prompt payment, follow certification rules, or implement corrective action can place DOT assistance at risk.

Operating administrations can conduct reviews, require remedies, defer approvals, withhold funds, or pursue other grant-compliance measures.

During the current reevaluation transition, ordinary shortfall compliance consequences are suspended for recipients whose UCP has not completed the process.

Contractor Enforcement

A prime contractor that falsely represents DBE commitments, removes firms without approval, fails to pay subcontractors, or uses pass-through arrangements can face:

  • withholding of payment;
  • liquidated damages or other contract remedies;
  • termination for default;
  • adverse responsibility or past-performance findings;
  • suspension or debarment;
  • False Claims Act liability;
  • criminal prosecution;
  • recovery of improperly credited funds; and
  • loss of future contracting opportunities.

Fraud and Abuse Risks

Documented risks include:

  • front companies controlled by non-qualifying contractors;
  • false ownership transfers;
  • nominal qualifying officers without real authority;
  • undisclosed family or former-employer control;
  • equipment and workers supplied by the prime;
  • pass-through material transactions;
  • fabricated DBE commitments;
  • false invoices and payment records;
  • unapproved termination and substitution;
  • overcounting trucking or supplier credit;
  • joint ventures dominated by non-DBEs;
  • misstated personal net worth or size;
  • concealed affiliates;
  • bribery or kickbacks; and
  • continued claims after loss of eligibility.

African American–Owned Transportation Businesses

Black contractors historically faced exclusion from unions, equipment finance, surety bonding, public-works networks, suppliers, trade associations, and prime-contractor relationships.

The DBE program helped some Black-owned firms obtain subcontracting work, build transportation past performance, purchase equipment, hire workers, and move into prime contracting.

Benefits have varied by state, trade, project type, capital needs, certification administration, and prime-contractor practices.

Since October 2025, Black ownership no longer creates presumptive disadvantage. Every owner must submit individualized evidence under the same formal standard.

Historical analysis should distinguish the program’s remedial origins and prior race-conscious operation from its current race-neutral certification process.

Women-Owned Transportation Businesses

Women have faced exclusion from construction trades, engineering networks, capital, equipment ownership, bonding, and government-contracting relationships.

Women-owned firms historically qualified through a sex-based presumption incorporated into transportation statutes and regulations.

The October 2025 rule removed that presumption.

A woman owner can qualify by proving individualized social and economic disadvantage, ownership, control, size, and all remaining requirements.

State WBE or federal WOSB certification does not automatically establish DBE status.

Latino, Native, Asian American, Disabled, and Other Owners

Hispanic American, Native American, Asian Pacific American, and Subcontinent Asian American owners historically received presumptive social and economic disadvantage under the federal system.

Individuals outside the presumed groups could apply through an individualized showing before 2025.

The current rule subjects all individual owners to the individualized standard.

Disability, ethnicity, immigration history, language, geography, education, and other experiences may be relevant only where the evidence demonstrates personal barriers and economic harm under the rule.

Economic Effects

Market Entry

Contract goals and outreach can introduce certified firms to public-works markets dominated by established contractors.

Capacity Building

Transportation work can support equipment purchases, bonding, employees, safety systems, and past performance.

Competition

A larger supplier and subcontractor base can increase competition and reduce dependence on a few incumbents.

Working-Capital Pressure

DBEs may bear payroll, materials, fuel, equipment, and retainage costs before receiving payment.

Revenue Concentration

Firms dependent on goal contracts may struggle when programs, market conditions, or certification status change.

Development beyond DBE Work

The program encourages growth into prime, private, and non-goal markets, but does not impose the fixed graduation system used by SBA’s 8(a) program.

Program Achievements

  • It created a national certification framework for disadvantaged transportation businesses.
  • It made nondiscrimination and small-business participation conditions of DOT financial assistance.
  • It provided one-stop state certification through UCPs.
  • It created evidence-based overall goals rather than one uniform recipient percentage.
  • It requires maximum feasible use of neutral small-business measures.
  • It allows bidders to demonstrate good-faith efforts instead of imposing rigid quotas.
  • It protects committed DBEs against arbitrary termination and substitution.
  • It requires prompt payment and retainage protections.
  • It counts only commercially useful work.
  • It created specific rules for trucking, suppliers, manufacturers, brokers, and joint ventures.
  • It helped firms build public-works, engineering, airport, and transit experience.
  • It supports technical assistance and business development.
  • It created administrative certification appeals and recipient accountability.
  • It modernised interstate reciprocity and supplier counting in 2024.
  • It shifted to individualized, race-neutral eligibility in 2025.

Limitations and Failures

  • Certification does not guarantee work.
  • Recipient goals vary and may not create opportunities in every trade or region.
  • Contract goals can concentrate firms in subcontracting rather than prime contracting.
  • DBEs may remain dependent on a few prime contractors or public projects.
  • Working-capital and bonding barriers persist after certification.
  • Some primes treat DBE participation as a paperwork obligation rather than supplier development.
  • Commercially useful function abuse can divert benefits to non-DBEs.
  • Certification standards require complex financial, ownership, and control evidence.
  • UCP decisions have varied in quality and consistency.
  • Interstate certification historically imposed duplicative burdens.
  • Participation percentages do not directly measure business survival, wages, profits, or wealth.
  • Past racial and sex presumptions produced constitutional litigation.
  • The 2025 transition temporarily suspended goals and counting in many jurisdictions.
  • Individualized narrative review may be costly, subjective, and time consuming.
  • Recipients can miss overall goals without penalty if administration is otherwise in good faith.
  • Public data may not fully reveal subcontract payment, ownership benefit, or actual work performed.

Major Controversies

Constitutional Use of Race and Sex

Courts, contractors, DOT, and the Department of Justice disputed whether presumptions and contract goals satisfied equal-protection requirements.

Goals versus Quotas

Supporters describe flexible goals and good-faith efforts as lawful remedial tools, while critics argue that procurement pressure can operate like a numerical mandate.

Local Evidence

Disputes concern whether national evidence is sufficient or whether every jurisdiction needs strong local proof before using conscious goals.

Front Companies

Nominal ownership and pass-through arrangements can allow established non-DBEs to capture program benefits.

Personal-Net-Worth Cap

A cap targets assistance but can remove successful owners while their firms still face market barriers.

Counting Rules

Manufacturer, dealer, distributor, trucking, and brokerage distinctions can be difficult to apply and financially significant.

Contractor Good-Faith Efforts

Determinations involve judgement concerning solicitation, prices, scope, qualifications, and market conditions.

Current Reevaluation

Jurisdiction-wide recertification can remove long-certified firms, delay goals, disrupt solicitations, and create different timelines among states.

Documented Outcomes

The program has directed substantial federal-aid transportation contract and subcontract revenue to certified small businesses.

DBEs have expanded in highway construction, trucking, engineering, surveying, airport work, transit services, professional consulting, materials, and equipment.

Government Accountability Office reviews found that participation data did not always permit reliable conclusions about program impact.

Certification counts and award percentages do not establish whether a firm performed a commercially useful function, received payment promptly, survived, or became competitive without goals.

State results vary with transportation spending, industry structure, local discrimination, recipient administration, certification quality, and availability of qualified firms.

The full effect of the October 2025 individualized standard cannot yet be measured because UCP reevaluations remained incomplete in many jurisdictions as of August 2, 2026.

Historical Significance

The DBE Program made transportation financial assistance a major instrument of federal civil-rights and small-business policy.

It created a nationwide system linking grants, recipient goals, certification, subcontracting, prompt payment, monitoring, and administrative appeals.

The program influenced billions of dollars in highway, transit, and airport purchasing and helped develop minority-owned and women-owned firms in capital-intensive industries.

Its litigation history shaped modern constitutional standards for race-conscious government contracting.

The 2025 rule marked a fundamental shift from group presumptions to individualized disadvantage while preserving a program built to address discrimination in transportation markets.

Research Cautions

  • DBE is a DOT financial-assistance program. Do not confuse it with direct federal 8(a), SDB, WOSB, HUBZone, or VetCert contracting.
  • ACDBE is related but separate. Airport concessions operate under Part 23.
  • A contract goal is not a quota. Adequate good-faith efforts can qualify a bidder that does not meet the number.
  • DBE set-asides are ordinarily prohibited. Contract goals generally operate through subcontract participation.
  • The national 10-percent goal is aspirational. Recipient goals must be based on local evidence.
  • Certification is tied to work codes. A firm does not receive credit for every activity it performs.
  • Commitments and payments differ. Pre-award promises may be reduced by change orders, underruns, termination, or nonperformance.
  • DBE revenue and DBE credit differ. Only countable commercially useful work contributes to goals.
  • State minority certification is not DBE certification. The firm must be in the UCP directory.
  • Race and sex presumptions ended October 3, 2025. Current owners must prove individualized disadvantage.
  • Current program status varies by UCP. Goals and counting remain unavailable until jurisdiction-wide reevaluation is complete.
  • Historical thresholds change. Personal-net-worth, size, FTA, FAA, and supplier-counting figures should be dated.

Definitions

Commercially useful function
Genuine responsibility for executing, managing, supervising, financing, and where applicable supplying or installing the DBE’s work.
Contract goal
A percentage objective for DBE participation on a particular DOT-assisted contract.
DBE-conscious measure
A measure focused specifically on certified DBEs.
DBE-neutral measure
A measure available to all small businesses that can also increase DBE participation.
Declaration of Eligibility
The annual sworn or unsworn-under-penalty-of-perjury filing confirming continuing DBE eligibility.
Disadvantaged Business Enterprise
A certified for-profit small concern at least 51 percent owned and controlled by qualifying socially and economically disadvantaged individuals.
Distributor
A material or supply business meeting the current Part 26 distributor definition and generally eligible for 40-percent counting credit.
DOT-assisted contract
A contract or subcontract funded wholly or partly through qualifying DOT financial assistance.
Good-faith efforts
Intensive, timely, appropriate, and reasonable efforts that could be expected to achieve the program requirement even if unsuccessful.
Jurisdiction of original certification
The state UCP where the firm’s principal place of business is located at the time of its original application.
Manufacturer
A firm producing or substantially altering materials into the required product and eligible for full material-cost credit when conditions are met.
Overall goal
A recipient’s evidence-based estimate of expected DBE participation over a fiscal or project period absent the effects of disadvantage.
Personal narrative
The current individualized statement describing barriers, hardship, denied opportunity, and resulting economic harm.
Personal net worth
The net value of reportable personal assets and liabilities calculated under Part 26.
Regular dealer
An established material or supply business meeting inventory, facility, and sales requirements and generally eligible for 60-percent credit.
Socially and economically disadvantaged owner
An individual who proves personal social barriers and resulting economic disadvantage under the current individualized standard.
Unified Certification Program
The state or regional one-stop system making Part 26 certification decisions for DOT recipients.

Summary

The Disadvantaged Business Enterprise Program is a United States Department of Transportation civil-rights and small-business participation system for federally assisted highway, transit, and airport contracts.

It is administered through state and local recipients rather than functioning principally as a direct federal contracting preference.

A DBE must be a for-profit small business that is at least 51 percent owned and controlled by one or more qualifying socially and economically disadvantaged individuals.

Since October 3, 2025, no owner receives a presumption based on race, ethnicity, or sex. Every qualifying owner must submit individualized evidence, a personal narrative, and financial information proving disadvantage by a preponderance of the evidence.

The current personal-net-worth cap is $2,047,000. Firms must also satisfy applicable SBA size standards, and FHWA- and FTA-assisted firms are subject to the current $30.72 million affiliated gross-receipts cap.

State Unified Certification Programs provide one-stop certification and directories. The 2024 rule requires additional states to accept certification from the jurisdiction of original certification through a streamlined interstate process.

Recipients establish evidence-based overall goals and use neutral measures as much as possible. Contract goals may be used only for the portion not expected through neutral measures.

A bidder satisfies a contract goal through certified commitments or can qualify by proving adequate good-faith efforts. Quotas and ordinary DBE set-asides are prohibited.

Participation counts only when the DBE performs a commercially useful function. Detailed rules govern prime contractors, joint ventures, trucking, manufacturers, regular dealers, distributors, and brokers.

Prime contractors cannot remove committed DBEs without recipient consent and must comply with prompt-payment, substitution, monitoring, and commercially useful function requirements.

The October 2025 interim rule ordered every UCP to reevaluate every certified firm. Until a jurisdiction completes reevaluation, recipients covered by that UCP cannot set DBE contract goals or count participation toward overall goals.

The program has expanded transportation-contracting opportunities and business capacity while continuing to face constitutional disputes, certification burdens, front-company fraud, unequal market access, inconsistent administration, and difficulty measuring long-term economic impact.
