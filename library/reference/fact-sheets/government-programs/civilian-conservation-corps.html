Civilian Conservation Corps

The New Deal residential work-relief program that employed millions of men in forestry, soil conservation, park development, fire control, flood prevention, infrastructure construction, and natural-resource projects between 1933 and 1942.

Overview

The Civilian Conservation Corps was a federal work-relief program created during the first months of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency. It responded simultaneously to mass unemployment, declining family income, environmental degradation, forest loss, soil erosion, fire risk, and the limited infrastructure of public forests and parks.

Enrollees generally lived in federally operated residential camps near their work sites. The Army organised camp life, while professional employees of the Forest Service, National Park Service, Soil Conservation Service, Bureau of Reclamation, Grazing Service, and other agencies directed technical projects.

Participants planted trees, built roads and trails, fought forest fires, controlled erosion, improved watersheds, constructed park buildings and campgrounds, installed telephone lines, restored historic sites, developed wildlife habitat, and responded to floods and other disasters.

The program provided a monthly cash allowance, meals, lodging, clothing, medical care, and educational opportunities. Most of an enrollee's cash allowance was usually sent directly to his family, making the CCC a family-relief system as well as an individual employment program.

The program's public image emphasised healthy outdoor work, national service, physical improvement, discipline, and environmental restoration. Its military-style residential structure also gave the federal government extensive control over enrollee movement, schedules, conduct, and daily life.

The CCC's benefits were distributed unequally. African American men participated in large numbers, but state selection discrimination, enrolment caps, segregated companies, white community opposition, remote camp placement, occupational restrictions, and exclusion from most command positions limited their access and advancement.

Classification and Scope

This page treats the Civilian Conservation Corps as a government program because its primary subject is federal administration: eligibility, selection, compensation, camp operation, agency responsibilities, work assignments, training, racial implementation, funding, and outcomes.

The Emergency Conservation Work Act of 1933, Executive Order 6101, and Civilian Conservation Corps Act of 1937 should be cross-referenced from Laws and Legislation. The Department of Labor, War Department, Department of Agriculture, Department of the Interior, Forest Service, National Park Service, and related agencies belong under Institutions.

Individual camps, parks, structures, disasters, strikes, racial controversies, or preservation districts may receive separate pages when their local history is sufficiently distinct.

This fact sheet distinguishes the main CCC program from the separately administered Indian Emergency Conservation Work program and from the women's residential relief camps sometimes called “She-She-She” camps. Those initiatives were related to the New Deal but were not administratively identical to the principal CCC enrolment system.

Historical Context

The Great Depression produced mass unemployment, business failure, declining wages, reduced tax revenue, housing insecurity, hunger, and severe pressure on families and local relief institutions.

Young adults entering the labour market faced especially limited opportunities. Many left school, travelled in search of work, depended on relatives, or remained unemployed for extended periods.

Environmental problems intensified the economic crisis. Decades of deforestation, overgrazing, poorly managed farming, wildfire, flooding, drought, and soil erosion had damaged forests, watersheds, agricultural land, and rural communities.

Franklin D. Roosevelt had a longstanding interest in forestry and conservation. As governor of New York, he had supported state reforestation and work-relief initiatives that helped shape his proposal for a national conservation workforce.

Shortly after taking office in March 1933, Roosevelt directed his administration to design a program capable of rapidly enrolling unemployed men and assigning them to useful public work. Congress enacted broad authority on March 31, and the executive branch began mobilising the new system within days.

Program Purpose

Reduce Youth Unemployment

The CCC placed unemployed men in federally supported work when private employers could not absorb the available labour force.

Support Families

Mandatory or strongly structured allotments directed most of a junior enrollee's monthly cash allowance to parents, spouses, or other dependents.

Restore Natural Resources

Projects addressed deforestation, soil erosion, fire risk, damaged watersheds, degraded range, flooding, and neglected public lands.

Develop Public Parks

The Corps supplied labour for roads, trails, campgrounds, shelters, picnic areas, visitor facilities, landscaping, utilities, and historic-site work.

Improve Health and Morale

Administrators believed structured outdoor work, regular meals, medical attention, and camp routine would improve physical condition and reduce the demoralisation associated with prolonged unemployment.

Provide Education and Training

Camp programmes offered literacy instruction, general education, vocational courses, and experience in construction, forestry, mechanics, communications, surveying, and other fields.

Build Administrative Capacity

The CCC expanded federal, state, and local capacity to manage parks, forests, soil, water, wildlife, recreation, and public infrastructure.

Legal Context

Emergency Conservation Work Act of 1933

Congress enacted the initial authority on March 31, 1933. The law empowered the president to use federal resources to provide employment through useful public work involving forests, public lands, and natural resources.

Representative Oscar De Priest of Illinois, then the only Black member of Congress, successfully supported language prohibiting discrimination on account of race, color, or creed.

Executive Order 6101

Issued April 5, 1933, Executive Order 6101 established Emergency Conservation Work as an interdepartmental federal operation, appointed Robert Fechner as director, and created an advisory council representing the Departments of War, Labor, Agriculture, and the Interior.

Executive Order 6109

Issued April 12, 1933, Executive Order 6109 fixed the standard cash allowance at thirty dollars per month.

Veterans Enrolment Authority

In May 1933, executive action authorised enrolment of unemployed veterans without the junior programme's ordinary age and marital restrictions.

Civilian Conservation Corps Act of 1937

The act of June 28, 1937, established the Civilian Conservation Corps as an independent statutory agency and made CCC its official name. Congress continued the programme on a temporary statutory basis rather than granting the permanent status Roosevelt had requested.

Transfer to the Federal Security Agency

A 1939 federal reorganisation placed the CCC within the newly created Federal Security Agency while preserving its interagency operating system.

Liquidation Authority

Congress discontinued operational appropriations in 1942. Later legislation maintained limited authority necessary to liquidate property, accounts, records, and remaining administrative obligations.

Interagency Administration

The CCC did not operate through one department alone. Its rapid mobilisation depended on existing federal institutions that divided responsibility for recruitment, transportation, camp management, project planning, technical supervision, and supplies.

Office of the Director
Coordinated national policy, quotas, enrolment periods, agency relationships, camp allocations, reports, regulations, and programme administration.
Department of Labor
Organised selection of most junior enrollees through federal and state employment and relief systems and established labour-related standards.
War Department and United States Army
Received, conditioned, transported, housed, fed, clothed, disciplined, and medically supported enrollees and administered daily camp operations.
Department of Agriculture
Directed a large majority of work through the Forest Service, Soil Erosion Service and later Soil Conservation Service, Bureau of Biological Survey, and related agencies.
Department of the Interior
Directed projects through the National Park Service, Bureau of Reclamation, Office of Indian Affairs, Grazing Service, General Land Office, and other bureaus.
Veterans Administration
Selected unemployed veterans for special CCC companies and coordinated veterans' enrolment.
State and Local Governments
Proposed projects, supplied land or materials, participated in state-park development, and maintained many improvements after completion.
Technical Agencies
Professional foresters, engineers, architects, landscape architects, archaeologists, historians, soil specialists, and project superintendents designed and supervised the work.

Enrollee Categories

Junior Enrollees

Junior enrollees formed the principal programme population. They were generally young, unmarried men selected from families receiving or eligible for public relief.

Veterans

Unemployed veterans, principally of World War I, entered special companies without the ordinary junior age and marital restrictions. Approximately 25,000 veterans were authorised during the first expansion, and far more served over the life of the programme.

Locally Experienced Men

Locally experienced men, commonly called LEMs, were hired from communities near project sites because they possessed forestry, construction, equipment, horsemanship, guiding, or other practical skills.

Native American Enrollees

Native American participants generally worked through the Indian Emergency Conservation Work system administered by the Office of Indian Affairs. They frequently worked on or near their own reservations rather than living in Army-operated junior camps.

Territorial Enrollees

Men participated through programmes in Alaska, Hawai‘i, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, and other territories, with administrative arrangements adapted to local geography and government.

African American Enrollees

Black men entered junior and veterans' companies. Their legal eligibility was protected by the nondiscrimination clause, but actual selection and placement were constrained by racial quotas, state practices, segregated camps, and community opposition.

Eligibility and Selection

Eligibility rules changed over time as Congress and administrators adjusted the programme. The following features generally defined junior enrolment:

  • unemployment or inability to secure adequate private work;
  • membership in a family receiving or eligible for relief;
  • unmarried status during most of the programme;
  • an age within the current junior-enrollee range;
  • physical capacity for outdoor manual work;
  • willingness to accept residential camp life;
  • agreement to allot most cash compensation to dependents; and
  • enrolment for a fixed term, normally six months.

State relief and employment agencies commonly nominated or certified applicants. The Department of Labor supervised the selection network, while the Army conducted physical examinations, enrolment processing, conditioning, and transport.

Selection was not based solely on individual unemployment. Administrators treated family need as a central criterion because the programme was designed to transfer income to households.

Local officials exercised substantial discretion. Their decisions could determine who received notice, whose application was accepted, how racial quotas were interpreted, and whether Black applicants were placed below white applicants in priority.

Enrolment and Placement Process

  1. Application or relief referral. A prospective enrollee applied through a relief, employment, veterans, territorial, or Indian-affairs office.
  2. Eligibility certification. Officials reviewed age, family need, employment status, marital status, physical condition, and other current requirements.
  3. Selection and quota assignment. State and federal officials allocated available places according to enrolment quotas and programme categories.
  4. Army processing. Selected men reported to an Army post or conditioning camp for examination, paperwork, clothing issue, vaccination, orientation, and basic camp preparation.
  5. Company organisation. Enrollees were assigned to numbered companies, commonly organised around a strength of approximately two hundred men.
  6. Transportation to a work camp. The Army moved the company by train, truck, ship, or other transport to a camp near an approved project.
  7. Camp assignment. Army officers administered the company, while a civilian technical agency directed the daily work.
  8. Completion or re-enrolment. At the end of the term, an enrollee could be discharged, return home, transfer, or seek re-enrolment subject to service limits and available places.

Compensation and Benefits

Cash Allowance

Franklin D. Roosevelt set the standard junior cash allowance at thirty dollars per month. Most junior enrollees were required or expected to send approximately twenty-five dollars home, retaining a smaller amount for personal expenses.

Family Remittances

The allotment system transferred millions of dollars into households suffering from unemployment. The programme therefore functioned as indirect relief to parents, siblings, spouses, and children.

Housing and Food

Enrollees received barracks or tent housing and regular meals. Many participants arrived undernourished and gained weight after entering camp.

Clothing and Equipment

The Army supplied work clothing, uniforms or surplus garments, bedding, boots, and personal equipment. Technical agencies supplied project tools and machinery.

Medical and Dental Care

Camps provided routine medical services, examinations, infirmaries, and access to Army or civilian hospitals. Dental services were available in many camps but varied by period and location.

Education and Training

Enrollees could attend literacy, academic, vocational, safety, and leadership classes after work or during scheduled training.

Advancement

Selected enrollees could become assistant leaders or leaders and receive higher allowances. Advancement opportunities depended on camp policy, skill, vacancies, race, and the attitudes of supervising officers.

Camp Organisation

A typical junior camp contained one company of approximately two hundred enrollees. The physical layout and quality of construction differed according to climate, agency, period, and whether the camp was temporary or semi-permanent.

Camp facilities could include:

  • barracks or tents;
  • a mess hall and kitchen;
  • an infirmary or dispensary;
  • administrative offices;
  • a supply building and tool room;
  • workshops and vehicle garages;
  • latrines, showers, and laundry facilities;
  • classrooms or a recreation hall;
  • a library or reading room;
  • a canteen or camp exchange;
  • athletic fields and equipment; and
  • housing for Army and technical personnel.

Early camps often began in tents at remote and incompletely prepared sites. Later camps increasingly used standardised wooden buildings, roads, water systems, electricity, heating, and improved sanitation.

Camp Command and Work Supervision

Company Commander

An Army officer or reserve officer generally commanded the company and was responsible for discipline, housing, food, sanitation, transportation, personnel administration, recreation, and camp property.

Junior Officers and Army Personnel

Supporting officers, medical personnel, cooks, supply staff, and clerks maintained camp operations.

Project Superintendent

A civilian employee of the technical agency planned and supervised the conservation work independently of the Army command structure.

Foremen

Experienced foresters, builders, equipment operators, craftsmen, and locally experienced men directed crews at project sites.

Enrollee Leaders

Selected enrollees assisted with barracks order, work crews, instruction, records, recreation, and other responsibilities.

This dual structure divided responsibility between camp administration and technical work. Conflict could arise when Army officers and project officials disagreed about schedules, discipline, transportation, or the use of enrollee labour.

Daily Camp Life

Daily schedules varied, but camps generally followed a structured routine influenced by Army practice.

  1. reveille and morning formation;
  2. cleaning of barracks and camp areas;
  3. breakfast and preparation for work;
  4. transportation to the project site;
  5. a supervised workday with a meal period;
  6. return to camp, equipment care, and evening meal;
  7. classes, sports, recreation, religious services, or personal time; and
  8. lights-out and overnight camp regulations.

Enrollees were civilians rather than soldiers. They were not subject to the complete military legal system merely because the Army administered their camps. The programme nevertheless imposed uniforms, formations, inspections, schedules, passes, and disciplinary rules that made daily life quasi-military.

Camp newspapers, bands, sports teams, boxing matches, theatre groups, religious services, libraries, correspondence, and weekend travel helped create distinct camp communities.

Education and Vocational Training

Education was not the CCC's original primary mission, but it became a major programme function as administrators recognised the limited schooling and employment experience of many enrollees.

Camp education could include:

  • basic literacy and arithmetic;
  • high-school equivalency or completion work;
  • English-language instruction;
  • typing, bookkeeping, and clerical skills;
  • forestry and conservation science;
  • carpentry, masonry, plumbing, and concrete work;
  • automobile and equipment maintenance;
  • welding, blacksmithing, and machine work;
  • surveying, drafting, and mapping;
  • radio operation and electrical work;
  • first aid, fire control, and safety;
  • agriculture and soil conservation;
  • leadership, citizenship, and public speaking; and
  • music, art, journalism, and recreation.

Educational quality depended on the camp educational adviser, available instructors, enrollee interest, equipment, local partnerships, and the degree to which officers supported classes.

Training could improve post-CCC employment prospects, but not every enrollee received a formal credential or marketable trade. Much of the programme's value came through work experience, physical conditioning, punctuality, teamwork, and exposure to tools and machinery.

Conservation and Public Works

Forestry and Reforestation

  • planting trees and operating nurseries;
  • thinning forests and removing hazardous material;
  • building firebreaks, fire roads, and lookout towers;
  • controlling insects and tree disease;
  • constructing telephone lines for fire detection; and
  • fighting forest and range fires.

Soil and Water Conservation

  • constructing terraces, check dams, and erosion barriers;
  • stabilising gullies and stream banks;
  • planting shelterbelts and protective vegetation;
  • improving drainage and watersheds;
  • constructing small reservoirs and stock-water systems; and
  • demonstrating soil-conservation practices to landowners.

Park and Recreation Development

  • building roads, trails, bridges, and retaining walls;
  • constructing cabins, shelters, museums, ranger stations, and comfort facilities;
  • developing campgrounds, picnic areas, beaches, and scenic overlooks;
  • installing water, sewer, telephone, and electrical systems;
  • landscaping developed areas; and
  • creating or improving state, county, municipal, and national parks.

Historic and Archaeological Work

  • stabilising ruins and historic structures;
  • conducting excavations under professional supervision;
  • constructing museums and exhibits;
  • mapping historic sites; and
  • developing visitor access and interpretation.

Range, Wildlife, and Agricultural Work

  • building fences, corrals, wells, and stock facilities;
  • improving grazing land;
  • restoring fish habitat and stocking waters;
  • constructing wildlife refuges and habitat improvements;
  • controlling rodents, predators, insects, and invasive vegetation under period policies; and
  • supporting conservation demonstrations on public and cooperating private land.

Fire Control and Disaster Response

CCC companies served as an organised labour force during forest fires, floods, hurricanes, blizzards, drought emergencies, insect outbreaks, and other disasters.

Enrollees constructed levees, filled sandbags, cleared debris, repaired roads, restored telephone systems, evacuated residents, delivered food, fought fire, and supported local emergency services.

Emergency work could be dangerous. Enrollees faced fire, falling trees, machinery accidents, drowning, exposure, disease, vehicle crashes, and other hazards.

Disaster response strengthened public support for the Corps because communities could see a federal workforce providing immediate assistance outside ordinary project schedules.

African American Participation

The CCC offered Black men income, food, housing, healthcare, training, work experience, and a means of supporting families during the Great Depression.

Federal historical sources estimate that more than 200,000 and possibly approximately 250,000 African American men served. The variation reflects differing definitions, incomplete records, and whether veteran, territorial, or other enrollee categories are included.

Black companies planted forests, built parks, constructed roads and trails, fought fires, developed recreation areas, improved farms and watersheds, operated equipment, and completed many of the same technical projects assigned to white companies.

Examples include Company 1743 at Washington State Park in Missouri, whose members built stone structures and the 1,000 Step Trail, and Black enrollees who worked in national forests and at Grand Canyon.

The programme also supported education. National Archives records preserve photographs of African American enrollees studying radio, mechanics, academic subjects, and vocational skills.

The programme's nondiscrimination clause gave Black leaders a statutory basis for challenging exclusion. It did not prevent administrators from treating segregation as legally permissible or from allowing discriminatory local practices to shape selection and placement.

Racial Discrimination and Segregation

State Selection Barriers

Some state and local officials rejected Black applicants, provided inadequate notice, delayed selection, or assigned them lower priority even when African American unemployment and family need were severe.

Population-Based Quotas

National administrators sought Black participation roughly proportional to the African American share of the national population. A quota near ten percent limited participation below the level that might have resulted from equal consideration of economic need.

Early Integration and Separate Companies

Some of the earliest companies included Black and white enrollees. Other jurisdictions organised all-Black companies from the beginning, especially where the Black population was large.

1935 Segregation Directive

In 1935, Director Robert Fechner ordered complete separation of Black and white enrollees in company and camp organisation. He defended the policy by asserting that segregation was not discrimination.

White Community Opposition

White residents and officials sometimes opposed the placement of Black companies in or near their communities. Administrators relocated proposed camps, placed companies on remote federal land, or declined to establish Black camps in areas where projects existed.

White Officers

Most segregated Black companies remained under white Army officers. Black men encountered limited access to command, supervisory, technical, and advancement positions.

Occupational Segregation

At some sites, Black enrollees were concentrated in cooking, domestic, maintenance, or support work rather than receiving equal access to skilled conservation assignments.

Reduced Enrolment

The difficulty of placing segregated Black companies contributed to restrictions and depressed Black enrolment during portions of the programme.

Resistance and Advocacy

Black newspapers, the NAACP, Representative Oscar De Priest, community leaders, and applicants protested discriminatory selection, segregation, remote placement, and exclusion from leadership.

Racial Policy Distinctions

Nondiscrimination clause
The law prohibited discrimination in employment on account of race, color, or creed.
Segregation
CCC administrators treated separate Black and white companies as compatible with the nondiscrimination clause, reflecting the prevailing federal acceptance of “separate but equal” doctrine.
Enrolment quota
A numerical target or ceiling tied to population share rather than a guarantee that all qualified applicants would receive equal access.
Selection discrimination
Unequal action by officials responsible for identifying, ranking, approving, or rejecting applicants.
Placement discrimination
Unequal decisions concerning camp location, company assignment, project quality, skilled work, leadership, or transfer.
Community veto
The practical ability of local white opposition to prevent or relocate a Black company even without a formal statutory power to do so.

Indian Emergency Conservation Work and the CCC–Indian Division

Native American participation operated through a related but distinct administrative system initially known as Indian Emergency Conservation Work and later as the CCC–Indian Division.

The Office of Indian Affairs administered the programme through reservation agencies and regional structures. Native American enrollees generally remained near their homes and did not follow the same Army residential-company model used for most junior enrollees.

Projects included:

  • forestry and fire protection;
  • erosion control and water development;
  • road, bridge, and telephone construction;
  • range improvement and livestock facilities;
  • irrigation and drainage;
  • housing and community buildings;
  • fish and wildlife projects; and
  • archaeological, cultural, and land-management work.

More than 80,000 Native American men participated. The programme supplied wages and infrastructure but also operated within the federal Indian-administration system, which retained extensive outside control over tribal land, funding, and policy.

Women and Programme Exclusion

The main CCC enrolment system was designed for men. Women suffering unemployment were not admitted to ordinary CCC companies on equal terms.

Eleanor Roosevelt, Frances Perkins, Hilda Worthington Smith, and other officials supported separate residential programmes for unemployed women. These camps offered education, health, domestic, clerical, recreational, and vocational activities and were sometimes informally called “She-She-She” camps.

The women's camps were smaller, less consistently funded, and administratively separate from the principal CCC. They did not receive the same scale of appropriations, public visibility, military logistics, conservation work, or national political support.

Women did contribute to CCC operations as teachers, educational advisers, architects, landscape architects, nurses, clerks, writers, administrators, and technical professionals. Their participation as employees should not be confused with enrolment in the male residential corps.

Relationships with Local Communities

CCC camps purchased food, fuel, construction materials, vehicle services, tools, and other goods from nearby communities. Local men could obtain work as foremen or skilled employees.

Enrollees attended churches, sporting events, dances, theatres, and businesses and sometimes formed lasting relationships with local residents.

Communities generally welcomed the economic activity and public improvements. Opposition arose over competition for jobs, military appearance, land acquisition, enrollee conduct, political control, or the racial identity of a proposed company.

Black companies faced distinctive hostility. White communities could object even when projects clearly required labour, forcing federal officials to choose between enforcing equal access and accommodating segregationist pressure.

Programme Scale and Quantitative Outcomes

Federal historical summaries use varying totals, but the scale of the programme is clear.

  • approximately three million or more men enrolled during the programme's existence;
  • more than 500,000 enrollees served at the peak;
  • more than 4,500 camps operated over the programme's history;
  • approximately two hundred men formed a typical camp company;
  • more than three billion trees were planted;
  • more than six million erosion-control structures were constructed;
  • more than 125,000 miles of roads and trails were built or improved;
  • millions of workdays were devoted to forest-fire control;
  • hundreds of state and local parks were established or improved; and
  • family allotments transferred substantial income from federal payrolls to distressed households.

Numerical claims should identify whether they refer to individuals, enrolments, camp periods, companies, camps, work projects, or completed structures. One person could enrol more than once, and one camp site could house different companies over time.

Outcomes for Enrollees and Families

Income

The monthly allowance and family allotment supplied reliable cash during a period of severe unemployment.

Nutrition and Health

Regular food, physical examinations, medical care, sanitation, and outdoor work improved the condition of many undernourished enrollees.

Work Experience

Participants learned to follow schedules, operate tools, work in crews, complete projects, and meet supervisory expectations.

Education

Some enrollees learned to read, completed academic coursework, obtained vocational instruction, or developed leadership experience.

Mobility and Exposure

Men travelled beyond their home communities and encountered new landscapes, occupations, cultures, and federal institutions.

Military Preparation

Camp routine, physical conditioning, first aid, equipment use, and group discipline eased the later transition of many alumni into military service, although the CCC was not formally a combat-training organisation.

Unequal Benefits

Racial segregation, occupational restrictions, unequal promotion, disability exclusions, gender exclusion, and geographic differences meant that the programme's benefits were not equally available.

Environmental and Infrastructure Legacy

CCC roads, trails, bridges, shelters, campgrounds, lodges, fire towers, dams, retaining walls, nurseries, and administrative buildings remain in use across the United States.

The Corps accelerated reforestation, expanded fire-detection systems, demonstrated erosion-control methods, developed recreational access, and helped institutionalise professional management of parks, forests, watersheds, and soil.

The programme also shaped a recognisable architectural and landscape style based on local stone, timber, hand craftsmanship, and designs intended to blend with natural surroundings.

Not every period conservation practice is accepted today. Predator control, broad fire suppression, road building, channel modification, vegetation removal, and recreational construction sometimes reflected ecological assumptions later revised by conservation science.

Historical evaluation therefore requires distinguishing the durability and public value of CCC work from the environmental limits of 1930s management policy.

Programme Achievements

  • It mobilised a national employment system within weeks of enactment.
  • It provided paid work, subsistence, and family remittances during mass unemployment.
  • It restored forests, soil, watersheds, parks, and public infrastructure.
  • It expanded state and national park systems.
  • It provided education, training, healthcare, and work experience.
  • It supplied emergency labour during fires, floods, storms, and other disasters.
  • It created enduring public buildings, roads, trails, campgrounds, and recreation areas.
  • It increased federal administrative capacity in conservation and land management.
  • It provided opportunities to African American, Native American, veteran, territorial, rural, and urban participants despite unequal implementation.
  • It became an influential model for Job Corps conservation centres, state conservation corps, national service, and later environmental-employment proposals.

Limitations and Failures

  • The principal enrolment system excluded women.
  • Racial discrimination persisted despite an express statutory prohibition.
  • Black enrollees were subjected to quotas, segregated camps, white officers, unequal placement, and restricted advancement.
  • Local white opposition could determine whether a Black company entered a community.
  • Native American participation occurred within a separate federal system that retained extensive administrative control.
  • Camp discipline limited personal autonomy and could be paternalistic or abusive.
  • Educational and vocational quality varied widely.
  • Remote placement separated enrollees from family and community networks.
  • Work involved significant risks from fire, machinery, vehicles, falling timber, weather, and disease.
  • The programme did not provide long-term employment guarantees after discharge.
  • Some projects reflected conservation assumptions later reconsidered or rejected.
  • The Corps did not eliminate national unemployment or the structural economic causes of the Great Depression.

Shift Toward National Defence

Beginning in 1940, national policy increasingly redirected the CCC toward projects related to military reservations, forest protection, infrastructure, communications, and defence preparation.

Defence industries and military mobilisation absorbed young workers at wages higher than ordinary CCC allowances. Enrolment declined as private employment expanded and the armed services increased recruitment and conscription.

The Army's administrative experience with mass processing, camp logistics, transportation, health, food service, and personnel management provided useful institutional preparation for wartime mobilisation.

The Corps remained civilian. Its defence-related work and military-style camp administration did not convert enrollees into members of the armed forces.

Termination and Liquidation

The CCC had been repeatedly continued through temporary legislation and appropriations. Franklin D. Roosevelt favoured permanent status, but Congress did not establish the Corps as a permanent institution.

After the United States entered World War II in December 1941, unemployment declined rapidly, military and industrial labour demand increased, and Congress became less willing to fund a separate conservation corps.

Congress discontinued programme operations effective June 30, 1942. Camps closed, enrollees were discharged, property was transferred or sold, records were consolidated, and buildings were reassigned to military, park, state, local, or other federal uses.

Limited statutory authority continued during liquidation to resolve contracts, property, accounts, personnel matters, and administrative records.

Records and Research Value

The principal central records of the programme are maintained by the National Archives in Record Group 35, Records of the Civilian Conservation Corps.

Relevant records include:

  • individual enrollee records;
  • applications and selection records;
  • company rosters and strength reports;
  • camp inspection reports;
  • medical and hospital records;
  • monthly enrolment reports;
  • pay and allotment records;
  • project reports and work-progress records;
  • camp newspapers and educational publications;
  • photographs and motion pictures;
  • general correspondence and administrative directives;
  • reports concerning racial selection and segregated companies;
  • maps, architectural drawings, landscape plans, and engineering records;
  • Army corps-area camp records;
  • Forest Service, National Park Service, Soil Conservation Service, and Bureau of Reclamation project files; and
  • Indian Emergency Conservation Work records in Bureau of Indian Affairs holdings.

Individual records may identify the enrollee's name, serial number, date and place of birth, address, family contact, enrolment and discharge dates, company, camps, occupations, medical information, education, conduct, transfers, and reason for separation.

CCC records are distributed across National Archives facilities because operating agencies and regional offices retained project and camp documentation.

Research Cautions

  • Emergency Conservation Work and CCC are connected names. ECW was the formal administrative name from 1933 until CCC became the statutory name in 1937.
  • One enrollee could serve multiple terms. Enrolment totals may exceed the number of unique individuals.
  • Camp numbers and company numbers are not identical. A numbered company could move between camp sites, and one site could host several companies over time.
  • Camp prefix codes identify technical jurisdiction. Prefixes may refer to national forests, parks, soil conservation, private forest work, drainage, grazing, reclamation, military reservations, or other project categories.
  • Participation estimates vary. Published totals differ according to date, category, source, and whether re-enrolments are counted separately.
  • Racial records use period terminology. Archival files may use offensive, inconsistent, or administratively imposed racial labels.
  • Official photographs were promotional as well as documentary. Images may emphasise successful projects and orderly camps while underrepresenting conflict, discrimination, injury, or poor conditions.
  • Local results should not be generalised nationally. Camp quality, integration, work, leadership, education, and community relations differed substantially.

Executive Order 6101 establishes Emergency Conservation Work, appoints Robert Fechner as director, and creates the interdepartmental advisory council.

Executive Order 6109 sets the standard cash allowance at thirty dollars per month.

The first enrollee enters the programme, and Camp Roosevelt in Virginia becomes the first operating CCC camp.

The administration authorises special veterans' enrolment and develops separate Indian Emergency Conservation Work operations.
June 1933
More than 270,000 men are working in more than 1,300 camps as the programme completes an exceptionally rapid national mobilisation.

Early companies perform forestry, park, erosion-control, fire, road, and disaster-response work across the states and territories.
1934
Camp education becomes more formalised, and technical agencies expand specialised project programmes.
1935
Director Robert Fechner orders complete segregation of Black and white enrollees in company and camp organisation.
1935–1936
The Corps reaches peak strength with more than 500,000 enrollees at one time.
June 28, 1937
Congress enacts the Civilian Conservation Corps Act, making CCC the official programme name and establishing an independent statutory agency.
1938
Congress and the administration reduce the number of camps as economic conditions and federal relief policy change.

Federal reorganisation places the CCC under the Federal Security Agency. Robert Fechner dies, and James J. McEntee becomes director.

CCC activity increasingly includes work on military reservations and forest-protection projects connected to national defence.

The Selective Training and Service Act accelerates military mobilisation and changes the labour market for young men.

The attack on Pearl Harbor brings the United States directly into World War II.

1942–1943
Camps, property, records, accounts, and remaining functions are transferred, closed, sold, or liquidated.

CCC alumni enter military, industrial, construction, conservation, and public-service careers, while CCC-built infrastructure remains in use.
1960s and after
Job Corps conservation centres, state conservation corps, youth-service organisations, and later environmental-employment proposals draw on the CCC model.

Definitions

Camp
A residential and administrative site housing a CCC company and supporting its assigned project.
Camp commander
The Army officer responsible for company administration, discipline, housing, food, health, transportation, and camp property.
Camp exchange
A small camp store or canteen where enrollees could purchase approved personal items.
Civilian Conservation Corps
The official statutory name adopted in 1937 for the federal conservation work-relief programme previously administered as Emergency Conservation Work.
Company
The organised unit of enrollees assigned to a camp, commonly numbering approximately two hundred men.
Conditioning camp
An Army facility where selected enrollees received examinations, clothing, orientation, and initial preparation before assignment to a work camp.
Emergency Conservation Work
The formal administrative name of the programme from 1933 until the statutory CCC name took effect in 1937.
Enrollee
A participant formally accepted into a CCC term of service. Enrollees were federal programme participants rather than ordinary civil-service employees or soldiers.
Family allotment
The portion of an enrollee's monthly allowance sent directly to designated dependents.
Indian Emergency Conservation Work
The separately administered conservation-employment programme for Native American workers, later commonly called the CCC–Indian Division.
Junior enrollee
A participant in the principal young-men's relief category, generally selected according to age, unemployment, marital status, and family need.
Locally experienced man
A skilled or experienced local worker employed to guide CCC crews in forestry, construction, equipment, horsemanship, or other specialised work.
Project superintendent
The civilian technical official responsible for planning and supervising conservation work assigned to a company.
Re-enrolment
Acceptance of a former enrollee for an additional term, subject to current rules and service limits.
Tree Army
A popular nickname emphasising the programme's mass mobilisation, camp organisation, and forestry work.
Work relief
Public assistance delivered through paid employment on government-supported projects rather than through direct cash relief alone.

Geographic Scope

CCC camps and projects operated throughout the continental United States and in federal territories. Project type depended on local environment, land ownership, agency jurisdiction, and public need.

Northeast
Forestry, state parks, fire control, flood response, recreation areas, historic sites, and rural land improvement.
South
Forestry, erosion control, park development, agricultural demonstrations, drainage, fire work, and extensive segregated Black and white camp systems.
Midwest and Great Plains
Shelterbelts, soil conservation, prairie and range work, state parks, flood control, forestry, and drought response.
Mountain West
National forests, parks, range improvement, roads, trails, fire towers, reclamation, wildlife work, and remote public-land projects.
Pacific Coast
Reforestation, fire prevention, national forests, park infrastructure, erosion control, trails, roads, and watershed work.
Alaska Territory
Forestry, roads, recreation, territorial infrastructure, communications, and remote-resource projects.
Hawai‘i Territory
Forestry, parks, erosion control, trails, public facilities, and territorial conservation work.
Puerto Rico
Reforestation, roads, recreation, watershed protection, erosion control, and infrastructure adapted to tropical conditions.

Summary

The Civilian Conservation Corps was a federal residential work-relief programme that employed millions of men between 1933 and 1942. It combined unemployment relief, family-income support, conservation, public-land development, education, health, and vocational training.

The Department of Labor selected most junior enrollees, the Army administered camps, and Agriculture and Interior agencies supervised conservation projects. Participants received a monthly allowance, food, housing, clothing, medical care, and access to education while planting trees, controlling erosion, fighting fires, and building parks, roads, trails, bridges, and public facilities.

African American men made major contributions to the programme, but the CCC's statutory nondiscrimination rule was undermined by quotas, selection barriers, segregated companies, white community opposition, occupational restrictions, remote placement, and limited Black leadership. Native American men served through a separately administered conservation programme, while women were excluded from ordinary CCC enrolment.

The Corps ended in 1942 as military and industrial mobilisation absorbed young workers and Congress discontinued funding. Its physical legacy remains visible across national forests, parks, historic sites, recreation areas, watersheds, and state and local public lands.
