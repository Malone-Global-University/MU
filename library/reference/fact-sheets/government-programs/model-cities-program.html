Model Cities Program

The federal urban-development initiative that sought to improve selected low-income neighborhoods through coordinated physical investment, social services, economic opportunity, and resident participation from 1966 to 1974.

Overview

The Model Cities Program was a federal urban-policy initiative established by Title I of the Demonstration Cities and Metropolitan Development Act of 1966. It formed part of President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society agenda and represented an effort to move beyond the narrow land-clearance orientation associated with traditional urban renewal.

The program sought to concentrate coordinated public and private resources in selected urban neighborhoods for approximately five years. Participating local governments prepared comprehensive plans that could combine housing development and rehabilitation with employment, education, healthcare, transportation, public safety, recreation, environmental improvement, social services, and neighborhood administration.

A central feature was the statutory expectation of widespread citizen participation. Local governments commonly created neighborhood boards, citizen committees, task forces, and other structures through which residents could identify priorities, review proposals, recommend projects, and sometimes administer funded activities. The actual authority granted to residents varied significantly among cities.

HUD selected 150 participating communities in two rounds. The program produced local institutions, community facilities, service systems, housing projects, neighborhood plans, and a generation of resident leaders. It also faced inadequate funding, complicated federal coordination, conflicts between municipal officials and neighborhood organizations, administrative delay, uneven implementation, and disputes over whether citizen participation meant consultation or genuine decision-making authority.

The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 replaced Model Cities and several other categorical grant programs with the Community Development Block Grant. Many Model Cities activities, personnel, planning structures, and neighborhood organizations continued under the new block-grant framework.

Definitions

Model Cities Program
The popular name for the comprehensive city demonstration program authorized by Title I of the Demonstration Cities and Metropolitan Development Act of 1966.
Comprehensive city demonstration program
A coordinated local strategy intended to improve the physical environment, public services, economic conditions, and general welfare of residents within a selected urban area.
Model neighborhood area
The designated geographic area in which a participating city concentrated planning, investment, services, and demonstration activities.
City demonstration agency
The local governmental entity or administrative office responsible for coordinating the city’s Model Cities planning and implementation. The abbreviation CDA was commonly used.
Comprehensive demonstration plan
The local plan identifying neighborhood conditions, objectives, resident priorities, projects, financing, agency responsibilities, schedules, and methods for evaluating progress.
Planning grant
A HUD grant covering up to 80 percent of eligible costs incurred in preparing a comprehensive demonstration plan.
Supplemental grant
Flexible HUD funding used for innovative activities not supported by another federal grant program or, within statutory limits, to help satisfy nonfederal matching requirements for related federally assisted projects.
Federal grant-in-aid program
A federal program providing financial assistance to state or local governments for an authorized public purpose, usually subject to specific eligibility, matching, planning, and administrative requirements.
Widespread citizen participation
The program principle that residents of the model neighborhood should have meaningful opportunities to participate in planning and implementing the local program.
Citizen participation organization
A resident board, neighborhood council, committee, task force, corporation, or similar body created to represent community views within the local Model Cities system.
Comprehensive planning
A process that treats housing, employment, transportation, health, education, public safety, recreation, land use, and public services as connected components of neighborhood development.
Demonstration program
A time-limited initiative intended to test methods that could later inform broader federal, state, or local policy.
Target area
A defined geographic area selected for concentrated public intervention because of poverty, inadequate housing, unemployment, deficient services, physical deterioration, or related conditions.
Physical development
Investment in housing, streets, utilities, parks, public facilities, transportation, environmental improvements, and other elements of the built environment.
Human services
Programs addressing health, education, employment, childcare, social welfare, youth development, legal assistance, public safety, and related household or community needs.

Historical Development

Dissatisfaction with Urban Renewal

By the mid-1960s, traditional urban renewal faced intense criticism. Clearance projects frequently destroyed established neighborhoods, displaced residents and businesses, proceeded slowly, and emphasized land redevelopment more than the social and economic conditions of the people living in project areas.

Federal officials sought a program that would address neighborhood problems through coordinated housing, services, employment, education, transportation, and resident involvement rather than relying primarily on demolition and real-estate redevelopment.

Great Society and War on Poverty Context

Model Cities emerged during the expansion of Great Society policy. The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 had created antipoverty programs that treated local participation and community organization as instruments of reform. The creation of HUD in 1965 supplied a Cabinet-level institution capable of administering a major urban initiative.

The program attempted to combine two policy traditions: the physical redevelopment and planning tools of federal housing policy, and the social-service and participation principles associated with the War on Poverty.

Urban Unrest and Racial Inequality

The legislation was developed amid racial segregation, discriminatory housing markets, concentrated poverty, deindustrialization, inadequate municipal services, and major urban uprisings. Federal officials presented comprehensive neighborhood development as one means of addressing conditions contributing to urban crisis.

The program did not resolve the structural causes of metropolitan inequality, but it directed federal attention and resources toward neighborhoods in which African American, Latino, and other low-income residents were frequently concentrated.

Transition to Block Grants

By the early 1970s, federal policy shifted away from numerous separately administered categorical grants. The Nixon administration favoured broader revenue-sharing and local discretion, while Congress developed a consolidated community-development grant.

The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 created CDBG by combining Model Cities, urban renewal, and several other programs. The new structure preserved many eligible activities but no longer required the same model-neighborhood framework or comprehensive citizen-participation apparatus.

Program Design

Concentrated Neighborhood Strategy

Model Cities was designed around concentration. Rather than distributing limited resources evenly across an entire city, a local government selected one or more model neighborhood areas containing severe social, economic, housing, and environmental problems.

The concentration principle was intended to produce visible and measurable improvement within a five-year period. In practice, many target areas were too large and their needs too extensive for available resources to transform them completely.

Comprehensive Scope

Local plans could include projects in numerous policy fields:

  • Housing construction and rehabilitation
  • Relocation and housing assistance
  • Street, sewer, lighting, and utility improvements
  • Public transportation and pedestrian access
  • Healthcare and mental-health services
  • Education and school-related services
  • Job training, placement, and economic development
  • Childcare, youth programmes, and recreation
  • Public safety, legal services, and community relations
  • Sanitation, environmental improvement, and code enforcement
  • Community centres and neighborhood facilities
  • Planning, data collection, and administrative reform

Demonstration and Innovation

The program was not intended merely to finance existing municipal operations. Federal supplemental grants could support new or experimental activities that did not fit within established grant programs. Cities were expected to test methods capable of producing broader lessons for urban policy.

Intergovernmental Coordination

Model Cities depended on cooperation among HUD, other federal departments, state agencies, municipal departments, schools, housing authorities, transit bodies, health institutions, nonprofit organizations, businesses, and resident groups.

This design created potential leverage by combining multiple funding streams. It also created administrative complexity because each participating federal program retained its own eligibility rules, application procedures, appropriations, schedules, and agency priorities.

Planning and Administration

Application and Selection

Local governments submitted applications identifying proposed model neighborhoods, major conditions, planning capacity, anticipated citizen participation, and local commitment. HUD selected 75 first-round communities and later added 75 second-round communities.

Planning Phase

During the planning period, the city demonstration agency conducted surveys, consulted residents, coordinated municipal departments, identified projects, estimated costs, sought commitments from other agencies, and prepared a comprehensive demonstration plan for HUD approval.

Planning grants financed up to 80 percent of eligible planning costs. The local government was responsible for the remaining share and for organizing the personnel and institutions necessary to complete the plan.

Implementation Phase

After HUD approved a plan, the city could receive supplemental grants and pursue assistance through other federal programs. Local departments or partner organizations then carried out individual projects subject to applicable federal and local rules.

Annual Program Revision

Model Cities plans were not static. Local agencies revised programmes, budgets, schedules, and priorities as funding changed and implementation problems emerged. Citizen bodies frequently reviewed annual plans, although the degree of influence varied.

Evaluation and Reporting

Participating cities collected information on neighborhood conditions, project activity, expenditures, participation, and outcomes. Differences in data quality and evaluation methods made national comparison difficult.

Citizen Participation

Purpose

The program’s citizen-participation requirement reflected recognition that urban policies had often been imposed upon low-income neighborhoods without meaningful resident consent. Model Cities sought to incorporate residents into decisions affecting housing, services, public facilities, redevelopment, and neighborhood priorities.

Participation Structures

Local structures included:

  • Model neighborhood boards
  • Resident councils
  • Block clubs and district committees
  • Issue-specific task forces
  • Citizen planning staffs
  • Nonprofit development corporations
  • Advisory committees to mayors or city demonstration agencies
  • Public hearings, workshops, surveys, and neighborhood meetings

Representation

Participation systems had to determine who legitimately represented the neighborhood. Elections, appointments, organizational delegations, geographic districts, and combinations of these methods were used. Conflicts arose over whether established organizations, newly elected boards, activists, clergy, business owners, tenants, homeowners, youth, or city officials should exercise authority.

Advisory Participation and Citizen Control

The principal dispute concerned power. Municipal officials often viewed citizen participation as consultation within a government-administered program. Resident organizations frequently sought control over budgets, staff, project selection, contracts, and implementation.

Some communities created durable organizations and leadership networks. Others experienced conflict, co-optation, exclusion, factionalism, or administrative paralysis. The program demonstrated that participation without clearly defined authority could generate recurring institutional conflict.

Major Program Activities

Housing

Housing activities included rehabilitation loans and grants, code enforcement, new subsidized housing, public housing, homeownership initiatives, relocation assistance, demolition of unsafe structures, and support for nonprofit or community-based development.

Employment and Economic Development

Local programs supported job training, placement services, youth employment, minority business development, commercial revitalization, business loans, industrial development, and employment connected to publicly funded construction or services.

Health and Social Services

Projects included neighborhood health centres, mental-health services, maternal and child health programmes, substance-abuse treatment, family counselling, senior services, legal assistance, and coordinated referral systems.

Education and Youth Development

Activities included early-childhood education, tutoring, alternative schools, adult education, vocational programmes, recreational facilities, youth centres, arts programmes, and efforts to connect schools with neighborhood services.

Physical Infrastructure

Cities used program resources for streets, sidewalks, lighting, parks, playgrounds, sewer improvements, drainage, sanitation, public buildings, transportation facilities, and other neighborhood infrastructure.

Public Safety and Justice

Some plans supported community-police relations, neighborhood patrols, youth diversion, court services, legal aid, crime-prevention efforts, and rehabilitation programmes. These activities often generated debate over whether public safety policy should emphasize enforcement, prevention, employment, youth services, or community control.

African American Communities and Political Development

Target-Neighborhood Demographics

Many model neighborhoods were predominantly African American and had experienced housing discrimination, redlining, overcrowding, inadequate public services, unemployment, urban renewal displacement, and political underrepresentation.

Resource Access

The program directed federal and local resources toward communities that had often received less public and private investment than white neighborhoods. Funded activities could improve healthcare, housing, recreation, employment services, infrastructure, and organizational capacity.

Leadership Development

Citizen boards, program staffs, nonprofit organizations, and development corporations created positions through which residents gained experience in budgeting, planning, negotiation, administration, public speaking, contracting, and electoral politics.

Model Cities therefore contributed to the development of a broader generation of African American neighborhood leaders, administrators, organizers, and elected officials, even where the programme failed to achieve its physical or economic objectives.

Conflict with Municipal Power Structures

Black resident organizations frequently challenged mayors, planning departments, redevelopment authorities, and business coalitions over control of funds and project priorities. Disputes exposed the difference between including residents in meetings and transferring material decision-making authority.

Limits of Neighborhood Intervention

Concentrated neighborhood programmes could not by themselves reverse metropolitan segregation, employment decentralization, discriminatory lending, suburban exclusion, industrial decline, or unequal school systems. Model Cities often treated locally visible conditions without possessing authority over the regional structures producing them.

Outcomes and Limitations

Institutional Achievements

The program helped establish neighborhood planning offices, resident organizations, nonprofit service providers, development corporations, community centres, data systems, and interdepartmental working relationships. Some of these institutions survived after federal termination.

Service Expansion

Model Cities expanded or improved services in healthcare, childcare, employment, recreation, education, legal assistance, sanitation, and other fields. Service-oriented activities were often easier to launch than major housing or infrastructure projects.

Physical Improvements

Participating cities completed housing rehabilitation, public facilities, street improvements, parks, commercial projects, and other physical investments. Results varied widely among cities and neighborhoods.

Insufficient Scale

The programme’s ambitions exceeded its appropriations. Comprehensive plans frequently identified needs far beyond available federal, state, local, and private resources. The addition of a second group of cities broadened geographic participation but further divided limited funds.

Federal Coordination Problems

HUD could encourage other federal agencies to support approved plans, but it could not always compel them to reserve funds, change regulations, accelerate applications, or align their programmes with local schedules.

Administrative Complexity

Local agencies had to coordinate numerous departments and funding streams while satisfying different federal requirements. Planning and approval delays reduced the time available for implementation.

Political Conflict

Mayors, city councils, municipal agencies, resident boards, community organizations, and private institutions often pursued competing goals. Conflict could promote accountability, but it could also delay projects or weaken coherent administration.

Uncertain Measurable Impact

Because neighborhoods were affected by broader economic, demographic, and political change, it was difficult to attribute changes in poverty, employment, crime, health, or housing solely to Model Cities. Local variation further limited national conclusions.

Policy Legacy

The programme’s most durable legacy may be institutional rather than physical. It established citizen participation, comprehensive planning, neighborhood targeting, interagency coordination, and flexible supplementary funding as recurring features of later community-development policy.

Key Distinctions

Model Cities and Urban Renewal

Urban renewal centred on land acquisition, clearance, rehabilitation, and redevelopment. Model Cities was designed as a broader neighborhood programme combining physical development with social services, economic opportunity, institutional coordination, and citizen participation.

Model Cities and the Community Action Program

The Community Action Program was administered through the Office of Economic Opportunity and emphasized antipoverty services and the maximum feasible participation of poor residents. Model Cities was administered by HUD and placed greater emphasis on municipal government, physical development, and comprehensive neighborhood planning.

Model Cities and Community Development Block Grants

Model Cities was a competitive categorical programme serving 150 selected communities and concentrating resources in designated model neighborhoods. CDBG is a broader formula and state-administered block-grant system providing local governments greater discretion over eligible community-development activities.

Model Cities and Public Housing

Public housing provides publicly owned or controlled rental housing for eligible households. Model Cities could include public housing as one project component but was not itself a housing-tenure programme.

Model Cities and the Model Neighborhood

The federal program was the Model Cities Program. The model neighborhood was the local target area in which the comprehensive plan was carried out.

Model Cities and Later Enterprise Zones

Model Cities relied primarily on coordinated public grants, services, planning, and neighborhood institutions. Later enterprise-zone strategies placed greater emphasis on tax incentives, deregulation, and private investment.

Geographic Scope

HUD selected 150 participating communities. The programme included major industrial cities, regional centres, smaller cities, the District of Columbia, and a participating community in Puerto Rico. Participating locations were distributed across 45 states.

Model neighborhoods differed greatly in size, population, housing type, economic base, racial composition, and physical condition. Some encompassed compact inner-city districts; others covered extensive sections of a municipality or multiple neighborhoods.

Representative participating communities included Atlanta, Baltimore, Boston, Buffalo, Camden, Chicago, Cleveland, Detroit, Kansas City, Newark, New York City, Oakland, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, San Antonio, Seattle, and Washington, D.C.

The programme’s national design was uniform only at a broad level. Each city developed its own administrative system, neighborhood boundaries, citizen-participation structure, project portfolio, and relationship between city hall and resident organizations.

Legal Context

Demonstration Cities and Metropolitan Development Act of 1966

Title I authorized HUD to provide grants and technical assistance for planning and carrying out comprehensive city demonstration programmes. The Act required local plans to address physical and social conditions and called for widespread citizen participation.

Other titles addressed metropolitan planning, review of federal public-facility applications, mortgage insurance, new communities, historic preservation, mass transportation, water and sewer facilities, and related urban-development matters.

Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibited discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in programmes or activities receiving federal financial assistance. Model Cities governments and participating agencies were therefore subject to federal civil-rights obligations.

Department of Housing and Urban Development Act of 1965

This law created HUD as a Cabinet-level department and supplied the institutional framework through which Model Cities was administered.

Fair Housing Act of 1968

Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968 prohibited specified forms of housing discrimination and shaped the legal environment governing Model Cities housing, relocation, and neighborhood-development activities.

National Environmental Policy Act of 1969

NEPA introduced federal environmental-review requirements that could apply to major federally assisted development activities undertaken during the later programme years.

Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970

This law established national standards for relocation payments, advisory services, and property acquisition when federal or federally assisted projects displaced residents, businesses, farms, or nonprofit organizations.

Housing and Community Development Act of 1974

This statute created the Community Development Block Grant and terminated the Model Cities categorical grant. It provided transitional and hold-harmless arrangements that allowed former participating communities to continue selected activities.

Administrative and Constitutional Issues

Model Cities disputes generally concerned statutory administration, local governmental authority, contracting, resident representation, use of public funds, relocation, civil rights, and the allocation of decision-making power rather than one nationally controlling Supreme Court decision specific to the programme.

Summary

The Model Cities Program was a Great Society initiative that attempted to improve selected low-income urban neighborhoods through comprehensive planning, coordinated federal and local investment, social services, physical development, economic opportunity, and resident participation.

Authorized in 1966 and implemented in 150 communities, the program represented a deliberate departure from clearance-centred urban renewal. It recognized that deteriorated housing, unemployment, inadequate services, racial segregation, weak infrastructure, and political exclusion were interconnected problems requiring coordinated intervention.

Model Cities generated useful local projects, institutions, and leadership networks, particularly in African American communities. Its achievements were constrained by limited funding, fragmented federal authority, administrative complexity, local political conflict, and uncertainty over the actual power granted to residents.

The programme ended as a separate federal initiative in 1974, but its core concepts—neighborhood targeting, comprehensive planning, citizen participation, interagency coordination, and flexible community-development funding—continued through the Community Development Block Grant and later urban-policy programmes.
