Homeowner Assistance Fund

The temporary United States Treasury pandemic program providing nearly $10 billion through states, territories, Tribal governments, Tribally Designated Housing Entities, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands to prevent mortgage delinquency, default, foreclosure, utility loss, tax foreclosure, and displacement among financially distressed homeowners.

Terminology Note

The official program name is the Homeowner Assistance Fund, commonly abbreviated HAF.

HAF is a temporary federal financial-assistance program administered by the United States Department of the Treasury through governmental recipients. Treasury generally does not accept individual homeowner applications or make household-level eligibility decisions.

A homeowner ordinarily applies to the program operated by the homeowner's state, territory, Tribe, Tribally Designated Housing Entity, or the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

HAF should not be confused with mortgage forbearance, a mortgage modification, the Home Affordable Modification Program, the Hardest Hit Fund, FHA partial claims, VA or USDA loss mitigation, the Homeowner Assistance Program used in disaster recovery, or the Emergency Rental Assistance Program.

HAF assistance can be structured as a grant, forgivable loan, deferred-payment loan, recoverable loan, or another form approved in a recipient's Treasury-approved HAF Plan. The legal form and repayment conditions differ by jurisdiction and program element.

The federal HAF award period remains open through September 30, 2026. Individual programs may close earlier after exhausting funds, completing planned activities, or entering early closeout.

The statutory appropriation was available to Treasury through September 30, 2025 for federal allocation and payment. Recipient financial-assistance agreements establish a period of performance ending September 30, 2026.

Overview

HAF was created to preserve homeownership after the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted employment, income, health, childcare, small businesses, and household finances.

Federal mortgage forbearance temporarily allowed many borrowers to pause payments, but unpaid amounts remained due and homeowners also accumulated property-tax, insurance, utility, association, manufactured-home lot-rent, and other housing obligations.

HAF supplied flexible federal capital to locally designed programs rather than imposing one national payment formula.

A recipient could pay mortgage arrears, combine federal assistance with a servicer modification, reduce principal, lower an interest rate, pay delinquent property charges, prevent tax foreclosure, repair a home, clear title, or provide housing counselling and legal assistance.

The program was designed to reach homeowners who could not be stabilised through ordinary servicer loss mitigation alone.

State housing finance agencies administered most state programs. Tribal governments and housing entities adapted HAF to local land tenure, manufactured housing, utility, title, mortgage-access, and housing-condition needs.

Classification and Scope

This page belongs under Government Programs because HAF is a federal financial-assistance system with statutory appropriations, recipient eligibility, household standards, qualified expenses, federal agreements, local program term sheets, reporting, audits, and closeout.

The Department of the Treasury, Office of Capital Access, state housing finance agencies, and Tribal housing entities belong under Institutions.

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 belongs under Laws and Legislation.

Mortgage forbearance, foreclosure, mortgage servicing, loss mitigation, tax foreclosure, housing counselling, and manufactured-home title are related concepts.

Historical Context

Federal foreclosure-prevention policy expanded during the Great Depression, the savings-and-loan crisis, the Great Recession, and the COVID-19 emergency.

The Home Owners' Loan Corporation refinanced distressed mortgages during the 1930s. FHA and VA later transformed mortgage credit and foreclosure administration.

The 2007–2009 housing crisis produced Making Home Affordable, HAMP, HARP, FHA loss mitigation, and the Hardest Hit Fund.

HAF drew on lessons from the Hardest Hit Fund while expanding eligibility nationally and including nonmortgage homeownership costs.

Great Recession Predecessors

Treasury launched Making Home Affordable in 2009 to reduce mortgage payments, refinance eligible borrowers, and provide alternatives to foreclosure.

HAMP used servicer incentives and standard modification guidelines to lower monthly mortgage payments.

The Hardest Hit Fund began in 2010 and ultimately supplied approximately $9.6 billion to eighteen states and the District of Columbia.

HAF retained the locally tailored state-program model but used American Rescue Plan grants rather than Troubled Asset Relief Program contracts.

Pandemic Mortgage Crisis

The pandemic caused sudden unemployment, reduced hours, medical expenses, caregiving obligations, business losses, and other financial hardship.

At the peak of the crisis, millions of mortgages entered forbearance and an estimated 2.4 million homeowners were at least three months behind.

Property taxes, utilities, insurance, condominium charges, association dues, manufactured-home lot rent, and home-repair costs continued even where mortgage payments were paused.

Congress created HAF to address the residual gaps and protect household equity.

American Rescue Plan Act of 2021

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. signed the American Rescue Plan Act on March 11, 2021.

Section 3206 appropriated $9.961 billion and established HAF within the Department of the Treasury.

The statute defined covered mortgages, eligible entities, allocation factors, income targeting, Tribal funding, state minimums, and qualified expenses.

It required at least 60 percent of each award to serve homeowners at or below the lower-income targeting threshold and prioritised remaining funds for socially disadvantaged individuals.

Appropriation and Award Availability

The federal appropriation was made available to Treasury through September 30, 2025.

Treasury transferred allocations to approved recipients under financial-assistance agreements.

Those recipient awards have a period of performance extending through September 30, 2026.

The distinction explains why the statute's appropriations date and the recipient program end date differ.

Allocation Formula

Treasury allocated the principal state share according to relative homeowner need measured when the Act was enacted.

  • Average unemployment over a three-to-twelve-month period.
  • Mortgages more than thirty days past due.
  • Mortgages in foreclosure.
  • Minimum state allocations.
  • Territorial reservations.
  • Tribal formula allocations.

State and Puerto Rico Minimum

Every state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico received at least $50 million.

The minimum allowed smaller jurisdictions with lower absolute mortgage counts to construct viable programs.

Territorial Allocation

Congress reserved $30 million for Guam, American Samoa, United States Virgin Islands, and Northern Mariana Islands.

Distribution was based on each territory's share of the combined territorial population.

Puerto Rico was treated with the states for principal allocation and minimum purposes.

Tribal and Hawaiian Home Lands Allocation

Five percent of the appropriation was set aside for eligible Indian Tribes, Tribally Designated Housing Entities, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

Tribal programs could address mortgage scarcity, contract-for-deed arrangements, manufactured housing, lot rent, utilities, title problems, repairs, overcrowding, and other local priorities.

Financial Assistance Agreement

Every recipient signed a federal financial-assistance agreement with Treasury.

The agreement incorporated the statute, Treasury guidance, approved HAF Plan, reporting obligations, Uniform Guidance, civil-rights requirements, audit duties, and federal remedies.

The recipient remained responsible for subrecipients, contractors, servicer payments, data quality, internal controls, improper payments, and closeout.

Initial Ten-Percent Payment

Treasury made an initial payment equal to 10 percent of a recipient's allocation after execution of the agreement and commitment to lawful use.

No more than half of the initial payment could support planning, engagement, assessment, and administrative costs.

Remaining funds were released after approval of the recipient's HAF Plan.

Treasury-Approved HAF Plan

A recipient had to submit a detailed plan before receiving the balance of its allocation.

  • Homeowner needs and local data.
  • Public and community engagement.
  • Program elements and term sheets.
  • Homeowner eligibility and income targeting.
  • Application and payment processes.
  • Program maximums.
  • Coordination with servicers.
  • Equity and accessibility.
  • Performance goals and fraud controls.
  • Staffing, contractors, budget, and readiness.

HAF Plan Amendments

Recipients modified programs as mortgage delinquencies declined, insurance and tax arrears increased, servicer tools changed, and original categories exhausted demand.

Material changes required Treasury approval and updated term sheets.

A recipient could not expand beyond the statutory qualified expenses merely because funds remained.

Eligible Homeowner

A homeowner had to hold a vested legal or equitable ownership interest in the dwelling under applicable state or Tribal law.

The property had to be the homeowner's primary residence.

The homeowner had to experience a qualifying financial hardship after January 21, 2020.

Household income had to remain within the federal ceiling and any stricter local limit.

Primary Residence

HAF was limited to a homeowner's principal residence.

Vacation homes, second homes, and investment-only properties were not eligible.

A one-to-four-unit property could qualify when it contained the homeowner's principal residence.

Ownership Forms

  • Record title ownership.
  • Joint tenancy or tenancy in common.
  • Life estate.
  • Heir property or inherited ownership where recognised.
  • Cooperative shares with occupancy rights.
  • Manufactured-home ownership.
  • Contract for deed or land contract.
  • Tribal trust, allotted, or restricted ownership arrangements.
  • Other vested legal or equitable interests recognised by the administering program.

Qualifying Mortgage

A statutory mortgage is a credit transaction secured by the principal residence or residential real property containing a one-to-four-unit dwelling.

The original unpaid principal balance generally could not exceed the applicable conforming loan limit.

Reverse mortgages, manufactured-home loans, contracts for deed, and certain second mortgages could qualify when they met statutory and state-law requirements.

Homeowners without mortgages could still receive assistance for taxes, utilities, insurance, lot rent, repairs, association charges, title, or other approved housing costs.

Financial Hardship

Treasury defined financial hardship as a material reduction in income or material increase in living expenses associated with the pandemic that created or increased housing-stability risk.

  • Job loss or reduced work hours.
  • Business loss.
  • Medical expenses, illness, or disability.
  • Childcare and caregiving costs.
  • Death of a household member.
  • Increased utility or housing expenses.
  • Loss of rental income from an owner-occupied property.
  • Other material pandemic-related economic effects.

Hardship Attestation

Homeowners had to attest that a qualifying hardship occurred after January 21, 2020.

The attestation described the nature of the hardship rather than merely checking an unexplained box.

False hardship statements could create denial, recovery, civil liability, or criminal prosecution.

Income Ceiling

General HAF eligibility was limited to household income at or below 150 percent of area median income or 100 percent of United States median income, whichever was greater.

HUD published HAF income limits by household size and location.

Recipients could establish lower limits to target scarce funds.

Sixty-Percent Income Targeting

At least 60 percent of each recipient's award had to assist homeowners at or below 100 percent of area median income or 100 percent of national median income, whichever was greater.

This was a spending requirement applied to the recipient's award, not merely an outreach objective.

Priority for Socially Disadvantaged Homeowners

After satisfying the mandatory lower-income targeting share, recipients had to prioritise remaining funds for socially disadvantaged individuals.

Treasury guidance identified indicators involving impaired access to homeownership credit, majority-minority census tracts, limited English proficiency, persistent-poverty counties, Tribal or territorial residence, and other lawful recipient methods.

Programs also remained subject to federal fair-housing and nondiscrimination law.

Income Verification

A recipient could use HUD annual-income rules or adjusted gross income under federal tax forms.

  • Tax returns, W-2 forms, and 1099 forms.
  • Pay statements and employer attestations.
  • Bank statements and benefit letters.
  • Pension statements and business records.
  • Unemployment records.
  • Other reliable evidence.

Fact-Specific Income Proxies

Treasury permitted written household income attestations supported by a reasonable fact-specific proxy.

A recipient might rely on average income in a census tract, ZIP code, or another geographic area when sufficiently connected to the household's circumstances.

The recipient remained responsible for documenting a reasonable income determination.

Homeowner Application Process

  1. Locate the applicable program. The homeowner checks the state, territorial, Tribal, TDHE, or Hawaiian Home Lands program.
  2. Confirm the application window. Programs may be open, limited, paused, waitlisted, or closed.
  3. Submit ownership and occupancy information.
  4. Document or attest hardship.
  5. Establish household income.
  6. Identify housing obligations.
  7. Coordinate loss mitigation.
  8. Receive a determination and execute agreements.
  9. Payment and confirmation.

Common Application Documents

  • Government-issued or alternative identity evidence.
  • Deed, title, cooperative share, contract for deed, or heirship information.
  • Primary-residence evidence.
  • Mortgage statement or servicer information.
  • Property-tax bill.
  • Utility or home-energy bill.
  • Insurance statement.
  • Association ledger or lien.
  • Manufactured-home title and lot-rent statement.
  • Household income evidence.
  • Hardship attestation.
  • Foreclosure, default, or delinquency notice.
  • Repair estimate or inspection.
  • Authorisation for communication with servicers and providers.

Mortgage-Servicer Coordination

Mortgage servicers were essential to accurate arrears figures, payment posting, reinstatement quotes, foreclosure holds, partial claims, modifications, and principal reduction.

Treasury encouraged common data files, standard agreements, secure portals, designated contacts, and rapid response.

National servicers had to work with dozens of different HAF programs, and delays could cause reinstatement amounts to change before payment arrived.

Payment to Mortgage and Housing Providers

HAF ordinarily paid a servicer, taxing authority, utility, insurer, association, manufactured-home community, contractor, counsellor, or attorney.

Payments had to be credited to the eligible homeowner's approved obligation.

Programs required confirmation, reconciliation, refund procedures, and treatment of overpayments.

Direct unrestricted payments to homeowners were uncommon and depended on the approved HAF Plan.

Mortgage Payment Assistance

Programs could pay current or prospective mortgage payments for a limited period.

Payment assistance could bridge unemployment, temporary disability, income recovery, or implementation of permanent loss mitigation.

Local term sheets set monthly limits, total limits, eligibility periods, and whether payments were grants or loans.

Mortgage Reinstatement

Reinstatement pays the amount needed to bring a delinquent mortgage current.

The amount can include missed principal and interest, escrow advances, allowable fees, taxes, insurance, and other servicer advances.

Programs used reinstatement when the homeowner could resume future payments after the arrears were cured.

Reinstatement without sustainable future affordability could delay rather than prevent foreclosure.

Forbearance and Deferred Balances

Pandemic forbearance permitted payment pauses but did not generally forgive the unpaid amount.

Servicers could move arrears into a partial claim, deferred balance, modification, repayment plan, or maturity extension.

HAF could pay arrears directly, supplement loss mitigation, or pay certain deferred balances and partial claims when the approved plan allowed.

Treasury encouraged recipients to avoid replacing servicer or federal loss-mitigation resources that otherwise would be available.

Mortgage Principal Reduction

HAF could reduce the principal balance of a first or qualifying second mortgage.

Principal reduction could enable a recast, support a modification, lower monthly payment, restore equity, or eliminate an unaffordable subordinate obligation.

Programs often limited principal reduction to the amount necessary for affordability or long-term sustainability.

Some recipients used liens or recapture provisions when providing large principal assistance.

Interest-Rate Reduction

HAF could facilitate a lower mortgage interest rate.

Assistance might be combined with a recast, modification, refinancing mechanism, buydown, or payment to satisfy arrears that prevented modification.

The structure depended on servicer capability, mortgage investor rules, and the approved program term sheet.

Partial Claims and Deferred Liens

FHA, VA, USDA, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and private systems can place arrears into non-interest-bearing or deferred obligations.

These tools prevent immediate foreclosure but reduce homeowner equity and can become due at sale, refinance, payoff, or maturity.

Treasury allowed HAF programs to pay qualifying partial claims or deferred balances where doing so promoted housing stability and met the approved plan.

Property-Tax Assistance

HAF could pay delinquent property taxes to prevent tax foreclosure.

Assistance was available to homeowners with or without a mortgage.

Programs coordinated with tax collectors to verify principal, interest, penalties, sale dates, redemption periods, and payment posting.

Tax foreclosure can extinguish mortgage interests and homeowner equity, making timely intervention especially important.

Utilities and Home-Energy Assistance

HAF could pay electric, gas, water, wastewater, heating oil, firewood, and other qualifying home-energy obligations.

Internet and broadband service were also included in the statutory qualified-expense list.

Programs were encouraged to coordinate with LIHEAP and other utility programs before using HAF where another source was available.

Homeowners, Flood, and Mortgage Insurance

HAF could pay homeowners insurance, flood insurance, and mortgage-insurance premiums.

Insurance lapse can violate mortgage covenants, cause force-placed insurance, expose the property to catastrophic loss, and increase foreclosure risk.

Rising insurance costs became an important later-program concern in disaster-prone markets.

Homeowner and Condominium Association Charges

HAF could pay homeowner-association fees or liens, condominium assessments, common charges, and comparable cooperative obligations.

Unpaid charges can produce liens, legal fees, foreclosure, loss of cooperative occupancy rights, and damage to the community's finances.

Manufactured Housing

Manufactured-home owners may hold real-property mortgages, personal-property loans, retail instalment contracts, or own the home while renting the land.

HAF could support eligible manufactured-home loans, taxes, utilities, insurance, association charges, repairs, title assistance, and lot rent.

Lot-rent assistance was expressly recognised when it promoted housing stability and prevented default.

Reverse Mortgages

A qualifying reverse mortgage can fall within the HAF mortgage definition.

Reverse-mortgage borrowers may face foreclosure after failing to pay property taxes, insurance, association charges, or maintain the home.

HAF could cure qualifying property-charge defaults and preserve occupancy.

Contracts for Deed and Land Contracts

A contract for deed can qualify where it creates a secured credit transaction or equitable ownership under state law.

These arrangements can involve weak title protection, forfeiture clauses, undisclosed liens, and poor records.

Programs evaluated ownership, principal residence, payment obligations, seller authority, and risk of displacement.

Property Assessed Clean Energy Loans

Treasury's 2023 guidance allowed payment assistance or principal reduction for qualifying PACE loans.

PACE obligations are generally repaid through property-tax assessments and can create tax-lien or foreclosure risk.

Home Repairs and Habitability

HAF could finance repairs necessary to maintain habitability and prevent homeowner displacement.

  • Roof, heating, cooling, plumbing, sewage, and electrical failure.
  • Structural deficiencies and code violations.
  • Accessibility modifications.
  • Mould, moisture, water, or fire remediation.
  • Septic and well failure.
  • Limited habitable-space additions to relieve overcrowding.

Repair Program Controls

Repair programs required inspections, scopes of work, cost estimates, contractor eligibility, procurement, permits, payment controls, and completion verification.

Programs had to distinguish habitability and displacement prevention from general renovation, luxury upgrades, or deferred maintenance unrelated to hardship.

Clear Title and Heir Property Assistance

HAF could help households obtain clear title where unresolved ownership threatened housing stability.

Assistance could involve probate, heirship, deed correction, title search, land records, legal services, or resolution of informal ownership.

Title defects can prevent mortgage modification, insurance payment, tax relief, repair grants, sale, or succession.

Housing Counselling

Recipients could spend up to 5 percent of their allocation on approved counselling and legal services.

Counsellors helped homeowners understand forbearance, modification, partial claims, payment plans, HAF, budgets, foreclosure notices, reverse mortgages, taxes, and insurance.

Nonmonetary counselling counted among homeowners assisted in Treasury reporting.

Legal Services

HAF could fund legal services targeted to foreclosure prevention or homeowner displacement.

  • Foreclosure defence and loss-mitigation appeals.
  • Tax foreclosure and association liens.
  • Reverse-mortgage default.
  • Probate and heir property.
  • Manufactured-home title and contracts for deed.
  • Bankruptcy coordination and servicing errors.
  • Post-foreclosure eviction.

Planning and Administrative Costs

Up to 15 percent of a recipient's allocation could support planning, community engagement, needs assessment, and administration.

  • Staffing, application portals, and call centres.
  • Contractors and subrecipient oversight.
  • Fraud prevention and payment systems.
  • Data, reporting, outreach, and translation.
  • Legal review, audits, records management, and closeout.

Forms of Assistance

  • Grants and forgivable loans.
  • Deferred-payment or zero-interest loans.
  • Recapture liens and subordinate mortgages.
  • Direct provider payments.
  • Principal-reduction contributions.
  • Interest-rate assistance.
  • Nonmonetary counselling or legal services.

The recipient's term sheet and homeowner agreement determined repayment, forgiveness, lien, sale, refinance, transfer, and occupancy conditions.

Household Assistance Maximums

Federal law did not establish one nationwide household cap.

Each approved HAF Plan stated maximum assistance for each program element.

A homeowner could receive assistance through several program elements subject to the recipient's aggregate cap and duplication controls.

Duplication of Benefits

HAF could not pay a cost already paid by another federal source.

Recipients coordinated with mortgage servicers, insurance, LIHEAP, disaster programs, state funds, charitable assistance, and other pandemic programs.

Duplicate payment could create an improper payment, refund, recovery, or federal debt.

Relationship to Mortgage Forbearance

Forbearance temporarily reduces or pauses scheduled mortgage payments.

HAF provides money or services to resolve delinquency and housing costs.

HAF did not erase the borrower's contractual mortgage or replace the servicer's duty to evaluate available loss mitigation.

Relationship to FHA, VA, USDA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac

Federal and government-sponsored mortgage systems created separate partial claims, payment deferrals, modifications, payment supplements, and other loss-mitigation tools.

HAF could cure arrears, contribute to a modification, pay property charges, or address a balance not fully resolved by those tools.

Treasury encouraged recipients to prioritise mortgages serving low- and moderate-income borrowers.

Relationship to the Hardest Hit Fund

The Hardest Hit Fund used Troubled Asset Relief Program authority after the Great Recession and served eighteen states and the District of Columbia.

HAF used a similar state housing-finance-agency model, national coverage, statutory income targeting, Tribal funding, and a wider range of housing expenses.

Relationship to Emergency Rental Assistance

ERA paid rent and renter utilities. HAF served owner-occupied housing and homeownership expenses.

Both programs were Treasury-administered pandemic grants delivered through governmental recipients.

ERA ended by September 30, 2025. HAF remains within its award period through September 30, 2026.

Rural and Tribal Homeowners

Rural homeowners faced limited broadband, distant application offices, older housing, wells and septic systems, lower property values, title problems, and fewer counsellors or attorneys.

Tribal HAF programs operated within distinct sovereignty, trust-land, housing-finance, and infrastructure systems.

Treasury permitted Tribal programs to emphasise nonmortgage needs when mortgages were scarce or other qualified expenses were more urgent.

Racial and Economic Equity

Black, Latino, Native, female-headed, immigrant, disabled, rural, and low-wealth homeowners entered the pandemic with unequal access to credit, savings, insurance, legal assistance, and home equity.

HAF used income targeting, geographic strategies, limited-English-proficiency outreach, counselling, title assistance, and nonmortgage expense categories to reach underserved households.

Treasury's Q1 2026 reported data associated approximately 49 percent of approved applications with incomes at or below 50 percent of area median income.

Application Barriers

  • Unclear program availability.
  • Digital-only portals and limited broadband.
  • Complex income documents.
  • Servicer delay or error.
  • Title defects and informal ownership.
  • Language and disability-access barriers.
  • Multiple liens and payees.
  • Changing program maximums.
  • Waitlists and exhausted funds.
  • Different rules among jurisdictions.

Quarterly and Annual Reporting

Recipients reported obligations, expenditures, applications, approvals, denials, homeowners assisted, targeting, demographics, mortgage type, housing type, services, program design, and geographic information.

States, territories, and larger Tribal recipients submitted quarterly compliance reports.

Annual reports addressed civil rights, administration, subrecipients, program progress, and compliance.

Data Limitations

Applications, approvals, homeowners assisted, and payments are different measures.

A homeowner may receive monetary assistance, nonmonetary counselling, legal services, or more than one type of support.

Race categories can sum to more than the number of people where several races are reported.

Small Tribal recipients are not included in all public summary tables, and data are revised as recipients correct submissions.

Application Denials

  • Income above the program limit.
  • No qualifying pandemic hardship.
  • Property not the primary residence.
  • No eligible ownership interest.
  • Expense outside an approved program element.
  • Mortgage above the applicable original conforming limit.
  • Duplicate assistance or incomplete application.
  • Program maximum insufficient to resolve the delinquency.
  • Funds exhausted.
  • Fraud or material misrepresentation.

Reconsideration and Appeals

Treasury did not impose one national homeowner appeal procedure.

Recipients established local review, reconsideration, grievance, or appeal processes in their plans and policies.

Effective systems provided written denial reasons, deadlines, document-submission opportunities, and independent review.

Fraud Risks

  • False ownership, occupancy, hardship, or income.
  • Stolen identities.
  • Invented mortgage, tax, utility, or association obligations.
  • Duplicate applications.
  • Provider diversion of funds.
  • False contractor invoices.
  • Employee collusion, conflicts of interest, or kickbacks.
  • Cyber theft and payment diversion.
  • False title or heirship records.
  • Misuse of administrative funds.

Improper Payments

An improper payment can involve an ineligible homeowner, expense, property, period, amount, payee, unsupported decision, or duplicate benefit.

A payment can be improper without being fraudulent.

Recipients must identify, correct, recover, replace, or report improper payments according to Treasury guidance and federal award rules.

Civil Rights, Privacy, and Accessibility

HAF recipients were subject to applicable federal civil-rights requirements incorporated into the financial-assistance agreement.

Programs had to provide equal access without unlawful discrimination and address language and disability access.

Recipients also had to protect identity, mortgage, income, tax, utility, disability, banking, and property information.

Single Audit and Oversight

HAF is subject to the Uniform Guidance and Single Audit framework where federal expenditure thresholds apply.

Auditors examine allowable activities, eligible homeowners, targeting, period of performance, reporting, subrecipient monitoring, procurement, and cash management.

Treasury's Inspector General conducts audits and investigations and receives allegations of fraud, waste, and abuse.

Closeout Overview

Closeout confirms that the recipient completed approved objectives, used funds for allowable costs, returned remaining balances, accounted for property, and submitted accurate final reports.

Some recipients initiate early closeout before September 30, 2026 after completing programmes and spending or returning funds.

Recipients that remain open enter standard closeout when the award period ends.

Early Closeout

A recipient can request early closeout after completing objectives, resolving obligations, and preparing final data.

Treasury reviews readiness and opens final reporting when appropriate.

After final reports are submitted, the recipient cannot continue obligating or spending the remaining award.

Standard Closeout

The standard period of performance ends September 30, 2026.

All new program and administrative obligations must be made by that date.

Recipients then have 120 calendar days to liquidate valid obligations and submit final reports by January 28, 2027.

Subrecipients generally must complete liquidation and final reports within ninety days, by December 29, 2026, unless the recipient establishes an earlier deadline.

Restrictions after September 30, 2026

Recipients may not use HAF funds after the award period to:

  • accept or assess new homeowner applications;
  • make new obligations;
  • provide new counselling or legal services;
  • cover prospective mortgage periods beyond the award term;
  • begin new repairs;
  • launch new outreach or administrative activity; or
  • expand existing programme scope.

Recipients may pay valid pre-existing obligations during liquidation and complete necessary closeout administration.

Final Reports

Final closeout reporting includes a final quarterly report, final annual report, financial confirmation, property information, and certifications.

Recipients report final obligations, expenditures, homeowners, applications, demographics, targeting, programme activity, administration, subrecipients, and compliance.

Treasury can request clarification, correction, supporting documentation, or return of funds.

Return of Unused or Disallowed Funds

Unobligated and unexpended balances must be returned to Treasury.

Disallowed, unsupported, duplicate, or otherwise improper expenditures may also require repayment.

Return of funds does not end liability for later audit or investigation.

Program Income, Loans, and Liens

Some HAF assistance was provided as loans, recoverable grants, or liens that can generate principal, interest, fees, or recapture proceeds.

Program income earned during the period of performance must support eligible HAF purposes under Treasury guidance.

Post-closeout treatment depends on the financial-assistance agreement, approved plan, Uniform Guidance, Treasury instructions, and legal form of the obligation.

Recipients must maintain loan servicing, lien releases, collection, accounting, and records after household assistance ends.

Record Retention

Closeout does not eliminate recordkeeping obligations.

Recipients and subrecipients must retain eligibility, income, hardship, ownership, payment, procurement, subaward, property, reporting, audit, and programme-income records for the required period.

Records connected to litigation, audit, claim, investigation, unresolved findings, property, or long-term loans must be retained longer where required.

Program Achievements

  • Created a nationwide homeowner-stabilisation program after the pandemic.
  • Provided nearly $10 billion through governmental recipients.
  • Reached hundreds of thousands of homeowners.
  • Directed the majority of assistance to lower-income households.
  • Addressed mortgage and nonmortgage housing costs.
  • Supported principal reduction and sustainable mortgage modifications.
  • Prevented property-tax and association foreclosures.
  • Protected utilities, insurance, and home-energy service.
  • Included manufactured homes, reverse mortgages, contracts for deed, and lot rent.
  • Funded habitability repairs and title assistance.
  • Supported housing counselling and legal services.
  • Included states, territories, Tribes, TDHEs, and Hawaiian Home Lands.
  • Preserved homeowner equity and intergenerational wealth.
  • Applied lessons from the Hardest Hit Fund nationally.

Limitations and Failures

  • HAF was temporary rather than a permanent foreclosure-prevention entitlement.
  • Program rules and household maximums varied substantially.
  • Some programs launched slowly.
  • Mortgage-servicer coordination delayed payments.
  • Title and ownership problems excluded or delayed vulnerable homeowners.
  • Digital portals created access barriers.
  • Some recipients exhausted funds before the federal deadline.
  • Other recipients struggled to deploy full allocations.
  • Assistance could cure arrears without ensuring future affordability.
  • Rising insurance and property taxes outlasted pandemic relief.
  • Small Tribal recipients faced high administrative costs.
  • Repair programs were complex and slow.
  • Federal reporting did not include every recipient in the same public dataset.
  • Temporary federal grants could not resolve structural wealth and mortgage-access disparities.

Major Controversies

Uniformity versus Local Flexibility

Local design allowed adaptation but produced major differences in eligibility, maximums, documentation, and access.

Mortgage Assistance versus Other Housing Costs

Some jurisdictions prioritised mortgage reinstatement while others faced greater tax, utility, insurance, lot-rent, repair, or title needs.

Temporary Cure versus Sustainable Affordability

Paying arrears can prevent immediate foreclosure without correcting an unaffordable future payment.

Grants versus Recapture Liens

Recoverable assistance stretches public funds but can reduce equity and complicate later sale or refinancing.

Spending Speed versus Fraud Control

Simplified applications improve access while increasing documentation and improper-payment risk.

Closeout Timing

Programs must end federal obligations even where homeowners continue to face elevated insurance, tax, and mortgage costs.

Documented Outcomes

Treasury's Q1 2026 reporting summary identified 594,539 homeowners receiving monetary or nonmonetary assistance among states, territories, and Tribal recipients with allocations over $5 million.

Approximately 521,224 of those homeowners were reported within the statutory lower-income targeting category.

Reported expenditures exceeded $9.15 billion in that reporting universe.

The data show extensive use of mortgage assistance and substantial nonmortgage spending on property taxes, utilities, insurance, repairs, counselling, legal services, and related expenses.

Measuring foreclosures prevented requires caution because assistance interacted with servicer modifications, forbearance, economic recovery, state laws, and other federal policies.

Historical Significance

HAF was the first nationwide federal homeowner-assistance grant of its scale delivered through every state, territories, and Tribal recipients.

It treated homeownership instability as broader than mortgage delinquency by including taxes, insurance, utilities, association charges, lot rent, repairs, title, counselling, and legal services.

The program linked pandemic recovery with preservation of homeowner equity and intergenerational wealth.

HAF also created a national data and administrative infrastructure that can inform future recession, disaster, insurance, and foreclosure-prevention policy.

Research Cautions

  • HAF is not ERA. HAF serves homeowners; ERA served renters.
  • HAF is active federally through September 30, 2026. Local programs may close earlier.
  • The statute's September 2025 appropriation date differs from the award period. Recipient performance ends in September 2026.
  • Treasury does not usually approve household applications. Governmental recipients administer local programs.
  • There is no single national household maximum. Approved term sheets control.
  • Assistance is not always a grant. Some programs use loans, liens, forgiveness, or recapture.
  • A homeowner need not have a conventional mortgage. Taxes, utilities, insurance, lot rent, repairs, and title can qualify.
  • Forbearance and HAF are different. Forbearance pauses payments; HAF supplies approved financial assistance or services.
  • Homeowners assisted can include nonmonetary services. Do not treat the count as a count of mortgage payments.
  • Q1 2026 public data exclude some smaller Tribal recipients. They are not a final entire-program total.
  • Closeout does not end federal liability. Audits, records, recovery, loans, liens, and investigations continue.

Definitions

Area median income
HUD-calculated median family income for a metropolitan or nonmetropolitan area, adjusted for household size.
Conforming loan limit
The annual maximum original mortgage amount eligible for purchase by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac under federal law.
Eligible entity
A state, territory, eligible Tribe or TDHE, or the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands authorised to receive HAF funding.
Financial hardship
A material pandemic-associated reduction in income or increase in expenses creating or increasing housing-stability risk.
HAF Plan
Treasury-approved recipient plan describing need, program design, targeting, eligibility, administration, performance, and use of funds.
Homeowner
A person holding a vested legal or equitable ownership interest in the relevant dwelling under applicable law.
Mortgage reinstatement
Payment of the amount needed to bring a delinquent mortgage current.
Partial claim
A subordinate obligation used to move mortgage arrears out of the monthly payment, often due at sale, refinance, payoff, or maturity.
Period of performance
The award period during which the recipient may incur new HAF obligations, ending September 30, 2026.
Principal reduction
Assistance applied to reduce the outstanding mortgage or qualifying subordinate balance.
Program income
Income generated by a federally supported activity or property, including principal and interest from HAF-funded loans.
Qualified expense
An authorised mortgage, housing, utility, tax, insurance, repair, counselling, legal, administrative, or other housing-stability cost.
Socially disadvantaged individual
A homeowner whose ability to purchase or own a home was impaired by diminished access to reasonable credit under the statutory and Treasury targeting framework.
Subrecipient
An entity receiving a subaward to carry out part of a recipient's HAF program.

Summary

The Homeowner Assistance Fund is a temporary Treasury program created by the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021.

Congress appropriated $9.961 billion to prevent mortgage delinquency, default, foreclosure, utility loss, tax foreclosure, post-foreclosure eviction, and displacement associated with pandemic financial hardship.

Treasury distributed awards to states, the District of Columbia, territories, eligible Tribes and TDHEs, and the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands.

Homeowners had to occupy the property as a primary residence, hold a legal or equitable ownership interest, experience a material hardship after January 21, 2020, and meet income and program requirements.

General eligibility extended to households at or below 150 percent of area median income or 100 percent of national median income, whichever was greater.

At least 60 percent of each award had to serve homeowners at or below the lower 100-percent targeting threshold.

HAF could finance mortgage payments, reinstatement, principal reduction, interest-rate reduction, property taxes, utilities, insurance, association charges, manufactured-home lot rent, repairs, title assistance, counselling, legal services, and other approved housing-stability costs.

Recipients established their own program elements, household maximums, payment forms, documentation, appeals, and application windows through Treasury-approved HAF Plans.

Treasury's Q1 2026 summary for its principal reporting universe identified approximately $9.159 billion in expenditures and 594,539 homeowners assisted.

As of August 3, 2026, HAF remained active federally, but local availability varied because some jurisdictions had exhausted funds or entered early closeout.

All new HAF obligations must end by September 30, 2026. Standard liquidation continues through January 28, 2027, when final reports are due.

HAF preserved homes, utilities, equity, and financial stability for hundreds of thousands of households, while exposing continuing challenges involving local variation, servicer coordination, title defects, application access, future affordability, data quality, improper payments, and the lack of a permanent national homeowner-emergency program.
