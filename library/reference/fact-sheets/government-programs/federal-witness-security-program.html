Federal Witness Security Program

The federal program commonly known as WITSEC, created to protect, relocate, and support witnesses and authorized family members endangered by testimony against organized crime, drug-trafficking organizations, violent gangs, terrorist groups, corruption networks, and other major criminal enterprises.

What Is the Federal Witness Security Program?

The Federal Witness Security Program protects people whose cooperation with the government creates an extraordinary and continuing danger that ordinary witness assistance, police patrol, protective orders, detention, or short-term relocation cannot adequately address.

Protection can range from secure transportation and courtroom security to permanent relocation under a new identity. The extent of assistance depends on the threat, witness role, family circumstances, custody status, legal obligations, and approved Memorandum of Understanding.

The program does not erase a participant’s criminal convictions, debts, custody obligations, restitution, probation, parole, or responsibility for future crimes.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s organized-crime origins, 1970 authorization, 1984 reform, statutory structure, DOJ and USMS responsibilities, prisoner witnesses, state witnesses, admission standards, threat and public-risk assessments, psychological evaluation, relocation, identity documentation, subsistence, employment, healthcare, court appearances, participant duties, termination, family separation, custody, civil judgments, criminal activity by protected witnesses, victim compensation, terrorism-linked witnesses, funding, secrecy, oversight, effectiveness, criticism, and current status.

It distinguishes WITSEC from ordinary victim-witness services, emergency witness assistance, short-term police protection, protective custody, confidential informant programs, relocation by state prosecutors, and fictional portrayals of witness protection.

Geographic Scope

WITSEC operates nationally through the Department of Justice, the ninety-four federal judicial districts, U.S. Marshals Service offices, federal courts, federal prisons, state and local partners, and carefully selected relocation communities.

The statute defines “State” to include the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and United States territories and possessions.

International coordination can be required when participants are foreign nationals, testimony involves overseas organizations, or another country seeks guidance from USMS witness-security specialists.

Historical Context

Traditional organized-crime groups enforced silence through murder, assault, threats, bribery, intimidation, economic retaliation, and harm to family members.

Prosecutors often needed insiders who understood criminal hierarchies, coded language, meetings, finances, conspiracies, and murders. Those insiders faced risks that local police protection could not contain geographically or over time.

Witness protection emerged as an institutional answer to the Mafia code of silence and later expanded to many other serious offenses.

Organized-Crime Origins

The original program was built principally to support prosecutions of La Cosa Nostra and other traditional organized-crime enterprises.

Insiders could explain criminal structure and connect leaders to acts committed through subordinates, but public testimony made them targets for retaliation.

Joseph Valachi and the Informant Precedent

In 1963, Mafia member Joseph Valachi publicly testified before the Senate concerning La Cosa Nostra’s organization and practices.

His cooperation demonstrated both the evidentiary value of criminal insiders and the extraordinary protection required when an organized-crime member breaks the code of silence.

Valachi’s protection predated the formal statutory program but helped establish its practical rationale.

Gerald Shur

Department of Justice prosecutor Gerald Shur is widely identified as the principal architect and first leader of the modern federal witness-protection system.

Shur and other organized-crime prosecutors urged Congress to create a legal and administrative structure capable of relocating witnesses and supporting new identities.

Organized Crime Control Act of 1970

President Richard Nixon signed Public Law 91-452 on October 15, 1970.

Title V authorized the Attorney General to provide for the security of government witnesses and their families in legal proceedings involving organized criminal activity.

The statute supplied the legal foundation for the program that became operational through USMS in 1971.

Operational Start in 1971

The U.S. Marshals Service established witness security as a divisional program in 1971.

Early planners expected a relatively small number of witnesses, but use and costs expanded rapidly as prosecutors demonstrated the value of protected insider testimony.

Early Protection Model

The early model provided new identities, relocation to communities away from the criminal organization, temporary living allowances, and support toward lawful employment and self-sufficiency.

The program sought not merely to hide a witness temporarily but to create a viable new life.

Expansion Beyond the Mafia

By the late 1970s and early 1980s, protected testimony was increasingly used in narcotics, murder, racketeering, gang, robbery, corruption, and other serious cases.

Later use expanded to domestic and international terrorism, prison organizations, transnational criminal groups, human trafficking, public corruption, and complex violent conspiracies.

GAO’s 1983 Review

GAO found that procedural deficiencies had allowed some relocated witnesses to avoid civil obligations owed to creditors, spouses, and other third parties.

It also concluded that the program lacked adequate information and procedures for comprehensive evaluation.

These concerns contributed to the statutory reforms enacted in 1984.

Witness Security Reform Act of 1984

Congress enacted the Witness Security Reform Act as part of the Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984.

The reform broadened eligibility beyond traditional organized crime, required public-risk assessments, formalized the MOU, addressed probation and parole, created procedures for civil judgments and child custody, authorized victim compensation, and structured cooperation with states and other federal agencies.

Title 18, Chapter 224

Current witness-protection law is principally codified at 18 U.S.C. 3521–3528.

The chapter covers relocation and protection, supervision of probationers and parolees, civil judgments, child custody, victim compensation, cooperation with agencies and states, additional authority of the Attorney General, and the statutory definition of State.

18 U.S.C. 3521

Section 3521 authorizes relocation and other protection for federal or qualifying state witnesses when violent retaliation, obstruction, or a similar state offense is likely.

It authorizes protection for family members and close associates, identity documentation, housing, subsistence, employment assistance, healthcare, transportation, services, and secure program procurement.

It also establishes suitability review, the MOU, termination, and disclosure rules.

18 U.S.C. 3522

Section 3522 permits federal probation officers, with state consent, to supervise protected participants who remain subject to state probation or parole.

A substantial breach of the MOU can provide grounds for revocation of probation or parole.

18 U.S.C. 3523

Section 3523 addresses civil judgments against protected people.

It permits a judgment holder to seek enforcement while preserving security through controlled disclosure, judicial review, and appointment of a guardian empowered to act on the judgment without unnecessarily revealing the participant’s location.

18 U.S.C. 3524

Section 3524 protects the rights of nonrelocated parents and other legal custodians.

The Attorney General cannot relocate a child when another person appears to hold legal custody and must examine custody and visitation orders before relocating a custodial parent.

Legal modification or special procedures may be required when ordinary visitation would compromise security.

18 U.S.C. 3525

Section 3525 establishes authority to compensate victims of crimes committed by protected participants during the protected period when the crime causes or threatens death or serious bodily injury.

Congress authorized annual appropriations for the Victims Compensation Fund, and DOJ must report covered payments.

18 U.S.C. 3526

Section 3526 authorizes cooperation by other federal agencies and state governments and permits reimbursement agreements.

The program depends on secure assistance with identification, records, benefits, supervision, courts, corrections, and lawful state cooperation.

18 U.S.C. 3527

Section 3527 provides additional Attorney General authority connected to protection and secure administration.

It operates as part of the broader chapter rather than creating a separate state or private witness-protection entitlement.

18 U.S.C. 3528

Section 3528 defines “State” for Chapter 224 to include the several states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and United States territories or possessions.

Authority of the Attorney General

The Attorney General holds the statutory power to provide protection, determine suitability, impose conditions, authorize disclosure, terminate protection, cooperate with states, and administer the program.

Much of that authority is delegated to designated senior DOJ officials and OEO.

Office of Enforcement Operations

OEO controls admission to the program and provides Department-wide oversight.

It reviews applications, testimony, threats, criminal history, alternatives, psychological evaluations, family issues, public risk, legal obligations, and recommendations from prosecutors and agencies.

OEO also resolves program conflicts, coordinates sensitive use of participants, approves certain returns or contacts, and makes termination and compensation decisions.

United States Marshals Service

USMS administers physical protection and long-term relocation for community witnesses and authorized family members.

Its responsibilities include preliminary interviews, secure movement, high-threat protection, new-identity documentation, relocation, subsistence, housing, healthcare arrangements, employment assistance, court production, contact management, and continuing security assessment.

USMS Witness Security Division

Witness Security is one of the principal operational divisions of the U.S. Marshals Service.

Personnel operate under strict need-to-know rules and use specialized procedures not publicly described in operational detail.

Federal Bureau of Prisons

BOP protects incarcerated witnesses through specialized custody, separation, transfer, housing, communication, visitation, and institutional-security arrangements.

Prisoner witnesses can later receive USMS relocation services if approved for community protection near release.

Sponsoring Prosecutor

A government attorney ordinarily initiates the application and must establish that the witness is significant and essential to a specific case.

The prosecutor summarizes expected testimony, credibility, cooperation, criminal history, case significance, legal obligations, and why ordinary protection is inadequate.

Sponsoring Investigative Agency

The investigative agency prepares or supports the threat assessment, supplies background information, maintains protection before formal acceptance, and coordinates with the prosecutor, OEO, and USMS.

An agent cannot promise admission, money, identity documents, or permanent protection without OEO authorization.

United States Attorney

The United States Attorney reviews and sponsors applications arising in the district and weighs prosecutorial need, credibility, security, cost, and public risk.

State requests generally must be routed through the appropriate United States Attorney.

Program Eligibility

DOJ policy permits consideration when a person is an essential witness in:

  • organized-crime or racketeering cases;
  • federal drug-trafficking cases;
  • other serious federal felonies involving likely violent retaliation;
  • qualifying state offenses; or
  • certain civil or administrative proceedings creating serious danger.

Significant and Essential Testimony

WITSEC is not ordinarily used merely because a witness is helpful or afraid.

The testimony should be important to the prosecution, unavailable from safer sources, credible enough to use, and sufficiently committed that the government reasonably expects the witness to testify.

Commitment to Testify

DOJ generally expects the prospective participant to testify before a grand jury or otherwise make a meaningful commitment to provide evidence.

This requirement reduces the risk of undertaking expensive relocation for a person who later refuses to cooperate.

Informants

A confidential informant is not automatically eligible.

The informant must also be a bona fide significant and essential witness meeting statutory and policy standards.

Ordinary informant safety remains the responsibility of the agency using the informant unless and until WITSEC accepts the person.

Innocent Victim-Witnesses

Some participants are victims, bystanders, employees, family members, public officials, or other witnesses without criminal involvement.

Their service needs and risk profile can differ substantially from those of cooperating defendants.

Cooperating Defendants

Many participants have criminal histories and provide insider evidence after agreeing to cooperate.

Protection does not create immunity from prosecution unless a separate lawful agreement grants immunity or charging consideration.

Defence counsel can challenge the witness’s motives, benefits, credibility, criminal conduct, and agreements at trial.

State and Local Witnesses

The 1984 reform expressly authorized protection for qualifying state witnesses.

State authorities generally apply through the relevant United States Attorney, agree to federal cooperation, and may be required to reimburse the United States for protection expenses.

State supervision may need to transfer to federal authorities.

Civil and Administrative Proceedings

DOJ policy permits consideration in certain civil or administrative proceedings when testimony creates a serious safety threat.

Such use is exceptional and must still satisfy statutory authority and suitability standards.

Authorized Family Members

Immediate family members can be protected when retaliation risk extends to them or when keeping the family together is necessary for viable relocation.

Inclusion is not automatic for every relative. Each proposed participant affects security, cost, legal obligations, and relocation feasibility.

Close Associates

The Attorney General can protect a person closely associated with the witness when that relationship creates danger.

Examples can include domestic partners, caregivers, or other essential household members, depending on the approved case.

Children in WITSEC

Children may have to change names, schools, records, friendships, relatives, medical providers, and community identity because of an adult witness’s cooperation.

Protection planning must consider custody, visitation, education, healthcare, developmental needs, consent, trauma, and the child’s ability to maintain secrecy.

Prisoner Witnesses

Federal and state prisoners can participate if they meet ordinary criteria.

Incarcerated witnesses are generally protected through BOP custody arrangements rather than immediate community relocation.

A state prisoner must normally transfer to federal custody when the state agrees.

Protective Custody Units

BOP uses restricted institutional arrangements to separate protected prisoner witnesses from people who may harm them or learn sensitive information.

Operational details, locations, movement, communication rules, and other security information are not publicly described.

Polygraph Requirement for Prisoner Candidates

DOJ policy requires incarcerated candidates to undergo a polygraph examination intended to identify plans to harm, expose, or compromise other protected prisoners.

Polygraph results are controversial because scientific reliability and admissibility are limited, but DOJ uses the process as an internal security tool.

Prisoner Release Planning

A full USMS preliminary interview for community relocation is ordinarily delayed until the protected prisoner approaches release.

Officials then assess identity, destination, supervision, employment, family, healthcare, legal obligations, and continuing threat.

Application Process

The sponsoring prosecutor submits a confidential application to OEO.

The application contains identity information, case numbers, expected testimony, cooperation history, threats, criminal record, medical and psychological issues, family participants, debts, custody, immigration, supervision, and public-risk information.

Threat Assessment

The sponsoring agency analyses the organization or person posing danger, capability, intent, history of retaliation, access to the witness, geographic reach, communications, associates, available intelligence, and likely duration of risk.

Fear alone is insufficient, but a completed violent attack is not required before protection can be authorized.

Consideration of Alternatives

OEO must consider whether safer or less disruptive alternatives can secure the testimony.

Alternatives can include short-term protection, detention, separation, sealed proceedings, protective orders, local relocation, remote communication, or testimony from another source.

Risk to the Relocation Community

The Attorney General must assess the danger the witness and adult household members may pose to a new community.

Factors include criminal history, violence, sexual offenses, substance use, psychological condition, supervision, family conduct, credibility, and ability to comply with rules.

Admission must be denied when prosecution need is outweighed by public danger.

Psychological Evaluation

Prospective community witnesses and adult household members generally undergo psychological testing and evaluation before admission.

The evaluation assists with public-risk assessment, adjustment, treatment needs, family dynamics, and ability to live under program restrictions.

Relevant results can create disclosure obligations in the criminal case.

Criminal-History Review

Officials examine arrests, convictions, violence, sexual offenses, supervision, pending charges, warrants, and patterns of conduct under old and known identities.

A criminal record does not automatically disqualify an essential cooperating witness, but it affects conditions, local notification, credibility, and public risk.

Medical and Healthcare Review

Applications identify major medical, dental, mental-health, medication, disability, pregnancy, and substance-use needs of proposed participants.

Relocation planning must prevent dangerous interruptions while preserving identity security.

Debts and Legal Obligations

Before admission, a witness must disclose outstanding judgments, fines, restitution, child support, debts, community service, court orders, and other legal obligations.

Known obligations must be satisfied or addressed through acceptable arrangements.

Child-Custody Documentation

Applicants must provide custody and visitation orders.

The program cannot be used to conceal a child from a lawful custodian or evade family-court authority.

Immigration Status

Noncitizen candidates require lawful coordination with immigration authorities before relocation.

Witness protection itself does not automatically create immigration status, although other legal mechanisms can apply in qualifying cases.

USMS Preliminary Interview

USMS explains the services the program can and cannot provide, security rules, relocation realities, financial expectations, family restrictions, employment duties, and MOU obligations.

The interview also identifies practical or legal obstacles before final approval.

Emergency Protection

OEO can authorize emergency protection when a witness faces imminent danger and the sponsoring agency cannot provide adequate security.

Emergency action can precede completion of the full written assessment and MOU, but required review must be completed as soon as practicable.

Final Approval

OEO makes the final admission determination under delegated Attorney General authority.

The sponsoring agency, United States Attorney, and USMS supply essential input, but none can independently guarantee admission.

Unauthorized Promises

Investigators and trial attorneys cannot promise admission, relocation, financial support, or other WITSEC benefits.

Even a plea agreement cannot bind the program without specific OEO authorization.

Memorandum of Understanding

Before ordinary admission, the witness and participating adult household members sign an MOU with the government.

The document defines authorized services, duties, conditions, legal obligations, contact procedures, protection limits, and grounds for termination.

Duty to Testify and Cooperate

The protected person must provide truthful information and testimony to appropriate officials as required by the cooperation agreement and MOU.

False testimony can result in prosecution, loss of benefits, impeachment, and termination.

Duty to Obey the Law

Participants must not commit crimes.

A new identity does not confer immunity, erase criminal history, or shield new offenses from investigation and prosecution.

Duty to Avoid Detection

Participants must follow security instructions and take reasonable steps to prevent discovery.

Unauthorized contact, social-media use, disclosure of location, return to danger areas, contact with criminal associates, or sharing identity information can endanger an entire household.

Duty to Cooperate with Protectors

Participants must comply with reasonable requests from officials providing protection.

Security cannot succeed when a participant withholds movements, contacts, threats, finances, criminal activity, or changes in household composition.

Agent for Service of Process

The MOU requires designation of another person to receive legal process.

This mechanism helps preserve access to courts without publicly revealing the protected person’s identity and location.

Continuing Contact and Location Reporting

Participants must keep the appropriate program official informed of activities and current location.

Loss of contact can prevent effective protection and interfere with court, supervision, and legal obligations.

Drug and Alcohol Conditions

DOJ can impose random testing, treatment, or counselling when substance-use history or current risk warrants it.

Historical oversight records report mandatory random testing for witnesses with known drug-use histories.

Notification of Local Law Enforcement

DOJ can confidentially notify local law enforcement that a protected person with specified criminal risk is living in the community.

Notification seeks to protect the public while avoiding disclosure of the participant’s former identity or cooperation.

Physical Protection

USMS provides continuous protection in high-threat environments, including trials, hearings, interviews, travel, and other appearances.

The form and intensity of protection are operational matters not disclosed publicly.

Secure Transportation

Protected witnesses may require controlled movement between secure locations, courts, prisons, medical providers, airports, and relocation sites.

Travel planning must avoid predictable routes, public exposure, unauthorized contact, and digital traces.

Courtroom and Trial Security

A witness accepted into WITSEC receives protection when returning to testify in the case for which entry was authorized, including after active participation has ended.

USMS coordinates courthouse access, holding, movement, lodging, identity protection, and threat response with prosecutors and judicial-security personnel.

Return to a Danger Area

Participants ordinarily cannot return freely to former communities or other high-risk locations.

Necessary court appearances, interviews, emergencies, or approved family matters require advance security review and controlled arrangements.

Relocation

Long-term community participants are moved to a location intended to reduce the ability of the threatening organization to find them.

Location selection considers risk, criminal contacts, family, employment, healthcare, climate, cost, language, supervision, and the participant’s ability to blend into the new community.

Secrecy of Relocation Sites

The government does not publicly disclose relocation sites or operational selection methods.

Relocation secrecy protects both participants and uninvolved residents of the receiving community.

New Identities

Witnesses and authorized family members typically receive new identities supported by government documentation.

Identity changes are designed for lawful daily life, not to facilitate evasion of debts, custody orders, prosecution, supervision, or background obligations.

Identity Documentation

The statute authorizes suitable documents needed to establish a protected identity.

Public descriptions identify records such as Social Security documentation and other identity credentials, but specific processes and document chains are security sensitive.

Secure Linkage of Old and New Records

Government systems must preserve lawful access to criminal history, supervision, court, tax, benefit, and identity information without exposing the protected identity.

Historical oversight found that weak linkage could create false “no record” results and endanger receiving communities.

Movement of Household Property

The statute authorizes transportation of household furniture and personal property where approved.

Security can require limits on what is moved, how it is moved, and whether identifiable property creates a traceable link.

Housing Assistance

Participants may receive temporary support for housing while establishing the new household.

The MOU and USMS determine authorized cost, duration, location, and conditions; WITSEC does not guarantee permanent government-paid housing.

Subsistence Assistance

Initial subsistence can cover basic living expenses while the witness transitions to lawful income.

Assistance is intended to be reasonable and temporary rather than maintain the participant’s previous lifestyle.

Medical and Dental Assistance

USMS may arrange or fund necessary medical and dental care during transition according to approved program terms.

Long-term care can be difficult when prior records, insurance, specialists, disability benefits, or rare conditions risk exposing the former identity.

Mental-Health Support

Relocation can involve trauma, grief, guilt, fear, isolation, identity disruption, family conflict, and chronic vigilance.

Psychological counselling can be provided or arranged based on need, but secure access and continuity can be challenging.

Employment Assistance

USMS can provide job training, résumé preparation, referrals, and other support to help the witness become self-sufficient.

Employment must be compatible with the new identity, criminal record, skills, supervision, security, and local labour market.

Expectation of Self-Sufficiency

Participants are expected to seek lawful employment or other legitimate support aggressively.

Refusal of reasonable employment or failure to pursue work can result in discontinuation of subsistence and referral to ordinary public-assistance systems.

Education and Training

Job training and education can be approved when needed for employability and safe integration.

Schools and credentialing institutions may require secure reconstruction or verification of records.

Children’s School Records

Relocated children need school placement, transcripts, disability services, immunization records, and age-appropriate identity documentation.

Records must be accurate enough to preserve educational rights while avoiding disclosure of the former identity.

Integration into a New Community

Participants are expected to become law-abiding and economically stable members of the receiving community.

Success can require learning a new history, avoiding former contacts, changing employment, modifying habits, and managing explanations for missing records or relationships.

Family Separation

Some relatives cannot or will not relocate, and unauthorized contact can expose the protected household.

Separation can produce grief, resentment, loneliness, conflict, and pressure to violate security rules.

Contact with Relatives and Friends

Contact is controlled according to threat and security rules.

Modern communications, location services, social media, photographs, financial transfers, travel data, and shared devices make secret contact more difficult than during the program’s early decades.

Digital Security

Digital footprints can connect old and new identities through facial recognition, data brokers, location history, social networks, breached databases, biometrics, photographs, and consumer records.

Public program sources do not disclose operational countermeasures, but technological change creates a continuing security challenge.

Ubiquitous Technical Surveillance

Cameras, licence-plate readers, commercial data, phone applications, online records, and automated identification can make covert relocation more difficult.

As of 2026, DOJ OIG was separately examining the Department’s broader efforts to mitigate ubiquitous technical surveillance, an issue relevant to witness protection even though the review was not limited to WITSEC.

Termination of Protection

DOJ can terminate protection when a participant substantially breaches the MOU or provides materially false information concerning program obligations or admission circumstances.

Termination does not necessarily eliminate all future courtroom security connected to the original testimony.

Voluntary Departure

A participant can choose to leave active protection, but that decision may end services and increase danger.

Officials should document the decision, remaining threat, continuing court obligations, records, property, and emergency contact procedures.

Protection After Termination

If a former participant faces immediate danger from the original cooperation through no fault of the witness, DOJ can reassess whether renewed protective services are appropriate.

The program is not required to eliminate every risk created by deliberate violation of security rules.

Probation, Parole, and Supervised Release

Protected participants remain subject to lawful supervision.

State supervision can transfer to federal probation with state consent; federal supervision continues through secure procedures.

MOU violations can also constitute supervision violations.

Civil Judgments

Protection does not extinguish judgments for debt, damages, support, or other civil obligations.

Section 3523 provides a controlled method for enforcement that balances the judgment holder’s legal rights against the participant’s safety.

Creditors and Third Parties

Historical weaknesses allowed some witnesses to use secrecy to frustrate creditors and others with lawful claims.

The 1984 reforms require disclosure of obligations and create judicial mechanisms that do not depend on public revelation of the relocation site.

Child Custody and Visitation

WITSEC cannot be used as a private child-concealment system.

DOJ must examine court orders, preserve lawful parental rights where possible, and use litigation or protected procedures when ordinary visitation would compromise security.

Rights of a Nonrelocated Parent

A parent who does not enter protection can seek enforcement or modification of custody and visitation rights.

Courts and DOJ must structure proceedings to protect both due process and witness security.

Victims Compensation Fund

The fund provides a limited federal remedy for victims of serious crimes committed by protected participants.

It reflects congressional recognition that relocating a dangerous cooperating witness can impose risk on innocent people in the new community.

Criminal Activity by Protected Participants

Some participants continue criminal activity after admission.

A protected identity cannot lawfully be used to conceal new crime, and agencies can obtain secure access to relevant records.

New offenses can result in arrest, prosecution, termination, supervision action, and victim compensation.

GAO’s 1984 Arrest Findings

GAO examined participants entering during fiscal years 1979 and 1980 and found that approximately 21.4 percent of the 365 witnesses in its recidivism analysis were arrested within two years.

Arrest is not conviction, and the historical estimate should not be presented as a current recidivism rate.

GAO’s 1984 Prosecution Findings

In 220 case summaries involving protected witnesses entering from June 1979 through May 1980, GAO found that 75 percent of defendants were convicted.

Among identified principal targets, 88 percent were convicted and received a median prison sentence of 11.2 years.

These are historical case results, not a modern universal conviction rate.

Effectiveness

WITSEC’s principal measurable success is enabling testimony while preventing retaliation against compliant participants.

The 2026 USMS fact sheet reports approximately 19,300 protected participants and states that no participant following program guidelines has been harmed or killed.

Secrecy limits independent verification and public analysis of admissions, threats, costs, terminations, quality of life, and long-term outcomes.

Interpreting the Safety Claim

The statement that no guideline-following participant has been harmed or killed is an official USMS claim.

It is narrower than saying no participant or family member has ever suffered crime, self-harm, accidental death, domestic violence, or danger after entering WITSEC.

The claim is specifically tied to compliance with program guidelines and retaliation connected to participation.

Organized-Crime Prosecutions

Protected insider testimony helped prosecutors explain Mafia families, leadership, murders, labour racketeering, extortion, gambling, corruption, and criminal conspiracies.

WITSEC reduced the power of organized crime to rely on silence enforced by fear.

Drug-Trafficking Cases

By the late twentieth century, narcotics cases accounted for a large portion of new admissions.

Cooperating defendants can identify suppliers, transporters, distributors, money launderers, corrupt officials, murders, and organizational leadership.

Gang and Violent-Enterprise Cases

WITSEC supports cases involving violent street gangs, prison gangs, motorcycle gangs, racketeering enterprises, and retaliatory murder.

Gang cases can endanger extended family members and require coordination across prisons, states, and local communities.

Terrorism Cases

DOJ has used WITSEC in major terrorism prosecutions, including cases arising from the 1993 World Trade Center bombing, Oklahoma City bombing, and later terrorism plots.

Terrorism-linked participants create additional watchlisting, travel, immigration, intelligence, and national-security coordination requirements.

2013 Terrorism-Linked Witness Oversight

DOJ OIG identified weaknesses in the handling and monitoring of former known or suspected terrorists admitted to WITSEC.

DOJ responded with enhanced information sharing among USMS, OEO, FBI, the Terrorist Screening Center, the National Joint Terrorism Task Force, the National Security Division, and immigration authorities.

DOJ stated that no terrorism-linked witness had committed an act of terrorism after admission.

Public-Corruption Cases

Insiders in corruption cases can face retaliation, economic destruction, harassment, or violence from officials and criminal associates.

WITSEC can be used when the threat and testimony meet program standards.

Human-Trafficking and Exploitation Cases

Victims and insiders in trafficking organizations can face threats from networks operating across states or countries.

Protection decisions must account for trauma, immigration, language, family, exploitation, and the risk of treating victims primarily as prosecutorial assets.

Witness Credibility

Protected witnesses can receive relocation, money, housing, healthcare, employment help, charging consideration, sentence reductions, or other benefits.

Those benefits can create incentives to cooperate and are relevant to credibility.

Prosecutors must disclose material impeachment evidence, and defence counsel can cross-examine witnesses concerning benefits and motives within security limits.

Brady and Giglio Disclosure

The government must disclose material exculpatory and impeachment information under constitutional and related disclosure rules.

Criminal history, false statements, benefits, cooperation agreements, psychological material affecting credibility, prior testimony, and misconduct can be relevant.

Security permits protective procedures but does not erase the defendant’s fair-trial rights.

Confrontation and Cross-Examination

A defendant generally has the right to confront and cross-examine government witnesses at trial.

Courts can limit disclosure of addresses and sensitive identity information when safety requires it, while preserving sufficient inquiry into bias, perception, credibility, and motive.

Sealed and Protected Records

Courts can seal filings, restrict access, use protective orders, close limited proceedings, redact addresses, and control sensitive discovery.

Secrecy must be no broader than necessary to protect safety and judicial fairness.

Freedom of Information and Privacy

WITSEC files include admission requests, background, funding, movement, identity, family, and security information.

Access is restricted to officials with a need to know, and records can be withheld under privacy, law-enforcement, security, and statutory protections.

Witness Security Files Information System

USMS maintains a protected information system supporting requests, planning, funding, moving, identity, and program administration.

Safeguards include controlled access, physical security, passwords, encryption, and need-to-know limitations.

Why the Program Is Secretive

Publishing participant identities, locations, movement methods, documentation processes, safe sites, communication channels, or operational weaknesses could cause death and undermine prosecutions.

Secrecy is therefore integral rather than incidental, but it also limits transparency, research, accountability, and public knowledge of participant experiences.

Participant Privacy

Protected people retain privacy interests even when they have criminal histories or received government benefits.

Unauthorized disclosure can create physical danger, family harm, stigma, identity theft, and obstruction of justice.

Rights of the Receiving Community

Residents generally are not told that a protected witness has been relocated nearby.

DOJ must balance secrecy against the community’s interest in accurate criminal records, lawful supervision, protection from dangerous participants, and effective investigation of new offenses.

Local Law-Enforcement Access

State or local agencies investigating a protected person for a violent crime or felony can seek relevant information through secure DOJ procedures.

Disclosure decisions are structured to avoid unnecessary exposure of the protected identity and original cooperation.

Domestic Violence Within Protected Households

New identity and location secrecy can complicate reporting, protective orders, custody, service access, and accountability when abuse occurs within a protected family.

WITSEC security cannot supersede the safety and legal rights of an abused partner or child.

Family Member Autonomy

Adult relatives can face relocation because of another person’s cooperation even when they did not participate in the case.

They must understand restrictions, financial realities, family separation, identity changes, and the consequences of refusing or leaving protection.

Child Trauma and Identity

Children may be required to conceal personal history and lose contact with relatives, schools, friends, culture, and community.

Age-appropriate counselling, education support, family stability, and future disclosure planning are important to long-term well-being.

Cultural and Religious Needs

Relocation can separate participants from language communities, tribal lands, religious institutions, cultural practices, and extended family support.

Security planning should consider whether a distinctive cultural identity makes anonymity more difficult or whether relocation creates disproportionate hardship.

Disability and Accessibility

Participants with disabilities may require accessible housing, transportation, assistive technology, medical specialists, educational services, interpreters, and benefit continuity.

Rare conditions or specialized providers can increase traceability and limit safe relocation options.

Race, Cooperation, and Criminal Justice

WITSEC developed during punitive organized-crime and drug-enforcement eras that produced racial and socioeconomic disparities.

Cooperating witnesses can receive benefits unavailable to similarly situated defendants who lack valuable information, creating questions about proportionality, bargaining power, sentence equity, and prosecutorial discretion.

Plea Agreements and Cooperation Benefits

A defendant’s cooperation can influence charging and sentencing through separate prosecutorial and judicial processes.

WITSEC protection is not itself a sentence reduction, and prosecutors cannot promise program admission without OEO approval.

Moral Complexity of Insider Witnesses

The witnesses best able to prove secret criminal enterprises are often people who participated in serious wrongdoing.

Protecting and rewarding them can be necessary to prosecute leaders while appearing unfair to victims, less culpable defendants, and witnesses who cooperate without bargaining leverage.

Popular Myths

Myth: Every threatened witness receives a new identity.
Most witnesses receive other forms of assistance; permanent WITSEC relocation is reserved for qualifying extraordinary threats.
Myth: The government erases a participant’s past.
Legal obligations, criminal history, supervision, custody, and judgments remain enforceable through secure systems.
Myth: Participants receive luxury housing and lifelong income.
Support is generally transitional and aimed at reasonable living needs and self-sufficiency.
Myth: WITSEC is run only by the U.S. Marshals Service.
OEO controls admission and oversight, USMS protects relocated witnesses, and BOP protects prisoner witnesses.
Myth: Any informant can demand admission.
Admission is discretionary and limited to significant, essential, endangered witnesses.
Myth: New crimes are hidden automatically.
Protected participants can be investigated, prosecuted, supervised, terminated, and held financially accountable.

Fiction and Reality

Films and television commonly portray instant admission, unrestricted identity choice, dramatic cash payments, secret rural homes, and permanent disappearance.

Actual admission is document intensive, legally constrained, conditional, disruptive, and focused on safety and subsistence rather than adventure or reinvention.

Distinction from Victim-Witness Assistance

Ordinary victim-witness programs provide information, referrals, court accompaniment, compensation assistance, notices, and support to many victims and witnesses.

WITSEC is a much narrower security program for exceptional retaliation threats.

Distinction from Emergency Witness Assistance

The Emergency Witness Assistance Program can address immediate safety, transportation, shelter, food, clothing, or urgent needs when formal WITSEC is unnecessary or unavailable.

EWAP does not ordinarily provide permanent identity change and long-term relocation.

Distinction from Short-Term Protection

Short-term police or marshal protection can remove a witness temporarily, guard a residence, provide hotel lodging, or secure court attendance.

WITSEC is designed for threats that can persist across time and geography and may require permanent identity reconstruction.

Distinction from Protective Custody

Protective custody separates an incarcerated person from danger within correctional systems.

A prisoner can be in protective custody without formal WITSEC admission, and a WITSEC prisoner can receive broader long-term protection planning.

Distinction from Confidential Informant Programs

Informant programs manage people who provide information during investigations.

WITSEC protects qualifying witnesses whose testimony creates extraordinary danger. The two systems can overlap but are not interchangeable.

Distinction from State Witness Protection

States and local prosecutors operate their own witness-protection and relocation programs with varied authority, duration, and benefits.

Federal WITSEC can accept qualifying state witnesses through statutory cooperation and reimbursement procedures.

Funding Structure

WITSEC uses several DOJ resources rather than one easily interpreted public account.

USMS salaries and expenses fund personnel and operations; the centrally managed Fees and Expenses of Witnesses account funds protection-related witness expenses; BOP bears correctional costs; OEO uses Criminal Division resources; and sponsoring agencies bear pre-admission expenses.

Fiscal Year 2026 USMS Protection Activity

The fiscal year 2026 USMS budget request and current-services table identified 245 positions, 229 full-time equivalents, and $85.103 million for Protection of Witnesses.

This amount concerns USMS salaries and expenses and should not be confused with direct witness subsistence and relocation expenses in the FEW account.

Fiscal Year 2026 Fees and Expenses of Witnesses

The fiscal year 2026 enacted FEW appropriation totalled $301.760 million.

Within that account, $47.628 million was identified for Protection of Witnesses.

The remainder supported ordinary witness fees and expenses, private counsel, foreign counsel, and alternative dispute resolution.

Why the Budget Lines Should Not Be Added Casually

The USMS program-activity amount and FEW protection amount represent different budget structures, cost types, and documents.

Adding them can provide a rough picture of federal resources but not an audited total cost of WITSEC because BOP, OEO, sponsoring agencies, reimbursements, shared services, and prior-year balances can also be involved.

Procurement

WITSEC requires secure purchasing of housing, travel, transportation, documentation, medical services, moving, training, equipment, facilities, and other goods or services.

Ordinary transparency can expose participants or methods, so the statute permits specialized procurement flexibility while retaining accountability requirements.

Current OIG Procurement Audit

As of August 2026, DOJ OIG listed an active audit of USMS and OEO Witness Security Program procurements.

The stated preliminary objective is to assess acquisition practices involving the program.

Because the audit was ongoing, no findings should be inferred before a report is issued.

The 2002 OIG Audit

A redacted OIG audit reviewed admission, termination, program management, data accuracy, and performance reporting.

OIG documented the division of responsibility among OEO, USMS, and BOP and examined whether DOJ could demonstrate the prosecutorial value of protected testimony.

Program Data Limitations

Historical GAO and OIG reports repeatedly identified weaknesses in data collection, outcome tracking, and program evaluation.

Secrecy makes public reporting difficult, but Congress requires credible information to assess funding, public risk, prosecution results, and program viability.

Performance Measurement

Useful measures include:

  • participant safety;
  • successful court appearances;
  • convictions and case outcomes linked to testimony;
  • admission-processing time;
  • termination and rule-violation rates;
  • participant criminal activity;
  • victim-compensation claims;
  • employment and self-sufficiency;
  • family and child outcomes;
  • cost by service category;
  • security breaches;
  • public-risk notifications; and
  • participant well-being.

Secrecy and Oversight

Effective protection requires secrecy, while democratic accountability requires audit, legal review, financial controls, and congressional oversight.

Redacted reports, classified or closed briefings, secure data access, inspectors general, and judicial procedures attempt to reconcile those needs.

International Training and Assistance

USMS witness-security personnel provide guidance and training to foreign governments developing protection systems.

International programs must adapt to local law, corruption risk, identity systems, geography, institutional capacity, and human-rights standards.

Current Status as of August 3, 2026

The Federal Witness Security Program remains active under 18 U.S.C. Chapter 224.

USMS reports approximately 19,300 protected witnesses and authorized family members since 1971 and continues to identify WITSEC as a core operational mission.

OEO remains responsible for admission and oversight, USMS for community relocation and protection, and BOP for protected prisoner witnesses.

Fiscal year 2026 enacted FEW funding includes $47.628 million for Protection of Witnesses, while USMS budget materials identify a separate protection personnel-and-operations activity.

DOJ OIG is conducting an audit of WITSEC procurement practices; its findings were not available as of this fact sheet’s date.

Major Achievements

  • Made insider testimony against secret and violent criminal organizations more feasible.
  • Protected approximately 19,300 participants according to the 2026 USMS report.
  • Maintained a government-reported record of no guideline-following participant being harmed or killed because of participation.
  • Supported major organized-crime, drug, gang, terrorism, murder, and corruption prosecutions.
  • Created long-term relocation and lawful new-identity capacity.
  • Integrated prosecutors, investigators, USMS, BOP, courts, probation, states, and service providers.
  • Formalized public-risk review before relocating dangerous witnesses.
  • Created legal mechanisms for custody, civil judgments, supervision, and victim compensation.
  • Developed specialized expertise shared with governments around the world.
  • Adapted from Mafia prosecutions to transnational and national-security cases.

Limitations and Failures

  • Relocation can impose severe psychological and family disruption.
  • Protected participants can commit new crimes.
  • Historical procedures allowed some witnesses to evade civil and family obligations.
  • New identities can complicate criminal-history checks and local public safety.
  • Benefits to cooperating criminals can appear unfair to victims and noncooperating defendants.
  • Secrecy limits independent evaluation and public accountability.
  • Program data and prosecutorial-outcome tracking have historically been incomplete.
  • Digital surveillance and data brokers make permanent concealment increasingly difficult.
  • Children and nonwitness relatives bear consequences for another person’s cooperation.
  • Employment and self-sufficiency can be difficult under a reconstructed identity.
  • Terrorism-linked participants exposed information-sharing and watchlisting weaknesses.
  • Costs are spread among multiple accounts and agencies, complicating transparent totals.

Major Controversies

Protection of criminals
The government can relocate and support people who committed serious crimes because their testimony is valuable.
Credibility
Financial, legal, and protective benefits can create incentives to testify favourably for the prosecution.
Receiving-community risk
Secrecy can place a participant with a serious criminal history into a community whose residents are not informed.
Family rights
Relocation can disrupt custody, visitation, marriage, culture, and extended family relationships.
Civil obligations
Identity secrecy can obstruct creditors, victims, spouses, and other judgment holders.
Transparency
Operational secrecy protects lives but limits external scrutiny of costs, decisions, and participant outcomes.
Digital viability
Modern commercial and biometric data make traditional identity concealment more difficult.
Equal justice
Cooperators with valuable information can receive benefits unavailable to equally culpable people without bargaining value.

Research should identify the legal period, witness type, case category, responsible component, protection stage, services, participant obligations, threat, public-risk assessment, outcome, and limits imposed by secrecy.

Why the Federal Witness Security Program Matters

WITSEC changes the balance of power between criminal organizations and insiders by making it possible for a witness to testify without remaining indefinitely accessible to the organization.

The program helped federal prosecutors penetrate criminal structures that could not be proved through seized documents, surveillance, or outsider testimony alone.

It also forced the federal government to confront difficult questions: whether dangerous cooperators should receive protection, how to preserve defendants’ confrontation rights, how to protect receiving communities, how to enforce custody and civil judgments, and how much secrecy a democratic justice system can tolerate.

Its modern future depends on maintaining identity security amid pervasive digital surveillance while providing transparent enough oversight to demonstrate safety, fairness, effectiveness, and lawful expenditure.

Summary

The Federal Witness Security Program protects government witnesses and authorized family members whose cooperation creates an extraordinary risk of violent retaliation.

Congress authorized witness security through the Organized Crime Control Act of 1970, and USMS began operating the program in 1971.

The Witness Security Reform Act of 1984 created the modern statutory framework now codified at 18 U.S.C. 3521–3528.

OEO controls admission and program oversight, USMS protects and relocates community witnesses, and BOP protects prisoner witnesses.

Admission requires significant and essential testimony, a credible threat, extensive background review, consideration of alternatives, psychological evaluation in applicable cases, and a finding that prosecution need outweighs danger to the relocation community.

Relocated participants can receive secure transportation, new identity documents, housing, subsistence, medical assistance, employment help, and protected court appearances.

Participants must sign an MOU, testify truthfully, obey the law, avoid detection, satisfy legal obligations, disclose custody and supervision duties, cooperate with protectors, and maintain contact.

Protection does not erase criminal history, civil judgments, child custody, restitution, probation, parole, or responsibility for new crimes.

USMS reports approximately 19,300 participants protected since 1971 and states that no participant following program guidelines has been harmed or killed because of participation.

Historical GAO research found strong prosecution results in reviewed cases but also documented participant arrests and weaknesses concerning civil obligations, custody, and program evaluation.

Fiscal year 2026 enacted FEW funding contains $47.628 million for Protection of Witnesses. A separate USMS budget activity supports witness-security personnel and operations.

As of August 3, 2026, WITSEC remains active, and DOJ OIG is auditing program procurement practices.
