Federal Surplus Property 1033 Program

The federal law-enforcement support program that transfers excess military property—including general supplies, emergency-response equipment, firearms, aircraft, and demilitarized tactical vehicles—to qualifying federal, state, territorial, tribal, and local law-enforcement agencies.

What Is the Federal Surplus Property 1033 Program?

The 1033 Program transfers excess military personal property to eligible governmental law-enforcement agencies.

Property can range from ordinary office equipment, tools, medical supplies, generators, and vehicles to firearms, night-vision devices, aircraft, and demilitarized tactical vehicles.

The program does not provide unrestricted grants for agencies to purchase any equipment they choose. Agencies request items that the military has declared excess and that the department has made available through its reutilization, transfer, and donation process.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the 1033 Program’s origins, statutory authority, federal and state administration, agency eligibility, property categories, request and approval process, ownership and accountability, training, local-government authorization, prohibited equipment, compliance, transparency, major reforms, current 2026 policies, benefits, criticisms, and related federal property programs.

It does not provide operational advice concerning weapons, tactical deployment, or equipment use.

Geographic Scope

The program operates through participating states, territories, federal agencies, and tribal law-enforcement organizations.

State and local participation depends on a governor-appointed state coordinator, a federal memorandum of agreement, and state-level agreements with participating agencies.

State law or local ordinance can impose requirements beyond the federal minimum.

Historical Context

The program emerged during the intensification of the federal War on Drugs and expanded as Congress sought to reuse military property for civilian law enforcement rather than destroy, store, or sell it.

Its later history became connected to counterterrorism after September 11, border security, disaster response, SWAT operations, civil unrest, police reform, and national debate over the militarization of civilian policing.

War on Drugs Origins

The original transfer authority was closely tied to counterdrug enforcement.

During the 1980s, Congress expanded military support for civilian anti-drug operations while preserving statutory limits on direct military participation in ordinary law enforcement.

Excess-property transfers offered material support without assigning military personnel to make arrests or conduct routine police operations.

Section 1208 Authority

Section 1208 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Years 1990 and 1991 authorized the Secretary of Defense to transfer excess personal property to federal and state agencies for law-enforcement purposes.

The authority was temporary and was extended several times through September 30, 1997.

Program transfers began in 1990, which DLA treats as the start of the modern program.

Section 1033 of the Fiscal Year 1997 NDAA

Section 1033 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1997 replaced the temporary Section 1208 authority with permanent statutory authority.

The section number produced the enduring name “1033 Program.”

The authority was codified as 10 U.S.C. 2576a.

Codification in Title 10

The current statute is located in Chapter 153 of Title 10 of the United States Code.

It authorizes transfer of excess personal property that the Secretary determines is suitable for law-enforcement activities and excess to military needs.

The statute expressly permits small arms and ammunition, subject to statutory and departmental restrictions.

Statutory Purposes

Property must serve bona fide law-enforcement activities.

Current law expressly identifies:

  • counterdrug activity;
  • counterterrorism;
  • disaster-related emergency preparedness; and
  • border security.

These examples do not make every requested item automatically suitable or justified.

Department of War and Statutory Terminology

The department and DLA used the Department of War designation in 2026 following a September 2025 executive action.

The statutory language of 10 U.S.C. 2576a still refers to the Secretary and Department of Defense.

Historical sources likewise use Department of Defense, or DOD, and should not be retroactively rewritten when quoting or describing earlier periods.

Defense Logistics Agency

The Defense Logistics Agency manages military supply, disposition, reutilization, transfer, donation, recycling, and sale processes.

DLA Disposition Services identifies excess property and makes approved items available through online systems and disposition sites.

Law Enforcement Support Office

The Law Enforcement Support Office manages the 1033 Program from DLA Disposition Services headquarters in Battle Creek, Michigan.

LESO validates participants, reviews requests, tracks accountable property, coordinates compliance reviews, publishes public data, manages returns and transfers, and works with state coordinators and federal agencies.

Position Within the Federal Disposal Process

Military organizations first identify property they no longer require.

Property enters a reutilization, transfer, and donation process in which military reuse and other federal or special-program claims can receive priority according to current policy.

Items not reused or transferred can later be donated, sold, recycled, demilitarized, or destroyed.

Eligible Law-Enforcement Agency

DLA defines an eligible law-enforcement agency as a governmental agency whose primary function is enforcement of federal, state, territorial, tribal, or local law and whose compensated officers possess arrest and apprehension powers.

An agency must have at least one full-time compensated officer.

Organizations That Do Not Qualify

Private security companies, volunteer groups without compensated arrest officers, nongovernmental organizations, and agencies whose primary mission is not law enforcement generally do not qualify.

Fire departments and emergency-management agencies use different surplus-property authorities unless they possess a separately eligible law-enforcement component.

Federal Law-Enforcement Agencies

Federal law-enforcement agencies can participate through designated federal coordinators.

Federal acquisitions are reported separately in DLA public information and do not use the ordinary governor-appointed state coordinator structure.

State and Local Law-Enforcement Agencies

State police, highway patrols, sheriff’s offices, municipal police, corrections or investigative agencies with qualifying arrest powers, and other eligible governmental agencies may participate.

State coordinator approval is required before a request reaches LESO.

Tribal Law-Enforcement Agencies

DLA interprets the statutory authority to include eligible tribal law-enforcement agencies.

Tribal participants apply through the federal-coordinator process and must satisfy law-enforcement, accountability, training, and controlled-property requirements.

Governor-Appointed State Coordinator

A participating state’s governor appoints a state coordinator in writing.

The coordinator serves as the principal intermediary among DLA, state government, and participating local agencies.

Responsibilities include agency approval, request review, accountability, training communication, compliance monitoring, violation reporting, transfer coordination, and annual certification.

Federal-State Memorandum of Agreement

Each participating state signs a memorandum of agreement with LESO.

The agreement establishes responsibilities concerning eligibility, requests, controlled property, inventory, training, compliance, suspension, termination, reporting, and return.

State Plan of Operation

A State Plan of Operation is executed between the state coordinator and a participating law-enforcement agency.

It mirrors federal requirements and can impose additional state restrictions.

State plans can address local approval, tactical equipment, public reporting, weapons, vehicle use, disposal, and disciplinary consequences.

Local Governing-Body Authorization

Agencies requesting controlled property must obtain authorization from the relevant local governing body or authority.

Depending on governmental structure, the approving body can be a city council, county commission, mayor, board, or another authorized institution.

State law can require public hearings, ordinances, annual reports, or separate approval for specified equipment.

Publicly Available Controlled-Property Protocols

A recipient must certify annually that it has publicly available protocols covering:

  • appropriate use of controlled property;
  • supervision of use;
  • evaluation of effectiveness;
  • auditing; and
  • accountability.

Application for Participation

A state or local agency begins with the state coordinator and submits the standardized participation application and supporting information.

The coordinator confirms eligibility and execution of the State Plan of Operation before forwarding the application to LESO.

Federal and tribal agencies use the appropriate federal coordinator.

Department Activity Address Code

Approved participants receive a Department of Defense Activity Address Code, commonly called a DODAAC.

The code supports identification, requisition, shipment, billing, location, and property-accountability functions.

Online Property Systems

Participating agencies use DLA web applications to search available excess property and submit requests.

Access does not guarantee an award; the state coordinator and LESO must approve requests.

Property Request and Justification

Every request must explain the bona fide law-enforcement use of the property.

Review can consider:

  • agency mission;
  • number of officers;
  • geographic responsibility;
  • operational need;
  • existing equipment;
  • requested quantity;
  • training capacity;
  • maintenance capacity;
  • storage and security; and
  • state and local policy.

Approval Process

State and local requests ordinarily pass through two substantive reviews.

  1. The state coordinator decides whether the agency and request satisfy state and federal requirements.
  2. LESO reviews the property type, quantity, justification, availability, status, and federal policy.

Either level can deny or modify a request.

Statutory Transfer Preference

Current law gives preference to applications stating that property will be used for counterdrug, counterterrorism, disaster-related emergency preparedness, or border-security activities.

Requests for disaster-response vehicles such as high-water rescue vehicles receive the highest statutory preference.

2025 Reutilization Priority Change

In August 2025, DLA changed policy to prioritise law-enforcement requisitions above other Department of War special programs after military reuse needs are satisfied.

DLA reported that the change increased the success rate of LESO property requests.

Transfer Without Charge

The federal government can transfer property without charging the recipient the original purchase price or current market value.

“Free equipment” is an incomplete description because agencies remain responsible for transportation and continuing costs.

As-Is, Where-Is Condition

Recipients accept property in its existing condition and location.

The government does not promise that an item is roadworthy, immediately operational, complete, or economical to repair.

Agencies should inspect property and estimate total ownership cost before requesting it.

Recipient Costs

Post-transfer costs can include:

  • shipping and pickup;
  • storage;
  • repair and refurbishment;
  • fuel;
  • maintenance;
  • replacement parts;
  • insurance;
  • registration;
  • training;
  • secure storage;
  • inspection;
  • return transportation; and
  • demilitarization or disposal coordination.

Original Acquisition Value

DLA reports property value using the price paid when the item was originally purchased.

Original acquisition value does not account for age, depreciation, condition, missing parts, repair cost, or current resale value.

Historical claims concerning billions of dollars in transferred equipment should therefore be described as original acquisition value.

Controlled Property

Controlled property consists of items assigned specified demilitarization codes or otherwise placed under continuing federal accountability.

Examples include small arms, selected tactical vehicles, aircraft, and night-vision equipment.

Controlled property traditionally remained federal property transferred conditionally rather than donated outright.

Statutory Definition of Controlled Property

Section 2576a defines controlled property by reference to demilitarization codes B, C, D, E, G, or Q under the Defense Materiel Disposition Manual or a successor document.

The code reflects disposal and security requirements rather than a general judgement that every controlled item is a weapon.

Non-Controlled or General Property

General property includes commercial items DLA could otherwise sell to the public.

Examples include office equipment, first-aid supplies, hand tools, computers, cameras, generators, sleeping bags, maintenance equipment, and many ordinary vehicles.

After receipt acknowledgement, the item ordinarily becomes the agency’s property and leaves the LESO controlled inventory.

Immediate Title Transfer for General Property

In October 2025, DLA began immediate title transfer for non-controlled, commercially available property, except specified Humvee models.

Earlier policy had retained federal title and inventory responsibility for some general property for a year.

DLA described the change as a reduction in paperwork for agencies and state coordinators.

2026 Small-Arms Title-Transfer Pilot

In March and April 2026, DLA transferred title to selected small arms held by Michigan law-enforcement agencies as part of a policy test.

DLA reported approximately 40,000 small arms on its LESO inventory in participating agency armouries.

The policy was in phased implementation, so general program guidance still described most controlled small arms as federally owned and accountable.

Small Arms

Federal law expressly allows small arms, subject to department policy, agency justification, training, security, inventory, and transfer restrictions.

Common transferred weapons have included pistols and rifles.

Small arms represent a small share of total transferred items but receive substantial public and compliance attention.

Ammunition

The program can transfer excess small-arms ammunition under statutory authority and department policy.

DLA states that current small-arms ammunition transfers under .50 calibre are for law-enforcement training only.

Ammunition allocation, shipment, storage, recordkeeping, and use must comply with federal, state, local, and agency requirements.

Tactical Vehicles

Agencies can request demilitarized High Mobility Multipurpose Wheeled Vehicles, mine-resistant ambush-protected vehicles, and other approved tactical rolling stock.

Controlled tactical vehicles generally remain federally accountable and do not receive an ordinary federal certificate of title.

DLA can issue a certificate supporting state registration when authorised.

Armoured Vehicles

Armoured-vehicle requests require specialized justification and documentation.

DLA considers availability, request date, High Intensity Drug Trafficking Area status, geographic responsibility, and fair distribution.

Weapon systems and military-specific technology are removed or disabled before transfer when required.

Aircraft

Approved fixed-wing aircraft and helicopters can be transferred for law-enforcement purposes.

Recipients must satisfy aviation, maintenance, registration, pilot, insurance, parts, storage, and federal accountability requirements.

Weaponized aircraft are not available.

Night-Vision Equipment

Night-vision devices can be controlled property because of military sensitivity and demilitarization requirements.

Agencies must maintain secure storage, inventory, authorized use, training, and loss reporting.

Communications Equipment

Radios, antennas, computers, and communications components can be transferred when releasable.

Classified, encrypted, military-specific, or incompatible components may be removed before transfer.

Agencies remain responsible for licensing, cybersecurity, interoperability, repair, and network compatibility.

Medical and Rescue Equipment

The program can provide first-aid supplies, stretchers, medical equipment, generators, shelters, rescue tools, protective items, and disaster-response vehicles.

DLA and participating agencies cite emergency response as a major non-tactical use of the program.

Office and Industrial Property

Most property requests concern ordinary non-controlled items.

Office furniture, computers, cameras, forklifts, tools, storage systems, generators, and maintenance equipment can reduce local procurement cost.

The visibility of armoured vehicles and firearms can obscure the numerical dominance of general property.

Statutory Prohibited Property

Section 2576a prohibits transfer to tribal, state, or local law-enforcement agencies of:

  1. bayonets;
  2. grenades other than stun and flash-bang grenades;
  3. weaponized tracked combat vehicles; and
  4. weaponized drones.

Department Policy Prohibitions

Department policy excludes property beyond the four statutory categories.

DLA identifies 133 Federal Supply Classes as unsuitable for law enforcement and lists additional prohibited items including inherently weaponized aircraft, vessels, or vehicles; specified crew-served or large-calibre weapons and ammunition; explosives and pyrotechnics; military uniforms; body armour; and Kevlar helmets.

Policy restrictions can change without amendment of the statute.

Demilitarization

Demilitarization removes or destroys military capabilities, sensitive technology, weapon mounts, communications systems, armour components, or other features before release or final disposal.

Demilitarized does not necessarily mean that a vehicle loses its protective body or rugged appearance.

Military Appearance and Source

Not every military-looking item used by police came from the 1033 Program.

Agencies can acquire similar equipment through federal grants, asset-forfeiture funds, state programs, commercial purchase, donations, or other surplus-property authorities.

Equipment origin should be verified through inventory records rather than inferred from appearance.

Annual Controlled-Property Training

Each recipient must certify annually that relevant personnel receive training on maintenance, sustainment, and appropriate use of controlled property.

Current statutory training must include respect for constitutional rights and de-escalation of force.

Maintenance and Sustainment Training

Agencies must train personnel to inspect, store, maintain, operate, and account for controlled equipment safely.

Military equipment can create specialised maintenance burdens that exceed ordinary municipal fleet or armoury capacity.

Use Policies

Public protocols should identify authorised missions, command approval, operator qualifications, deployment documentation, prohibited uses, post-deployment review, and complaint procedures.

Local or state law can require more detailed policies than federal law.

Constitutional Rights

Training and deployment remain subject to the First, Fourth, Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments and other applicable constitutional law.

Receipt of federal equipment does not expand an agency’s arrest, search, seizure, surveillance, or use-of-force authority.

De-Escalation

Congress added explicit de-escalation language to the training certification in the Fiscal Year 2021 National Defense Authorization Act.

De-escalation requirements coexist with state standards, agency policy, accreditation, and case law.

Annual Inventory

Participating states and territories must conduct a certified one-hundred-percent inventory of federally accountable controlled property each fiscal year.

Agencies verify serial numbers, location, condition, assigned unit, and continued need.

State Coordinator Compliance Reviews

Each state must conduct annual compliance reviews covering at least 8 percent of participating agencies that possess program property.

Reviews examine records, physical inventory, security, training, policies, use, transfer, and reporting.

Federal Program Compliance Reviews

LESO conducts federal-level compliance reviews of participating states on a biennial cycle.

Review teams inspect selected agencies and compare physical property with federal and state records.

Lost, Stolen, or Missing Property

Agencies must promptly report loss, theft, destruction, or unexplained inventory discrepancies.

Small arms and sensitive equipment can trigger law-enforcement investigation, federal notification, suspension, recovery, and additional controls.

Transfer Between Participating Agencies

An agency cannot independently give controlled property to another department.

Retransfer requires state-coordinator and LESO approval and updated accountability records.

Return of Controlled Property

Controlled property that is no longer needed, authorised, serviceable, or justified must be returned or otherwise disposed of according to LESO instructions.

The recipient ordinarily bears transportation and preparation costs.

Disposal of General Property

After title passes, non-controlled property is generally disposed of under state and local laws governing public property.

Hazardous, export-controlled, restricted, or misclassified property can remain subject to additional federal requirements.

Restricted Status

LESO or a state coordinator can restrict an agency or state from receiving a particular item, commodity, or category of controlled property.

Restriction is used for isolated issues, active compliance concerns, allocation limits, or federal law-enforcement oversight conditions.

Suspension

Suspension temporarily prohibits a state or agency from requesting or receiving additional property.

Causes can include overdue inventory, missing property, inaccurate records, unauthorised transfer, failure to cooperate with review, or misuse.

Termination

Termination removes a state or agency from the program.

The terminated participant must return or transfer all controlled property as directed, generally at its own expense.

Department of Justice Coordination

LESO coordinates with the Department of Justice to validate agencies and identify participants under federal investigation, consent decrees, or other oversight.

DOJ receives notice concerning enrollment, suspension, termination, and selected allocations including weapons, tactical vehicles, and aircraft.

Consent Decrees and Federal Investigations

An active consent decree or federal investigation does not automatically produce one uniform outcome.

LESO can impose restrictions, request additional information, coordinate with DOJ, or deny controlled property according to program and department policy.

Executive policy concerning federal consent decrees changed substantially in 2025.

Publicly Accessible Website

Federal law requires a public website providing information on controlled property and recipients.

Required information includes current inventory and publicly releasable information concerning requests, transfers, denials, repossessions, and recipient reports.

Quarterly Public Data

DLA publishes spreadsheets identifying controlled and selected non-controlled property by state and agency.

The most recent inventory available on August 2, 2026 was dated June 30, 2026.

DLA also publishes quarterly shipment, cancellation, and federal-agency acquisition files.

Limitations of Public Data

Controlled property remains visible because it stays on federal accountability records.

General property is ordinarily removed from the database after title and receipt processing, even though the agency may continue using it.

A current inventory spreadsheet therefore does not represent every item transferred since 1990.

GAO’s 2017 Undercover-Test Findings

In 2017, the Government Accountability Office created a fictitious federal law-enforcement agency and obtained controlled equipment through the program.

GAO concluded that DLA had not adequately verified the identity and eligibility of the applicant before approving transfers.

The report prompted stronger validation, documentation, site review, and program controls.

Agency Identity Verification

LESO now uses federal and state information, law-enforcement directories, official contact methods, DODAAC controls, and DOJ coordination to validate applicants.

Verification must establish that an agency is real, governmental, eligible, and represented by authorised officials.

Ferguson and the 2014 National Debate

Police deployment of armoured vehicles, rifles, camouflage, and tactical formations during protests in Ferguson, Missouri, brought the 1033 Program into national public debate.

Images of heavily equipped police led to calls for restrictions, local oversight, public inventories, training, and broader police reform.

Not every item used in Ferguson was necessarily obtained through 1033, but the event became the defining symbol of the program’s militarization controversy.

Executive Order 13688 and the 2015 Working Group

President Barack Obama issued Executive Order 13688 in January 2015 to improve federal coordination and oversight of law-enforcement equipment acquisition.

The Law Enforcement Equipment Working Group established a prohibited-equipment list, a controlled-equipment list, local approval, use policies, training, incident reporting, and transfer safeguards across several federal programs.

Fiscal Year 2016 NDAA Reforms

Congress amended 10 U.S.C. 2576a in 2015.

The amendments placed local governing-body authorization, publicly available protocols, annual training, and public-inventory requirements into statute.

These provisions remained law after later executive orders changed administrative restrictions.

Executive Order 13809

President Donald Trump issued Executive Order 13809 in August 2017.

The order revoked Executive Order 13688 and directed restoration of access to equipment restricted under the 2015 executive policy.

Statutory controls enacted by Congress remained in force.

Fiscal Year 2021 NDAA Reforms

Congress added disaster-related emergency preparedness to the listed law-enforcement purposes and transfer preferences.

It gave highest preference to requests for disaster vehicles such as high-water rescue vehicles.

Congress also added constitutional-rights and de-escalation language to annual training and placed four prohibited property categories in the statute.

Executive Order 14074

President Joseph Biden issued Executive Order 14074 on May 25, 2022.

The order restricted federal transfer or purchase support for specified military-style equipment and re-established interagency accountability requirements.

It operated alongside the statutory 1033 requirements until January 2025.

Revocation of Executive Order 14074

President Donald Trump revoked Executive Order 14074 on January 20, 2025.

Revocation ended the executive-order restrictions but did not repeal 10 U.S.C. 2576a, the four statutory prohibitions, local approval, public protocols, annual training, or public-inventory requirements.

Executive Order 14288

Executive Order 14288, signed April 28, 2025, directed the Attorney General and the Secretary of Defense to increase provision of excess military and national-security assets to state and local law enforcement.

It also directed review of how military assets, training, nonlethal capabilities, and personnel could be used to prevent crime.

DLA’s 2025–2026 changes were presented as implementation of this policy direction.

2025–2026 Program Policy Changes

DLA reported several current reforms:

  • higher priority for LESO requisitions during the federal reuse cycle;
  • immediate title transfer for most non-controlled commercial property;
  • pilot title transfers for selected small arms;
  • reduced continuing inventory burden for property no longer requiring federal title;
  • greater emphasis on participant access and customer education; and
  • continued biennial federal compliance reviews.

State and Local Controlled-Equipment Laws

Several states and local governments regulate police acquisition or use of military equipment beyond federal requirements.

Measures can require ordinances, public hearings, use policies, annual inventories, deployment reports, budget approval, or bans on selected property.

Federal program approval does not preempt generally applicable state or local restrictions unless federal law expressly controls.

Claimed Public-Safety Benefits

Supporters argue that the program:

  • reuses property already purchased by taxpayers;
  • equips small or rural agencies that cannot afford commercial alternatives;
  • supports disaster rescue and emergency logistics;
  • provides protective vehicles for active-shooter or barricade incidents;
  • supports search, surveillance, aviation, medical, and communications missions;
  • improves interoperability; and
  • reduces federal storage and disposal cost.

Disaster and Emergency Response

Agencies have used transferred high-water vehicles, generators, aircraft, trucks, shelters, and rescue equipment during floods, storms, snow emergencies, wildfire evacuations, and other disasters.

Current law expressly recognizes disaster-related emergency preparedness.

Counterdrug Use

Counterdrug enforcement was the program’s original policy focus and remains a preferred statutory purpose.

Equipment can support surveillance, transportation, communications, interdiction, rural operations, and tactical warrant service.

Critics argue that the drug-war origin encouraged aggressive equipment acquisition and tactical policing.

Counterterrorism Use

Counterterrorism became a prominent justification after the 1990s and September 11 attacks.

Equipment can support critical-infrastructure protection, bomb response, aviation, mass-casualty preparedness, and coordinated tactical response.

Border-Security Use

Border-security activity is an express statutory preference.

Eligible agencies can request vehicles, surveillance, communications, and support equipment when the request is justified and the item is available and releasable.

SWAT and High-Risk Operations

Controlled equipment is frequently justified for hostage rescue, active shooters, barricaded suspects, high-risk warrants, armed confrontations, and officer or civilian rescue.

Critics question whether equipment availability increases tactical deployment in situations that could be handled through less militarized methods.

Protests and Civil Disturbances

Use of military-origin equipment during protests raises First Amendment, proportionality, de-escalation, surveillance, and public-trust concerns.

Federal transfer does not authorize suppression of protected speech or assembly.

Public policies should distinguish protective rescue use from intimidation or indiscriminate crowd control.

Police Militarization

“Police militarization” refers to adoption of military equipment, organization, language, tactics, appearance, and operational culture by civilian police.

The 1033 Program is one source of military-origin equipment but not the sole cause of militarized policing.

Research debates whether transferred equipment changes police behaviour, increases tactical deployment, improves safety, or primarily reflects pre-existing agency practices.

Community Trust

Highly visible tactical equipment can reassure some residents during dangerous incidents and alarm others during routine policing or public demonstrations.

Advance public approval, transparent policies, deployment reporting, and credible after-action review can affect legitimacy.

Race and Unequal Policing

The program developed from a drug-war system that disproportionately affected Black and Latino communities.

Critics connect military equipment with racialized enforcement, high-risk drug raids, protest response, and aggressive policing in marginalised neighbourhoods.

Equipment accountability should include examination of where, why, and against whom controlled property is deployed.

Civil-Rights Oversight

Federal property agreements do not replace civil-rights statutes, constitutional remedies, DOJ pattern-or-practice authority, state oversight, civilian review, or judicial enforcement.

An agency’s compliance with LESO inventory rules does not establish that every deployment was lawful or appropriate.

Officer Safety

Supporters emphasise that armour, rescue vehicles, night vision, and specialized equipment can protect officers and civilians during shootings, disasters, or hazardous operations.

Safety claims should be evaluated against maintenance, training, tactical policy, deployment frequency, and alternative equipment.

Financial Cost to Local Agencies

The lack of an acquisition price can make equipment appear inexpensive.

Fuel, repair, tyres, parts, secure storage, insurance, training, and operator certification can create substantial continuing cost.

Older military platforms can be difficult to maintain after parts and technical support become scarce.

Opportunity Cost

Funds used to maintain transferred equipment cannot be used for personnel, training, crisis response, investigations, community services, or other equipment.

Local approval should consider full life-cycle cost rather than acquisition price alone.

Evaluating Effectiveness

A publicly available protocol should define expected outcomes before acquisition.

Evaluation can examine:

  • number and type of deployments;
  • rescues or emergencies supported;
  • injuries prevented or caused;
  • complaints and civil claims;
  • maintenance and downtime;
  • training completion;
  • cost per deployment;
  • availability of less militarized alternatives;
  • community impact; and
  • whether the original justification remains valid.

Other Federal Equipment Sources

Law-enforcement agencies can obtain equipment through:

  • Department of Homeland Security grants;
  • Department of Justice assistance grants;
  • federal and state asset-forfeiture programs;
  • General Services Administration surplus donation;
  • federal purchasing programs;
  • state surplus programs;
  • commercial purchase; and
  • local donation or interagency transfer.

Distinction from the GSA Surplus Property Program

The General Services Administration Federal Surplus Personal Property Donation Program transfers a broad range of federal surplus property through State Agencies for Surplus Property to eligible public and nonprofit recipients.

The 1033 Program is a separate Title 10 authority specifically for excess military property and law-enforcement recipients.

Distinction from 10 U.S.C. 2576

Section 2576 authorizes sale of surplus military equipment to state and local law-enforcement, firefighting, homeland-security, and emergency-management agencies.

Section 2576a authorizes transfer of excess personal property for law-enforcement activities and is the legal basis of the 1033 Program.

Distinction from Firefighter Property Transfers

Section 2576b and related Forest Service programs support excess-property transfers to firefighting agencies.

Firefighter property programs have separate eligibility, ownership, inventory, and disposal rules.

How the 1033 Program Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the item, quantity, acquisition date, original acquisition value, current title status, controlling policy, agency, intended use, actual use, and disposition.

Important distinctions include:

  • excess property versus surplus property;
  • Section 1208 versus Section 1033;
  • controlled versus non-controlled property;
  • original acquisition value versus current value;
  • federal title versus local title;
  • statutory prohibitions versus department policy restrictions;
  • 1033 equipment versus commercially purchased military-style equipment;
  • current inventory versus cumulative historical transfers;
  • disaster use versus tactical policing;
  • federal approval versus state and local authorization; and
  • enacted law versus executive policy.

Why the Federal Surplus Property 1033 Program Matters

The 1033 Program sits at the intersection of military logistics, federalism, policing, emergency response, civil rights, and public finance.

It allows communities to reuse equipment already purchased by the federal government, including ordinary tools and rescue property that many agencies could not otherwise afford.

It also places military-origin weapons and tactical platforms in civilian institutions whose mission, legal authority, training, culture, and relationship with the public differ from those of the armed forces.

The program’s significance cannot be measured solely by the number or original price of transferred items. Meaningful evaluation requires examining local approval, purpose, actual deployment, maintenance cost, constitutional compliance, community impact, and whether less militarized alternatives would achieve the same public-safety objective.

Summary

The Federal Surplus Property 1033 Program transfers excess military personal property to qualifying federal, state, territorial, local, and tribal law-enforcement agencies.

Congress first authorized temporary transfers through Section 1208 of the fiscal years 1990–1991 defense authorization. Section 1033 of the fiscal year 1997 authorization created permanent authority, now codified at 10 U.S.C. 2576a.

DLA’s Law Enforcement Support Office administers the program. State and local agencies participate through governor-appointed state coordinators, federal-state memoranda, State Plans of Operation, local governing-body approval, public controlled-property policies, and federal request review.

Property is accepted as-is and can be transferred without an acquisition charge, but recipients bear transportation, repair, maintenance, storage, training, registration, and return costs.

Controlled property traditionally remains federally accountable, while non-controlled commercial property generally passes into local ownership. DLA began broader title-transfer reforms during 2025–2026, including a pilot involving selected small arms.

Federal law prohibits bayonets, most grenades, weaponized tracked combat vehicles, and weaponized drones. Department policy prohibits additional property and 133 Federal Supply Classes.

The program requires annual inventories, annual controlled-property training, constitutional-rights and de-escalation instruction, state and federal compliance reviews, public protocols, and public inventory data.

Approximately 6,500 agencies were participating in July 2026. DLA reports approximately $7.6 billion in original acquisition value transferred since 1990, with small arms and tactical vehicles forming small fractions of total transfers.

The program remains controversial because ordinary rescue and support equipment is distributed through the same system as firearms, aircraft, and armoured vehicles. Supporters emphasize reuse, emergency response, and officer safety; critics emphasize militarization, racial inequality, public protest, local cost, and weak democratic oversight.

As of August 2, 2026, the program remained active under 10 U.S.C. 2576a and Executive Order 14288, with DLA implementing expanded access and reduced administrative burdens.
