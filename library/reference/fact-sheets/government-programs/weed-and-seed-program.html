Weed and Seed Program

The federal place-based initiative launched in 1991 to combine concentrated law enforcement with community policing, prevention, treatment, resident participation, social services, economic development, and neighborhood restoration in targeted high-crime communities.

What Was the Weed and Seed Program?

Operation Weed and Seed was a Department of Justice strategy for reducing crime and improving quality of life in selected high-crime neighborhoods.

The initiative sought to concentrate enforcement, prosecution, supervision, prevention, treatment, social services, economic development, housing improvement, and community organizing within a small target area.

It was more accurately described as a strategy than as one uniform program because each site created a local plan, selected interventions, assembled partners, and leveraged resources according to local conditions.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the origins, federal administration, pilot sites, program model, site-recognition process, steering committees, Safe Havens, grants, enforcement activities, social services, neighborhood restoration, community participation, evaluations, management problems, racial and civil-rights concerns, funding decline, phaseout, successor initiatives, and current inactive status.

It distinguishes the national Weed and Seed initiative from unrelated local projects that later used the same phrase and from cannabis policy.

Geographic Scope

Weed and Seed operated in urban, suburban, rural, tribal, and territorial communities across the United States.

Most sites focused on one or more compact neighborhoods rather than an entire city or county.

A jurisdiction could contain multiple Weed and Seed sites, each with separate boundaries, priorities, partners, and strategic plans.

Historical Context

The program emerged during the crack-cocaine era, the War on Drugs, high violent-crime rates, gang conflict, open-air drug markets, urban disinvestment, and public concern about neighborhood disorder.

Federal policy increasingly combined targeted enforcement with community policing and place-based revitalization.

Weed and Seed attempted to prevent enforcement from becoming an isolated crackdown by pairing it with services and neighborhood investment.

War on Drugs Context

The early “weeding” model emphasized drug trafficking, violent offenders, gang activity, weapons, and street-level markets.

Federal, state, and local agencies used investigations, arrests, prosecution, probation, parole, nuisance enforcement, and intelligence to disrupt targeted activity.

This origin connected Weed and Seed to broader concerns about aggressive drug enforcement, incarceration, racial disparity, and crime displacement.

Community Revitalization Context

Policymakers recognized that enforcement alone could leave a neighborhood with vacant property, weak institutions, limited employment, unsafe housing, and insufficient youth services.

The “seeding” concept therefore connected crime control to social services, education, treatment, housing, economic development, recreation, environmental improvement, and resident organization.

Philadelphia Precursor

Justice Department officials later identified collaborative anti-crime work in Philadelphia’s Mantua and Spring Garden neighborhoods as an important conceptual precursor.

That effort brought federal and local prosecutors, law enforcement, and community groups together before the national pilot grants were launched.

The first formally funded national pilot sites were Kansas City, Omaha, and Trenton.

National Launch in 1991

The Department of Justice launched Operation Weed and Seed during August and September 1991.

Bureau of Justice Assistance funding supported three pilot demonstrations selected to test comprehensive, geographically targeted crime control and neighborhood restoration.

Kansas City Pilot

Kansas City, Missouri, was one of the three initial grant sites.

Its early work included concentrated enforcement and community-policing strategies addressing guns, drug markets, and violent crime.

The site contributed to later federal interest in problem-oriented patrol and gun-violence reduction.

Omaha Pilot

Omaha, Nebraska, was one of the three original pilot sites.

Its experience demonstrated how a local U.S. Attorney, police, prosecutors, neighborhood organizations, and service providers could coordinate enforcement and community programming.

Trenton Pilot

Trenton, New Jersey, was one of the three original pilot sites and was frequently described as the first official Weed and Seed community.

Schools served as neighborhood centers, while community policing and intensified enforcement operated in surrounding target areas.

Expansion in 1992

Sixteen additional cities received demonstration grants in 1992.

Selection considered severe crime, existing anti-drug capacity, potential community participation, geographic diversity, and representation of large and mid-sized cities.

Los Angeles Expansion After the 1992 Uprising

Los Angeles received an unusually large early Weed and Seed award following the civil unrest that occurred after the Rodney King trial verdicts.

The award illustrated the program’s use as both a crime-control initiative and a response to neighborhood distress and damaged police-community relations.

Executive Office for Weed and Seed

The Department of Justice established the Executive Office for Weed and Seed in June 1992 within the Office of the Deputy Attorney General.

EOWS provided national leadership, site selection, grants, technical assistance, training, monitoring, publications, and coordination with United States Attorneys.

Placement Within the Office of Justice Programs

The federal Weed and Seed office later operated within the Office of Justice Programs.

This location connected the initiative to BJA, NIJ, OJJDP, BJS, victim services, research, and other federal grant programs.

Community Capacity Development Office

During the 2000s, the Executive Office for Weed and Seed became part of or was renamed the Community Capacity Development Office.

CCDO administered Weed and Seed grants and coordinated broader OJP work intended to strengthen community capacity to prevent and address crime and violence.

Legal and Appropriations Context

Weed and Seed was created administratively by the Department of Justice rather than as an individual entitlement or permanent formula grant.

Congress sustained the initiative through annual discretionary appropriations, including a dedicated Weed and Seed Program Fund.

Award conditions also incorporated federal grant rules, civil-rights laws, appropriations restrictions, financial standards, environmental requirements, and program guidance.

Discretionary Status

Communities did not receive Weed and Seed money through an automatic formula.

DOJ selected sites through pilot, demonstration, official-recognition, competitive, continuation, and enhancement processes that changed over time.

Recognition did not guarantee continuation funding.

The Two-Pronged Model

The strategy combined:

  1. Weeding—coordinated efforts to identify, arrest, prosecute, adjudicate, incarcerate, and supervise people engaged in violent crime, drug trafficking, and gang activity.
  2. Seeding—prevention, intervention, treatment, social services, community institution building, and neighborhood restoration.

Effective implementation required the two functions to operate together rather than in separate grant silos.

Four Components of the Strategy

  1. Law enforcement.
  2. Community policing.
  3. Prevention, intervention, and treatment.
  4. Neighborhood restoration and economic development.

Community policing was designed to bridge enforcement and community revitalization.

Four Foundational Principles

  1. Collaboration.
  2. Coordination.
  3. Community participation.
  4. Leveraging resources.

These principles distinguished the initiative from a short-term police operation or isolated social-service grant.

Collaboration

Collaboration brought agencies, residents, businesses, service providers, and organizations into a formal process for joint planning and action.

Intended results included permanent communication channels, shared goals, innovation, broader political support, and access to additional resources.

Coordination

Coordination sought to reduce duplication and align services delivered by separate institutions.

Vertical coordination linked law enforcement, social services, housing, health, and economic development.

Horizontal coordination linked federal, state, local, nonprofit, business, and resident actors.

Community Participation

Residents were expected to identify problems, select priorities, participate in steering committees, organize local activity, monitor implementation, and help sustain the strategy.

Federal guidance described resident participation as essential rather than symbolic.

Leveraging Resources

Federal Weed and Seed grants were intentionally too small to finance every enforcement, service, housing, and economic-development need.

Sites were expected to leverage money and services from federal agencies, state and local governments, foundations, corporations, nonprofit organizations, faith institutions, universities, and residents.

Concentration of Resources

The strategy focused multiple resources within a small geographic area rather than distributing modest assistance across an entire jurisdiction.

Concentration was intended to produce visible change, interrupt entrenched crime patterns, and make coordination manageable.

Narrow boundaries also raised concerns about displacement and exclusion of adjacent neighborhoods.

Law-Enforcement Component

Law-enforcement activity coordinated federal, state, and local agencies in the target area.

Common activities included:

  • violent-offender investigations;
  • drug-trafficking investigations;
  • gang enforcement;
  • firearms enforcement;
  • warrant service;
  • fugitive apprehension;
  • targeted patrol;
  • probation and parole operations;
  • nuisance abatement;
  • asset forfeiture;
  • code enforcement; and
  • federal or state prosecution.

Prosecution

Prosecutors were expected to coordinate charging, case review, witness support, sentencing, and federal-state jurisdiction.

The 1999 national evaluation found prosecution to be a relatively weak link at many evaluated sites because special prosecution and case tracking were not consistently maintained.

Community-Policing Component

Community policing sought to build mutual trust, increase police visibility, solve recurring problems, and involve residents in public safety.

Activities included foot patrols, police ministations, problem-solving meetings, neighborhood watch, victim referrals, youth contact, nuisance abatement, and joint cleanup or restoration work.

Community Policing as the Bridge

Community policing connected weeding and seeding.

Officers could receive information from residents for enforcement while helping residents obtain services, address disorder, organize activities, and connect with government.

The bridge failed when police contact was dominated by raids, arrests, surveillance, or short-term enforcement without reciprocal service and accountability.

Prevention

Prevention sought to reduce risk factors and strengthen protective factors before crime occurred.

Projects included after-school programs, recreation, mentoring, youth leadership, school partnerships, family support, conflict resolution, employment preparation, substance-use education, and community events.

Intervention

Intervention focused on people or situations already showing elevated risk.

Examples included gang outreach, truancy response, probation support, family crisis services, domestic-violence programs, victim assistance, youth case management, and targeted employment support.

Treatment

Treatment services addressed substance use, mental health, trauma, family needs, and other conditions connected to victimization or offending.

Sites relied heavily on existing public and nonprofit providers because Weed and Seed grants could not build a complete treatment system.

Neighborhood Restoration

Neighborhood restoration sought to improve living conditions and strengthen legitimate community institutions.

Projects included housing rehabilitation, home security, cleanup, graffiti removal, vacant-lot improvement, lighting, code enforcement, transportation coordination, parks, recreation, public health, and community facility improvement.

Economic Development

Economic-development activity included job training, employment placement, business support, commercial-corridor improvement, financial education, adult literacy, and partnerships with employers.

The scale of federal Weed and Seed grants limited direct economic transformation, making outside investment and agency coordination essential.

Housing and Physical Conditions

Housing activity could involve rehabilitation, public-housing coordination, landlord engagement, tenant organizing, abandoned-property action, home repair, and security improvements.

Enforcement tools such as nuisance abatement and eviction required safeguards to avoid displacement of innocent tenants and destabilization of vulnerable families.

Safe Haven

A Safe Haven was a visible, accessible multiservice center serving youth, adults, and families.

Schools, recreation centers, churches, community centers, and other trusted institutions commonly hosted Safe Havens.

Federal guidance expected every Weed and Seed site to maintain at least one Safe Haven.

Safe Haven Services

Services could include:

  • after-school and weekend programming;
  • tutoring and literacy;
  • recreation and sports;
  • mentoring;
  • computer access;
  • employment preparation;
  • family support;
  • health and substance-use services;
  • adult education;
  • community meetings;
  • police-community contact; and
  • referral to public benefits and social services.

Safe Haven Limitations

A Safe Haven required sustained staffing, security, transportation, programming, trust, and operating funds.

Creating a facility did not ensure that target-area residents could use it or that services matched community needs.

Drug Education for Youth

Weed and Seed partnered with the Department of the Navy to operate Drug Education for Youth, commonly called DEFY.

The initiative combined summer camps, mentoring, parent participation, and continuing youth activities.

It began at three sites in 1996 and later expanded to dozens of Weed and Seed communities.

Steering Committee

The Steering Committee was the primary governing body for a local Weed and Seed site.

It identified needs, approved goals, balanced enforcement and services, mobilized resources, assigned responsibilities, monitored performance, resolved conflict, and guided sustainability.

Steering Committee Membership

Membership commonly included:

  • the United States Attorney or representative;
  • the mayor or county executive;
  • police and sheriff leadership;
  • state and local prosecutors;
  • federal investigative agencies;
  • probation and parole;
  • courts;
  • housing and community-development agencies;
  • health and social-service providers;
  • schools;
  • businesses;
  • faith-based organizations;
  • community organizations;
  • youth representatives; and
  • target-area residents.

Steering Committee Leadership

Committees were often co-chaired by the United States Attorney and a key local official such as the mayor or police chief.

This structure gave the initiative access to federal enforcement and local executive authority but could also concentrate decision-making among officials rather than residents.

Role of the United States Attorney

The United States Attorney coordinated federal, state, and local enforcement and prosecution, supported formation of the steering committee, reviewed the strategy, and transmitted the application.

The strength of this role varied among districts and depended on leadership, staffing, priorities, and relationships.

Role of the Mayor and Local Government

Local executive leadership coordinated city or county agencies and connected the target area to housing, sanitation, code enforcement, parks, transportation, employment, schools, and development resources.

Weak local-government commitment could leave the initiative dependent on police and short-term grant staff.

Site Coordinator

The site coordinator managed daily implementation, steering-committee activity, reporting, partnerships, grants, Safe Havens, communication, and coordination of weed and seed projects.

Some sites used separate weed and seed coordinators because the two areas required different expertise and institutional relationships.

Role of Residents

Residents were expected to participate from the beginning rather than approve a completed police or government plan.

Activities included meetings, committees, marches, rallies, cleanups, watches, youth projects, service design, evaluation, and neighborhood leadership.

Role of Businesses

Businesses could provide jobs, training, donations, event support, rehabilitation investment, equipment, and commercial development.

Business involvement could strengthen local institutions but required safeguards against displacement and development that excluded existing residents.

Role of Faith-Based Organizations

Churches and other faith institutions supplied meeting space, volunteers, mentoring, social services, local credibility, and long-term neighborhood presence.

Public funds and official partnerships remained subject to constitutional and civil-rights limits concerning religion and nondiscrimination.

Strategic Plan

Every recognized site developed a strategic plan through its steering committee.

The plan identified target-area conditions, priorities, partners, existing resources, service gaps, enforcement strategies, seeding activities, schedules, measures, and responsibilities.

Needs Assessment

Planning began with analysis of crime, disorder, housing, services, health, economic conditions, institutions, resident concerns, and existing resources.

Weak data could cause sites to select visible symptoms rather than underlying problems.

Target-Area Boundaries

Sites defined a focused geographic area using crime data, neighborhood identity, institutional capacity, physical boundaries, and available resources.

Boundaries needed to be narrow enough for concentration but large enough to contain meaningful community institutions and crime patterns.

Implementation Plan

The implementation plan converted broad goals into actions, responsible organizations, budgets, schedules, outputs, and outcomes.

Sites needed to sequence enforcement and services so that seeding began early rather than after an extended crackdown.

Official Recognition

Communities that developed a strategy with their United States Attorney could apply to DOJ for official recognition.

Recognition signaled that the site had boundaries, a steering committee, a strategic plan, required components, and federal coordination.

Recognition could make a site eligible for funding but did not guarantee a large grant.

Recognition Duration and Recertification

Official recognition ordinarily lasted five calendar years under later program guidance.

Sites could seek recertification by updating their strategy and demonstrating continued compliance and local commitment.

Demonstration Sites

Early demonstration sites received relatively large multiyear awards to test the strategy.

The 1999 national evaluation reported that demonstration sites generally received $500,000 to $750,000 annually for four consecutive years.

Official-Recognition Awards

Officially recognized sites often received much smaller startup or support awards.

Some early recognition grants were approximately $35,000, intended to support planning, coordination, or limited implementation while sites leveraged other resources.

Later Competitive Site Awards

Later funding models provided new competitive sites with multiyear eligibility and continuation awards.

Award amounts varied by year, appropriations, site age, grant category, and federal priorities.

In 1999, most active sites reportedly received approximately $225,000 annually.

Continuation Grants

Existing sites applied for continuation funding and reported progress, activities, leveraged resources, performance, and sustainability.

Federal funding was expected to decline or end as sites became self-sustaining.

Graduated Sites

A graduated site had completed its federal funding cycle but could continue the Weed and Seed strategy using local, state, private, philanthropic, or other federal resources.

Graduation did not necessarily mean that crime and neighborhood distress had been resolved.

Grantee and Fiscal Agent

A city, county, state agency, nonprofit organization, police department, or other eligible institution could serve as fiscal agent depending on the award.

The fiscal agent managed federal money but was expected to act consistently with steering-committee priorities and the approved plan.

No Formula Allocation

Weed and Seed did not distribute money according to population or crime statistics alone.

Federal selection considered readiness, crime concentration, partnerships, strategy quality, U.S. Attorney support, community participation, sustainability, and available appropriations.

Matching and Leveraging

The program did not operate through one permanent statutory matching formula applied identically in every year.

Sites were nevertheless expected to contribute and leverage substantial non-Weed-and-Seed resources.

Federal grants were catalytic rather than sufficient to finance the complete strategy.

Federal Partners

Sites sought resources and participation from DOJ components and other departments.

Potential partners included:

  • Federal Bureau of Investigation;
  • Drug Enforcement Administration;
  • Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives;
  • United States Marshals Service;
  • Department of Housing and Urban Development;
  • Department of Labor;
  • Department of Health and Human Services;
  • Department of Education;
  • Environmental Protection Agency;
  • Small Business Administration;
  • Department of the Treasury; and
  • Department of the Navy through DEFY.

State and Local Partners

State and local police, prosecutors, courts, corrections, probation, parole, schools, health agencies, housing authorities, workforce institutions, parks, sanitation, code enforcement, and planning departments could participate.

Effective participation required assignment of actual staff and resources rather than attendance at meetings alone.

Relationship to Project Safe Neighborhoods

During the 2000s, Weed and Seed sites often coordinated with Project Safe Neighborhoods.

PSN concentrated on gun and gang violence, while Weed and Seed supplied neighborhood partnerships, community policing, prevention, services, and revitalization.

Public Housing Coordination

Many target areas contained public or federally assisted housing.

Partnerships could address violent crime, drug markets, tenant safety, youth services, physical conditions, resident leadership, and redevelopment.

Enforcement and redevelopment could also displace residents or criminalize tenancy when safeguards were weak.

Environmental and Brownfields Partnerships

Some sites coordinated neighborhood restoration with environmental cleanup, vacant-property reuse, and brownfields initiatives.

Physical revitalization could reduce abandoned spaces associated with crime while supporting housing, parks, businesses, and community facilities.

Reentry and Community Supervision

Probation, parole, corrections, and reentry providers participated in some Weed and Seed strategies.

Services could include supervision, treatment, employment, housing, mentoring, and family support for people returning from incarceration.

Enforcement-heavy supervision without adequate support could increase revocation and reincarceration.

Community Prosecution

Community prosecutors worked with residents and police on recurring neighborhood problems rather than limiting their role to individual criminal cases.

Activities included nuisance cases, problem properties, code enforcement, victim support, case screening, community meetings, and coordination of federal and state charges.

Nuisance Abatement

Civil and administrative tools targeted properties associated with repeated crime, drug sales, violence, or dangerous conditions.

Nuisance enforcement could improve safety but risked eviction, homelessness, weak due process, or punishment of tenants who were not responsible for criminal activity.

Asset Forfeiture

Some enforcement partnerships used federal or state forfeiture laws against property connected to drug trafficking or other crimes.

Forfeiture resources could support enforcement or community activity but raised concerns about financial incentives, due process, innocent owners, and equitable distribution.

Community Mobilization Activities

Neighborhood watches, marches, rallies, cleanup days, graffiti removal, festivals, youth events, safety fairs, and resident training promoted visible participation.

These activities could strengthen cohesion but should not substitute for structural investment or measurable crime and service outcomes.

Civil-Rights Requirements

Federal grantees and participating agencies remained subject to the Constitution, federal civil-rights statutes, DOJ regulations, and grant conditions.

Targeted enforcement did not authorize unlawful searches, discriminatory stops, excessive force, retaliation, or denial of due process.

Racial Profiling and Selective Enforcement

Weed and Seed frequently operated in predominantly Black, Latino, Native, immigrant, or low-income neighborhoods.

Concentrated enforcement could amplify racial disparities when police intelligence, stop practices, charging, probation, or nuisance enforcement were biased or insufficiently supervised.

Resident participation did not eliminate the need for independent civil-rights review and disaggregated data.

Language Access

Sites receiving federal assistance had to provide meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency.

Translation, interpretation, multilingual outreach, trusted messengers, and accessible public meetings were necessary in linguistically diverse neighborhoods.

Disability Access

Safe Havens, meetings, services, police contact, and grant-funded activities were subject to federal disability nondiscrimination requirements.

Facilities and communications needed to be accessible, and agencies had to provide reasonable modifications where required.

Youth Services and Criminalization Risk

Youth programs provided recreation, tutoring, mentoring, structure, and opportunity.

Simultaneous enforcement, school policing, gang identification, probation involvement, and surveillance could increase youth contact with the justice system.

A balanced strategy required clear separation between voluntary services and coercive enforcement.

Community Trust

Trust increased when residents influenced priorities, received meaningful services, observed fair enforcement, and saw government commitments fulfilled.

Trust declined when participation was symbolic, enforcement preceded services indefinitely, promised resources failed to arrive, or residents experienced abusive policing.

The “Weed” Metaphor

Supporters used the metaphor to describe removal of criminal activity, not removal of entire populations.

Critics argued that describing people as weeds dehumanized residents, simplified neighborhood problems, and legitimized displacement or incarceration.

The metaphor remains central to historical criticism of the program.

Crime Displacement

Concentrated enforcement can move drug markets, violence, or disorder into nearby blocks rather than eliminate them.

Evaluation required comparison areas, spatial analysis, and attention to diffusion of benefits as well as displacement.

Revitalization and Resident Displacement

Neighborhood improvement could increase property values, attract investment, and reduce vacancy.

Without affordable-housing protections, existing tenants and small businesses could be displaced by redevelopment, rent increases, demolition, or ownership change.

Grant Management

Grantees were responsible for financial controls, procurement, records, subawards, performance reports, property, audits, and compliance with the approved strategy.

Steering-committee governance did not replace federal accountability of the legal grantee.

Performance Reporting

Sites reported activities, funding, partnerships, enforcement, services, leveraged resources, neighborhood conditions, and other measures.

DOJ later used average homicides per funded site as a national performance measure, although this measure could not capture the full diversity of target areas or interventions.

GAO Management Findings

GAO found weaknesses in grant files, documentation of site-qualification decisions, monitoring records, sustainability criteria, and outcome measurement.

DOJ subsequently strengthened files, monitoring, performance-measure development, and guidance.

Sustainability Criteria

GAO concluded that federal administrators lacked clear criteria for deciding when a site had become self-sustaining and should receive reduced or no continuation funding.

Without such criteria, long-funded sites could compete with new communities despite the strategy’s expectation that local resources would eventually replace federal grants.

Outputs and Outcomes

Early federal monitoring often counted meetings, arrests, activities, participants, grants, and services.

GAO emphasized the need for outcome or intermediate measures demonstrating progress toward crime reduction, safety, trust, service coordination, and neighborhood restoration.

Self-Sustainability

Sustainability meant preserving effective partnerships, services, enforcement coordination, resident leadership, and restoration work after dedicated federal funding ended.

Sites with strong local institutions and diversified funding were better positioned to continue.

The 1999 National Evaluation

NIJ’s national evaluation examined eight sites and one or two target areas within each.

The evaluation used crime data, interviews, community surveys, program records, and comparison with broader city or county trends.

Results varied substantially among neighborhoods and components.

1999 Crime Findings

Nine target areas had sufficient data comparing the year before implementation with the second Weed and Seed year.

Six experienced declines in Part 1 crime and three experienced increases.

In seven areas, target-area crime declined more or increased less than crime in the surrounding city or county.

These descriptive patterns did not establish uniform causal effectiveness.

1999 Implementation Findings

Strong implementation was associated with dedicated staff, sustained enforcement, early seeding, active community participation, high-level coordination, and organizations capable of delivering services.

Weak prosecution, leadership turnover, insufficient community infrastructure, overly broad boundaries, and poorly sequenced activity limited effectiveness.

Coalition Building

The national evaluation identified coalition building as one of the strategy’s strongest contributions.

In several sites, public agencies, residents, businesses, and nonprofit organizations collaborated in ways unlikely to have occurred without the federal initiative.

Complexity of Seeding

Evaluators found seeding broader and often more difficult than enforcement.

Law enforcement operated through established hierarchies, while social services and revitalization required consensus among many independent organizations, long timelines, and resources outside DOJ control.

The 2010 Independent Evaluation

A later independent evaluation examined 187 active sites and 87 graduated sites and conducted in-depth analysis of thirteen Weed and Seed neighborhoods and thirteen comparison neighborhoods.

Researchers surveyed 1,353 stakeholders and 2,200 residents and reviewed implementation and outcome information.

The final report concluded that the strategy demonstrated sufficient positive benefits to continue, while recommending improvements in funding, components, and principles.

Miami Evaluation

CrimeSolutions reviewed one evaluation of Weed and Seed in Miami and rated that specific program ineffective.

The study found no statistically significant difference in violent-crime rates between treatment and comparison areas and found a relative increase in detected drug offenses.

The finding applied to that implementation and should not be treated as a universal rating of every Weed and Seed site.

Mixed Evidence

Evaluation evidence varied by site, boundary, implementation strength, funding, baseline conditions, crime type, time period, and research design.

Crime declines could reflect broader national trends, local initiatives, displacement, improved reporting, or Weed and Seed activity.

The program therefore cannot be classified accurately as uniformly successful or uniformly ineffective.

Funding History

Weed and Seed grew from three pilot grants to hundreds of communities.

By August 2001, DOJ reported more than $237 million awarded to nearly 300 communities.

By 2003, the Executive Office reported nearly $340 million provided to 315 communities.

DOJ later reported 1,885 grants totalling more than $409 million between 2001 and 2008 alone, reflecting continuation, site, enhancement, and related awards.

Mid-1990s Funding

By 1995, DOJ reported thirty-six communities funded at approximately $750,000 and forty additional communities receiving one-time official-recognition grants of approximately $35,000.

Funding structures evolved as the initiative expanded.

Fiscal Year 2008 Funding

The dedicated Weed and Seed Program Fund received approximately $32.1 million in fiscal year 2008.

This level was much smaller than the combined resources sites were expected to leverage from other sources.

Fiscal Year 2009 Funding

DOJ budget materials identify approximately $25 million for Weed and Seed and the Community Capacity Development Office in fiscal year 2009.

The administration had previously proposed moving related activity into a broader Byrne public-safety program, illustrating growing uncertainty about the dedicated account.

Fiscal Year 2010 Funding

Fiscal year 2010 was the last year in which dedicated federal Weed and Seed grants were funded.

Archived OJP records include a fiscal year 2010 continuation program for existing sites.

Declining Number of Funded Sites

DOJ projected that the number of funded sites would fall from approximately 332 in fiscal year 2005 to 132 by the end of fiscal year 2010.

Since 2005, OJP reported funding only about one-third of fully submitted new-site applications.

The decline reflected shrinking appropriations, multiyear cycles, graduation, and competition among communities.

Fiscal Year 2011 Phaseout

Dedicated federal funding was discontinued in fiscal year 2011, and no new Weed and Seed awards were made.

CCDO continued programmatic management and oversight of existing grants through the end of their award periods.

The office closed at the end of fiscal year 2011.

Discontinued Federal Performance Measure

DOJ discontinued the national homicide-per-site performance measure after the program ended.

The reported average increased from a revised 3.6 homicides per site in calendar year 2009 to 4.6 in 2010, exceeding the target of 3.7.

DOJ noted that part of the 2010 measurement period was not federally funded and stopped future reporting.

Byrne Criminal Justice Innovation Program

DOJ’s fiscal year 2012 budget proposed the Byrne Criminal Justice Innovation Program as the “next generation” of Weed and Seed.

BCJI retained place-based targeting, resident participation, cross-sector partnership, law-enforcement collaboration, physical revitalization, and problem solving.

It added stronger emphasis on data, evidence, research partnerships, crime hot spots, and measurable outcomes.

Successor but Not Legal Continuation

BCJI inherited policy lessons but did not continue every Weed and Seed designation, grant agreement, Safe Haven, steering committee, or U.S. Attorney structure.

Former Weed and Seed sites had to compete or sustain themselves through other resources.

Local Continuation After Federal Funding

Some communities retained the Weed and Seed name, committee, Safe Haven, police partnership, youth program, or revitalization project after federal funding ended.

Later local use of the name does not establish that a project remained federally recognized or funded.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

Operation Weed and Seed is inactive as a national federally funded grant program.

The Community Capacity Development Office no longer exists, and DOJ does not publish current national Weed and Seed funding opportunities.

Historical materials remain available through OJP, NIJ, CrimeSolutions, DOJ archives, U.S. Attorney pages, grant audits, and local records.

Major Achievements

  • Established a national model combining enforcement and neighborhood investment.
  • Expanded from three pilot sites to more than 300 communities.
  • Created local steering committees joining residents with government and private institutions.
  • Made community policing a bridge between enforcement and services.
  • Supported Safe Havens, youth programs, treatment, employment, education, and family services.
  • Mobilized housing, economic-development, environmental, and physical-restoration resources.
  • Encouraged U.S. Attorneys to coordinate federal, state, and local partners.
  • Produced significant coalition building and interagency communication at many sites.
  • Generated national and local evaluations of place-based crime reduction.
  • Influenced later evidence-based, community-oriented, place-based grant programs.

Limitations and Failures

  • Federal grants were too small to address the full scale of neighborhood disinvestment.
  • Enforcement could begin faster and receive more institutional support than seeding.
  • Prosecution was a weak link at several evaluated sites.
  • Community participation varied and could become symbolic.
  • Sites differed too greatly for one national implementation model.
  • Crime outcomes were mixed and difficult to attribute.
  • Concentrated enforcement could displace crime to adjacent areas.
  • Racially disparate policing and incarceration could be intensified.
  • Revitalization could contribute to resident displacement.
  • Early federal management lacked complete files, outcome measures, and sustainability criteria.
  • Sites became dependent on continuation funding despite expectations of self-sustainability.
  • The dedicated federal program ended before many neighborhoods achieved lasting structural change.

Major Controversies

Enforcement versus investment
Police institutions could deploy quickly, while housing, treatment, education, and economic development required longer timelines and outside resources.
The “weed” metaphor
Critics viewed the language as dehumanizing and linked it to removal of people rather than reduction of harmful conduct.
War on Drugs
Drug enforcement and prosecution connected the program to incarceration and racial-disparity concerns.
Resident power
Steering committees included residents, but government and law-enforcement officials often controlled money and institutional authority.
Crime displacement
Targeted crackdowns could move crime beyond site boundaries.
Gentrification
Successful revitalization could increase rents and displace existing residents without housing protections.
Evaluation
Mixed local findings and inconsistent methods made national claims difficult.
Federal sustainability
The program expected local continuation while repeatedly funding established sites without clear self-sufficiency criteria.
Phaseout
Ending dedicated funds disrupted partnerships and services even where local implementation was considered successful.

How Weed and Seed Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the specific site, target boundaries, years, official-recognition status, funding cycle, grantee, steering committee, Safe Haven, enforcement strategy, seeding activities, outside resources, and outcome measures.

Important distinctions include:

  • Weed and Seed as a strategy versus one uniform program;
  • official recognition versus federal funding;
  • active funded sites versus graduated sites;
  • the national program versus later local use of the name;
  • weeding activity versus seeding activity;
  • community policing versus intensive enforcement;
  • federal grant dollars versus leveraged resources;
  • arrests and activities versus crime and quality-of-life outcomes;
  • crime reduction versus displacement;
  • revitalization versus gentrification;
  • resident participation versus resident decision-making power; and
  • the inactive Weed and Seed program versus later BCJI initiatives.

Why the Weed and Seed Program Matters

Weed and Seed attempted to connect criminal enforcement with the social, physical, and economic conditions of neighborhoods.

Its structure recognized that policing alone could not create stable communities and that social services alone could struggle in places experiencing concentrated violence and open-air drug markets.

The initiative helped institutionalize place-based partnerships, Safe Havens, U.S. Attorney coordination, community policing, strategic planning, resident participation, and cross-agency resource concentration.

Its central contradiction was structural: enforcement institutions possessed immediate authority and dedicated personnel, while seeding depended on slower, fragmented, and frequently underfunded housing, health, education, employment, and development systems.

The program’s legacy therefore includes both an influential model of comprehensive community safety and a warning about combining aggressive enforcement with limited social investment in racially and economically marginalized neighborhoods.

Summary

Operation Weed and Seed was a federal place-based crime-control and neighborhood-revitalization strategy launched by the Department of Justice in 1991.

The first pilot sites were Kansas City, Omaha, and Trenton. The initiative later expanded to more than 300 communities.

The model combined intensive enforcement and prosecution—“weeding”—with prevention, intervention, treatment, services, community policing, and neighborhood restoration—“seeding.”

Four components structured the strategy: law enforcement, community policing, prevention and treatment, and neighborhood restoration. Four principles guided implementation: collaboration, coordination, community participation, and leveraging resources.

Each site used a steering committee, strategic plan, site coordinator, defined target area, and at least one Safe Haven. United States Attorneys played a central federal coordinating role.

Weed and Seed was a discretionary strategy rather than a formula grant or entitlement. Federal awards were intended to catalyze much larger commitments from public agencies, businesses, philanthropy, nonprofit organizations, and residents.

Evaluations found mixed crime effects but identified meaningful coalition building, service coordination, and positive local outcomes where implementation was strong. Weak prosecution, delayed seeding, limited community power, poor measurement, displacement, and uneven sustainability reduced effectiveness.

GAO identified management weaknesses involving official files, monitoring, outcome measures, site selection, and criteria for self-sustainability.

Dedicated federal funding ended after fiscal year 2010. No new awards were made in fiscal year 2011, and the Community Capacity Development Office closed at the end of that year.

DOJ described the Byrne Criminal Justice Innovation Program as the next generation of Weed and Seed, retaining place-based partnership and revitalization principles while adding stronger data and evidence requirements.

As of August 2, 2026, Weed and Seed remained inactive as a national federal grant program, although former sites and local organizations continued parts of the strategy independently.
