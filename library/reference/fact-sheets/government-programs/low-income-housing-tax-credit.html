Low-Income Housing Tax Credit

The federal tax incentive that finances the acquisition, construction, rehabilitation, and preservation of income-restricted rental housing through state allocation agencies and private investment.

Overview

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is the principal federal incentive for producing and preserving affordable rental housing in the United States. Commonly abbreviated LIHTC, the credit was established by the Tax Reform Act of 1986 and is governed by Section 42 of the Internal Revenue Code.

LIHTC does not ordinarily provide a monthly subsidy directly to tenants. Instead, it reduces the federal income-tax liability of investors who supply equity to qualifying rental-housing developments. Developers generally sell partnership or limited-liability-company interests to investors, frequently through specialized syndicators. Investor equity reduces the amount of conventional debt and other financing the property must support, allowing designated units to charge restricted rents.

The federal government establishes the statutory credit, tax rules, state ceilings, and compliance framework. State and local housing credit agencies adopt Qualified Allocation Plans, evaluate applications, reserve and allocate credits, issue required tax forms, monitor compliance, and report noncompliance to the Internal Revenue Service.

Two major financing channels are commonly described as the “9-percent credit” and the “4-percent credit.” The competitive 9-percent credit generally supports new construction and substantial rehabilitation without federally subsidized financing. The 4-percent credit generally supports acquisition costs and developments financed with qualifying tax-exempt private-activity bonds. These names describe statutory or approximate credit rates used to produce prescribed present-value subsidies; they do not mean that every project receives credits equal to exactly 9 or 4 percent of total development cost.

Owners claim the credit over a ten-year credit period. In exchange, qualifying units must satisfy income, rent, occupancy, habitability, nondiscrimination, recordkeeping, and general-public-use requirements. The federal compliance period generally lasts fifteen years, and an extended-use agreement ordinarily restricts the property for at least thirty years. State agencies can require longer affordability periods through their allocation plans and recorded agreements.

LIHTC properties may be entirely income-restricted or may combine restricted and market-rate units. Some serve families, older adults, people with disabilities, people experiencing homelessness, rural residents, veterans, or other populations. LIHTC developments frequently combine tax-credit equity with tax-exempt bonds, conventional loans, HOME funds, Housing Trust Fund assistance, state or local subsidies, land contributions, rental assistance, and other financing.

The program has financed a substantial national inventory. HUD’s property database reports 55,345 LIHTC projects and approximately 3.9 million housing units placed in service from 1987 through 2024. Because the database counts all units in reported projects, the number of income-restricted LIHTC units is lower than the total project-unit count.

LIHTC has expanded affordable-housing production and preservation, but it does not guarantee affordability to every low-income household. Maximum LIHTC rents are tied to area income limits and assumed household size rather than each tenant’s actual income. Extremely low-income households frequently require Housing Choice Vouchers, Project-Based Rental Assistance, operating subsidies, or other support to afford LIHTC units.

Definitions

Low-Income Housing Tax Credit
A federal general-business tax credit under Internal Revenue Code Section 42 for investment in qualified low-income rental buildings.
Housing credit agency
A state or local agency authorized to allocate housing credit dollar amounts, approve bond-financed projects, issue required certifications, execute extended-use agreements, and monitor compliance.
Qualified Allocation Plan
A state or local housing credit agency’s governing document establishing selection criteria, preferences, application procedures, underwriting standards, affordability requirements, compliance expectations, and competitive priorities. The abbreviation QAP is commonly used.
9-percent credit
The competitive credit generally used for new construction and substantial rehabilitation not financed with federally subsidized debt. Federal law provides a minimum 9-percent applicable percentage for qualifying buildings.
4-percent credit
The credit generally used for acquisition costs and federally subsidized buildings, especially projects financed with qualifying tax-exempt private-activity bonds. Federal law provides a minimum 4-percent applicable percentage for qualifying buildings placed in service after 2020.
Applicable percentage
The credit rate applied to qualified basis to calculate a building’s annual credit amount. The rate depends on the building, financing, placed-in-service date, statutory floors, and applicable elections.
Eligible basis
The qualifying depreciable costs used in calculating the credit, generally including eligible building acquisition, construction, and rehabilitation costs but excluding land and specified nonqualifying expenses.
Qualified basis
Eligible basis multiplied by the applicable fraction, with any authorized basis adjustment. Qualified basis is used with the applicable percentage to calculate the annual credit.
Applicable fraction
The smaller of the unit fraction or floor-space fraction representing the portion of a building devoted to qualifying low-income units.
Unit fraction
The number of qualifying low-income units divided by the total number of residential rental units in the building.
Floor-space fraction
The total floor space of qualifying low-income units divided by the total floor space of all residential rental units in the building.
Credit allocation
A state housing credit agency’s award of a specified annual credit amount to a building or project from the state housing credit ceiling.
Carryover allocation
An allocation permitting a project additional time to satisfy placed-in-service requirements after meeting statutory expenditure and documentation conditions.
Credit period
The ten-year period during which an owner generally claims the annual LIHTC amount.
Compliance period
The fifteen-year federal period beginning with the first taxable year of the credit period during which noncompliance can reduce credits or trigger recapture.
Extended-use period
The additional affordability period established by a recorded agreement with the housing credit agency, generally producing a minimum total restriction of at least thirty years.
Extended low-income housing commitment
The binding agreement recorded against the property that establishes income, rent, occupancy, enforcement, and extended-use requirements.
Minimum set-aside
The project-level income and occupancy election that a development must satisfy to qualify under Section 42.
20–50 test
A minimum set-aside requiring at least 20 percent of residential units to be both rent-restricted and occupied by households with incomes at or below 50 percent of area median gross income.
40–60 test
A minimum set-aside requiring at least 40 percent of residential units to be both rent-restricted and occupied by households with incomes at or below 60 percent of area median gross income. A 25-percent threshold applies to qualifying projects in New York City.
Average-income test
A minimum set-aside under which at least 40 percent of units are designated at income levels from 20 through 80 percent of area median gross income, in ten-percentage-point increments, and the average designation does not exceed 60 percent.
Area median gross income
The local income benchmark used under Section 42, derived from HUD income limits and adjusted for household size. The abbreviation AMGI is commonly used in tax-credit practice.
Rent-restricted unit
A unit whose gross rent does not exceed the Section 42 maximum associated with the applicable income designation and imputed household size.
Gross rent
Rent paid by the tenant plus applicable utility allowance and certain mandatory charges, subject to statutory exclusions.
Utility allowance
An estimate of tenant-paid utility costs included in determining whether a unit’s gross rent complies with the maximum LIHTC rent.
Placed in service
The date a building is ready and available for its intended residential rental use. Placed-in-service timing affects credit commencement, applicable percentages, allocation deadlines, and compliance.
Tax-credit investor
An individual or entity that acquires an ownership interest in a qualifying project and claims allocated credits, commonly a bank, insurance company, corporation, or investment fund.
Syndicator
An intermediary that assembles investor capital, acquires partnership interests, conducts underwriting, and manages tax-credit investments through proprietary or multi-investor funds.
Tax-credit equity
Capital contributed by investors in exchange for anticipated tax credits, tax losses, and associated economic rights.
Qualified nonprofit organization
An eligible nonprofit that satisfies Section 42 requirements and materially participates in the development and operation of a project receiving credit from the nonprofit set-aside.
Qualified Census Tract
A census tract designated by HUD based principally on poverty or household-income criteria. Projects in a QCT may qualify for increased eligible basis under Section 42. The abbreviation QCT is commonly used.
Difficult Development Area
An area designated by HUD because construction, land, or utility costs are high relative to local incomes. Projects in a DDA may qualify for increased eligible basis. The abbreviation DDA is commonly used.
Basis boost
An authorized increase, commonly up to 30 percent, in eligible basis for qualifying projects, allowing additional tax-credit equity when needed for financial feasibility.
Qualified contract
A statutory process that historically permitted certain owners after year fourteen to request that the housing credit agency locate a buyer at a formula price, subject to exceptions, state restrictions, and the terms of the extended-use agreement.
Recapture
The recovery of previously claimed credit, with interest, when a building’s qualified basis decreases or specified noncompliance occurs during the compliance period.

Program Purpose and Design

Production Incentive

LIHTC addresses the financing gap between the cost of developing rental housing and the amount of debt that restricted rents can support. Investor equity reduces the permanent debt, public subsidy, or developer capital required to make a project financially feasible.

Decentralized Allocation

Federal law gives states substantial control over project selection. Each housing credit agency uses a Qualified Allocation Plan to translate federal requirements into local priorities concerning location, population served, affordability, preservation, rural housing, supportive services, energy performance, development cost, ownership, and other policy goals.

Public–Private Structure

The program combines federal tax expenditure, state regulation, local land-use authority, private development, institutional investment, nonprofit participation, conventional lending, and public gap financing. This structure mobilizes private capital but creates complex transactions and multiple layers of oversight.

Project Rather Than Household Subsidy

LIHTC restricts units and rents at the property level. Unlike Housing Choice Vouchers, it does not ordinarily calculate assistance from each tenant’s actual income or send a monthly housing assistance payment to the owner. A tenant can use a voucher in an eligible LIHTC unit, creating a layered household and project subsidy.

Temporary Credit and Long-Term Restriction

Investors receive credits for ten years, but the property must comply with federal requirements for fifteen years and remain subject to an extended-use agreement for a longer period. This separation between the benefit period and affordability period is central to LIHTC policy.

How LIHTC Financing Works

Development Proposal

A developer identifies a site or existing property, prepares preliminary designs, estimates development costs and operating income, assembles a development team, obtains local approvals, and seeks financing commitments.

Application to Housing Credit Agency

The developer applies to the state or local housing credit agency under the current Qualified Allocation Plan. The agency evaluates threshold eligibility, underwriting, site control, market demand, development capacity, costs, financing, readiness, affordability, and competitive criteria.

Reservation or Bond Approval

A competitive 9-percent project generally receives a credit reservation from the state ceiling. A 4-percent project commonly receives an allocation of tax-exempt private-activity bonds and a determination that the project satisfies the Qualified Allocation Plan and Section 42 requirements.

Ownership Entity

The development is typically owned by a limited partnership or limited-liability company formed for the project. The developer or affiliate serves as general partner or managing member. The tax-credit investor becomes a limited partner or investor member.

Investor Equity

The investor contributes equity in installments tied to construction, placed-in-service, occupancy, cost certification, permanent-loan closing, issuance of Form 8609, or other milestones. The amount paid depends on anticipated credits, losses, compliance risk, market conditions, investor demand, and project characteristics.

Construction or Rehabilitation

The owner acquires, constructs, or rehabilitates the property using tax-credit equity, loans, grants, bonds, deferred fees, and other sources. The project must satisfy building, accessibility, environmental, labor, relocation, and funding requirements applicable to its particular sources.

Placed in Service and Cost Certification

After completion, the owner documents eligible costs, financing, units, placed-in-service dates, and elections. The housing credit agency determines the final credit amount needed for financial feasibility and issues Form 8609 for each building.

Credit Claims

The owner claims the credit over ten years and allocates it among partners or members under federal tax law. Investors file their tax returns using project certifications and annual compliance information.

Operations and Compliance

The property leases units to eligible households, charges restricted rents, maintains records, submits annual owner certifications, undergoes file and physical inspections, and reports changes affecting qualified basis.

Investor Exit

After the compliance period, the investor commonly exits the ownership entity through a sale, transfer, redemption, or exercise of contractual rights. The property remains subject to the extended-use agreement and other financing restrictions.

Credit Calculation

Basic Formula

A simplified annual-credit calculation is:

Eligible basis × applicable fraction × applicable percentage = annual credit amount

The annual credit is generally claimed for ten taxable years. Actual calculations involve building-specific rules, basis adjustments, acquisition and rehabilitation treatment, placed-in-service timing, qualified-basis changes, first-year proration, federal subsidies, and state allocation limits.

Eligible Basis

Eligible basis generally includes qualifying depreciable costs associated with acquiring, constructing, or rehabilitating a residential rental building and eligible common areas. Land, permanent financing costs, commercial space, tax reserves, syndication costs, and other specified expenses are excluded or treated separately.

Applicable Fraction

The applicable fraction limits credit to the qualifying low-income portion of a building. It is the smaller of the unit fraction or floor-space fraction, preventing a development from obtaining disproportionate credit by designating unusually small units as low-income.

Basis Boost

Buildings in Qualified Census Tracts or Difficult Development Areas can generally receive up to a 30-percent increase in eligible basis. Housing credit agencies can also designate certain competitive-credit buildings for a discretionary basis boost when necessary for financial feasibility.

Credit Allocation Limit

Even when a formula produces a larger amount, a state housing credit agency cannot allocate more credit than it determines is necessary for the project’s financial feasibility and long-term viability.

First-Year Rule

Credit in the first year can be reduced when qualifying units are not occupied for the full year. The balance attributable to first-year proration is generally claimed in the year following the normal ten-year credit period.

Nine-Percent and Four-Percent Credits

Competitive 9-Percent Credit

The 9-percent credit is ordinarily allocated through competitive state application rounds. Demand frequently exceeds available authority, and projects are ranked under QAP scoring, set-asides, preferences, and threshold requirements.

It generally supports new construction or substantial rehabilitation without federally subsidized financing. The minimum 9-percent rate is intended to produce a present value approximately equal to 70 percent of qualified basis over the ten-year credit period.

Bond-Financed 4-Percent Credit

The 4-percent credit generally accompanies qualified residential rental projects financed with tax-exempt private-activity bonds. These credits do not reduce the state’s ordinary housing credit ceiling when statutory bond-financing conditions are satisfied.

The minimum 4-percent rate is intended to produce a present value approximately equal to 30 percent of qualified basis over the ten-year credit period. Four-percent projects typically require substantial additional debt or gap financing because the credit generates less equity than the 9-percent credit.

Acquisition Credit

Acquisition of an existing building generally qualifies for the 4-percent credit when statutory ownership, related-party, holding-period, rehabilitation, and other requirements are satisfied. Rehabilitation expenditures are treated as a separate new building for credit purposes.

Tax-Exempt Bond Threshold

Historically, a building generally needed at least 50 percent of aggregate building-and-land basis financed with qualifying tax-exempt bonds to receive credits outside the state ceiling. Public Law 119-21 retained that rule while adding a 25-percent alternative for qualifying buildings placed in service in taxable years beginning after 2025 when statutory post-2025 bond conditions are met.

Not a Percentage of Total Cost

The “9-percent” and “4-percent” labels can be misleading. The applicable percentage is applied annually to qualified basis, not directly to total development cost, and the credit is claimed over ten years.

State Allocation Process

State Housing Credit Ceiling

Federal law establishes an annual ceiling based on state population and a minimum small-state amount, both subject to inflation adjustment. Beginning with calendar year 2026, Public Law 119-21 permanently multiplies the ordinary ceiling amount by 1.12.

Qualified Allocation Plan

The housing credit agency must allocate credits under a Qualified Allocation Plan approved after public notice and opportunity for comment. Federal law requires specified selection criteria and preferences, but states can add substantial policy requirements.

Federal Selection Criteria

Section 42 requires QAPs to consider criteria including:

  • Project location
  • Housing needs characteristics
  • Project characteristics
  • Sponsor characteristics
  • Tenant populations with special housing needs
  • Public-housing waiting lists
  • Tenant populations of individuals with children
  • Projects intended for eventual tenant ownership
  • Energy efficiency
  • Historic character

Federal Preferences

Federal law gives preference to projects serving the lowest-income tenants, obligated to serve qualified tenants for the longest periods, and located in Qualified Census Tracts with a concerted community-revitalization plan.

State Priorities

State QAPs commonly add priorities involving rural housing, preservation, permanent supportive housing, veterans, older adults, geographic distribution, transit access, high-opportunity areas, disaster recovery, energy performance, local funding, readiness, cost containment, nonprofit sponsorship, and deeper affordability.

Nonprofit Set-Aside

At least 10 percent of each state’s ordinary housing credit ceiling must be reserved for projects involving qualified nonprofit organizations that own an interest and materially participate in development and operation.

Underwriting and Subsidy Layering

Allocating agencies evaluate development costs, sources and uses, projected rents, operating expenses, reserves, debt, developer fees, investor pricing, and other subsidies. The agency must limit the allocation to the amount necessary for financial feasibility.

Carryover and Placed-in-Service Deadlines

A project receiving a carryover allocation generally must satisfy a statutory expenditure test and place the building in service by the end of the second calendar year after the allocation year, subject to disaster relief and other authorized extensions.

Minimum Set-Asides and Income Targeting

Project Election

An owner elects one of the federal minimum set-asides. Failure to satisfy the elected test by the required date can prevent the project from qualifying for LIHTC.

20–50 Test

At least 20 percent of the project’s residential units must be rent-restricted and occupied by households whose incomes do not exceed 50 percent of area median gross income.

40–60 Test

At least 40 percent of the project’s residential units must be rent-restricted and occupied by households whose incomes do not exceed 60 percent of area median gross income. For qualifying projects in New York City, the minimum unit share is 25 percent.

Average-Income Test

At least 40 percent of units must be designated as low-income at one or more levels of 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, or 80 percent of area median gross income. The average of the designated imputed income limitations cannot exceed 60 percent.

Minimum Versus Actual Commitment

The federal minimum set-aside establishes project eligibility, not necessarily the final number or targeting of assisted units. A project can restrict a larger share of units, serve lower incomes, or accept longer affordability through its application, allocation, financing agreements, or state requirements.

Income at Initial Occupancy

A household generally must satisfy the applicable income limit when initially occupying a designated unit. Annual income certifications and recertification rules depend on project type, state monitoring procedures, and statutory exceptions.

Income Growth After Occupancy

A household is not automatically displaced because its income later rises above the applicable limit. The available-unit rule can require the owner to rent the next comparable or smaller available unit to a qualifying household when an existing tenant’s income exceeds 140 percent of the applicable limitation.

Rent Restrictions

Maximum Gross Rent

The maximum gross rent generally equals 30 percent of the applicable income limitation attributed to the unit, adjusted through statutory imputed household-size rules and divided into a monthly amount.

Imputed Household Size

Section 42 generally imputes 1.5 persons per bedroom and one person for a unit without a separate bedroom. As a result, the maximum rent is based on the unit’s assumed occupancy rather than the actual number of people living there.

Actual Household Income

LIHTC rent is not ordinarily recalculated to equal 30 percent of an individual household’s actual income. A household with income well below the unit’s designation can therefore face a rent exceeding 30 percent of its own income.

Utility Allowances

When tenants pay utilities directly, an approved utility allowance is included in gross rent. The amount collected by the owner generally must be reduced so that rent plus the utility allowance does not exceed the LIHTC maximum.

Rental Assistance

A tenant can use a Housing Choice Voucher in an eligible LIHTC unit. Project-based rental assistance can also operate within a tax-credit development. Federal rules generally prevent an owner from refusing an otherwise eligible voucher holder merely because voucher assistance would cause the owner to receive more than the LIHTC tenant-rent limit, provided applicable Section 42 requirements are satisfied.

Mandatory Fees

Nonoptional charges connected to occupancy can be treated as part of gross rent. Owners must distinguish lawful optional services from mandatory fees that could cause rent to exceed the maximum.

Rent Limits and Inflation

HUD publishes Multifamily Tax Subsidy Project income limits used to determine LIHTC income and rent limits. Statutory “hold harmless” protections can prevent income and rent limits for older properties from declining under specified circumstances.

Tenant Application and Occupancy

Property-Level Application

There is no single national LIHTC tenant application. Prospective tenants generally apply directly to the owner or management agent of an individual property. State agencies can provide property lists but ordinarily do not place tenants into units.

Income Verification

Management verifies household composition, income, assets, student status, and other eligibility factors. Documentation may include employment records, benefit statements, asset information, tax records, affidavits, or third-party verification.

Tenant Selection

Owners can apply lawful screening criteria concerning rental history, credit, criminal history, references, occupancy, and ability to comply with the lease. Screening must comply with fair-housing, disability, consumer-reporting, state, local, and other applicable law.

Waiting Lists

Individual properties commonly maintain waiting lists. Some serve designated populations or receive referrals through supportive-housing, homelessness, disability, veterans, public-housing, or other systems.

Student Rule

A unit occupied entirely by full-time students generally cannot qualify as a low-income unit unless the household satisfies a statutory exception, such as specified assistance, single-parent, foster-care, job-training, or marital-status circumstances.

General Public Use

Qualifying units generally must be available for use by the general public and cannot be restricted to members of a particular social organization or employer group. Housing for specified populations can qualify when consistent with Section 42 and fair-housing law.

Lease Terms

Units must be used other than on a transient basis. Owners cannot evict or terminate tenancy except for good cause during the extended-use period where the statutory commitment applies.

Compliance and Monitoring

Owner Records

Owners must maintain building and unit records concerning eligible basis, qualified basis, unit status, household income, rents, utilities, vacancies, student status, occupancy, certifications, and other compliance matters.

Annual Certification

Owners submit annual certifications to the housing credit agency affirming compliance with income, rent, occupancy, habitability, use, nondiscrimination, and extended-use requirements.

Agency Inspection

Housing credit agencies inspect a sample of low-income units and review tenant files according to federal monitoring rules. Agencies can impose additional monitoring requirements through their QAPs and compliance manuals.

Physical Condition

Units must be suitable for occupancy and comply with applicable local health, safety, and building codes. Serious deficiencies can make units ineligible and generate reportable noncompliance.

Form 8823

When a housing credit agency identifies reportable noncompliance or a building disposition, it files Form 8823 with the IRS. The form identifies the category, correction status, and relevant dates.

Correction Period

Owners generally receive an opportunity to correct identified noncompliance. Correction can limit ongoing consequences but does not necessarily erase prior noncompliance for tax purposes.

IRS Enforcement

The IRS determines federal tax consequences. State agencies monitor and report; they do not make the final federal determination concerning credit disallowance, recapture, penalties, or taxpayer liability.

Recapture Risk

A decrease in qualified basis during the compliance period can trigger recapture of a portion of previously claimed credits plus interest. Investor agreements commonly allocate compliance responsibility and require guarantees or indemnities from development sponsors.

Extended Use and Preservation

Thirty-Year Minimum

For most post-1989 allocations, the housing credit agency and owner execute an extended low-income housing commitment producing at least thirty years of affordability: the fifteen-year compliance period plus an additional extended-use period.

Longer State Restrictions

States frequently award points or impose requirements for affordability lasting forty, fifty, fifty-five, or more years. Other loans, grants, land covenants, or rental-assistance contracts can impose separate restrictions.

Year Fifteen

The end of the federal compliance period commonly changes ownership, investor, guaranty, refinancing, and tax-risk relationships. It does not automatically terminate the recorded affordability covenant.

Qualified-Contract Process

Section 42 historically allowed certain owners after year fourteen to request that the state agency locate a buyer at a statutory formula price. If no qualified contract was presented within the prescribed period, the extended-use agreement could terminate, subject to a three-year tenant-protection period.

Many states have restricted or eliminated qualified-contract rights through QAP requirements, allocation documents, waivers, or state law. The availability and effect of the process therefore vary by property and jurisdiction.

Right of First Refusal

Section 42 permits qualifying nonprofit organizations, tenant organizations, government agencies, and specified parties to hold a right of first refusal to acquire a property after the compliance period at a statutory minimum price. Transaction disputes can concern the difference between a right of first refusal and an ordinary purchase option.

Preservation Risk

Properties approaching the end of restrictions can face conversion pressure, deferred maintenance, refinancing needs, investor exits, ownership disputes, or market-rate repositioning. Preservation strategies include resyndication, new credits, tax-exempt bonds, acquisition funds, rental assistance, public purchase, and extended affordability agreements.

Development Types and Uses

New Construction

LIHTC finances new apartment buildings, garden apartments, townhouses, scattered-site housing, mixed-use developments, and other rental configurations.

Acquisition and Rehabilitation

Credits can preserve aging affordable housing, convert existing buildings, rehabilitate distressed properties, or recapitalize developments with expiring subsidies.

Mixed-Income Housing

A project can combine LIHTC units with market-rate or differently subsidized units. The applicable fraction limits credit to the qualifying low-income portion.

Supportive Housing

LIHTC frequently finances permanent supportive housing when combined with rental or operating assistance and service funding. Tax-credit equity does not itself pay for long-term supportive services.

Senior Housing

Properties can serve older adults when they satisfy federal fair-housing requirements for housing for older persons and applicable QAP standards.

Rural Housing

Credits can preserve or develop rural rental housing, including properties receiving United States Department of Agriculture financing or rental assistance. Rural projects can face smaller scale, higher per-unit costs, limited investor demand, and operating challenges.

Historic Rehabilitation

LIHTC can be combined with federal or state historic rehabilitation tax credits when a property and transaction satisfy both programs.

Adaptive Reuse

Developers can convert schools, warehouses, hotels, hospitals, offices, and other buildings into qualifying rental housing, subject to land-use, building, financing, and basis rules.

Racial and Geographic Context

Affordable-Housing Access

LIHTC provides income-restricted rental housing to populations affected by unequal income, wealth, credit access, and housing markets. African American and Latino households are substantially represented among residents because they experience disproportionate rent burdens and barriers to homeownership and private rental housing.

Location Debate

Project location affects access to employment, transportation, education, healthcare, environmental quality, grocery stores, public safety, and community networks. State allocation systems therefore influence whether affordable housing is concentrated in high-poverty neighborhoods, preserved in gentrifying communities, or developed in higher-opportunity areas.

Qualified Census Tracts

QCT basis boosts and community-revitalization preferences can direct investment into low-income neighborhoods. Supporters view this as essential reinvestment and preservation; critics warn that concentrating new affordable housing in distressed areas can reproduce segregation if not paired with meaningful revitalization.

High-Opportunity Areas

Many QAPs award points for developments in low-poverty areas, near high-performing schools, transit, employment, healthcare, or other resources. Such projects can face land costs, zoning restrictions, local opposition, infrastructure requirements, and political resistance.

Displacement and Preservation

Acquisition and rehabilitation can preserve affordability in neighborhoods experiencing rising rents. Poorly structured rehabilitation or redevelopment can also disrupt residents through temporary relocation, screening changes, construction conditions, or loss of units.

Fair-Housing Obligations

Allocating agencies, local governments, owners, managers, lenders, and other participants operate within federal and state civil-rights law. Site selection, marketing, tenant selection, accessibility, local-approval requirements, occupancy restrictions, and screening can create fair-housing concerns.

Community Preference

Local-residency or community preferences can help existing residents remain in changing neighborhoods. They can also perpetuate segregation when a jurisdiction’s population reflects historical exclusion. Preferences require careful fair-housing analysis.

Benefits and Limitations

Housing Production

LIHTC has financed millions of rental units and provides a durable national platform for new construction and rehabilitation.

Private Capital

The credit attracts equity from banks, insurance companies, corporations, and investment funds, reducing the public cash subsidy required for each development.

State Flexibility

Qualified Allocation Plans permit states to target local housing needs and revise priorities through public processes.

Long-Term Restrictions

Recorded extended-use agreements preserve affordability beyond the ten-year credit-claim period.

Complexity and Transaction Cost

LIHTC transactions involve tax counsel, accountants, syndicators, investors, lenders, appraisers, market analysts, architects, consultants, agencies, and multiple legal entities. These costs can reduce efficiency, especially for small projects.

Not Deeply Affordable by Itself

Maximum rents tied to 50, 60, or higher percentages of area income may be unaffordable to extremely low-income households. Operating or rental subsidies are often necessary to serve households with little or no income.

Dependence on Investor Demand

Credit pricing can change with corporate tax rates, bank investment demand, Community Reinvestment Act considerations, economic conditions, perceived risk, and the supply of credits.

Cost Control

High land, labor, materials, financing, insurance, regulatory, and professional costs can reduce the number of units produced. Agencies use cost limits and underwriting, but projects differ substantially by location and population served.

Monitoring Fragmentation

The IRS administers tax liability, state agencies monitor compliance, HUD supplies income limits and data, and other funders impose separate rules. Fragmentation can create inconsistent procedures and gaps in public transparency.

Expiration Risk

Properties can lose affordability after extended-use restrictions expire or through qualifying termination procedures. Preservation requires early planning and additional capital.

Tenant Information

Residents may find LIHTC rules difficult to understand because tax-credit restrictions coexist with leases, vouchers, state requirements, and other subsidies. Clear notice and accessible grievance or enforcement channels vary across jurisdictions.

Major Policy Debates

Production Versus Tenant Assistance

LIHTC expands the supply of restricted housing, while vouchers reduce rent burdens for individual households. Policy debates concern the appropriate balance between capital subsidies and direct rental assistance.

High-Poverty and High-Opportunity Locations

Allocation agencies must balance reinvestment in historically disinvested communities with expansion into areas offering stronger schools, employment, environmental conditions, and other opportunities.

Federal Uniformity and State Control

State flexibility allows local adaptation but produces different application systems, cost standards, compliance rules, tenant protections, preservation policies, and geographic outcomes.

Investor Efficiency

Supporters argue that private underwriting and capital impose discipline and leverage public resources. Critics argue that investor returns, syndication costs, legal complexity, and tax-market volatility make direct federal spending more efficient.

Permanent Affordability

Thirty-year restrictions are long relative to the credit period but temporary relative to continuing housing need. Some jurisdictions require longer terms or pursue public, nonprofit, cooperative, or land-trust ownership.

Qualified Contracts

Owners view the qualified-contract process as a statutory exit mechanism built into earlier transactions. Preservation advocates argue that it can terminate affordability earlier than communities and residents expect.

Development Cost

Debate concerns whether higher costs reflect necessary accessibility, durability, wage, environmental, service, urban, and financing requirements or whether procurement, regulation, fees, and transaction design reduce program efficiency.

Community Opposition

Local support requirements can give municipalities influence over development but can also enable exclusionary opposition. Federal tax law does not require housing credit agencies to reject projects lacking local approval.

Key Distinctions

LIHTC and Housing Choice Vouchers

LIHTC subsidizes the development and preservation of designated units through investor tax credits. Housing Choice Vouchers provide tenant-based monthly rental assistance to eligible households. A voucher can be used in a qualifying LIHTC unit.

LIHTC and Public Housing

Public housing is generally owned or controlled by public housing agencies and supported through federal operating and capital funds. LIHTC properties are typically privately or nonprofit owned and financed through tax-credit equity and other sources.

LIHTC and Project-Based Section 8

LIHTC provides capital financing and rent restrictions. Project-Based Section 8 provides recurring rental assistance attached to units. The programs can be layered in the same property.

LIHTC and a Tax Deduction

A tax credit reduces federal income-tax liability dollar for dollar, subject to applicable rules. A deduction reduces taxable income. LIHTC investors can receive credits and separately allocated tax losses.

9-Percent and 4-Percent Credits

The 9-percent credit generally provides more equity and is competitively allocated from the state ceiling. The 4-percent credit generally produces less equity and is commonly paired with tax-exempt private-activity bonds.

Income Limit and Rent Limit

An income limit determines whether a household qualifies for a designated unit. A rent limit establishes the maximum gross rent for the unit. The tenant’s actual income does not ordinarily determine the maximum LIHTC rent.

Compliance Period and Extended-Use Period

The federal compliance period is fifteen years and carries recapture risk. The extended-use agreement ordinarily continues affordability for at least fifteen additional years and can last longer under state or financing requirements.

LIHTC and Affordable Housing

Affordable housing is a broad category that includes LIHTC properties, public housing, Section 8 developments, nonprofit housing, inclusionary units, cooperative housing, community land trusts, and naturally occurring lower-cost rentals.

Housing Credit Agency and HUD

State and local agencies allocate and monitor LIHTC. The IRS administers the federal tax credit. HUD does not allocate the credit but publishes income limits, designates QCTs and DDAs, and maintains national property and tenant data.

Geographic Scope

LIHTC operates throughout the United States, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and participating United States possessions. Federal law provides the credit framework, while each jurisdiction develops its own allocation process.

Properties are located in central cities, suburbs, small towns, rural communities, tribal areas, high-cost markets, disaster-recovery areas, and regions experiencing population or employment decline.

Geographic distribution reflects state QAP priorities, land costs, zoning, tax-exempt bond authority, market demand, local subsidies, developer capacity, investor demand, infrastructure, and political approval.

HUD designates Qualified Census Tracts and Difficult Development Areas annually. These designations affect potential basis boosts but do not guarantee an allocation or establish that every project in the area is financially feasible.

Some states divide credits among geographic regions or rural and urban pools. Others use statewide competitions with location scoring. Local permitting and zoning remain critical even when federal and state financing is available.

Legal Context

Internal Revenue Code Section 42

Section 42 establishes the credit calculation, state ceiling, allocation process, income and rent restrictions, minimum set-asides, compliance period, recapture rules, nonprofit set-aside, bond-financing rules, Qualified Allocation Plans, extended-use requirements, and related definitions.

Tax Reform Act of 1986

The Act created LIHTC while restructuring federal tax incentives and limiting several earlier real-estate tax benefits.

Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1989

This law made extensive technical changes and established the extended low-income housing commitment for later allocations, strengthening long-term affordability.

Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993

This law made the previously temporary LIHTC program permanent.

Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008

HERA temporarily increased credit ceilings, expanded state basis-boost authority, revised acquisition and income-recertification rules, and required housing credit agencies to submit tenant data to HUD.

Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2018

This law temporarily increased state credit ceilings and created the average-income test.

Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021

This law established a permanent 4-percent minimum applicable percentage for qualifying buildings placed in service after December 31, 2020.

Public Law 119-21

Section 70422 permanently increases the ordinary state housing credit ceiling by 12 percent for calendar years after 2025. It also permits qualifying buildings to access credits outside the state ceiling when at least 25 percent of aggregate building-and-land basis is financed with eligible bonds and the statute’s post-2025 bond conditions are satisfied. The revised threshold applies to qualifying buildings placed in service in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025.

Fair Housing Act

The Fair Housing Act prohibits specified forms of discrimination in rental housing, advertising, financing, design and construction, and related housing activity. LIHTC owners, managers, developers, agencies, lenders, and other participants can be subject to its requirements.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI applies when an agency or project receives covered federal financial assistance in addition to tax credits. State housing agencies and other public entities can also have separate civil-rights obligations.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Section 504 applies to covered federally assisted developments and can require accessibility, reasonable accommodation, and nondiscrimination beyond the requirements arising solely from LIHTC.

Americans with Disabilities Act

The ADA applies to covered public entities, public accommodations, leasing offices, services, and other activities connected with development and property operations.

Violence Against Women Act

VAWA applies when a LIHTC property also participates in a covered federal housing program or is otherwise brought within applicable statutory requirements. State agencies can impose additional protections through allocation or financing documents.

Summary

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is the principal federal incentive for financing income-restricted rental housing. Established in 1986 and codified in Internal Revenue Code Section 42, the program exchanges federal tax benefits for private equity investment and long-term affordability obligations.

State and local housing credit agencies select projects through Qualified Allocation Plans, limit awards to financial need, execute extended-use agreements, and monitor compliance. Developers and investors use partnership structures to convert future tax credits into current equity for acquisition, construction, rehabilitation, and preservation.

The competitive 9-percent credit generally supports new construction and substantial rehabilitation. The 4-percent credit generally supports acquisition and developments financed with tax-exempt private-activity bonds. Beginning in 2026, federal law permanently increases state credit ceilings by 12 percent and permits a qualifying 25-percent bond-financing threshold under specified conditions.

LIHTC units must satisfy income, rent, occupancy, general-public-use, physical-condition, recordkeeping, and extended-use requirements. Owners claim credits over ten years, remain subject to federal recapture risk during a fifteen-year compliance period, and ordinarily restrict the property for at least thirty years.

The program has financed millions of rental units and established a nationwide affordable-housing delivery system. Its limitations include transaction complexity, uneven state administration, high development costs, temporary affordability, geographic concentration, and insufficient rent depth for households with extremely low incomes.

LIHTC is therefore best understood as a housing-production and preservation mechanism rather than a complete rental-affordability system. Its strongest projects combine tax-credit equity with rental assistance, public gap financing, sound location policy, fair-housing compliance, durable ownership, and long-term preservation planning.
