Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act Programs

The federal workforce-development system connecting job seekers, workers, youth, adults with limited basic skills, people with disabilities, employers, education providers, and public agencies through American Job Centers, employment services, training, adult education, and vocational rehabilitation.

Overview

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act, commonly abbreviated WIOA, is the principal federal law organizing the public workforce-development system of the United States. It supports employment services, job search, career counseling, occupational training, apprenticeships, work experience, adult education, youth services, vocational rehabilitation, employer assistance, labor-market information, and coordinated access through American Job Centers.

President Barack Obama signed WIOA into law on July 22, 2014. The Act received broad bipartisan support and replaced the Workforce Investment Act of 1998 as the principal federal workforce statute. Most provisions took effect July 1, 2015, and comprehensive final regulations were issued in 2016.

WIOA is both a collection of programs and a governance framework. It directly authorizes major employment and training programs, reauthorizes adult education, amends the Wagner-Peyser Act Employment Service, amends the Rehabilitation Act’s vocational rehabilitation system, and requires coordinated state planning, performance measures, workforce boards, one-stop partnerships, and shared service delivery.

The law contains four principal titles. Title I covers state and local workforce-development activities, Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth formula programs, Job Corps, YouthBuild, Indian and Native American Programs, the National Farmworker Jobs Program, National Dislocated Worker Grants, and other national activities. Title II contains the Adult Education and Family Literacy Act. Title III amends the Wagner-Peyser Act. Title IV amends the Rehabilitation Act of 1973.

Six programs are designated as the WIOA core programs: Adult, Dislocated Worker, Youth, Adult Education and Family Literacy, Wagner-Peyser Employment Service, and State Vocational Rehabilitation Services. The six share state-planning and performance-accountability requirements.

American Job Centers, also called one-stop career centers, are the public-facing access points for WIOA and partner programs. The Department of Labor identifies approximately 2,400 centers nationwide. Customers can receive information, job-search assistance, career services, referrals, labor-market data, unemployment-insurance assistance, employer services, and access to training and supportive-service programs.

WIOA does not create a single universal cash benefit or guarantee that every applicant will receive funded training. Many labor-exchange and self-service resources are broadly available, while individualized career services, supportive services, and training depend on eligibility, assessment, local policy, priority rules, provider availability, and funding.

Under the Adult Program, individuals age eighteen and older may receive services. When Adult funds are used for individualized career services and training, priority must be given to public-assistance recipients, other low-income individuals, and people who are basic-skills deficient. Veterans and eligible spouses also receive statutory priority in Department of Labor-funded employment and training programs.

The Dislocated Worker Program serves people affected by layoffs, plant closures, major economic change, military transition, self-employment failure, and displacement from unpaid household work. It emphasizes rapid reemployment, retraining, and transition into occupations with current demand.

The Youth Program serves eligible in-school and out-of-school youth facing barriers to education and employment. Local areas generally must spend at least 75 percent of local Youth funds on out-of-school youth and at least 20 percent on work-experience activities. Fourteen program elements must be available.

Title II supports adult basic education, adult secondary education, English-language acquisition, family literacy, correctional education, integrated education and training, and integrated English literacy and civics education.

Title III integrates Wagner-Peyser Employment Service staff and labor-exchange services into American Job Centers. Services include job listings, referrals, recruitment, job-search assistance, labor-market information, employer services, and support for unemployment-insurance work-test and reemployment requirements.

Title IV reauthorized and amended the public vocational rehabilitation system. State vocational rehabilitation agencies assist eligible people with disabilities in preparing for, obtaining, retaining, advancing in, or regaining competitive integrated employment. States must reserve at least 15 percent of federal State Vocational Rehabilitation funds for pre-employment transition services.

WIOA gives state and local governments substantial operational authority. Governors designate local workforce-development areas, appoint state boards, submit four-year Unified or Combined State Plans, distribute formula funds, negotiate performance targets, and oversee statewide initiatives. Local boards plan services, select one-stop operators, develop employer strategies, and monitor local outcomes.

Business representatives constitute a majority of state and local workforce boards. Labor organizations, apprenticeship representatives, education providers, government agencies, and community organizations also participate. The business-majority structure is intended to connect training with industry demand but raises debate about worker voice and public accountability.

WIOA eliminated the former requirement that a participant complete a rigid sequence of core and intensive services before receiving training. Training can be approved when assessment determines that it is necessary and appropriate.

Many participants receive training through an Individual Training Account used to choose a program from a state Eligible Training Provider List. Other training can be delivered through on-the-job training, customized training, incumbent-worker training, Registered Apprenticeship, transitional jobs, cohort contracts, and other authorized arrangements.

WIOA also permits supportive services when necessary for participation and unavailable through other sources. Depending on local policy, assistance can include transportation, childcare, housing, tools, work clothing, testing fees, educational supplies, technology, and needs-related payments.

For Program Year 2026, Congress appropriated approximately $948.13 million for WIOA Youth Activities, $875.649 million for Adult Activities, $1.396412 billion for Dislocated Worker Activities, and $675.052 million for Wagner-Peyser Employment Service grants. These four appropriations total approximately $3.895 billion before Title II, Title IV, Job Corps, YouthBuild, and other national programs are counted.

The latest Department of Labor national summary reports that the Adult, Dislocated Worker, Youth, and Wagner-Peyser programs served more than 2.7 million participants during Program Year 2024. The total does not include every Title II or Title IV participant and can include co-enrolled individuals.

In 2025, the Departments of Education and Labor implemented an interagency workforce-development partnership. Labor began providing selected day-to-day administrative services for Adult Education and Family Literacy programs while Education retained statutory authority, policymaking, program determinations, and oversight.

WIOA remains in force in 2026. H.R. 8210, the A Stronger Workforce for America Act of 2026, was introduced in April 2026 and ordered reported by the House Committee on Education and the Workforce. As of August 2, 2026, it had not become law.

WIOA has major racial and economic significance because Black workers and communities experience disproportionate unemployment, layoffs, occupational segregation, incarceration, school inequality, limited wealth, and barriers to transportation and training. Formally race-neutral eligibility can direct resources toward people affected by these conditions, but equitable outcomes depend on outreach, local priorities, provider quality, supportive services, representation, employer practices, and civil-rights enforcement.

Definitions

Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act
The 2014 law organizing employment, training, adult education, public employment service, vocational rehabilitation, workforce planning, and accountability.
Core program
One of six programs subject to common state-planning and performance-accountability requirements.
American Job Center
A physical or virtual access point in the WIOA one-stop system.
One-stop delivery system
The state and local network coordinating required partner programs through comprehensive and affiliate centers and electronic access.
Workforce-development area
A state-designated region governed by a local workforce-development board and chief elected officials.
State workforce-development board
A governor-appointed body advising on statewide plans, policy, alignment, performance, and system development.
Local workforce-development board
A business-majority local body overseeing workforce strategy, American Job Centers, provider policy, and employer engagement.
One-stop operator
The competitively selected entity or consortium coordinating partner service delivery.
Unified State Plan
A four-year plan covering the six WIOA core programs.
Combined State Plan
A plan covering the core programs plus additional workforce or human-service programs.
Individual with a barrier to employment
A person within a statutory category such as low income, disability, justice involvement, homelessness, foster care, English-language need, farmwork, or long-term unemployment.
Basic-skills deficient
Unable to read, write, speak English, compute, or solve problems at a level necessary to function effectively at work, in family life, or in society.
Dislocated worker
A person meeting an authorized layoff, closure, self-employment loss, displaced-homemaker, military-transition, or related category.
In-school youth
An eligible youth generally age fourteen through twenty-one who is attending school, is low income, and has a statutory barrier.
Out-of-school youth
An eligible youth generally age sixteen through twenty-four who is not attending school and has a statutory barrier.
Career services
Basic and individualized employment services including assessment, counseling, job search, planning, work experience, referrals, and follow-up.
Training services
Occupational, apprenticeship, work-based, entrepreneurial, literacy-integrated, and other authorized skill-development activities.
Individual Training Account
A payment mechanism allowing an eligible participant to select approved training from an Eligible Training Provider List.
Eligible Training Provider List
The state-maintained public list of approved providers and programs.
Career pathway
An aligned sequence of education, training, credentials, supportive services, employment, and advancement.
Sector partnership
A regional employer-led collaboration involving workforce, education, labor, and community partners.
Registered Apprenticeship
Paid employment combining structured on-the-job learning with related instruction under federal or state registration.
Supportive services
Assistance addressing practical barriers to participation, such as transportation, childcare, housing, tools, technology, or testing.
Rapid Response
State and local services responding to layoffs, closures, disasters, and other dislocations.
National Dislocated Worker Grant
A time-limited discretionary grant responding to major economic dislocation or disaster.
Recognized postsecondary credential
A degree, license, industry certification, apprenticeship certificate, or other recognized credential.
Measurable skill gain
Documented academic, technical, occupational, or other progress toward a credential or employment.
Competitive integrated employment
Community employment with comparable wages, interaction, and advancement for people with disabilities.

The Six Core Programs

Adult Program

Provides employment and training services to adults, with mandatory priority for public-assistance recipients, other low-income individuals, and people who are basic-skills deficient when individualized career and training services are provided.

Dislocated Worker Program

Assists workers who have lost employment or face significant economic dislocation and need reemployment, retraining, credentialing, or career transition.

Youth Program

Provides education, work experience, occupational training, mentoring, supportive services, and follow-up to eligible youth with barriers to employment.

Adult Education and Family Literacy

Helps adults build literacy, numeracy, English-language, digital, secondary-completion, civics, and workforce skills.

Wagner-Peyser Employment Service

Provides universal labor-exchange and employer services through the public workforce system.

Vocational Rehabilitation

Provides individualized employment services to eligible people with disabilities and transition services to students and youth with disabilities.

Shared Accountability

The six programs participate in common state planning and aligned measures involving employment, earnings, credentials, skill gains, and employer service.

Governance Structure

Federal Government

Labor and Education issue regulations, allot funds, approve plans, negotiate performance levels, monitor grants, provide technical assistance, and enforce program requirements.

Governors

Governors appoint state boards, designate local areas, submit state plans, distribute formula funds, approve transfers, reserve statewide and Rapid Response funds, negotiate performance, and oversee statewide policy.

State Boards

State boards advise on plans, career pathways, sector strategies, one-stop standards, labor-market information, accessibility, performance, and continuous improvement.

Local Boards

Local boards develop strategy, oversee American Job Centers, select one-stop operators, coordinate employers and education, establish provider policy, monitor performance, and arrange youth services.

Business Majority

Business representatives constitute a majority of state and local boards. Labor, apprenticeships, education, government, and community representatives also participate.

Fiscal Agents

Local governments, workforce agencies, nonprofits, or other entities can receive and manage funds under written agreements and accountability requirements.

State and Local Planning

Four-Year Plan

Each state submits a four-year Unified or Combined State Plan describing economic conditions, workforce needs, strategies, administration, performance, and coordination.

Two-Year Modification

States update plans midway through the cycle to reflect labor-market changes, funding, performance, waivers, and policy developments.

Unified Plan

A Unified Plan covers all six core programs.

Combined Plan

A Combined Plan can include additional programs such as TANF, career and technical education, unemployment insurance, senior employment, veterans’ services, and community-development activities.

2026–2027 Modifications

States are modifying plans for Program Years 2026 and 2027 through the integrated federal portal hosted by Labor.

Local and Regional Plans

Local boards describe regional labor demand, services, partnerships, providers, youth strategy, employer engagement, accessibility, and performance.

Public Participation

Plans are subject to public review and comment, creating a formal role for workers, employers, educators, providers, and community organizations.

American Job Centers and the One-Stop System

Universal Access

American Job Centers provide public access to job listings, labor-market information, self-service resources, and partner-program information without requiring every visitor to qualify for funded training.

Common Services

  • Job listings and referrals
  • Résumé and application assistance
  • Career counseling
  • Skills assessment
  • Labor-market and wage information
  • Employer recruitment and hiring events
  • Training and financial-aid information
  • Unemployment-insurance assistance
  • Veterans’ services
  • Adult-education and rehabilitation referrals
  • Computer and online access

Comprehensive Centers

Each local area must have at least one comprehensive center providing access to required partner programs and specified Title I and Wagner-Peyser services.

Affiliate and Virtual Sites

Libraries, colleges, community organizations, government offices, mobile units, and online systems can extend local access.

Accessibility

Centers must provide physical, communication, programmatic, and technological access for people with disabilities and meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency.

Partner Agreements

Required partners enter memoranda addressing services, referrals, shared costs, infrastructure, data, and coordination.

Certification

State boards establish criteria for local certification and continuous improvement of one-stop systems.

Required One-Stop Partners

The one-stop system coordinates numerous programs. Required partners include:

  • WIOA Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth programs
  • Wagner-Peyser Employment Service
  • Adult Education and Family Literacy
  • Vocational Rehabilitation
  • Job Corps
  • YouthBuild
  • Indian and Native American Programs
  • National Farmworker Jobs Program
  • Senior Community Service Employment Program
  • Postsecondary career and technical education
  • Trade Adjustment Assistance
  • Jobs for Veterans State Grants
  • Unemployment Compensation programs
  • Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, subject to statutory state options
  • Community Services Block Grant employment and training activities
  • Housing and Urban Development employment and training programs
  • Second Chance Act and related reentry activities

Separate Statutes

Coordination does not merge every appropriation or eligibility rule. Each partner retains its own purpose, records, funds, and federal oversight.

Co-Enrollment

A person can be served by multiple programs when eligible, allowing one program to finance training while another supplies literacy, rehabilitation, transportation, childcare, or employment services.

WIOA Adult Program

Purpose

The Adult Program helps people obtain employment, advance, increase earnings, and gain occupational skills while helping employers meet workforce needs.

Eligibility

An individual generally must be age eighteen or older and satisfy citizenship or work-authorization and Selective Service requirements when applicable.

Priority

For individualized career and training services, local areas must prioritize public-assistance recipients, other low-income individuals, and people who are basic-skills deficient. Veterans and eligible spouses receive separate priority.

Basic Career Services

Services include intake, eligibility determination, initial assessment, labor exchange, labor-market information, referrals, and self-service resources.

Individualized Career Services

Participants may receive comprehensive assessment, employment planning, counseling, internships, workforce preparation, English-language services, financial literacy, relocation assistance, and follow-up.

Training

Eligible participants needing training to obtain or retain employment can receive occupational instruction, apprenticeship, or work-based learning.

Employed Workers

Employment does not automatically bar participation. Underemployed, low-wage, basic-skills-deficient, and incumbent workers can be served under local policy.

WIOA Dislocated Worker Program

Purpose

The Dislocated Worker Program helps workers return to employment quickly or transition into new occupations after layoffs, closures, economic change, or other dislocation.

Common Categories

  • Workers laid off and unlikely to return to the prior industry or occupation
  • Workers affected by permanent closure or substantial layoff
  • Formerly self-employed individuals whose work ended because of economic conditions or disaster
  • Displaced homemakers
  • Transitioning service members and certain military spouses
  • Other categories established by statute and state policy

No General Poverty Test

Eligibility is based principally on job loss and dislocation rather than low income, although income can affect local supportive-service policy.

Services

Career assessment, job search, counseling, occupational training, credentialing, relocation, work-based learning, supportive services, and unemployment-insurance coordination may be provided.

Transfer Authority

With gubernatorial approval, local areas may transfer up to 100 percent between Adult and Dislocated Worker formula funds.

Program Year 2026

The total appropriation is $1.396412 billion, including state formula funds and a National Reserve.

Rapid Response and National Dislocated Worker Grants

Early Intervention

Rapid Response teams contact employers, workers, unions, and communities when layoffs or closures are announced.

Services

  • Worker information sessions
  • Unemployment-insurance guidance
  • Job-search and résumé assistance
  • Training and aid information
  • Worker surveys and skills analysis
  • Hiring events
  • Labor-management transition committees
  • Health, pension, legal, and community referrals

State Reserve

States may reserve up to 25 percent of Dislocated Worker formula funds for statewide Rapid Response activities.

Employment Recovery Grants

National Dislocated Worker Grants can expand capacity after mass layoffs, plant closures, military realignments, or unusually high demand.

Disaster Recovery Grants

Disaster grants can finance temporary recovery employment and career or training services for affected workers.

Supplemental Nature

National grants are time-limited supplements rather than permanent replacements for formula programs.

WIOA Youth Program

Purpose

The Youth Program helps eligible young people overcome barriers, complete education, gain work experience, obtain credentials, enter postsecondary education, and secure employment.

Out-of-School Youth

Out-of-school youth generally must be ages sixteen through twenty-four, not attending school, and have at least one statutory barrier.

In-School Youth

In-school youth generally must be ages fourteen through twenty-one, attending school, low income, and have at least one statutory barrier.

Out-of-School Priority

Local areas generally must spend at least 75 percent of local Youth funds on out-of-school youth.

Work Experience

At least 20 percent of local Youth funds must support paid or unpaid work experiences and associated education.

Assessment and Strategy

Programs conduct objective assessment and develop an individual service strategy connected to education, training, and employment goals.

Follow-Up

Follow-up services must be available for at least twelve months after exit.

Program Year 2026

The Youth appropriation totals $948.13 million, including state and outlying-area allotments and Native American and farmworker-youth reservations.

Fourteen Youth Program Elements

  1. Tutoring, study skills, instruction, and dropout-prevention or recovery strategies leading to a diploma or recognized equivalent
  2. Alternative secondary-school or dropout-recovery services
  3. Paid and unpaid work experiences, including summer and year-round employment, pre-apprenticeship, internships, job shadowing, and on-the-job training
  4. Occupational skill training
  5. Education offered concurrently with workforce preparation and occupational training
  6. Leadership-development opportunities
  7. Supportive services
  8. Adult mentoring for at least twelve months
  9. Follow-up services for at least twelve months
  10. Comprehensive guidance and counseling
  11. Financial-literacy education
  12. Entrepreneurial-skills training
  13. Labor-market and employment information about in-demand industries and occupations
  14. Preparation for transition to postsecondary education and training

Availability

All fourteen elements must be available, but each participant receives the elements identified as necessary in the individual service strategy.

Providers

Local boards can contract with schools, nonprofits, community colleges, employers, apprenticeship sponsors, and other qualified organizations.

Title I National and Discretionary Programs

Job Corps

A residential education and career-training program for eligible low-income youth and young adults.

YouthBuild

A community-based program combining education, occupational training, leadership, and service, often through construction of affordable housing and community assets.

Indian and Native American Programs

Grants support employment, training, education, and community development for American Indians, Alaska Natives, and Native Hawaiians.

National Farmworker Jobs Program

Grants provide career, training, youth, housing, and supportive services to eligible migrant and seasonal farmworkers and dependents.

National Dislocated Worker Grants

Discretionary grants respond to major layoffs, disasters, military realignment, and other workforce emergencies.

Reentry Employment Opportunities

Demonstration and grant activities support employment preparation for justice-involved youth and adults.

Research and Demonstration

WIOA authorizes workforce research, evaluations, pilots, technical assistance, data improvement, and innovative strategies.

Distinct Rules

National programs do not use the same local formula or participant rules as the Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth formula programs.

Title II: Adult Education and Family Literacy

Purpose

Title II creates a federal-state-local partnership helping adults become literate, obtain employment and self-sufficiency skills, complete secondary education, transition to postsecondary education and training, and support children’s learning.

Activities

  • Adult basic education
  • Adult secondary education
  • English-language acquisition
  • Family literacy
  • Workforce preparation
  • Integrated education and training
  • Integrated English literacy and civics education
  • Correctional and institutional education

Eligible Providers

States conduct competitions involving school systems, community colleges, libraries, nonprofits, volunteer literacy groups, public housing authorities, correctional institutions, and other qualified providers.

Integrated Education and Training

IET combines adult education, workforce preparation, and occupational training within an organized program.

Local Coordination

Providers coordinate with local workforce boards and American Job Centers and align services with local plans.

Current Administration

Education retains statutory authority, policy, determinations, and oversight. Labor provides selected day-to-day administrative and grant-system services under the 2025 interagency agreement.

Funding Context

Adult Education State Grants were funded at $715.455 million in fiscal years 2023, 2024, and 2025 according to current federal budget materials.

Title III: Wagner-Peyser Employment Service

Public Labor Exchange

The Employment Service connects job seekers with openings and employers with workers through state agencies and American Job Centers.

Job-Seeker Services

  • Job search and placement
  • Career counseling
  • Job-bank access
  • Skills assessment
  • Labor-market information
  • Training referrals
  • Reemployment services for unemployment claimants

Employer Services

  • Job-order posting
  • Candidate referral
  • Recruitment events
  • Skills matching
  • Labor-market information
  • Rapid Response coordination
  • Tax-credit and workforce-program information

Unemployment Insurance

Employment Service staff support work-test, profiling, referrals, and meaningful claim-filing assistance.

Program Year 2026

The appropriation totals $675.052 million. After specified set-asides, approximately $673.252 million is distributed to states and approximately $1.645 million to Guam and the United States Virgin Islands.

State Use

States generally use 90 percent for labor-exchange and related career services and reserve 10 percent for gubernatorial activities involving incentives, special needs, exemplary models, and staff development.

Title IV: Vocational Rehabilitation

Purpose

State Vocational Rehabilitation assists eligible people with disabilities in achieving employment consistent with their strengths, priorities, abilities, interests, and informed choice.

Eligibility

An individual generally must have an impairment creating a substantial impediment to employment, require VR services, and be able to benefit in terms of an employment outcome.

Individualized Plan

Eligible participants develop an individualized plan identifying employment goals, services, providers, responsibilities, and evaluation criteria.

Services

  • Vocational counseling
  • Assessment
  • Education and occupational training
  • Assistive technology
  • Job development and placement
  • Supported employment
  • Transportation
  • Personal assistance
  • Benefits counseling
  • Post-employment services

Pre-Employment Transition Services

Required activities include job exploration, work-based learning, postsecondary counseling, workplace readiness, and self-advocacy.

Fifteen-Percent Reserve

State VR agencies must reserve at least 15 percent of federal State VR funds for pre-employment transition services.

Competitive Integrated Employment

WIOA strengthened the preference for integrated community employment at competitive wages.

American Indian VR

Federal grants support Tribal vocational-rehabilitation services for American Indians with disabilities living on or near reservations.

Career Services

Basic Career Services

  • Eligibility determination
  • Outreach, intake, and orientation
  • Initial skills and support-needs assessment
  • Job search and placement
  • Career counseling
  • In-demand occupation information
  • Labor-market and wage data
  • Training-provider performance and cost information
  • Local performance information
  • Supportive-service referrals
  • Unemployment-insurance assistance
  • Financial-aid information

Individualized Career Services

Services can include comprehensive assessment, testing, individual employment planning, counseling, career planning, short-term prevocational services, internships, work experience, workforce preparation, financial literacy, English-language acquisition, relocation assistance, and out-of-area job search.

No Mandatory Sequence

Training can be authorized when assessment determines that it is appropriate without requiring completion of every possible career service.

Follow-Up

Adults and dislocated workers placed in unsubsidized employment can receive retention and workplace follow-up.

Training Services

Occupational Skills Training

Classroom or online training can prepare participants for specific occupations and recognized credentials.

On-the-Job Training

An employer hires and trains a participant while receiving reimbursement for part of extraordinary training costs.

Customized Training

Employers and workforce agencies design training for specific business or industry needs, generally with an employer contribution and employment commitment.

Incumbent-Worker Training

Local areas can use limited Adult and Dislocated Worker funds to upskill employees, avert layoffs, and support advancement.

Registered Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship sponsors can participate as eligible providers and combine paid employment with structured instruction.

Entrepreneurial Training

Participants can receive education concerning business planning, finance, marketing, licensing, and operations.

Integrated Basic Skills

Adult education, English, secondary completion, and occupational education can be combined so foundational learning occurs alongside career preparation.

Prerequisites

WIOA funds can support prerequisite instruction when it is part of an approved employment and training strategy.

Individual Training Accounts and Eligible Training Providers

Customer Choice

Participants receiving an ITA select an approved program from the state ETPL with counseling and labor-market information.

Eligible Providers

Providers can include community colleges, universities, technical schools, private career schools, apprenticeship sponsors, and community organizations.

Performance Information

States collect and publish completion, credential, employment, earnings, cost, and related program information.

Continued Eligibility

Providers must maintain state approval and comply with reporting, consumer-information, nondiscrimination, financial, and performance requirements.

Exceptions to ITAs

Contracts can be used for on-the-job, customized, incumbent-worker, transitional, cohort, community-based, pay-for-performance, and other authorized training.

Funding Limits

States and local areas can establish ITA dollar or duration limits if policies do not unlawfully deny access or undermine customer choice.

Other Aid

WIOA coordinates with Pell Grants, scholarships, employer support, veterans’ benefits, and other resources and can cover allowable unmet costs.

Work-Based Learning and Employer Services

Paid Experience

Work-based learning gives participants occupational exposure, supervision, work history, references, income, and skill development.

On-the-Job Training

Employers can receive reimbursement for part of training costs for eligible new employees.

Customized Training

Training can serve one employer or a group of employers with shared skill needs.

Incumbent Workers

Services can help employers retain workers and adapt to technology, regulation, production changes, or new markets.

Transitional Jobs

Time-limited subsidized employment can help people with chronic unemployment, justice involvement, homelessness, or limited work history.

Apprenticeships

American Job Centers can recruit apprentices, finance related instruction or supportive services, and coordinate sponsors, colleges, unions, and employers.

Employer Services

Workforce agencies help businesses recruit, screen, assess, train, retain, and advance workers and respond to layoffs.

Worker Protection

Subsidized employment remains subject to wage, hour, safety, nondiscrimination, displacement, and labor-law protections.

Supportive Services and Needs-Related Payments

Purpose

Training alone may be ineffective when transportation, childcare, housing, health, technology, clothing, or income prevents participation.

Potential Assistance

  • Transportation and fuel
  • Childcare and dependent care
  • Housing and emergency assistance
  • Work clothing and uniforms
  • Tools, supplies, books, and equipment
  • Testing, licensing, and certification fees
  • Technology and internet access
  • Disability-related referrals
  • Needs-related payments

Local Policy

States and local boards establish written eligibility, amount, duration, documentation, and coordination policies.

Necessary and Unavailable Elsewhere

Supportive services generally must be necessary for participation and unavailable through other sources.

Not an Entitlement

Even when a cost is allowable, local policy and funds determine whether assistance is provided.

Equity Importance

Supportive services are often decisive for low-income participants, parents, rural residents, people with disabilities, and workers entering lower-paid training periods.

Employers, Sector Partnerships, and Economic Development

Demand-Driven Strategy

WIOA directs systems to identify in-demand industries and occupations and engage employers in planning and training.

Sector Partnerships

Regional partnerships bring employers together with unions, education providers, workforce boards, community organizations, and economic-development agencies.

Business Services

American Job Centers can provide recruitment, hiring events, screening, labor-market data, layoff aversion, tax-credit information, apprenticeship assistance, and training subsidies.

Small Businesses

Local systems can develop shared training and recruitment strategies for firms unable to operate large internal training departments.

Economic Development

WIOA plans can align workforce investments with regional development, infrastructure, manufacturing, health care, technology, logistics, construction, and energy.

Public Benefit

Employer engagement should produce additional employment and advancement rather than simply subsidizing activities employers would otherwise finance.

Worker Voice

Labor and apprenticeship representatives participate in boards, but debate continues about whether workers possess sufficient power within a business-majority system.

Performance Accountability

Second-Quarter Outcome

Adult, Dislocated Worker, Wagner-Peyser, and VR measure unsubsidized employment; Youth measures education, training, or employment.

Fourth-Quarter Outcome

The programs measure whether employment or education outcomes continue approximately one year after exit.

Median Earnings

The system reports median earnings during the second quarter after exit among applicable employed participants.

Credential Attainment

Applicable programs measure recognized credentials or qualifying secondary credentials obtained during participation or within one year after exit.

Measurable Skill Gains

Applicable participants in education or training are measured for documented progress during the program year.

Employer Indicator

The current joint definition uses retention with the same employer between specified post-exit quarters.

Negotiated Levels

Federal agencies and states negotiate levels using economic conditions, participant characteristics, prior performance, and statistical models.

Public Reports

Performance is published through federal dashboards, state reports, local-area data, and provider systems.

Sanctions

States failing performance standards can receive technical assistance, corrective action, and financial sanctions.

Program Scale and Recent Performance

Program Year 2024 Participation

The Department of Labor reports that Adult, Dislocated Worker, Youth, and Wagner-Peyser collectively served more than 2.7 million participants.

Overlap

A person can receive services from more than one program, so combined counts are not necessarily unduplicated.

Broader System

Title I and III figures do not include every Adult Education, Vocational Rehabilitation, Job Corps, YouthBuild, farmworker, Native American, or partner-program participant.

Median Earnings

The Program Year 2024 national dashboard reports approximately $8,754 for Adult, $9,897 for Dislocated Worker, $5,038 for Youth, and $8,558 for Wagner-Peyser in the applicable second-quarter employment cohorts.

Interpretive Limits

Results reflect participant characteristics, regional economies, service intensity, co-enrollment, exit definitions, and wage-record coverage and do not by themselves establish causal impact.

Local Variation

Outcomes differ among states and more than 550 local workforce-development boards.

2025–2026 Education–Labor Partnership

Interagency Agreement

Education and Labor signed an Economy Act agreement in May 2025 allowing Labor to provide administrative services for Adult Education and Family Literacy and career and technical education grants.

Division of Authority

Labor performs selected administrative, payment-system, grant-management, and state-plan functions. Education retains statutory responsibilities, policymaking authority, program determinations, and oversight.

State-Plan Portal

The agencies transferred the WIOA state-plan portal to Labor in September 2025.

Grant Systems

Adult-education grantees were onboarded to Labor’s GrantSolutions and Payment Management System.

Rationale

The administration describes the arrangement as reducing duplication and aligning education with employment and training.

Concerns

Critics question agency reorganization without legislation, staff capacity, disruption, program identity, and whether literacy and civic purposes could be subordinated to short-term workforce goals.

Current Status

The partnership remains operational in 2026 while the statute continues assigning Title II authority to Education.

Reauthorization and H.R. 8210

Expired Authorization Levels

Many WIOA authorization levels expired after fiscal year 2020, but appropriations have continued and the statutory programs remain active.

2024 House Bill

The House passed a bipartisan reauthorization in 2024, but it was not enacted before the 118th Congress ended.

2026 Proposal

Chairman Tim Walberg introduced H.R. 8210, A Stronger Workforce for America Act of 2026, on April 6, 2026.

Committee Action

The House Education and Workforce Committee approved the bill on April 21, 2026 and ordered it reported.

Proposed Changes

  • Transfer adult-education administration to Labor
  • Increase emphasis on skills development, ITAs, work-based learning, and employer-led training
  • Strengthen performance and provider accountability
  • Expand upskilling for employed workers
  • Modify governance, data, and program-quality requirements

Not Current Law

The proposal does not change WIOA unless enacted.

Policy Division

Supporters describe modernization and accountability. Critics object to moving adult education, consolidating authority, and emphasizing employer outcomes without sufficient worker protections.

Civil Rights and Equal Opportunity

Section 188

WIOA Section 188 prohibits discrimination in Title I financially assisted programs based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability, political affiliation or belief, and specified citizenship or participation status.

Disability Access

American Job Centers must provide physical, programmatic, communication, and technological accessibility and reasonable modifications.

Language Access

Recipients must provide meaningful access to people with limited English proficiency.

Equal Opportunity Officers

Governors and recipients designate officials responsible for compliance, complaints, monitoring, and corrective action.

Complaints

Participants can use local, state, or federal grievance and discrimination procedures depending on the issue.

Labor Standards

Applicable wage, hour, safety, workers’ compensation, collective-bargaining, and displacement protections apply.

No Displacement

WIOA-funded participants generally may not replace current workers, impair contracts, or displace employees involved in labor disputes.

Data Equity

Performance systems collect participant characteristics, but privacy, data quality, and inconsistent local practice can limit equity analysis.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Unemployment and Dislocation

Black workers have historically experienced higher unemployment, greater vulnerability to recession and layoffs, and slower recovery. Adult, Dislocated Worker, Rapid Response, and Employment Service programs can provide assistance.

Occupational Segregation

Career counseling and training can broaden access, but programs can reproduce occupational tracking if participants are steered toward the nearest low-wage opening rather than advancement.

Racial Wealth Gap

Limited wealth makes unpaid training, transportation, tools, childcare, and income loss more difficult. Supportive services and paid work-based learning are therefore central.

Adult Education

Title II can address literacy, secondary completion, digital skills, and postsecondary transition in communities affected by unequal schooling.

Justice Involvement

Black Americans are disproportionately affected by incarceration and criminal-record barriers. Youth, Adult, reentry, apprenticeship, and employer programs can support transition.

Disability

Black people with disabilities can face compounded discrimination, school exclusion, transportation barriers, and labor-market bias. VR and accessible Job Centers are important but uneven.

Board Representation

Equity depends on whether Black workers, unions, HBCUs, community colleges, neighborhood groups, reentry organizations, youth, and disability advocates influence planning and contracting.

Employer Accountability

Public training subsidies should be evaluated for wages, retention, advancement, discrimination, scheduling, safety, and job quality—not only placement.

Community Benefit

Effective strategy connects individual services with transportation, childcare, housing, broadband, neighborhood institutions, and economic development.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Integrated Access

American Job Centers can connect customers with multiple programs through one network.

Training Choice

ITAs and public provider lists allow participants to compare approved programs.

Priority for Need

The Adult Program directs individualized and training resources toward public-assistance recipients, low-income people, and people with basic-skills deficiencies.

Layoff Response

Rapid Response and Dislocated Worker funds can intervene during closures and economic shocks.

Youth Development

The Youth Program combines education, paid work, mentoring, occupational training, supportive services, and follow-up.

Adult Literacy

Title II connects literacy and English-language education with employment, family, civic participation, and postsecondary transition.

Disability Employment

Vocational Rehabilitation provides individualized services and strengthens school-to-work transition.

Employer Assistance

Businesses receive recruitment, training, labor-market, apprenticeship, and layoff-aversion services.

Public Accountability

Common indicators and provider data create a basis for comparing programs and outcomes.

State Flexibility

States and local areas can adapt services to regional labor markets within federal protections and priorities.

Limitations and Risks

Limited Training Funds

Most people using the public labor-exchange system do not receive extensive publicly funded occupational training.

Local Variation

Eligibility interpretation, ITA limits, supportive services, providers, and customer experience differ by state and locality.

Administrative Complexity

Multiple statutes, agencies, boards, partners, data systems, funding streams, and eligibility rules can make the one-stop system difficult to navigate.

Provider Quality

ETPL approval does not guarantee strong completion, credential, wage, or advancement outcomes.

Performance Incentives

Outcome measures can encourage programs to avoid people with severe barriers or prioritize quick placement over long-term advancement.

Support Gaps

Training may fail when childcare, transportation, housing, health, disability, or income needs remain unmet.

Employer Subsidy

Public funds can subsidize training an employer would otherwise finance unless local systems require genuine additional benefit.

Board Representation

Business-majority governance can improve labor-market alignment but may underrepresent workers, participants, neighborhoods, educators, and service organizations.

Fragmented Data

Privacy law, cross-state employment, self-employment, and missing wage records complicate reporting.

Funding Volatility

Annual appropriations, advance funding, shutdown risk, and reauthorization debates affect planning and staffing.

Major Policy Debates

Training Requirement

Some reformers seek to require a larger share of formula funds for skills training. Others argue that assessment, counseling, support, employer outreach, and career navigation are essential.

Adult Education Administration

Moving Title II to Labor could improve workforce integration, while critics fear reduced attention to literacy, family, and civic purposes.

Local Flexibility and National Standards

Local control allows adaptation but produces unequal access. Strong national standards can improve consistency while limiting innovation.

Employer Leadership and Worker Voice

Business-majority boards connect training with demand, while labor and community advocates seek stronger representation and job-quality standards.

Credentials and Degrees

Short-term credentials can provide rapid entry but may have limited transferability or advancement. Longer education requires greater time and support.

Performance Accountability

Employment, earnings, credentials, and retention are important but can overlook job quality, equity, worker choice, and long-term mobility.

One-Stop Funding

American Job Centers require stable infrastructure, yet partner cost sharing and separate appropriations create recurring conflict.

Automation

Artificial intelligence and automation increase demand for reskilling and layoff response while raising questions about employer responsibility and income security.

Consolidation

Consolidation can simplify administration but may weaken specialized services for youth, farmworkers, Native Americans, veterans, people with disabilities, and justice-involved participants.

Key Distinctions

WIOA and American Job Centers

WIOA is the governing law and program framework. American Job Centers are the service-delivery network.

Adult and Dislocated Worker

Adult is age-based and applies need priorities. Dislocated Worker is based principally on job loss and displacement.

Career and Training Services

Career services assess, counsel, refer, and support employment search. Training develops occupational or foundational skills.

Basic Access and Enrollment

A person can use self-service resources without receiving extensive staff-assisted services or becoming a participant for every reporting purpose.

ITA and Cash Grant

An ITA is a controlled payment for approved training, not unrestricted cash.

ETPL and Accreditation

ETPL approval permits WIOA payment but does not replace accreditation, licensing, authorization, or federal student-aid approval.

WIOA Youth and Job Corps

WIOA Youth is a local formula program. Job Corps is a separate federal residential program.

Title II and Occupational Training

Adult Education provides foundational instruction; occupational training can be integrated or financed through partner programs.

VR and Disability Benefits

Vocational Rehabilitation provides employment services. Social Security disability programs provide cash and health-benefit support under separate laws.

Current Law and H.R. 8210

The 2026 bill proposes amendments but does not control administration unless enacted.

Geographic Scope

WIOA operates throughout the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and eligible territories and outlying areas.

Governors designate local workforce-development areas based on labor markets, commuting patterns, population, government boundaries, economic development, service capacity, and statutory requirements.

American Job Centers serve urban neighborhoods, suburbs, rural counties, Tribal areas, border communities, islands, and regions affected by industrial change.

Service availability varies. Rural residents can face long travel distances, limited transit, broadband gaps, and few providers. Urban systems can face high demand, fragmented agencies, housing instability, and waiting lists.

Training-provider rules can permit interstate or regional participation, but authorization, tuition, residency, and local policy affect out-of-area training.

National programs operate through Tribal organizations, farmworker organizations, Job Corps centers, YouthBuild grantees, and emergency grants that do not follow ordinary local-area boundaries.

Economic conditions and formula data change annual state allotments, while stop-loss, stop-gain, and minimum-share rules limit abrupt changes.

Summary

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act is the principal federal law organizing employment, training, adult education, labor exchange, vocational rehabilitation, and public workforce governance.

President Barack Obama signed WIOA on July 22, 2014. Most provisions took effect July 1, 2015, replacing the Workforce Investment Act of 1998.

Title I authorizes Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth formula programs and national programs such as Job Corps, YouthBuild, Native American programs, farmworker programs, and National Dislocated Worker Grants.

Title II authorizes adult education and literacy. Title III integrates Wagner-Peyser Employment Service into American Job Centers. Title IV strengthens vocational rehabilitation and transition services for people with disabilities.

The six core programs share state-planning and performance requirements. States submit four-year plans, governors appoint workforce boards, and local business-majority boards oversee service strategy and American Job Centers.

American Job Centers provide job search, labor-market information, career counseling, employer services, unemployment coordination, referrals, and access to training and partner programs.

The Adult Program prioritizes public-assistance recipients, other low-income people, and people who are basic-skills deficient. The Dislocated Worker Program serves people affected by job loss and economic change.

The Youth Program concentrates resources on out-of-school youth, requires substantial investment in work experience, and makes fourteen program elements available.

Training can be financed through Individual Training Accounts, approved providers, apprenticeships, on-the-job training, customized training, incumbent-worker training, transitional jobs, and integrated education.

Supportive services can address transportation, childcare, housing, tools, clothing, technology, testing, and income needs, but assistance depends on local policy and funding.

For Program Year 2026, Youth, Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Wagner-Peyser appropriations total approximately $3.895 billion. The figure excludes Title II, Title IV, Job Corps, YouthBuild, and other national programs.

The latest national Labor Department report states that the four principal Title I and III programs served more than 2.7 million participants in Program Year 2024.

In 2025 and 2026, Education and Labor integrated selected administrative functions for adult education and state planning while Education retained statutory authority and oversight.

Congress is considering H.R. 8210, the A Stronger Workforce for America Act of 2026. The bill has been approved by the House committee but has not become law.

WIOA can expand opportunity by connecting job seekers with training, employers, literacy, rehabilitation, and supportive services. Its effectiveness depends on funding, provider quality, local administration, participant choice, worker protections, accessibility, and the quality of available jobs.

For African American communities, the program’s importance lies in its potential response to unequal unemployment, layoffs, school access, occupational segregation, incarceration, disability, and wealth. Formal eligibility alone does not ensure equity; outcomes depend on outreach, representation, job quality, civil-rights enforcement, and community influence over workforce investment.
