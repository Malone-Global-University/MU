Community Action Program

The War on Poverty framework through which urban and rural communities mobilized public and private resources, established local Community Action Agencies, coordinated antipoverty services, and involved residents of low-income areas in the development and administration of programs affecting their communities.

What Was the Community Action Program?

The Community Action Program was the principal local antipoverty framework created by Title II of the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964. It authorized communities to organize comprehensive programs that mobilized public and private resources, coordinated services, addressed local causes of poverty, and involved residents of low-income areas in program governance.

Community action was not one standardized service. It was a method for planning, financing, administering, and coordinating multiple services within a defined community. A local program could include preschool education, employment assistance, adult literacy, health care, legal services, housing support, youth programs, transportation, nutrition, welfare advocacy, emergency assistance, and neighborhood organization.

The program’s local institutional vehicle was usually a Community Action Agency. The agency prepared plans, received federal grants, governed program components, hired staff, coordinated public and private organizations, and represented community priorities before government.

Community Action Programs differed substantially by locality. Federal law defined broad purposes and eligibility, but local poverty conditions, political institutions, racial relations, civic organizations, administrative capacity, and resident leadership shaped actual operations.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the Community Action Program as a national policy framework and local administrative system. It covers statutory design, agency formation, maximum feasible participation, governing boards, services, funding, federalism, political conflict, outcomes, restructuring, and the Community Services Block Grant legacy.

It does not provide a complete history of every Community Action Agency or every service financed through community-action grants. Local agencies developed different program portfolios, and individual programs such as Head Start, Legal Services, and neighborhood health centers require separate fact sheets.

Modern Community Action Agencies frequently administer programs funded through several federal, state, local, charitable, and private sources. The presence of a service within a modern agency does not necessarily mean that the service originated under the 1964 Community Action Program.

Geographic Scope

The Community Action Program applied to urban, rural, and combined urban-rural communities throughout the United States and eligible territories.

Federal law allowed a “community” to include a state, metropolitan area, county, city, town, multicity unit, multicounty unit, or another appropriate geographic area. The scale was intended to correspond to the location of poverty and the institutions capable of addressing it.

Urban agencies often served neighborhoods affected by residential segregation, overcrowding, unemployment, urban renewal, unequal schools, fragmented services, and municipal disinvestment. Rural agencies addressed geographic isolation, agricultural change, low wages, weak infrastructure, limited transportation, inadequate health facilities, and small local tax bases.

Tribal communities and migrant-worker communities presented additional jurisdictional and service-delivery questions. Programs could require coordination across tribal governments, federal agencies, states, counties, agricultural labor routes, and nonprofit organizations.

Historical Context

The Community Action Program developed from concern that poverty persisted despite postwar economic growth. Low-income communities frequently faced several connected problems at once: limited employment, inadequate schools, poor health, unsafe housing, weak transportation, discrimination, and exclusion from government decision-making.

Existing services were often divided among welfare departments, school systems, health agencies, housing authorities, charities, employment offices, churches, and private organizations. Each institution operated under separate rules, budgets, eligibility standards, and geographic boundaries.

Federal and philanthropic experiments before 1964 attempted to coordinate neighborhood services and involve residents in planning. These included urban “gray areas” projects, juvenile-delinquency demonstrations, settlement-house traditions, rural development programs, and community-organization initiatives.

The civil-rights movement demonstrated that poverty could not be separated from political power, racial exclusion, voting rights, public accommodations, schools, housing, and employment. In many communities, the same officials responsible for administering services also maintained segregation or excluded low-income residents from decision-making.

The Johnson administration therefore treated community organization as both an administrative tool and an opportunity strategy. Programs would coordinate services while creating channels through which residents could influence institutions affecting their lives.

Legal Context

Title II of the Economic Opportunity Act established urban and rural Community Action Programs. Section 201 declared the purpose of stimulating communities to mobilize resources against poverty.

Section 202 defined a Community Action Program as one that mobilized public and private resources, provided services and activities of sufficient scope and size to promise progress against poverty, involved maximum feasible participation, and was conducted by a public or private nonprofit agency or combination of agencies.

Sections 204 and 205 authorized grants and contracts for program development, operation, administration, and component services. Sections 206 and 207 authorized technical assistance, training, research, and demonstrations.

The Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967 substantially reorganized community action. They increased the authority of state and local government to designate agencies and formalized governing-board structures balancing public officials, representatives of low-income residents, and other community interests.

The Community Services Act of 1974 replaced OEO with the Community Services Administration while continuing community-action functions. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 abolished the Community Services Administration and replaced the national direct-grant model with the Community Services Block Grant.

Modern Community Action Agencies operate under federal Community Services Block Grant law, state law, corporate charters, grant conditions, and the statutes governing each additional program they administer.

Original Statutory Definition

The 1964 law required a Community Action Program to contain four basic elements.

  1. Mobilization of resources. The program had to use public or private resources of an urban, rural, or combined community.
  2. An attack on poverty. Services and activities had to be broad enough to promise progress toward eliminating poverty or its causes.
  3. Maximum feasible participation. Residents of the areas and members of the groups served had to participate in development, conduct, and administration.
  4. Eligible administration. The program had to be operated, administered, or coordinated by a public or private nonprofit agency or combination of agencies, rather than a political party.

This definition made community action broader than a conventional welfare office. The local institution was expected to combine resources, build capacity, address causes as well as immediate needs, and include the people affected by poverty.

Program Purposes

Community Action Programs pursued several related purposes.

Coordinate fragmented services
Connect employment, education, health, housing, welfare, legal, and family services that otherwise operated separately.
Expand access
Bring services into communities where residents faced geographic, financial, racial, linguistic, or administrative barriers.
Develop local institutions
Create organizations capable of receiving grants, employing residents, maintaining records, planning programs, and representing community needs.
Build individual and family capacity
Improve education, employment, health, legal protection, and access to public benefits.
Increase resident influence
Provide formal roles in governing boards, advisory councils, staffing, program selection, and evaluation.
Mobilize private resources
Engage businesses, labor organizations, universities, charities, religious institutions, civic groups, and professionals.
Test new approaches
Use demonstrations and local experimentation to develop programs that could later receive permanent authority.

Maximum Feasible Participation

“Maximum feasible participation” was the Community Action Program’s defining and most disputed phrase.

The original statute required participation by residents of the areas and members of the groups served but did not establish one national selection method or exact proportion of governing authority.

OEO administrators interpreted participation through neighborhood councils, governing-board representation, public meetings, employment of local residents, surveys, outreach, advisory committees, and resident involvement in program design.

Participation could mean consultation, representation, employment, delegated authority, or independent organizing. These forms were not equivalent. A resident could attend meetings without controlling budgets, while a board member could possess legal voting authority.

The ambiguity gave local communities flexibility but generated conflict over whether residents were merely program clients or political participants entitled to influence institutional decisions.

Forms of Resident Participation

Neighborhood advisory councils
Local bodies identified needs, reviewed services, and selected or recommended representatives.
Governing-board membership
Representatives of low-income communities participated in formal agency decisions.
Program employment
Residents worked as outreach workers, aides, organizers, teachers, drivers, clerks, health workers, and administrators.
Community meetings
Agencies received public testimony and discussed program priorities.
Needs assessments
Residents supplied information concerning barriers, service gaps, and local conditions.
Client councils
Program participants advised Head Start, health, housing, legal, or employment components.
Community organizing
Residents organized around welfare practices, housing, schools, sanitation, transportation, policing, and municipal services.
Evaluation
Residents participated in judging whether services were accessible, appropriate, and effective.

Community Action Agency

A Community Action Agency was the local organization responsible for developing or administering a Community Action Program.

An agency could be a private nonprofit corporation, municipal office, county agency, political subdivision, combination of jurisdictions, or other public entity authorized by law.

Community Action Agencies commonly:

  • defined the geographic service area;
  • conducted needs assessments;
  • prepared grant applications;
  • administered federal and local funds;
  • established governing boards;
  • hired and supervised personnel;
  • coordinated public and private organizations;
  • operated service components;
  • maintained fiscal and program records;
  • performed outreach and referrals;
  • monitored civil-rights and grant compliance; and
  • represented community needs before government agencies.

The agency was not the federal government. It was a local entity receiving federal financial assistance and subject to federal program requirements.

Public and Private Agency Models

Private Nonprofit Agency

A nonprofit corporation could receive federal funds directly, employ staff, contract for services, and maintain a governing board independent of ordinary municipal departments.

Public Agency

A city, county, state, tribal government, or public authority could administer the program within government.

Combination of Jurisdictions

Several counties, municipalities, or public bodies could form a regional agency serving a broader poverty area.

Delegate Agency System

A central Community Action Agency could grant or contract with schools, nonprofit organizations, neighborhood groups, health providers, or other institutions to operate components.

Each model created different relationships between residents, elected officials, public law, nonprofit governance, procurement rules, personnel systems, and political accountability.

Governing Boards

Governing boards established policy, approved budgets, selected executive leadership, reviewed programs, and represented the major constituencies involved in community action.

Early boards varied widely because the 1964 act did not impose one exact composition. Some agencies gave low-income representatives substantial authority. Others were dominated by public officials, social-service professionals, or established civic institutions.

The 1967 amendments created a more formal tripartite pattern:

  • approximately one-third public officials or their representatives;
  • at least one-third representatives of low-income residents selected through democratic procedures; and
  • the remaining members drawn from business, labor, religious, education, welfare, civic, or other major community interests.

Modern federal law continues a tripartite-board structure for private nonprofit entities receiving Community Services Block Grant funds, although requirements for public entities may differ.

Board Powers and Responsibilities

A board’s authority depended on federal law, state law, corporate bylaws, grant terms, and whether the agency was public or private.

Typical responsibilities included:

  • establishing agency policy;
  • selecting, evaluating, or dismissing the executive director;
  • approving applications and community-action plans;
  • adopting budgets and financial controls;
  • reviewing audits;
  • approving personnel and procurement policies;
  • monitoring program performance;
  • ensuring low-income representation;
  • overseeing compliance with grant conditions;
  • identifying community priorities; and
  • protecting the agency’s legal and fiscal integrity.

Representation alone did not guarantee influence. Meeting procedures, access to information, committee assignments, staff control, technical knowledge, and voting coalitions affected actual board power.

Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967

Conflict between independent Community Action Agencies and elected officials led Congress to restructure the program in 1967.

The amendments increased the role of state and local governments in designating agencies and required a more regular governing-board structure. The changes are often associated with the “Green Amendment,” named for Representative Edith Green of Oregon.

Supporters argued that elected officials possessed democratic legitimacy and responsibility for local public administration. Critics argued that increased governmental control could weaken independent organizing and return authority to officials who had neglected or excluded low-income communities.

The resulting compromise preserved low-income representation but placed it within a tripartite governance system that also included public officials and established community institutions.

The amendments did not make every agency a municipal department. Public and private nonprofit Community Action Agencies continued, but local designation and governing-board rules became more structured.

Authorized Service Components

The original act identified broad fields in which Community Action Program components could operate.

Employment
Job referral, placement, work experience, employer outreach, and employment preparation.
Job training and counseling
Vocational instruction, career planning, work-readiness services, and coordination with manpower programs.
Health
Screening, primary care, referrals, preventive services, maternal and child health, and neighborhood health centers.
Vocational rehabilitation
Services assisting people whose disabilities affected employment.
Housing
Housing counseling, rehabilitation, relocation assistance, tenant services, weatherization, and development projects.
Home management
Household budgeting, nutrition, family support, and practical domestic services.
Welfare
Benefits outreach, application assistance, case advocacy, emergency services, and coordination with public-assistance agencies.
Remedial and noncurricular education
Adult literacy, tutoring, after-school services, school-readiness programs, and educational support outside general school aid.

Head Start as a Community-Action Component

Head Start began under OEO in 1965 and frequently operated through Community Action Agencies or local delegate organizations.

The program combined preschool education, health screening, nutrition, social services, disability services, and parent participation.

Head Start demonstrated the community-action principle that one service should address several connected barriers. A child’s educational readiness could depend on health care, nutrition, family support, transportation, and parental involvement.

Parent councils and policy committees became important participation institutions. Head Start transferred to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare in 1969 but continued to operate through many Community Action Agencies.

Legal Services

OEO legal-services projects supplied civil representation to people unable to afford lawyers. Many projects were connected to Community Action Agencies, although legal-services organizations also developed independent structures.

Lawyers represented clients in housing, public benefits, family law, employment, consumer, debt, and administrative matters.

Legal representation could challenge the practices of the same public agencies and local officials involved in community-action governance. This made Legal Services one of the most politically contested components.

The Legal Services Corporation Act of 1974 created an independent successor institution, separating national civil legal-aid funding from the community-action agency framework.

Neighborhood Health Centers

Community action supplied a local framework for developing comprehensive health centers in medically underserved neighborhoods.

Centers could combine primary care, preventive services, outreach, transportation, nutrition, sanitation, employment, and community participation.

Early centers demonstrated that effective health policy required attention to conditions beyond medical treatment, including housing, food, environment, and access to institutions.

The neighborhood health-center program transferred to the Public Health Service in 1971 and contributed to the modern federal Health Center Program.

Neighborhood Service Centers

Neighborhood centers gave residents a local point of access to otherwise fragmented institutions.

A center might provide intake, benefits screening, employment referral, health outreach, legal referral, youth programs, day care, adult education, housing counseling, transportation assistance, and meeting space.

Centers reduced the need for residents to navigate distant offices with separate eligibility systems. Staff familiar with the community could identify household needs that crossed agency boundaries.

Neighborhood centers also provided physical space for resident councils, public meetings, organizing, and contact with government officials.

Employment and Training Services

Community Action Agencies coordinated with Job Corps, Neighborhood Youth Corps, manpower training programs, public employment offices, schools, employers, unions, and vocational institutions.

Local services could include:

  • job listings and referral;
  • application and interview assistance;
  • transportation to work;
  • child-care coordination;
  • basic education;
  • vocational counseling;
  • work-experience placements;
  • employer outreach;
  • support for work clothing or tools; and
  • assistance obtaining identification or records.

Employment services could improve access but could not create sufficient jobs where local labor demand was weak or discrimination restricted hiring.

Education and Literacy

Community Action Programs supported adult basic education, tutoring, school-readiness services, college preparation, youth development, and educational counseling.

The original act prohibited general aid to elementary or secondary school systems under Community Action Program grants. Assistance was instead directed toward remedial, special, or noncurricular services for low-income individuals and families.

Local agencies often worked with schools while also criticizing unequal access, exclusionary discipline, segregation, and inadequate services.

Education programs became sites of conflict when residents sought influence over curricula, school administration, hiring, or allocation of resources beyond the formal authority of the Community Action Agency.

Housing and Neighborhood Conditions

Community Action Agencies addressed housing through counseling, referrals, rehabilitation, tenant organization, relocation assistance, home repair, weatherization, energy assistance, and development projects.

Agencies could help households apply for public housing, mortgage assistance, rent relief, utility support, or code-enforcement remedies.

Housing problems frequently exceeded agency authority. Community action could not independently control zoning, metropolitan segregation, lending markets, property values, urban renewal, or the supply of affordable units.

Legal services and organizing components sometimes challenged landlords, housing authorities, discriminatory practices, and displacement.

Public Assistance and Welfare Rights

Community-action staff assisted residents with applications, hearings, documentation, referrals, and communication with welfare departments.

Outreach could identify people entitled to assistance but excluded by lack of information, transportation, language access, or administrative barriers.

Resident organizations and legal-services attorneys also challenged benefit terminations, intrusive eligibility practices, unequal treatment, and inadequate hearing procedures.

This advocacy generated conflict because Community Action Agencies could receive public funds while opposing the conduct of other public agencies.

Emergency and Crisis Services

Although community action emphasized long-term opportunity and institutional capacity, local agencies also confronted immediate crises.

Emergency assistance could involve food, shelter, rent, utilities, transportation, clothing, medical referral, disaster response, and protection from eviction or service termination.

Crisis services were necessary because employment training or education could not proceed effectively when a household lacked food, heat, housing, or medical care.

Agencies attempted to connect emergency assistance with case management, benefits access, employment, family support, and longer-term stabilization.

Community Organizing and Advocacy

Community Action Programs sometimes moved beyond service delivery into organizing and public advocacy.

Residents addressed sanitation, housing, schools, welfare administration, transportation, employment, neighborhood redevelopment, consumer practices, and police-community relations.

Supporters viewed organizing as necessary because institutions producing or maintaining poverty would not change through services alone. Critics argued that federal antipoverty funds should not finance political conflict or opposition to elected government.

Federal law restricted partisan political activity, but the distinction between prohibited partisan activity and permitted civic advocacy was often disputed.

Mobilization of Private Resources

Community action was intended to use more than federal funds.

Agencies sought contributions and participation from:

  • businesses and employers;
  • labor unions;
  • universities and colleges;
  • hospitals and health professionals;
  • bar associations and law schools;
  • religious institutions;
  • foundations and charities;
  • civic organizations;
  • volunteers;
  • public schools; and
  • local media and professional associations.

Private participation could expand resources and expertise. It could also preserve the influence of established institutions that low-income residents believed had failed to address community needs.

Federal Funding

OEO could make grants or contracts with public or private nonprofit agencies for development, operation, and administration of approved Community Action Programs.

Federal funds could support both central agency administration and program components. The original statute permitted high federal cost participation during the early years while anticipating greater nonfederal contributions later.

Nonfederal contributions could consist of cash or fairly valued in-kind resources, including facilities, equipment, professional services, and volunteer labor.

Funding decisions considered poverty concentration, unemployment and underemployment, public-assistance participation, school dropout, health conditions, housing, migration, community facilities, and the applicant’s administrative capacity.

Annual appropriations and grant renewal made agencies vulnerable to political changes, administrative findings, audit disputes, and shifting federal priorities.

Program Development and Grant Process

  1. Define the community. Organizers identified the city, county, region, neighborhood, tribal area, or combined service territory.
  2. Assess poverty and resources. Applicants examined population, employment, education, health, housing, welfare, and existing services.
  3. Establish or designate an agency. A public or nonprofit entity assumed legal responsibility for the program.
  4. Create governance mechanisms. The agency formed a board and resident-participation system.
  5. Develop program components. Local planners selected services and coordinating arrangements.
  6. Prepare the application. The agency submitted budgets, objectives, staffing, service plans, and supporting documentation.
  7. Federal and state review. OEO and relevant state officials reviewed the application under applicable procedures.
  8. Award and implementation. The agency hired staff, contracted with providers, and began services.
  9. Monitoring and renewal. Continued funding depended on reports, audits, compliance, performance, and appropriations.

Technical Assistance and Training

The Economic Opportunity Act authorized OEO to provide technical assistance and train specialized personnel needed to develop, conduct, and administer Community Action Programs.

Assistance could address:

  • community-needs assessment;
  • grant writing;
  • board development;
  • accounting and fiscal controls;
  • personnel systems;
  • procurement and contracting;
  • civil-rights compliance;
  • program evaluation;
  • resident-participation methods;
  • data collection; and
  • coordination with federal departments.

Technical assistance was essential because many communities lacked established nonprofit organizations or personnel experienced in federal grant administration.

Research, Training, and Demonstration Authority

OEO could conduct or fund research, training, and demonstrations related to community action.

Demonstration authority enabled communities to test new models before Congress established permanent programs. Legal Services, neighborhood health centers, and specialized neighborhood initiatives developed partly through this flexible authority.

Research examined poverty, local institutions, service delivery, participation, employment, education, health, and program outcomes.

Flexibility promoted innovation but complicated accountability. Demonstrations could lack long-term funding, uniform standards, or evaluation designs capable of separating program effects from broader economic change.

Federalism and Intergovernmental Relations

Community action altered conventional relationships among federal, state, and local governments.

OEO could fund local organizations directly, reducing the ability of governors or mayors to control every antipoverty grant. Federal officials considered this necessary where state and local institutions were unwilling or unable to address poverty.

Governors received notice of proposed assistance and possessed review authority under statutory procedures. The exact balance changed through amendments.

Local elected officials argued that direct federal grants bypassed democratic government and created competing administrative structures. Community organizations replied that existing governments often excluded low-income residents and racial minorities.

The 1967 amendments moved community action toward a more intergovernmental model while preserving federal funding and resident representation.

Political Conflict

Community action became politically controversial because it concerned power as well as services.

Control of funds
Mayors, governors, nonprofit boards, neighborhood groups, and OEO disputed authority over budgets and grants.
Board appointments
Parties disagreed over who legitimately represented low-income residents and major community interests.
Staff hiring
Agencies became important employers and sources of patronage, professional opportunity, and community leadership.
Advocacy
Organizing and legal action could challenge landlords, welfare departments, school boards, police departments, and elected officials.
Race
Black, Latino, Native American, and other excluded communities sought representation in institutions previously controlled by white officials or established organizations.
Partisanship
Officials accused some agencies of political activity, while agencies distinguished nonpartisan advocacy from electoral campaigning.
Administrative performance
Audits, rapid expansion, weak controls, and uneven program quality supplied grounds for criticism and intervention.

Civil Rights and Racial Equality

The Community Action Program operated under the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and in communities where public institutions remained segregated or discriminatory.

Federal funds could be used to encourage compliance with nondiscrimination requirements. OEO could investigate complaints, impose grant conditions, negotiate changes, or threaten withdrawal of assistance.

Community Action Agencies created paid positions and leadership roles for African Americans, Mexican Americans, Puerto Ricans, Native Americans, and other groups excluded from local government.

In some Southern communities, antipoverty funding supported organizations connected to civil-rights activism. In Northern and Western cities, agencies became arenas for conflict over segregated neighborhoods, employment, schools, welfare, and policing.

The program did not uniformly enforce equality. Local officials could dominate boards, resist integrated services, manipulate appointments, or withhold cooperation.

African American Communities

African American communities were central to the history of community action because poverty was connected to segregation, employment exclusion, unequal schools, housing discrimination, disenfranchisement, and discriminatory public administration.

Community Action Agencies provided preschool education, health services, legal assistance, youth employment, adult education, housing support, benefits advocacy, and neighborhood institutions.

Agencies also offered paid administrative and professional positions that helped develop a generation of community leaders, organizers, lawyers, educators, health workers, and public officials.

Conflict emerged when Black-led neighborhood organizations sought control over resources or challenged established city governments. The 1967 governance amendments retained low-income representation but increased the role of elected officials.

Community action expanded institutional access but did not eliminate unemployment, wealth inequality, residential segregation, school inequality, discriminatory policing, or metropolitan disinvestment.

Latino and Puerto Rican Communities

Mexican American, Puerto Rican, and other Latino communities used community-action funding to establish educational, health, housing, legal, employment, and neighborhood organizations.

Programs confronted language barriers, unequal schools, farm-labor conditions, housing discrimination, limited representation, and exclusion from existing welfare and health institutions.

Community-action employment helped build organizational capacity and leadership within emerging Latino civil-rights movements.

Program effectiveness depended on language access, resident control, staff recruitment, immigration and citizenship issues, regional labor markets, and relationships with local government.

Tribal Communities

Community-action initiatives served tribal communities facing inadequate housing, limited employment, weak infrastructure, geographic isolation, and insufficient health and educational services.

Administration involved tribal sovereignty, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, treaty responsibilities, federal grant law, and state or county institutions.

Programs supported self-determination when tribal governments and residents controlled priorities. They reproduced paternalism when outside officials imposed programs without meaningful tribal authority.

Modern Community Services Block Grant law permits direct federal funding for eligible tribes and tribal organizations under specified procedures.

Rural Community Action

Rural Community Action Agencies often covered large multicounty territories with dispersed populations and limited transportation.

Services addressed agricultural unemployment, mechanization, small-farm decline, migrant labor, health access, housing, nutrition, adult literacy, transportation, and limited local government capacity.

Rural agencies frequently became regional institutions because no single town or county possessed sufficient staff or resources.

Community participation could be difficult where residents lived far from meeting locations, lacked transportation, or depended economically on employers or landowners who opposed organizing.

Urban Community Action

Urban agencies operated amid segregation, industrial job loss, overcrowded housing, urban renewal, unequal schools, public-assistance conflict, and strained relations between residents and municipal government.

Citywide Community Action Agencies frequently used neighborhood centers or delegate agencies to reach particular districts.

Political conflict was especially intense where neighborhood councils claimed authority independent of mayors, city councils, political machines, or established social-service institutions.

The size and diversity of cities made it difficult to define who represented “the poor.” Race, ethnicity, neighborhood, ideology, organization, and service interest all shaped representation.

Local Employment and Leadership Development

Community Action Agencies were themselves employers. They hired outreach workers, clerks, teachers, aides, drivers, caseworkers, organizers, administrators, accountants, lawyers, nurses, and program specialists.

Employment created income and professional experience for residents who had been excluded from government or nonprofit institutions.

Staff learned grant administration, budgeting, public speaking, board governance, service coordination, legal procedure, and political negotiation.

Many community-action participants later moved into nonprofit leadership, elected office, public administration, civil-rights organizations, education, law, and health care.

Hiring also created patronage disputes and questions concerning qualifications, resident preference, professional standards, and political loyalty.

Administrative Capacity

Community Action Agencies had to build administrative systems rapidly.

Core requirements included:

  • legal incorporation or public authorization;
  • governing bylaws;
  • accounting and internal controls;
  • personnel policies;
  • procurement procedures;
  • property management;
  • grant reporting;
  • civil-rights compliance;
  • data collection;
  • program monitoring;
  • insurance and risk management; and
  • coordination among multiple funders.

Weak systems could produce questioned costs, delayed reports, audit findings, contract disputes, or federal suspension. Strong systems allowed agencies to survive program transfers and diversify funding.

Oversight and Accountability

OEO monitored agencies through applications, grant conditions, regional offices, audits, site visits, program reports, civil-rights reviews, and performance assessments.

Congress conducted hearings and investigations concerning program effectiveness, political activity, fiscal management, and community participation.

State and local officials also exercised oversight through designation powers, board representation, matching contributions, state law, and public-agency controls.

Accountability was complicated because agencies answered to several constituencies: federal funders, local governments, boards, residents, service participants, auditors, and private partners.

These constituencies could define success differently. Federal officials might emphasize compliance, residents access and responsiveness, and local officials coordination or political stability.

Political-Activity Restrictions

Federal funds could not lawfully be used for partisan electoral activity. Agencies and employees were subject to statutory and grant restrictions concerning political campaigns, lobbying, voter influence, and use of program resources.

Nonpartisan civic engagement could include public education, resident meetings, testimony, benefits advocacy, and communication with government officials.

The boundary between advocacy and prohibited political activity generated disputes. A campaign for improved sanitation or welfare procedures could affect elected officials without constituting partisan campaigning.

Allegations of political activity were sometimes based on genuine misuse and sometimes reflected opposition to resident organizing or civil-rights advocacy.

United States v. Orleans

In United States v. Orleans (1976), the Supreme Court considered whether a local Community Action Agency was a federal agency for purposes of liability under the Federal Tort Claims Act.

The Court held that federal funding, regulation, and oversight did not make the local agency a federal instrumentality when day-to-day operations remained under local control.

The decision confirmed an important institutional distinction: Community Action Agencies implement federally supported programs but are ordinarily separate local entities rather than components of the federal government.

The case does not mean that agencies are free from federal requirements. They remain subject to grant law, civil-rights obligations, audits, and program conditions.

Documented Outcomes

Community action created a national network of local antipoverty organizations and introduced new services into communities with limited institutional capacity.

Outcomes included:

  • greater coordination among employment, education, health, housing, welfare, and legal services;
  • local access points through neighborhood centers;
  • expanded preschool and family services;
  • development of community health and legal-aid institutions;
  • employment and leadership opportunities for residents;
  • formal low-income representation in agency governance;
  • improved outreach to people excluded from public benefits;
  • creation of local nonprofit and public administrative capacity;
  • development of community needs assessments and service plans;
  • new channels for civil-rights enforcement and resident advocacy; and
  • institutional continuity after OEO’s dissolution.

Results varied by program, locality, funding, governance, economic conditions, and administrative quality.

Major Achievements

  • Made local participation a statutory element of federal antipoverty administration.
  • Created Community Action Agencies across urban and rural America.
  • Supported direct federal-local relationships where state or municipal institutions were resistant.
  • Connected multiple services within one local planning framework.
  • Provided institutional homes for Head Start, health, legal, employment, and neighborhood programs.
  • Developed leadership and employment among residents of low-income communities.
  • Expanded the capacity of nonprofit and community-based organizations.
  • Created tripartite governance combining public officials, low-income representatives, and private community interests.
  • Established local needs assessment as a foundation for program planning.
  • Preserved a national community-action network through later administrative restructuring.

Limitations and Failures

  • Funding was small relative to the scale of poverty and local infrastructure needs.
  • Agency quality and administrative capacity varied widely.
  • Participation requirements were ambiguous and inconsistently implemented.
  • Representation did not always produce actual resident control.
  • Political conflict diverted time and resources from service delivery.
  • Local officials could capture boards or obstruct independent agencies.
  • Federal officials could suspend funding or impose priorities without sustained local agreement.
  • Programs could coordinate services but could not control regional labor markets, housing supply, or private investment.
  • Short grant periods undermined long-term planning.
  • Multiple funding streams created complex compliance burdens.
  • Community agencies sometimes duplicated existing services.
  • Program evaluation was uneven and objectives were difficult to measure consistently.
  • Political support weakened before many local institutions matured.

The program’s central limitation was the gap between comprehensive ambition and limited authority. Agencies were asked to attack the causes of poverty while lacking control over many institutions producing employment, housing, education, health, credit, and public safety outcomes.

How the Program Should Be Evaluated

Community action should not be evaluated as though it were one uniform treatment.

Evaluation should identify:

  • the specific agency and service area;
  • the applicable year and statutory regime;
  • the program components funded;
  • the population reached;
  • the board and participation structure;
  • the agency’s fiscal and administrative capacity;
  • the local economic and political environment;
  • the outcomes being measured;
  • the role of other public programs; and
  • whether institutional capacity survived after the grant ended.

Service delivery, political participation, organizational development, and poverty reduction are different objectives. An agency might succeed in one area and fail in another.

Nixon-Era Reorganization

President Richard Nixon sought to transfer major OEO programs to established cabinet departments and reduce the agency’s independent operating role.

Community action survived longer within OEO than several other programs because it lacked a clear departmental equivalent and possessed a national network of local agencies.

The administration criticized advocacy, duplication, weak management, and conflict with elected officials. Community-action supporters argued that departmental transfer would dilute antipoverty focus and resident participation.

By the early 1970s, OEO had lost many major programs. Community action, Legal Services, research, and residual economic-development functions remained central to its reduced portfolio.

Community Services Administration

The Headstart, Economic Opportunity, and Community Partnership Act of 1974 replaced OEO with the Community Services Administration in 1975.

The new agency inherited community-action grants, economic-development initiatives, emergency programs, and other remaining antipoverty functions.

Community Action Agencies continued to receive federal support and administer local services. The governing framework increasingly emphasized community services and economic development rather than the original presidential War on Poverty campaign.

The Community Services Administration was abolished in 1981.

Community Services Block Grant

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 replaced the national direct-grant community-action framework with the Community Services Block Grant, commonly abbreviated CSBG.

Under the block-grant model, federal funds generally flow to states, which distribute funds and oversee local eligible entities. Eligible tribes and tribal organizations may receive direct federal funding under applicable law.

Community Action Agencies remained the principal local delivery institutions. The block grant preserved local flexibility but increased state administrative authority.

Modern CSBG purposes include reducing poverty, revitalizing low-income communities, and helping individuals and families achieve greater self-sufficiency.

According to the federal Office of Community Services’ fiscal year 2023 reporting, tribes and approximately 1,000 local eligible entities delivered CSBG-funded services across the national network.

Modern Tripartite Board Structure

Federal Community Services Block Grant law requires private nonprofit eligible entities to administer programs through tripartite boards.

The general structure consists of:

  • one-third elected public officials or their representatives, subject to statutory alternatives when insufficient officials are available;
  • at least one-third people selected through democratic procedures adequate to represent low-income residents of the area served; and
  • remaining members from business, industry, labor, religious, welfare, education, or other major community groups and interests.

Public eligible entities may use a board or another state-approved mechanism ensuring low-income participation in program planning, implementation, and evaluation.

The modern structure preserves the 1960s compromise among public authority, community representation, and private institutional participation.

Modern Community Action Services

Modern Community Action Agencies frequently combine CSBG funds with other public and private funding.

Services may include:

  • employment and workforce development;
  • early-childhood education;
  • housing and homelessness assistance;
  • energy and utility assistance;
  • weatherization;
  • nutrition and food assistance;
  • transportation;
  • financial education and asset building;
  • emergency services;
  • health and family support;
  • senior services;
  • youth development;
  • community economic development; and
  • referral and case-management services.

CSBG is generally a flexible core funding source rather than the only source financing these activities.

Community Needs Assessment

Needs assessment is a central modern community-action practice.

Agencies collect and analyze information concerning:

  • population and income;
  • employment and wages;
  • education;
  • housing and homelessness;
  • health and disability;
  • food and energy insecurity;
  • transportation;
  • child care;
  • public benefits;
  • community assets;
  • resident priorities; and
  • service gaps.

Quantitative data are combined with surveys, interviews, public meetings, focus groups, program records, and partner input.

A needs assessment should guide strategic planning rather than merely satisfy a grant requirement.

Results-Oriented Management and Accountability

Results-Oriented Management and Accountability, commonly known as ROMA, became a national performance-management framework within the community-action network.

ROMA links assessment, planning, implementation, achievement of results, and evaluation in a continuous cycle.

The framework encourages agencies to distinguish among:

  • resources invested;
  • services delivered;
  • people reached;
  • changes in individual or family conditions;
  • changes in community conditions; and
  • organizational performance.

Performance systems improve comparability but cannot eliminate differences in local service portfolios and economic conditions.

Organizational Standards

Modern CSBG administration uses organizational standards to strengthen agency governance, fiscal management, human resources, data, planning, and board oversight.

Standards address:

  • consumer and community input;
  • community engagement;
  • community assessment;
  • organizational leadership;
  • board governance;
  • strategic planning;
  • human-resource management;
  • financial operations and oversight; and
  • data and analysis.

These requirements reflect lessons from the original program’s uneven administrative capacity and recurring audit problems.

Institutional Legacy

The Community Action Program outlived OEO because it created local organizations with legal identity, staff, boards, facilities, records, funding relationships, and community constituencies.

Its institutional legacy includes:

  • a national network of Community Action Agencies;
  • tripartite governing boards;
  • community-needs assessments;
  • resident participation in program governance;
  • multiservice neighborhood organizations;
  • local federal-grant administration;
  • cross-agency service coordination;
  • organizational capacity in low-income communities;
  • program-incubation models; and
  • the Community Services Block Grant.

The network remains decentralized. No single modern agency administers an identical set of services in every community.

Why the Community Action Program Matters

The Community Action Program changed federal antipoverty administration by recognizing low-income residents as potential decision-makers rather than merely recipients of services.

It created local institutions capable of receiving federal funds, coordinating multiple programs, employing residents, gathering data, and representing community needs.

The program also exposed a central democratic conflict: whether participation should supplement established government or redistribute authority away from officials and institutions that had failed low-income communities.

Its direct-grant model weakened after 1967 and ended with the 1981 block-grant transition, but the core institutions survived. Community Action Agencies, tripartite boards, needs assessments, and local multiservice planning remain part of federal social policy.

The program’s legacy is therefore both administrative and political. It established a durable service network while leaving unresolved questions concerning representation, local control, state oversight, federal responsibility, and the structural causes of poverty.

Summary

The Community Action Program was the local antipoverty framework established by Title II of the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964. It authorized urban and rural communities to mobilize public and private resources, coordinate services, and develop programs addressing employment, education, health, housing, welfare, legal needs, and other causes or conditions of poverty.

Its defining feature was the requirement for maximum feasible participation by residents of the areas and groups served. Local Community Action Agencies administered programs through public or nonprofit institutions, neighborhood councils, governing boards, delegate agencies, and service centers.

Community action generated political conflict because federal funds could support organizations independent of governors, mayors, and established service systems. The Economic Opportunity Amendments of 1967 increased local governmental authority and formalized tripartite board representation.

OEO was replaced by the Community Services Administration in 1975, and the direct-grant system was replaced by the Community Services Block Grant in 1981. Community Action Agencies nevertheless survived as a national network administering flexible antipoverty services through local boards, needs assessments, partnerships, and multiple funding sources.
