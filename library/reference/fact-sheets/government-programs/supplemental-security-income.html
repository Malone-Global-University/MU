What Is Supplemental Security Income?

Supplemental Security Income is a federal cash-assistance program for people with limited income and resources who are age sixty-five or older, blind, or disabled.

Social Security administers SSI, but SSI is not a Social Security insurance benefit. Eligibility does not depend on prior payroll contributions or insured work status.

The federal benefit is intended to provide a minimum level of income for food, clothing, shelter, and other basic needs. States may add supplementary payments.

SSI is means tested. Monthly eligibility and payment amounts can change with income, resources, living arrangements, marital status, work, institutional residence, and other circumstances.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains SSI’s historical origins, statutory structure, federal benefit, state supplementation, age and disability standards, financial eligibility, deeming, living arrangements, application, appeals, work incentives, Medicaid, overpayments, program integrity, and current 2026 statistics.

It does not replace an individualized Social Security determination. SSI rules are detailed and fact specific, and state supplementary and Medicaid rules vary.

Social Security Disability Insurance, retirement benefits, survivor benefits, Medicare, Medicaid, state general assistance, and Disabled Adult Child benefits are related but separate programs.

Geographic Scope

SSI is payable to eligible residents of the fifty states, the District of Columbia, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, and American Samoa are excluded from the federal SSI program.

Residents of excluded territories may receive separate locally administered aged, blind, or disabled assistance financed under federal territorial caps and local law.

In United States v. Vaello Madero (2022), the Supreme Court held that the Constitution did not require Congress to extend SSI to residents of Puerto Rico.

Public Assistance Before SSI

The Social Security Act of 1935 created federal grants for state Old-Age Assistance and Aid to the Blind programs.

Congress added Aid to the Permanently and Totally Disabled in 1950.

These three programs were known as the adult public-assistance categories.

States and localities determined eligibility and payment levels under federal matching arrangements, producing extensive variation in benefits, administration, definitions, and access.

Problems with the Adult Assistance Categories

Before SSI, adult assistance involved hundreds of state and local agencies and widely different payment standards.

Similar people could receive substantially different aid depending on residence.

Local investigations, state definitions of disability or blindness, administrative fragmentation, and uneven resources complicated access.

Policymakers increasingly argued for a nationally administered minimum payment.

Nixon Administration Welfare Reform

President Richard Nixon proposed broad welfare reform through the Family Assistance Plan.

Congress did not enact the complete family-assistance proposal, but proposals to federalize the adult assistance categories survived.

Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare Elliot Richardson recommended administration by the Social Security Administration because of its national field-office and data-processing infrastructure.

Social Security Amendments of 1972

President Nixon signed Public Law 92-603 on October 30, 1972.

Title XVI created the federal Supplemental Security Income program, effective January 1974.

The law established federal eligibility categories, income and resource tests, payment standards, state supplementation, and Social Security administration.

The legislation also made major changes to Social Security, Medicare, disability insurance, and automatic benefit adjustment.

Conversion to SSI in 1974

Social Security converted more than three million people from state adult-assistance programs into SSI.

The conversion required federal records, applications, benefit calculations, state supplementation agreements, notices, and coordination with Medicaid.

Special “grandfather” protections preserved selected state definitions and resource exclusions for some converted recipients.

General-Revenue Financing

SSI payments are financed through annual federal general revenues.

Payroll taxes finance Social Security insurance, not SSI.

SSI does not have a dedicated trust fund comparable to the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance or Disability Insurance trust funds.

Congress appropriates funds sufficient to pay benefits and administer the program under permanent law.

Federal Administration

The Social Security Administration administers SSI through headquarters, program-service centers, teleservice centres, hearings offices, and a nationwide field-office network.

Federal responsibilities include:

  • receiving applications;
  • determining nonmedical eligibility;
  • obtaining disability decisions from state Disability Determination Services;
  • calculating and issuing payments;
  • administering participating state supplements;
  • conducting redeterminations and continuing disability reviews;
  • processing appeals;
  • appointing and monitoring representative payees;
  • recovering overpayments;
  • administering work incentives;
  • coordinating Medicaid determinations; and
  • publishing statistics, policy, and program instructions.

SSI and Social Security Disability Insurance

SSI and Social Security Disability Insurance both use federal disability standards for adults but differ financially.

SSDI requires insured work credits and pays a benefit based on covered earnings. SSI requires limited income and resources and pays according to the federal benefit rate.

A person can receive both programs when the Social Security disability benefit is low enough to permit an SSI payment. This is called concurrent eligibility.

Work incentives and health-coverage rules differ between the programs.

Eligibility Categories

An applicant must satisfy at least one categorical requirement:

  • be age sixty-five or older;
  • meet the statutory definition of blindness; or
  • meet the applicable adult or childhood disability standard.

The applicant must also satisfy income, resource, residence, citizenship or noncitizen status, institutional, and procedural requirements.

Eligibility Based on Age

A person age sixty-five or older does not need to prove disability to qualify for SSI.

The person must still meet financial, residence, citizenship, application, and other program rules.

Older applicants may receive both SSI and Social Security retirement or survivor benefits, with the Social Security benefit counted as income.

Statutory Blindness

A person is statutorily blind when best-corrected central visual acuity is 20/200 or less in the better eye or the widest diameter of the visual field in the better eye is twenty degrees or less.

The blindness standard is distinct from broader medical or educational definitions of visual impairment.

Blind SSI recipients receive special earned-income and work-expense treatment.

Adult Disability Standard

An adult younger than sixty-five is disabled for SSI when a medically determinable physical or mental impairment prevents substantial gainful activity, has lasted or is expected to last at least twelve continuous months, or is expected to result in death.

The evaluation considers medical severity, residual functional capacity, past relevant work, age, education, and other work in the national economy.

A diagnosis alone does not establish disability.

Adult Sequential Evaluation

  1. Substantial gainful activity. Is the applicant working above the applicable level?
  2. Severe impairment. Does the impairment significantly limit basic work activity?
  3. Listings. Does the impairment meet or medically equal a listed impairment?
  4. Past relevant work. Can the person perform qualifying past work?
  5. Other work. Can the person adjust to other work existing in significant numbers?

Substantial Gainful Activity

Substantial gainful activity, commonly abbreviated SGA, is a threshold used principally when deciding whether an adult applicant is disabled.

In 2026, the ordinary nonblind SGA amount is $1,690 per month and the statutory-blind amount is $2,830.

Earnings below or above a threshold are important but do not always determine the result without considering subsidies, impairment-related expenses, unsuccessful work attempts, self-employment, and other rules.

Once a person receives SSI, payment is generally adjusted through SSI income rules rather than terminating solely because earnings exceed SGA.

Child Disability Standard

A child younger than eighteen is disabled when a medically determinable physical or mental impairment results in marked and severe functional limitations and has lasted or is expected to last at least twelve months or result in death.

The child must not be performing substantial gainful activity.

Childhood evaluation focuses on age-appropriate functioning rather than ability to perform adult work.

Childhood Functional Domains

Functional equivalence considers six domains:

  • acquiring and using information;
  • attending and completing tasks;
  • interacting and relating with others;
  • moving about and manipulating objects;
  • caring for oneself; and
  • health and physical well-being.

A child generally functionally equals the listings with an extreme limitation in one domain or marked limitations in two domains.

Age-Eighteen Redetermination

A child receiving disability-based SSI must be redetermined under the adult disability standard after turning eighteen.

Childhood eligibility does not guarantee adult eligibility.

Parental income and resource deeming generally ends at age eighteen, which can make some young adults financially eligible even when they were not eligible as children.

Section 301 protections can continue payments temporarily for some recipients participating in approved education, vocational rehabilitation, or employment programs when statutory conditions are met.

Presumptive Disability or Blindness

Social Security may authorize temporary payments when available evidence strongly indicates disability or blindness and final medical development will take additional time.

Presumptive payments can continue for as many as six months.

They generally need not be repaid solely because the final disability decision is unfavorable, although overpayments can arise for nonmedical reasons.

Compassionate Allowances

Compassionate Allowances identify diseases and conditions that clearly meet Social Security’s disability standards.

The initiative accelerates case processing but does not waive nonmedical SSI eligibility.

Financial Eligibility

SSI eligibility and payment depend on countable income and resources.

The program considers income received during a month and resources owned at the beginning of a month under detailed federal rules.

Some income and property are excluded completely, excluded temporarily, or reduced through statutory disregards.

The Federal Benefit Rate is the maximum federal SSI payment before reduction for countable income, living arrangements, institutional status, or other factors.

Cost-of-Living Adjustments

SSI federal benefit rates increase automatically when a Social Security cost-of-living adjustment is payable.

The adjustment is based on the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers.

The 2026 adjustment is 2.8 percent.

Automatic benefit adjustment does not apply to the general SSI income exclusions or the resource limits.

State Supplementation

States may supplement the federal SSI payment.

Supplement amounts can vary by living arrangement, age, disability category, institutional residence, or other state classifications.

Some supplements are administered by Social Security and combined with the federal payment. Others are paid directly by the state.

Several states and the Northern Mariana Islands do not provide an optional state supplement.

Mandatory State Supplementation

States were required to protect specified people converted from pre-1974 adult-assistance programs from losing income because of federalization.

Mandatory minimum supplementary payments preserve designated historical payment levels for qualifying converted recipients.

The number of recipients protected by these rules has declined over time.

What Counts as Income?

Income is money or support a person receives that can be used to meet food or shelter needs.

SSI distinguishes:

  • earned income;
  • unearned income;
  • deemed income; and
  • in-kind support and maintenance involving shelter.

Food provided by other people is no longer included in SSI in-kind support calculations as of September 30, 2024.

Earned Income

Earned income includes wages, net self-employment earnings, sheltered-workshop payments, and certain royalties or honoraria.

SSI excludes the first sixty-five dollars of monthly earned income plus any unused portion of the general twenty-dollar exclusion, then excludes one-half of the remaining earnings.

As a result, after applicable exclusions, the federal SSI payment generally falls by one dollar for every two dollars of countable earnings.

Unearned Income

Unearned income includes Social Security benefits, pensions, unemployment compensation, cash gifts, interest, dividends, some child support, and other income not produced by current work.

SSI generally excludes the first twenty dollars of combined unearned and earned income each month, subject to statutory exceptions.

Countable unearned income generally reduces SSI dollar for dollar after exclusions.

Major Income Exclusions

Excluded income can include:

  • the first twenty dollars of most monthly income;
  • the first sixty-five dollars of monthly earnings and one-half of additional earnings;
  • Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits;
  • most home-energy assistance;
  • specified educational grants and scholarships;
  • income set aside under an approved Plan to Achieve Self-Support;
  • impairment-related work expenses;
  • blind work expenses;
  • certain disaster assistance;
  • specified housing assistance;
  • some Native trust and settlement payments;
  • tax refunds; and
  • other payments excluded by federal statute.

Student Earned-Income Exclusion

A person younger than twenty-two who regularly attends school may receive an additional earned-income exclusion.

In 2026, Social Security can exclude as much as $2,410 per month, up to $9,730 for the year.

This exclusion is applied before the ordinary earned-income exclusions.

Deeming of Income and Resources

Deeming treats some income and resources of another person as available to the SSI applicant or recipient even when the money is not actually transferred.

Deeming can apply from:

  • an ineligible spouse to an eligible spouse;
  • a parent or stepparent to a child younger than eighteen;
  • a sponsor to a sponsored noncitizen; and
  • the sponsor’s spouse in qualifying cases.

Federal allocations protect part of the other person’s income for personal needs and other household members.

Spousal Deeming

When an eligible person lives with an ineligible spouse, part of the spouse’s income and resources may be deemed to the applicant or recipient.

When both spouses are eligible, Social Security applies the lower eligible-couple federal rate and the couple resource limit.

The SSI couple rate is less than twice the individual rate.

Parental Deeming

Income and resources of parents living with a child younger than eighteen may be deemed to the child.

Social Security deducts allocations for parents and other children before determining deemed income.

Deeming usually ends the month after the child turns eighteen.

Noncitizen Sponsor Deeming

Income and resources of a sponsor can be deemed to a sponsored noncitizen for a statutory period, subject to exceptions.

Indigence, abuse, sponsor death, work-history rules, refugee status, and other provisions can affect the result.

Countable Resources

Resources are cash or property a person owns and can convert to cash for support.

Countable resources include bank accounts, cash, investments, some life insurance, additional vehicles, and nonexcluded real property.

A person whose countable resources exceed the limit at the beginning of a month is generally ineligible for that month.

Major Resource Exclusions

Major exclusions include:

  • the home in which the person lives and its land;
  • household goods and personal effects;
  • one vehicle used for transportation;
  • specified burial spaces and limited burial funds;
  • property essential to self-support;
  • approved PASS property and funds;
  • up to $100,000 in an eligible ABLE account;
  • certain Individual Development Accounts;
  • retroactive Social Security and SSI payments for nine months;
  • federal tax refunds for twelve months;
  • specified disaster, relocation, and crime-victim payments; and
  • other statutory exclusions.

Home Exclusion

The principal residence and the land on which it sits are generally excluded regardless of value.

The exclusion can continue during some temporary absences when the person intends to return.

Property not serving as the principal home may be countable unless another exclusion applies.

Vehicle Exclusion

One vehicle used by the recipient or household for transportation is excluded regardless of value.

Additional vehicles may be countable according to equity and use.

ABLE Accounts

Achieving a Better Life Experience accounts allow eligible people with disabilities to save for qualified disability expenses.

Up to $100,000 in an ABLE account is excluded from the SSI resource limit.

Balances above $100,000 can suspend SSI cash eligibility while Medicaid may continue under statutory conditions.

Qualified withdrawals are generally excluded from income and resources when used and retained under applicable rules.

Trusts and Special-Needs Planning

Trust assets can count as resources when the individual can revoke the trust or direct use of the assets.

Federal law provides exceptions for certain first-party special-needs trusts, pooled trusts, and qualifying third-party trusts.

Medicaid reimbursement, age, disability, trustee authority, and distribution terms affect treatment.

Trust analysis is legally complex and should not be reduced to the label “special-needs trust.”

Transfer of Resources

Transferring a resource for less than fair market value can produce SSI ineligibility for as many as thirty-six months.

Exceptions apply for transfers involving a home, spouse, disabled child, qualifying trust, or undue hardship.

The rule applies principally to transfers occurring after December 14, 1999.

Conditional Benefits While Selling Property

A person with nonliquid property exceeding the resource limit may receive conditional SSI while making reasonable efforts to sell it.

Payments may become repayable from sale proceeds.

The property must meet statutory and procedural conditions.

Living Arrangements

Living arrangements can affect the federal payment because SSI is intended to meet basic shelter needs.

Social Security considers whether a person lives independently, in another person’s household, in a public-assistance household, in an institution, in a shelter, or in another special setting.

The amount a person contributes toward shelter costs can affect the payment.

In-Kind Support and Maintenance

In-kind support and maintenance is noncash support involving shelter supplied by another person.

Shelter includes rent, mortgage payments, property taxes, heating fuel, gas, electricity, water, sewerage, and garbage collection.

Effective September 30, 2024, food is no longer included in in-kind support and maintenance calculations.

One-Third Reduction Rule

When a person lives throughout a month in another person’s household and receives all shelter support from the household, the federal payment can be reduced by one-third.

Detailed household, contribution, ownership, and public-assistance rules determine whether the rule applies.

Presumed Maximum Value Rule

When the one-third-reduction rule does not apply, Social Security may value shelter assistance under the presumed maximum value rule.

The recipient can rebut the presumption by proving the actual current market value of support minus payment is lower.

Rental Subsidy Policy

Paying less than market rent can constitute in-kind shelter support.

Effective September 30, 2024, Social Security expanded nationwide a rental-subsidy exception previously operating in seven states.

Under the revised policy, some applicants and recipients renting from a parent or child are not treated as receiving a subsidy when required rent meets the applicable threshold.

Public-Assistance Households

A person living in a qualifying public-assistance household is generally not treated as receiving in-kind support from other members.

Effective September 30, 2024, the definition was expanded so that the household needs:

  • an SSI applicant or recipient; and
  • at least one other household member receiving a qualifying public income-maintenance payment.

SNAP was added to the qualifying public income-maintenance programs.

Residence in Institutions

A person residing throughout a month in a public institution is generally ineligible for SSI.

Exceptions and special rules apply to medical facilities, publicly operated community residences, emergency shelters, educational settings, and temporary institutionalization.

A person in a Medicaid-funded medical institution may receive a federal payment limited to thirty dollars per month, sometimes increased by a state supplement.

Temporary Institutionalization Benefits

A recipient entering a medical institution may continue the full benefit temporarily when a physician certifies that the stay is not expected to exceed ninety days and the person needs funds to maintain a home.

The protection is designed to prevent loss of housing during short medical stays.

Homelessness and Public Shelters

A person does not need a fixed address to qualify for SSI.

A recipient living in a public emergency shelter for people experiencing homelessness can receive the full applicable benefit for up to six months in a nine-month period.

Address, communication, representative-payee, banking, medical evidence, and reporting problems can still create barriers.

Residence and Absence from the United States

An applicant must reside in the United States as defined for SSI.

A person generally becomes ineligible after being outside the covered United States for a full calendar month or thirty consecutive days, subject to narrow exceptions.

Eligibility ordinarily resumes after thirty consecutive days of presence in the covered United States.

Special exceptions can apply to children of military personnel and qualifying students temporarily abroad.

Citizenship and Noncitizen Eligibility

United States citizens residing in a covered jurisdiction may qualify if they meet all other requirements.

Noncitizen eligibility depends on qualified status, entry date, work history, refugee or humanitarian status, military connection, disability history, sponsorship, and other statutory categories.

The 1996 welfare-reform law substantially restricted noncitizen eligibility.

Some qualified noncitizens face a seven-year time limit tied to humanitarian status unless they obtain another qualifying basis.

Requirement to Apply for Other Benefits

SSI applicants and recipients must apply for other cash benefits for which they may be eligible.

These can include Social Security, pensions, unemployment compensation, veterans benefits, and workers’ compensation.

Other benefits generally count as unearned income and can reduce SSI.

Application Process

A person can begin an SSI application online in eligible circumstances, by telephone, or through a Social Security field office.

A complete application may require:

  • identity and age evidence;
  • citizenship or immigration records;
  • income and resource documentation;
  • living-arrangement and shelter information;
  • marriage and household information;
  • medical providers and records;
  • education and work history;
  • bank information;
  • permission to obtain records; and
  • applications for other benefits.

Protective Filing

A person who contacts Social Security and expresses intent to file can establish a protective filing date.

The protective date can preserve potential eligibility when the formal application is completed within the required period.

SSI payments cannot ordinarily begin before the first month after the application or protective filing date.

Disability Determination Services

State Disability Determination Services make initial medical disability decisions for Social Security under federal standards.

Federal funds finance these state agencies.

Medical consultants, disability examiners, and other staff review evidence and may order consultative examinations.

Medical and Functional Evidence

Evidence can include treatment records, diagnostic testing, clinical findings, medication, hospital records, school records, individualized education programs, employer information, third-party statements, and consultative examinations.

Social Security considers the combined effect of all medically determinable impairments.

Symptoms are evaluated with the medical and nonmedical evidence.

Representation

Applicants and recipients may appoint an attorney or qualified nonattorney representative.

Representatives can obtain records, submit evidence, communicate with Social Security, prepare appeals, and appear at hearings.

Fees generally require Social Security authorization and are often paid from past-due benefits under statutory limits.

Initial Determination

Social Security issues written notice concerning eligibility, payment amount, onset, past-due benefits, state supplementation, and appeal rights.

A favourable disability decision can still produce no payment when income, resources, residence, or another nonmedical requirement is not met.

Appeals

The ordinary administrative appeal sequence is:

  1. reconsideration;
  2. hearing before an administrative law judge;
  3. Appeals Council review; and
  4. federal district-court review.

Deadlines generally run sixty days from receipt of the notice, subject to good-cause extensions.

Benefit Continuation During Appeal

Recipients appealing certain suspensions, reductions, or disability cessations can request continued payments when they act within specified deadlines.

Continued benefits may become an overpayment if the final decision is unfavourable, although waiver can be available.

Payment Timing

SSI is ordinarily paid on the first day of the month for that month.

When the first falls on a weekend or federal holiday, payment is generally made on the preceding business day.

This schedule can produce two deposits in one calendar month without creating an extra month of benefits.

Past-Due Benefits

Past-due SSI can arise after application processing, appeal, correction, or reopening.

Large past-due payments to adults are generally paid in as many as three installments six months apart, subject to exceptions.

Past-due payments for children may require a dedicated account.

Dedicated Accounts for Children

When a child receives more than six months of past-due SSI, the representative payee generally must place the payment in a separate dedicated account.

Funds can be used principally for medical treatment, education, job-skills training, special equipment, personal assistance, housing modification, therapy, rehabilitation, and other impairment-related needs.

Ordinary food, clothing, and shelter use is restricted unless related to the child’s impairment or approved under applicable rules.

Representative Payees

Social Security appoints a representative payee when a recipient cannot manage or direct management of payments.

Most child recipients receive benefits through a parent or guardian payee.

Payees must use funds for the beneficiary’s current needs, conserve excess funds, keep records, report changes, and return misused or excess payments.

Financial Redeterminations

Social Security periodically reviews nonmedical eligibility.

Redeterminations examine income, resources, living arrangements, marriage, household members, citizenship, institutional status, and other conditions.

Frequency depends on the likelihood of payment change and administrative workload.

Continuing Disability Reviews

Social Security periodically reviews whether a disabled or blind recipient remains medically eligible.

Reviews are scheduled according to expected medical improvement, statutory requirements, and agency workload.

Most adult cessations require evidence of medical improvement related to ability to work or another statutory basis.

Children are reviewed under childhood continuing-disability standards and redetermined under adult rules at eighteen.

Reporting Responsibilities

Recipients must report changes that can affect eligibility or payment.

Reportable changes include:

  • wages and self-employment;
  • other income;
  • resources;
  • address and living arrangement;
  • marriage, separation, or divorce;
  • household composition;
  • institutional admission or discharge;
  • absence from the United States;
  • school attendance for student exclusions;
  • work expenses;
  • death; and
  • changes affecting citizenship or noncitizen status.

Changes generally must be reported no later than ten days after the end of the month in which they occur.

Wage Reporting

Recipients can report wages through approved mobile, telephone, online, mail, or field-office methods.

Timely monthly reporting reduces overpayments and underpayments.

Social Security encourages reporting by the sixth day of the following month when using monthly wage-reporting systems.

Overpayments

An overpayment occurs when Social Security pays more than the person was entitled to receive.

Causes include unreported income, delayed wage processing, resource changes, living-arrangement errors, duplicate payments, institutionalization, appeal continuation, and agency error.

The notice should identify the amount, reason, months, recovery proposal, appeal rights, and waiver rights.

Overpayment Recovery

Social Security ordinarily recovers SSI overpayments by withholding the lesser of ten percent of total monthly income or the entire SSI payment, unless another rate is agreed or ordered.

A recipient can request a lower recovery rate based on inability to meet ordinary and necessary living expenses.

Recovery can also involve voluntary repayment, tax refund offset, later Social Security benefits, or other authorized collection.

Waiver of Overpayment Recovery

Recovery can be waived when the person was without fault and collection would defeat the purpose of the program, be against equity and good conscience, or satisfy another statutory waiver basis.

Waiver is distinct from disputing whether the overpayment exists or whether the amount is correct.

Underpayments

Underpayments occur when a person receives less than legally due.

Causes include delayed application processing, incorrect income counting, missed exclusions, delayed state supplementation, appeal reversal, and agency error.

Correct administration requires protection against both overpayment and underpayment.

SSI Work Incentives

SSI contains provisions intended to allow recipients with disabilities to work while retaining some cash and health protection.

Major incentives include:

  • earned-income exclusions;
  • student earned-income exclusion;
  • impairment-related work expenses;
  • blind work expenses;
  • Plans to Achieve Self-Support;
  • Section 1619(a) special cash benefits;
  • Section 1619(b) continued Medicaid;
  • expedited reinstatement;
  • Ticket to Work;
  • ABLE accounts; and
  • property essential to self-support exclusions.

Impairment-Related Work Expenses

A disabled recipient can deduct reasonable, unreimbursed costs of disability-related items or services needed for work.

Examples can include attendant care, medication copayments, assistive technology, specialized transportation, vehicle modification, and medical devices.

The deduction reduces countable earned income and can increase the SSI payment.

Blind Work Expenses

A blind recipient can deduct ordinary and necessary expenses attributable to earning income, even when the item is not disability related.

Examples can include federal, state, and local income taxes, Social Security taxes, transportation, meals during work, service-animal expenses, and assistive services.

Blind work expenses are deducted after other earned-income exclusions.

Plan to Achieve Self-Support

A Plan to Achieve Self-Support, commonly called PASS, allows a person who is blind or disabled to set aside income or resources for an approved occupational goal.

Approved funds can pay for education, training, equipment, transportation, business start-up, assistive technology, and other necessary expenses.

Set-aside income and resources are excluded when the person follows the approved plan.

Section 1619(a) Special Cash Benefits

Section 1619(a) allows a disabled SSI recipient to continue receiving a reduced cash payment when earnings exceed the substantial-gainful-activity level.

The person must continue to meet disability and other eligibility requirements.

Payment is calculated under ordinary SSI income rules.

Section 1619(b) Continued Medicaid

Section 1619(b) can preserve Medicaid when earnings reduce the SSI cash payment to zero.

The person must remain blind or disabled, satisfy non-earnings SSI requirements, need Medicaid to continue working, and have earnings below an individualized or state threshold reflecting lost benefits and medical support.

State threshold amounts are updated annually.

Expedited Reinstatement

A former disability recipient whose benefits ended because of work can request expedited reinstatement within five years when the same or a related impairment prevents substantial work.

Provisional cash and health benefits can be available for as many as six months while Social Security decides the request.

Ticket to Work

Ticket to Work connects eligible disability beneficiaries with approved employment networks or state vocational-rehabilitation agencies.

Services can include career counselling, training, job placement, benefits counselling, and employment support.

Timely progress under an assigned ticket can protect a recipient from scheduled continuing disability reviews in specified circumstances.

Relationship to Medicaid

SSI often functions as a gateway to Medicaid.

Medicaid treatment falls into three broad state structures:

Section 1634 states
Social Security determines Medicaid eligibility through the SSI application under an agreement with the state.
SSI-criteria states without a Section 1634 agreement
The state requires a separate Medicaid application but generally uses SSI eligibility criteria.
Section 209(b) states
The state may use Medicaid standards no more restrictive than those in effect in January 1972 and must provide a spend-down pathway under applicable law.

Relationship to Medicare

SSI eligibility alone does not create Medicare eligibility.

A recipient can receive Medicare through age, Social Security disability insurance, end-stage renal disease, or another Medicare basis.

People receiving both Medicare and SSI commonly qualify for Medicaid and Medicare Savings Programs that assist with premiums and cost sharing.

Relationship to SNAP

SSI recipients may qualify for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program under household income and resource rules.

SSI counts as unearned income for SNAP, while SNAP benefits are excluded from SSI income and resources.

Effective September 30, 2024, SNAP was added to the public income-maintenance programs used in SSI public-assistance-household policy.

Relationship to Housing Assistance

Federal housing assistance is generally excluded from SSI income.

Waiting lists, rent rules, utility allowances, and household composition remain separate from SSI.

Housing assistance can be essential because the maximum SSI payment is below typical housing costs in many communities.

Relationship to TANF and General Assistance

A person can live in a household receiving TANF while applying for SSI.

State interim assistance programs can provide payments while an SSI disability claim is pending and recover authorized amounts from the first retroactive SSI payment.

State general assistance and SSI are separate, and receipt or repayment treatment depends on state and federal agreements.

Concurrent SSI and Social Security Benefits

A person can receive SSI and Social Security retirement, survivor, or disability benefits at the same time.

The Social Security benefit is generally countable unearned income after the twenty-dollar exclusion.

Concurrent recipients can have Medicaid, Medicare, or both.

Children and Families

Child SSI provides cash support to families caring for children with severe disabilities.

The benefit can help pay for rent, utilities, transportation, specialised food, clothing, medical needs, supervision, and other costs.

Parental deeming, fluctuating wages, representative-payee rules, dedicated accounts, school evidence, and age-eighteen redetermination make child cases administratively distinct.

Older Adults

SSI provides income to older adults with little or no Social Security or pension income.

Older recipients frequently receive partial SSI supplements to low Social Security benefits.

Access can be limited by lack of awareness, language, disability, application burden, immigration rules, and the belief that SSI requires prior work.

Women and SSI

Women historically have lower lifetime earnings, fewer pension benefits, longer life expectancy, and greater exposure to unpaid caregiving.

SSI therefore plays an important role in income security for older women and women with disabilities.

Spousal deeming and the eligible-couple rate can reduce payments after marriage.

Race and Economic Inequality

SSI eligibility reflects unequal exposure to disability, low wages, limited wealth, occupational injury, segregated health care, and unstable employment.

Black, Native, Latino, and other underserved populations can rely heavily on SSI while also encountering administrative, medical-evidence, language, and geographic barriers.

Civil-rights compliance requires nondiscrimination, accessible service, language assistance, and fair disability evaluation.

Rural Access

Rural applicants can face shortages of medical providers, transportation, broadband, legal representation, and Social Security field offices.

Limited specialist evidence can delay disability determinations.

SSI income and Medicaid access can be especially important where employment opportunities and employer-sponsored insurance are limited.

Applicants Experiencing Homelessness

Homeless applicants may lack medical records, identification, stable contact, transportation, and a safe place to store documents.

Outreach programs, shelters, medical providers, legal services, and representative payees can assist with applications and ongoing eligibility.

SSI can support housing stability, but the federal benefit alone is frequently insufficient to rent housing without additional assistance.

Incarceration and Fugitive-Felon Rules

SSI is generally suspended when a person is confined in a jail, prison, or other public institution for a full calendar month.

Release procedures can permit reinstatement when eligibility resumes.

Federal law also restricts payments to certain people fleeing to avoid prosecution or custody and to people violating probation or parole, subject to warrant and enforcement standards.

Marriage and the Eligible-Couple Rate

Two eligible spouses receive a combined federal maximum below twice the individual maximum.

Spousal deeming can reduce or eliminate SSI when one spouse is ineligible.

Critics describe these rules as a marriage penalty because two unmarried eligible people can receive more combined federal SSI than an eligible married couple.

The Asset-Limit Controversy

The $2,000 individual and $3,000 couple resource limits have not changed since 1989.

Inflation has greatly reduced their real value.

Critics argue that low limits prevent emergency savings, reliable transportation, and financial stability. Supporters argue that an asset test targets benefits to people with the greatest financial need.

ABLE accounts, excluded retirement arrangements, PASS, and special-needs trusts provide limited pathways around the ordinary limits.

Benefit Adequacy

The maximum federal SSI benefit remains below the federal poverty guideline for an individual.

State supplements improve adequacy in some states but vary widely.

Housing, utilities, food, transportation, medical expenses, and personal care can exceed the federal payment.

SSI recipients commonly depend on Medicaid, SNAP, housing assistance, informal support, and state programs.

Administrative Complexity

SSI combines medical disability law with monthly means testing, living-arrangement rules, state supplements, Medicaid coordination, work incentives, and continuing reviews.

Complexity creates processing delays, payment errors, reporting burdens, and difficulty predicting how work or household changes affect benefits.

The September 2024 simplifications reduced selected in-kind-support burdens but did not eliminate the program’s broader complexity.

Improper Payments

SSI is vulnerable to overpayments and underpayments because eligibility can change monthly and depends on information from recipients, employers, banks, states, institutions, and other agencies.

Improper payment does not necessarily indicate recipient fraud.

Agency delay, complex rules, retrospective wage information, and outdated records are major causes.

Program Integrity

Program integrity includes accurate eligibility, timely payment, identity verification, wage and resource matching, representative-payee oversight, disability review, overpayment recovery, and fraud investigation.

Integrity also requires correcting underpayments, protecting due process, and avoiding burdens that wrongfully exclude eligible people.

Fraud and Penalties

Knowingly making false statements, concealing changes, misusing another person’s benefits, or submitting fraudulent medical or financial evidence can produce administrative penalties, repayment, civil liability, or criminal prosecution.

Social Security can impose nonpayment sanctions for specified false or misleading statements.

Fraud must be distinguished from mistake, misunderstanding, incapacity, or agency error.

Suspension and Termination

SSI can be suspended for excess income or resources, institutional residence, absence from the United States, failure to provide information, or another temporary condition.

Eligibility can be terminated after twelve consecutive months of suspension, requiring a new application in many cases.

Section 1619 status and specified exceptions alter this rule for qualifying workers.

Program Scale in June 2026

Social Security reported 7,323,731 recipients of federally administered SSI or state supplementary payments at the end of June 2026.

  • 1,185,336 were classified as aged;
  • 6,138,395 were classified as blind or disabled;
  • 1,017,718 were younger than eighteen;
  • 3,800,166 were ages eighteen through sixty-four;
  • 2,505,847 were age sixty-five or older; and
  • 2,530,466 also received Social Security benefits.

Payments in June 2026

Total federally administered payments for June 2026 were approximately $5.780 billion, including retroactive payments.

The average monthly payment excluding retroactive amounts was $738.10.

The average federal component was $712.27 among recipients receiving a federal payment, while state-supplement averages varied according to state and eligibility category.

Recipient Profile

Most SSI recipients qualify because of blindness or disability rather than age alone.

Children represent approximately one million recipients, working-age adults form the largest age group, and more than two million recipients are age sixty-five or older.

Many older recipients also receive Social Security, while many children and working-age adults rely primarily on SSI.

Program Cost

Federal SSI expenditures include monthly benefits, retroactive payments, administrative expense, vocational-rehabilitation reimbursements, and program operations.

State supplementary payments add further public spending.

Costs change with benefit rates, demographics, disability incidence, immigration law, income, participation, state supplements, and administrative performance.

Economic and Social Effects

SSI increases cash income for people with severe disabilities and older adults with limited income.

It reduces poverty and material hardship, supports housing and food purchases, and brings federal funds into local economies.

Medicaid linkage can be more valuable than the cash payment for people requiring intensive health and personal-care services.

Low benefit and asset limits restrict SSI’s ability to create long-term financial security.

Major Achievements

  • Federalized inconsistent state adult-assistance programs.
  • Created a national minimum federal payment for eligible aged, blind, and disabled people.
  • Removed prior-work requirements from disability-based cash assistance.
  • Extended cash assistance to children with severe disabilities.
  • Linked millions of recipients to Medicaid.
  • Established automatic cost-of-living adjustments.
  • Created work incentives protecting cash and health coverage.
  • Allowed state supplementation above the federal minimum.
  • Developed national disability, appeals, representative-payee, and payment systems.
  • Provided a central federal safety net for people with little connection to contributory Social Security.

Limitations and Failures

  • The maximum federal payment remains below the poverty guideline.
  • Resource limits have not increased since 1989.
  • The eligible-couple rate and spousal deeming can penalize marriage.
  • Parental deeming can exclude children in families with modest income.
  • Medical decisions and appeals can take many months.
  • Monthly means testing produces frequent overpayments and underpayments.
  • Living-arrangement rules remain complex and intrusive.
  • State supplementation creates geographic inequality.
  • SSI is unavailable in Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, and American Samoa.
  • Work incentives are difficult to understand and administer.
  • Continuing disability reviews can disrupt medically vulnerable recipients.
  • The federal payment is often insufficient for independent housing.

Major Controversies

Adequacy versus cost
Debate concerns whether the federal rate should rise to the poverty level and whether exclusions should be indexed.
Asset testing
Low limits target need but discourage savings and financial resilience.
Disability adjudication
Strict standards protect program integrity but can delay or deny support to severely impaired people.
Marriage rules
The eligible-couple rate and deeming reduce combined benefits.
Territorial exclusion
Residents of several territories receive less generous assistance outside SSI.
Work incentives
The program encourages work through exclusions but imposes complex reporting and payment adjustments.
Overpayment recovery
Recovery protects public funds but can impose severe hardship when errors accumulated over long periods.
In-kind support
Counting informal shelter assistance has been criticized as administratively costly and punitive.

The 2024 SSI Simplification Rules

Three major regulatory changes took effect September 30, 2024.

  1. Food removed from in-kind support calculations. Informal food assistance no longer reduces SSI.
  2. Rental-subsidy policy expanded nationwide. A favourable exception formerly limited to seven states now applies nationally.
  3. Public-assistance household definition expanded. The household needs an SSI applicant or recipient and at least one other person receiving a qualifying public benefit; SNAP was added to the list.

These changes reduced reporting burden and increased benefits for some recipients.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

SSI remained active under Title XVI of the Social Security Act and continued to be administered by the Social Security Administration.

The 2026 federal maximums were $994 for an individual and $1,491 for an eligible couple.

The resource limits remained $2,000 and $3,000.

The 2024 living-arrangement simplifications remained in effect, and Social Security’s June 2026 statistics reported approximately 7.324 million recipients.

Legislative proposals to increase benefits or resource limits should be distinguished from enacted law.

Why Supplemental Security Income Matters

SSI transformed fragmented state adult assistance into a federally administered national program.

It provides cash to people who cannot qualify for adequate Social Security benefits because of limited work history, low lifetime earnings, childhood disability, unpaid caregiving, or immigration and labour-market circumstances.

The program is also a health-coverage gateway. For many recipients, Medicaid access and Section 1619(b) protection are as important as the monthly cash payment.

SSI’s national structure promotes uniformity, but state supplements and Medicaid rules continue to produce geographic variation.

The program’s continuing policy challenge is the conflict between strict means testing and meaningful economic security. Benefit and resource rules target limited public funds but can leave recipients below poverty and unable to accumulate ordinary savings.

Summary

Supplemental Security Income is the federal means-tested cash-assistance program for people who are age sixty-five or older, blind, or disabled and have limited income and resources.

Congress created SSI in the Social Security Amendments of 1972, and payments began in January 1974. The program replaced state-administered Old-Age Assistance, Aid to the Blind, and Aid to the Permanently and Totally Disabled.

Social Security administers SSI, but general federal revenues—not Social Security payroll taxes—finance the program. In 2026, the maximum federal payment is $994 for an individual and $1,491 for an eligible couple.

Countable resources generally cannot exceed $2,000 for an individual or $3,000 for a couple. These limits have not changed since 1989.

SSI eligibility depends on categorical status, income, resources, residence, citizenship or qualifying noncitizen status, living arrangements, and other rules. Children use a marked-and-severe-functional-limitations standard, while adults are evaluated according to inability to perform substantial gainful activity.

SSI commonly provides access to Medicaid and contains work incentives including earned-income exclusions, PASS, Section 1619(a), and Section 1619(b).

As of June 2026, approximately 7.324 million people received federally administered SSI or state supplementary payments, with an average monthly payment of approximately $738.10.

SSI created a national minimum-income system for low-income aged and disabled people, but benefit inadequacy, frozen resource limits, administrative complexity, territorial exclusion, and marriage-related rules remain central controversies.
