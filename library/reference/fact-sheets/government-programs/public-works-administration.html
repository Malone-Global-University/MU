Public Works Administration

The New Deal capital-construction program that financed and supervised major federal, state, territorial, and local infrastructure projects through grants, loans, direct federal allotments, contracts, engineering review, and construction oversight.

Terminology Note

The program is commonly called the Public Works Administration or PWA throughout its history. Its original formal administrative name was the Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works, commonly abbreviated FEAPW.

The agency was formally renamed the Public Works Administration when it entered the Federal Works Agency on July 1, 1939. This fact sheet uses PWA for the entire operating program unless the formal administrative distinction is material.

The PWA must not be confused with the Works Progress Administration, later the Work Projects Administration. Both used similar abbreviations in period records and later writing, but their financing, worker eligibility, project scale, administrative structure, and principal policy objectives were different.

Overview

The Public Works Administration was a New Deal program created to stimulate economic recovery by financing large, useful, and durable public construction.

Unlike direct work-relief agencies, the PWA generally did not place unemployed people on federal relief rolls and assign them to short-term projects. It financed construction through federal departments, state and local governments, public authorities, and private contractors operating under public contracts.

The program sought to create employment at multiple levels. Workers obtained jobs at construction sites, while factories, mines, mills, railroads, equipment manufacturers, and suppliers received orders for steel, cement, lumber, machinery, electrical equipment, glass, pipe, vehicles, and other materials.

PWA administrators also expected completed infrastructure to support future economic development. Schools educated students, hospitals expanded healthcare, water and sewer systems improved public health, airports and ports increased transportation capacity, dams generated power and controlled water, and roads and bridges connected markets.

Harold Ickes built a reputation for detailed review, competitive procurement, documentation, inspection, and resistance to corruption. This approach protected federal funds and produced high-quality facilities, but it also made the PWA slower than emergency-relief programs designed to create jobs immediately.

The agency's record combined national investment with local inequality. PWA rules attempted to protect wages and reduce racial discrimination, but construction unions, contractors, state governments, local sponsors, segregated housing policy, and unequal access to public institutions limited the distribution of benefits.

Classification and Scope

This page treats the PWA as a government program because the primary subject is its financing and operating system: project eligibility, applications, loans, grants, direct federal allotments, engineering review, labor standards, construction oversight, public housing, racial administration, expenditures, and outcomes.

The National Industrial Recovery Act, annual public-works appropriations, the 1938 PWA legislation, Reorganization Plan No. I of 1939, and Executive Order 9357 belong primarily under Laws and Legislation.

The Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works and Public Works Administration may also be profiled under Institutions. Individual dams, bridges, tunnels, airports, housing developments, schools, hospitals, and public buildings may warrant separate entries under Institutions, Historical Events, Geography, or Technology.

The PWA Housing Division is discussed here as an operating branch, while the broader Federal Public Housing Program should receive a separate fact sheet covering the PWA demonstrations, United States Housing Authority, local housing authorities, later public-housing administration, and long-term policy development.

Historical Context

The Great Depression caused severe unemployment, bank failure, falling prices, reduced industrial production, collapsing construction, business insolvency, farm distress, and declining tax revenue.

Construction was especially important to the national economy because it employed workers directly and created demand across manufacturing, mining, transportation, finance, architecture, engineering, and building materials.

State and local governments possessed extensive unmet infrastructure needs but faced reduced revenue and limited access to credit. Many municipalities could not finance schools, water systems, sewage treatment, hospitals, roads, or public buildings through ordinary bond markets.

Franklin D. Roosevelt proposed a federal public-works program as part of the National Industrial Recovery Act. The act combined industrial regulation under Title I with public construction under Title II.

Congress approved the law on June 16, 1933. Roosevelt immediately created an emergency public-works administration and authorised early allocations for highways and naval construction.

Program Purpose

Increase Employment

Public construction would hire building-trades workers, engineers, architects, equipment operators, clerks, inspectors, and support personnel.

Stimulate Industrial Demand

Projects would generate orders for construction materials, machinery, vehicles, electrical systems, furnishings, and transportation services.

Restore Purchasing Power

Wages paid to workers and income received by suppliers would circulate through local and national markets.

Finance Essential Infrastructure

Federal credit and grants would allow public bodies to undertake projects delayed by declining revenue or frozen capital markets.

Improve Public Health and Safety

Water, sewer, drainage, hospital, school, fire, road, and building projects addressed conditions that threatened health and public safety.

Expand Productive Capacity

Dams, power plants, airports, ports, roads, bridges, tunnels, and reclamation projects supported long-term commerce, transportation, energy, and development.

Modernise Government Facilities

Public agencies acquired buildings and systems better suited to expanding administrative, educational, health, judicial, and public-service responsibilities.

Demonstrate Federal Public Housing

The Housing Division attempted to demonstrate that government could clear selected slum areas and construct planned, sanitary, low-rent housing.

Legal and Administrative Basis

National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933

Title II authorised preparation of a comprehensive public-works program and empowered the president to construct, finance, or assist public projects through federal agencies and public bodies.

The act authorised projects involving highways, public buildings, water control, conservation, power development, transportation, housing, and other public facilities.

Executive Order 6174

Issued on June 16, 1933, the order appointed Colonel Donald H. Sawyer as temporary administrator, created a Special Board for Public Works, and authorised up to $400 million for highways and up to $238 million for naval construction.

Executive Order 6198

Roosevelt appointed Harold L. Ickes to exercise the office of Federal Emergency Administrator of Public Works.

Executive Order 6252

Issued on August 19, 1933, the order delegated project-financing powers and established federal rules governing labor, wages, hours, payment, posting, and enforcement.

Emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1935

Congress continued the public-works administration and allowed it to perform functions assigned under the National Industrial Recovery Act and the new relief appropriation.

Public Works Administration Program of 1938

Congress authorised a renewed construction program intended to counter the recession of 1937–1938. Local sponsors generally financed 55 percent of project costs, while federal grants could provide 45 percent.

Reorganization Plan No. I of 1939

The plan placed the program within the newly created Federal Works Agency and formally renamed it the Public Works Administration.

Executive Order 9357

Effective July 1, 1943, the order transferred remaining PWA functions, powers, records, property, personnel, and funds to the Office of the Federal Works Administrator.

Organisational Structure

Federal Emergency Administrator of Public Works

Harold Ickes directed national policy, project approval, funding recommendations, contract standards, investigation, and public accountability.

Special Board for Public Works

The initial board brought together cabinet officers and federal officials to review the proposed national program and major allocations.

Projects Control Division

Project-control units established priorities, processed applications, maintained federal and nonfederal project records, tracked construction, and prepared reports and statistics.

Engineering Division

Engineers and architects reviewed plans, specifications, construction methods, materials, costs, changes, and inspection findings.

Legal Division

Attorneys evaluated sponsor authority, bond legality, property rights, contracts, loan security, procurement, and statutory compliance.

Finance and Accounting Divisions

Financial personnel reviewed municipal credit, processed payments, audited costs, supervised accounts, and monitored federal loans and grants.

Division of Investigation

Investigators examined personnel matters, contractor conduct, corruption allegations, project performance, labor complaints, and compliance with PWA requirements.

Housing Division

The Housing Division developed low-rent demonstration projects, acquired sites, cleared selected areas, planned housing, supervised construction, and established management arrangements.

State and Regional Field Offices

Engineers, advisers, inspectors, attorneys, and administrative personnel worked with sponsors and monitored local projects.

Federal Projects

Federal projects were undertaken by departments, bureaus, commissions, corporations, or other agencies of the United States government.

The PWA allocated funds to the responsible federal agency, which designed, contracted, and constructed the project under applicable federal rules.

Federal projects included:

  • naval vessels, shipyards, and shore facilities;
  • Army posts, military buildings, and defence infrastructure;
  • federal office buildings and courthouses;
  • reclamation dams, irrigation works, and power systems;
  • river, harbour, levee, and flood-control projects;
  • roads, parkways, and federal-aid highways;
  • national-park facilities;
  • Indian-affairs schools, hospitals, roads, water, and community facilities;
  • territorial public works;
  • public housing developed directly through the federal Housing Division; and
  • aviation, communications, research, and specialised infrastructure.

Direct federal projects allowed national agencies to advance large or technically specialised facilities without depending on a state or municipal borrower.

Nonfederal Projects

Nonfederal projects were sponsored by states, territories, counties, municipalities, school districts, public authorities, and other legally constituted public bodies.

Sponsors retained responsibility for project ownership and operation. Federal assistance could take the form of a grant, loan, combined loan and grant, or purchase of public obligations.

Common nonfederal projects included:

  • elementary and secondary schools;
  • colleges, universities, dormitories, and laboratories;
  • hospitals, clinics, sanatoriums, and public-health buildings;
  • courthouses, city halls, fire stations, police stations, and civic buildings;
  • water-treatment, distribution, and storage systems;
  • sewers, drainage, and sewage-treatment plants;
  • roads, bridges, tunnels, viaducts, and transit facilities;
  • airports and aviation facilities;
  • ports, docks, terminals, and warehouses;
  • public power plants and distribution systems;
  • parks, stadiums, auditoriums, swimming pools, and recreation buildings;
  • jails, correctional facilities, and public institutions; and
  • local low-rent housing and slum-clearance initiatives.

Financing Methods

Direct Federal Allotment

Congress or the president allocated PWA funds to a federal agency responsible for construction.

Grant

The federal government supplied a portion of eligible project cost that the sponsor was not required to repay.

Loan

The PWA or a participating federal institution advanced funds to a public sponsor, with repayment secured through bonds, revenues, taxes, or other lawful obligations.

Combined Loan and Grant

A sponsor received a federal grant for part of the project and borrowed the remainder necessary to supplement its own contribution.

Bond Purchase

The federal government could purchase municipal or public-authority securities when private capital markets would not supply acceptable financing.

Contract Financing

Federal departments and public sponsors awarded construction contracts, and PWA funds reimbursed or paid eligible costs according to approved terms.

Financing formulas changed over time. Early PWA assistance frequently combined loans and grants under rules developed from the National Industrial Recovery Act. The 1938 program commonly offered a 45 percent federal grant with the sponsor responsible for the remaining 55 percent.

Nonfederal Application and Approval Process

  1. Identify a public need. A sponsor determined that a school, hospital, road, water system, bridge, public building, utility, or other facility was required.
  2. Establish legal authority. The sponsor demonstrated that it could own, construct, finance, operate, and maintain the proposed project.
  3. Prepare plans and cost estimates. Engineers, architects, attorneys, and financial officers developed preliminary plans, estimated costs, and identified revenues or tax support.
  4. Submit an application. The sponsor requested a federal loan, grant, or combined assistance and provided supporting documentation.
  5. Conduct engineering review. PWA personnel examined feasibility, design, construction standards, estimated quantities, site conditions, and cost.
  6. Conduct financial review. Analysts evaluated debt capacity, revenue projections, tax authority, bond legality, and ability to finance the sponsor's share.
  7. Evaluate employment and recovery effects. Officials considered direct construction labor, material purchases, indirect employment, public need, and readiness.
  8. Approve or reject the project. Approved projects received an allotment, loan commitment, grant agreement, or bond-purchase arrangement.
  9. Complete local legal procedures. Sponsors could need elections, ordinances, bond validation, land acquisition, permits, or court approval.
  10. Advertise and award contracts. Construction was generally awarded through competitive bidding under federal and sponsor requirements.
  11. Begin construction. Contractors hired workers and purchased materials, while the sponsor and PWA supervised compliance.
  12. Inspect and approve payments. Federal personnel reviewed progress, materials, payrolls, change orders, and completed work before funds were released.
  13. Complete and operate the facility. The sponsor accepted ownership and assumed maintenance, staffing, debt service, and public operation.

Project Selection Standards

PWA officials generally examined whether a proposal:

  • served a lawful and substantial public purpose;
  • was technically feasible;
  • could begin within the required construction period;
  • had complete or sufficiently advanced plans;
  • used sound engineering and architectural standards;
  • had a reasonable cost estimate;
  • could be financed without creating unsustainable public debt;
  • would generate employment and industrial orders;
  • avoided unnecessary duplication of existing facilities;
  • possessed a suitable site and clear property rights;
  • included lawful procurement and contracting procedures;
  • could be operated and maintained after construction; and
  • complied with federal labor, wage, and nondiscrimination rules.

A valuable project could be rejected or delayed because the sponsor lacked borrowing authority, failed to secure a site, submitted incomplete plans, could not repay a loan, or missed a statutory deadline.

Harold Ickes and Administrative Control

Harold L. Ickes combined his role as secretary of the interior with leadership of the public-works administration.

Ickes emphasised documentation, engineering review, competitive bidding, financial capacity, inspection, and investigation. He sought to prevent politically favoured, technically unsound, or corrupt projects from receiving federal money.

The administrator maintained direct oversight over major approvals and created an investigation division reporting to him.

This administrative culture produced the nickname “Honest Harold” and helped protect the PWA's reputation. It also slowed the movement of funds into active construction during an emergency in which speed was a central policy objective.

The tension between rapid stimulus and prudent capital administration remained one of the defining features of the program.

Construction and Contracting

Most nonfederal PWA construction was performed by private firms selected through competitive public bidding.

Contractors employed workers, purchased materials, managed subcontractors, operated equipment, and completed the work according to approved plans and specifications.

PWA and sponsor inspectors reviewed construction quality, quantities, payrolls, material compliance, safety, wage practices, and progress.

Contractors could not freely modify approved designs or costs. Change orders required justification and review because alterations could increase federal exposure, reduce project value, or evade competitive bidding.

The program's use of private contractors distinguished it from direct federal work relief. PWA workers usually entered ordinary employer-employee relationships under public contracts rather than becoming project enrollees of a federal relief agency.

Labor Standards

Hours

Executive Order 6252 generally limited work to thirty hours per week for contractors not governed by an approved industrial code, with specified exceptions for weather, labor shortages, and remote camps.

Just and Reasonable Wages

Workers were to receive wages sufficient to support a standard of living in decency and comfort within the applicable hour limits.

Weekly Cash Payment

Wages were to be paid in lawful money at least weekly and without deductions for rent, goods, or other obligations that had not been collected through legal process.

Posted Wage Rates

Contractors and subcontractors had to post wage rates prominently at the work site.

Back-Pay Enforcement

Where workers received less than the required amount, the administrator could order the employer to pay wages due and refer continuing violations for legal action.

Prohibition of Convict Labor

PWA rules prohibited employment of convict labor and prohibited use of materials manufactured or produced by convict labor on covered projects.

Collective Bargaining and Union Conditions

PWA construction operated within the rapidly changing labor environment created by New Deal collective-bargaining policy, union organising, trade jurisdiction, closed-shop practices, strikes, and local wage agreements.

Direct and Indirect Employment

Direct employment occurred at construction sites and in architectural, engineering, administrative, inspection, transport, and project-support work.

Indirect employment occurred when contractors ordered steel, cement, lumber, glass, brick, pipe, machinery, electrical systems, furnishings, vehicles, and other goods.

Additional economic effects arose through railroad and shipping activity, quarrying, mining, wholesale trade, financial services, and worker spending.

The PWA therefore measured its effect differently from a direct work-relief agency. The number of workers physically employed at project sites captured only part of the intended stimulus.

Estimates of indirect jobs depend on assumptions about supply chains, displacement, timing, and how much production would have occurred without the project. Such estimates should be presented as economic analysis rather than precise enrollee counts.

Construction Speed and the Readiness Problem

Large infrastructure requires surveys, design, land acquisition, financing, legal authority, bidding, material orders, and construction management.

Many state and local governments entered 1933 without complete plans for projects large enough to absorb major federal funds.

Sponsors also faced debt limits, election requirements, court challenges, bond-market weakness, and difficulty financing their share.

The PWA consequently moved more slowly than officials and the public initially expected. Critics argued that funds remained unspent while unemployment continued.

PWA leaders responded that poorly reviewed projects could waste federal money, create excessive municipal debt, or produce unusable facilities.

The experience strengthened later federal interest in maintaining advance plans and “shelf” projects that could begin more quickly during recession.

Roads, Bridges, Tunnels, and Transportation

Transportation projects connected cities, rural areas, ports, industrial districts, farms, public institutions, and emerging metropolitan regions.

Projects included:

  • federal-aid highways and state roads;
  • urban arterial streets and parkways;
  • large suspension, arch, truss, and concrete bridges;
  • railroad grade separations and viaducts;
  • vehicular tunnels;
  • subway and rapid-transit improvements;
  • bus, rail, and ferry terminals;
  • ports, wharves, docks, and navigation facilities;
  • airports, runways, terminals, and hangars; and
  • roads serving parks, dams, military facilities, and public institutions.

Major PWA-associated transportation projects included the Triborough Bridge, Lincoln Tunnel, Overseas Highway connections, and extensive state and municipal road systems.

Water, Sewer, and Public-Health Infrastructure

Many communities lacked modern water treatment, storage, distribution, sewer collection, and sewage disposal.

PWA assistance financed reservoirs, intake systems, pumping stations, filtration plants, wells, water towers, mains, sewers, drainage channels, and treatment plants.

These projects reduced waterborne disease, supported fire protection, enabled housing and industrial development, and improved environmental sanitation.

Revenue-producing water and sewer systems were often suited to loan financing because user charges could support debt repayment.

Poor communities sometimes struggled to meet federal financing or sponsor-contribution requirements even when their public-health need was severe.

Schools and Educational Facilities

Schools became one of the most visible categories of PWA construction.

Projects replaced unsafe buildings, added classrooms, improved sanitation, expanded laboratories and vocational facilities, and provided auditoriums, gymnasiums, libraries, cafeterias, and athletic grounds.

Colleges and universities received dormitories, classrooms, laboratories, libraries, utilities, hospitals, stadiums, and administrative buildings.

PWA publications credited the program with supporting a large share of the new school construction completed during the principal New Deal years.

In segregated jurisdictions, federal financing could produce separate and unequal schools. A new Black school could represent a major local improvement while remaining underfunded, isolated, or inferior to the white system.

Hospitals and Health Facilities

PWA funds supported general hospitals, public-health buildings, tuberculosis sanatoriums, mental-health institutions, clinics, nurses' residences, medical schools, and institutional utilities.

New facilities expanded bed capacity, improved fire safety and sanitation, replaced obsolete buildings, and supported professional medical care.

Federal summaries credited the program with financing a substantial share of hospital construction during the 1930s.

Access remained unequal. Hospitals and wards could be segregated, Black communities could receive fewer facilities, and local medical systems could exclude Black physicians, nurses, and patients from equal participation.

Dams, Reclamation, Flood Control, and Public Power

PWA financing supported some of the New Deal's largest water and power projects.

Major objectives included:

  • hydroelectric generation;
  • irrigation and agricultural development;
  • municipal and industrial water supply;
  • flood control;
  • navigation;
  • soil and watershed conservation;
  • regional development; and
  • competition with or regulation of private power systems.

PWA-associated projects included Grand Coulee Dam, Fort Peck Dam, Bonneville-related development, and numerous municipal power and water systems.

Large dams created employment and public benefits but could also displace communities, alter ecosystems, inundate land, transform Indigenous resources, and distribute power and irrigation benefits unevenly.

Public Buildings and Architecture

The PWA financed federal, state, territorial, county, municipal, educational, medical, judicial, institutional, and recreational buildings.

Construction frequently used reinforced concrete, structural steel, masonry, modern mechanical systems, fire-resistant materials, and simplified monumental design.

The phrase PWA Moderne is commonly used for buildings combining classical symmetry and civic scale with restrained ornament, vertical lines, geometric relief, modern materials, and Art Deco influence.

Not every PWA building followed one style. Designs reflected local architects, institutional purpose, climate, regional materials, federal standards, and contemporary architectural movements.

Harold Ickes appointed a Committee on Architectural Survey to photograph and evaluate PWA buildings. The resulting records document thousands of projects and helped establish the agency's architectural legacy.

PWA Housing Division

The PWA Housing Division represented an early direct federal effort to construct low-rent public housing and clear selected areas described as slums.

Initial attempts to finance limited-dividend private or public housing corporations produced fewer projects than expected. Legal, financial, land-assembly, and profitability problems made private sponsorship difficult.

The federal government therefore acquired sites and directly developed a group of demonstration projects through the Public Works Emergency Housing Corporation and Housing Division.

Fifty-one demonstration projects were developed nationwide. Techwood Homes in Atlanta became the first completed project and an influential model for federal design and management standards.

Projects generally included low-rise apartments or row houses, planned open space, modern plumbing, improved light and ventilation, playgrounds, landscaping, and community facilities.

Public housing was racially segregated. Separate projects were designated for white and Black occupancy, and site selection frequently reflected existing racial boundaries and political resistance.

Slum clearance could displace more households than the new development rehoused. Eligibility, rent limits, family composition, and racial designation determined who could return.

The Housing Act of 1937 shifted the permanent federal model toward federally assisted local housing authorities. The United States Housing Authority inherited major policy and administrative experience from the PWA Housing Division.

African American Employment

African American construction workers entered the Depression with high unemployment, occupational exclusion, unequal apprenticeship, segregated unions, closed-shop barriers, and concentration in lower-paid work.

PWA construction created potential employment in skilled, semiskilled, and unskilled occupations. Actual access depended on contractors, unions, local labor markets, project location, federal enforcement, and the availability of Black workers in particular trades.

Harold Ickes issued a September 21, 1933, directive stating that PWA employment should not discriminate because of color or religious affiliation.

Robert C. Weaver and other Black advisers argued that general nondiscrimination language was insufficient. Contractors could claim equal treatment while hiring few or no Black skilled workers.

Selected public-housing contracts therefore used numerical evidence standards. In one Atlanta provision, failure to direct at least 12 percent of skilled-labor payments to Black skilled workers in a month could serve as prima facie evidence of discrimination.

This mechanism shifted part of the evidentiary burden to the contractor and represented an early federal experiment in measurable contract-compliance enforcement.

Racial Discrimination and Program Limits

Union Exclusion

Craft unions in many cities excluded Black workers or confined them to segregated locals with restricted access to jobs.

Contractor Discrimination

Contractors could refuse Black applicants, assign them only to unskilled work, deny advancement, or rely on discriminatory union referral systems.

Local Occupational Patterns

Federal officials sometimes measured participation against existing local employment patterns, which already reflected decades of discrimination.

Segregated Facilities

PWA financing supported schools, hospitals, housing, parks, and institutions operating under Jim Crow segregation.

Unequal Project Distribution

Communities with greater legal, fiscal, engineering, and political capacity obtained more assistance than communities with severe need but limited tax bases or borrowing authority.

Public Housing Segregation

Federal public-housing demonstrations recognised Black housing need but institutionalised separate projects and did not challenge residential segregation as a national policy.

Enforcement Constraints

Investigation and contract clauses could improve access on particular projects, but the PWA lacked a comprehensive civil-rights enforcement system capable of restructuring the construction industry nationwide.

Women and PWA Employment

Most on-site construction employment went to men because the building trades, unions, contractors, apprenticeships, and heavy-construction occupations were overwhelmingly male.

Women worked as architects, engineers, landscape architects, attorneys, accountants, clerks, stenographers, researchers, housing managers, social workers, designers, and administrative personnel.

Women also benefited as teachers, nurses, patients, students, tenants, public employees, and community residents using completed facilities.

The program did not establish equal access to construction trades or systematically challenge gender discrimination in hiring and promotion.

Selected Major Projects

PWA assistance reached projects of widely different scale. Some were nationally prominent engineering works; others were local schools, water systems, bridges, hospitals, and public buildings.

  • Grand Coulee Dam, Washington: large federal reclamation and hydroelectric project.
  • Fort Peck Dam, Montana: major Missouri River flood-control and power project.
  • Triborough Bridge, New York: metropolitan bridge and roadway complex.
  • Lincoln Tunnel, New York and New Jersey: vehicular tunnel connection beneath the Hudson River.
  • Overseas Highway, Florida: reconstruction and connection of transportation infrastructure through the Florida Keys.
  • Bonneville-area power and navigation works, Oregon and Washington: Columbia River infrastructure supported through federal public works.
  • Washington National Airport, District of Columbia and Virginia: federal airport planning and construction.
  • Techwood Homes, Georgia: first completed PWA low-rent housing demonstration.
  • Williamsburg Houses, New York: large urban public-housing development.
  • University, school, hospital, and civic-building programs: thousands of facilities across states and territories.
  • Naval construction: warships, shipyards, docks, and shore facilities authorised under early PWA allocations.

Attribution should identify whether the PWA supplied the entire cost, a grant, a loan, a direct federal allotment, or only one phase of a larger project.

Economic Effects

Construction Employment

Contractors hired workers in industries that had experienced severe Depression-era collapse.

Industrial Orders

Material purchases supported steel, cement, lumber, machinery, electrical, transportation, and manufacturing employment.

Municipal Credit

Federal loans and bond purchases restored access to capital for public bodies unable to borrow on acceptable private terms.

Local Fiscal Obligations

Sponsor contributions and debt service required taxes, utility revenues, or other income, creating long-term obligations alongside new assets.

Long-Term Productivity

Infrastructure reduced transportation costs, expanded public services, supported industry, and enabled urban and regional growth.

Timing Limitation

Because major construction required long preparation, the program's employment effect developed more slowly than direct emergency work relief.

Crowding-Out Debate

Economists and historians continue to debate how much public construction added to total employment versus displacing projects, labor, credit, or materials that private markets would otherwise have used.

Relationship to the Civil Works Administration

The Civil Works Administration was created in November 1933 to provide rapid winter employment.

Roosevelt allocated PWA funds to help finance the CWA, while the Federal Emergency Relief Administration supplied administrative and relief resources.

The CWA hired workers directly and began projects much faster than the PWA's capital-financing process.

Its brief operation demonstrated the federal government's capacity to employ millions quickly but also generated high costs and political concern. It ended in 1934.

The contrast between CWA speed and PWA deliberation influenced later creation of the Works Progress Administration.

Relationship to the United States Housing Authority

The PWA Housing Division directly developed a limited number of demonstration projects.

The Housing Act of 1937 created the United States Housing Authority and shifted the long-term public-housing system toward loans and subsidies supporting locally created housing authorities.

The USHA inherited design standards, management experience, personnel, project knowledge, and racial policies developed during the PWA demonstrations.

This institutional transition separated public housing from the broader PWA capital-construction portfolio and established a dedicated federal housing program.

The 1938 PWA Program

The recession of 1937–1938 increased unemployment and reversed part of the earlier recovery.

Congress authorised a renewed PWA construction program in 1938 with firm deadlines intended to accelerate applications, approvals, contract awards, and construction.

Communities submitted close to 10,000 applications during the first 101 days after Roosevelt signed the legislation.

The standard partnership required sponsors to provide 55 percent of project cost, while the federal government supplied a 45 percent grant.

The renewed program benefited from years of accumulated plans, administrative experience, and completed engineering. It moved more rapidly than the original 1933 mobilisation.

The 1938 program demonstrated the value of maintaining advance public-works plans and project pipelines before an economic downturn.

Transfer to the Federal Works Agency

Reorganization Plan No. I of 1939 consolidated major federal public-works institutions under the Federal Works Agency.

The new agency included the Public Works Administration, Works Progress Administration, Public Buildings Administration, Public Roads Administration, and United States Housing Authority, among other functions.

The reorganisation sought improved coordination of federal buildings, roads, public works, grants, loans, work relief, and housing.

As part of the change, the Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works received the formal name Public Works Administration.

The program increasingly concentrated on completing existing projects, settling accounts, administering remaining contracts, and supporting defence-related construction.

World War II and Program Termination

Defence mobilisation increased demand for military installations, airports, ports, power, water, roads, housing, factories, and transportation.

PWA-financed infrastructure and administrative experience contributed to the national capacity used during mobilisation.

As unemployment declined and federal construction shifted toward direct war appropriations, the rationale for a separate emergency public-works administration weakened.

Executive Order 9357 transferred all remaining PWA functions to the Federal Works Administrator effective July 1, 1943.

The transfer included records, property, personnel, contracts, assets, and unexpended balances. The Federal Works Administrator completed remaining obligations and liquidated the agency.

Program Achievements

  • It financed roughly 34,000 to 34,500 federal and nonfederal construction projects.
  • It restored large-scale public construction during the Great Depression.
  • It created direct construction employment and indirect industrial employment.
  • It expanded schools, hospitals, water systems, sewers, roads, bridges, airports, ports, and civic buildings.
  • It supported major dams, reclamation, power, flood-control, and transportation works.
  • It enabled public bodies to borrow when private capital markets were impaired.
  • It established durable federal engineering, architectural, accounting, inspection, and procurement standards.
  • It developed an early federal low-rent public-housing program.
  • It introduced measurable contract-compliance tools for detecting racial employment discrimination on selected projects.
  • It strengthened intergovernmental grant and loan administration.
  • It left public assets that remained in use for generations.
  • It demonstrated the economic and administrative value of advance capital planning.

Limitations and Failures

  • Project planning and review moved too slowly to provide immediate relief at the required scale.
  • Construction employment reached fewer people than direct work-relief programs.
  • Financially strong governments were better positioned to prepare applications and finance sponsor shares.
  • Poor communities could lack engineers, credit, legal authority, or matching funds.
  • Loans created debt obligations that some sponsors were reluctant or unable to assume.
  • Racial discrimination persisted among unions, contractors, local officials, and public institutions.
  • Many completed schools, hospitals, housing projects, and recreation facilities were segregated.
  • Public-housing clearance displaced households and institutionalised racially separate occupancy.
  • Women received limited access to construction trades and leadership.
  • Large dams and infrastructure could displace communities and damage ecosystems.
  • Some projects benefited politically influential communities more than areas with the greatest social need.
  • The program relied on temporary emergency authority rather than a permanent national capital-investment system.

Major Controversies

Speed versus Accountability

Critics argued that Ickes's review procedures delayed recovery. Supporters argued that the controls prevented corruption and unsound construction.

Loans versus Grants

Public sponsors sought larger grants, while federal officials used loans and sponsor contributions to conserve federal funds and test local commitment.

Geographic Distribution

States and cities disputed whether allotments reflected population, unemployment, political influence, project readiness, or fiscal capacity.

Public versus Private Power

Municipal power and dam projects generated conflict with private utilities over rates, markets, federal competition, and public ownership.

Labor and Union Access

Trade unions supported public construction but could resist hour rules, wage policies, nonunion labor, or federal intervention. Segregated and exclusionary unions restricted Black participation.

Public Housing

Housing projects generated conflict over federal ownership, slum clearance, tenant selection, rents, segregation, site location, property acquisition, and competition with private landlords.

Environmental and Community Costs

Large water, road, and power projects transformed landscapes and could impose costs on rural, Indigenous, low-income, or geographically isolated communities.

Documented Outcomes

The PWA restored construction activity and generated large orders for materials at a time when private building investment remained depressed.

Thousands of communities received infrastructure that they could not have financed under ordinary Depression-era conditions.

Public buildings and utility systems improved the operating capacity of state and local government.

Large projects contributed to regional development, public power, irrigation, flood control, aviation, metropolitan transportation, and military readiness.

The public-housing demonstrations established proof of federal construction capacity but also carried forward segregation and displacement.

PWA labor rules created federal leverage over wages, hours, contractor conduct, payment, and racial discrimination, although enforcement remained incomplete.

The agency's slow start limited its capacity as an immediate unemployment program, but its assets produced benefits extending far beyond the emergency period.

Architectural and Civic Legacy

PWA buildings often became central civic landmarks because they housed schools, courts, hospitals, libraries, city government, universities, utilities, and community activities.

Durable materials and conservative engineering helped many projects remain functional for decades.

The program increased professional opportunities for architects, engineers, planners, landscape architects, contractors, inspectors, and construction managers.

Federal review encouraged standardisation of plans, specifications, procurement, cost accounting, construction documentation, and photographic records.

Preservation assessment should identify later alterations, local architectural contribution, racial and community history, engineering significance, and the particular form of PWA assistance.

Records and Research Value

Principal records of the Public Works Administration are preserved by the National Archives in Record Group 135.

Records include:

  • general administrative files;
  • minutes of the Special Board for Public Works;
  • policy memoranda, orders, bulletins, and regulations;
  • federal and nonfederal project applications;
  • project docket files and indexes;
  • loan, grant, allotment, and bond records;
  • engineering plans, specifications, estimates, and correspondence;
  • construction and status reports;
  • inspection reports and change orders;
  • contracts, bids, and procurement records;
  • wage, hour, payroll, and labor-compliance files;
  • racial-discrimination complaints and employment reports;
  • investigation case files;
  • accounting and audit records;
  • Housing Division and slum-clearance records;
  • maps and charts;
  • architectural surveys;
  • photographs of projects and construction;
  • speeches, press releases, and public-information materials; and
  • liquidation and successor-agency files.

Related records appear in the files of the Department of the Interior, Federal Works Agency, Public Housing Administration, Bureau of Public Roads, Bureau of Reclamation, War Department, Navy Department, Indian Affairs, state agencies, municipal archives, public authorities, and project sponsors.

Research Cautions

  • PWA and WPA are not interchangeable. A building commonly attributed to “the WPA” may have been financed by the PWA or another New Deal agency.
  • The formal agency name changed. Records before July 1939 may use Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works even when contemporary publicity used PWA.
  • Financing is not the same as construction. A PWA grant or loan does not mean the federal agency designed or physically built the entire project.
  • One project could have multiple funding sources. Sponsors could combine PWA assistance with bonds, local funds, other federal allotments, and later appropriations.
  • Project totals vary. Sources may count applications, allotments, federal projects, nonfederal projects, contracts, buildings, or completed facilities differently.
  • Expenditure totals vary. Some figures count only original NIRA funds, while others include later emergency, relief, housing, and 1938 appropriations.
  • Public-housing records may be housed elsewhere. Housing Division files are also associated with Record Group 196 and Department of the Interior records.
  • Nondiscrimination policy did not guarantee equal outcomes. Local payrolls, union records, contractor files, and facility-use policies must be examined.
  • Architectural style does not prove PWA funding. A building resembling PWA Moderne requires documentary confirmation.
  • Promotional publications emphasised achievements. Agency reports should be compared with audits, investigations, local records, labor files, and later historical research.

Definitions

Allotment
A federal allocation of appropriated funds to a department, agency, sponsor, or approved project.
Bond purchase
Federal acquisition of public debt securities to supply capital to a state, municipality, or public authority.
Capital project
A long-lived public investment involving construction, major improvement, equipment, land, or infrastructure.
Direct federal project
A public work constructed by or through a federal department or agency using a federal allotment.
Federal Emergency Administration of Public Works
The formal name of the public-works administration from 1933 until the 1939 federal reorganisation.
Grant
Federal financial assistance that an eligible public sponsor was not required to repay when used in compliance with the agreement.
Loan
Repayable federal financing secured by bonds, taxes, utility revenues, assessments, or other lawful sponsor resources.
Nonfederal project
A public work owned and sponsored by a state, territory, municipality, county, public authority, school district, or other nonfederal public body.
Prima facie evidence
Evidence sufficient to support a finding unless rebutted; used in selected PWA contract provisions to identify probable racial discrimination.
Project docket
The official administrative file documenting an application, review, approval, financing, construction, changes, and completion.
Public authority
A legally created governmental or quasi-governmental body authorised to construct, own, finance, and operate public facilities.
Public Works Administration
The common name of the New Deal capital-construction program and the formal agency name adopted in 1939.
PWA Moderne
A descriptive architectural term for many 1930s public buildings combining monumental civic design with simplified classical, geometric, and Art Deco features.
Sponsor contribution
The local or state share of project cost supplied through cash, bonds, land, materials, equipment, engineering, or other eligible resources.
Work relief
Public assistance delivered through direct government-supported employment; a model associated more closely with the WPA than the PWA.

Summary

The Public Works Administration was the New Deal's principal capital-construction and infrastructure-finance program.

Created under the National Industrial Recovery Act in 1933, it financed federal, state, territorial, and local public works through direct allotments, grants, loans, bond purchases, and construction contracts.

The PWA supported roughly 34,000 to 34,500 projects, including schools, hospitals, roads, bridges, tunnels, dams, airports, water systems, sewers, public buildings, power facilities, naval construction, and low-rent public housing.

Most workers were employed by contractors or public sponsors rather than enrolled directly as relief workers. Federal rules regulated wages, hours, weekly payment, posted rates, convict labor, contractor conduct, and selected racial-employment standards.

Harold Ickes's detailed engineering, legal, financial, and investigative controls helped protect federal funds and produce durable facilities but slowed the agency's immediate employment effect.

African American workers gained access to some federally financed construction, and PWA officials developed early measurable tools for identifying discrimination. Nevertheless, exclusionary unions, contractors, Jim Crow institutions, segregated housing, and unequal local capacity restricted equitable participation.

The agency entered the Federal Works Agency in 1939 and was abolished as a separate organisation in 1943. Its infrastructure, public-housing experiments, contract standards, project records, and intergovernmental financing model became enduring components of federal public-investment policy.
