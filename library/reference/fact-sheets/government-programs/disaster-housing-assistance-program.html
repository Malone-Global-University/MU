Disaster Housing Assistance Program

The joint FEMA–HUD pilot programme that used local public housing agencies to provide temporary rental subsidies, landlord payments, housing placement, and recovery support to selected households displaced by major hurricanes.

Naming and Programme Variants

“Disaster Housing Assistance Program” refers to a family of closely related temporary programmes rather than one continuously operating programme with a single permanent rulebook.

KDHAP
The earlier Katrina Disaster Housing Assistance Program established in 2005 for certain households that had received HUD assistance or qualified through specified special-needs housing programmes before Hurricane Katrina.
DHAP-Katrina
The broader 2007 FEMA–HUD Disaster Housing Assistance Program for selected households displaced by Hurricanes Katrina and Rita.
DHAP-Ike
The programme established in 2008 for eligible households displaced by Hurricanes Gustav and Ike.
DHAP-Sandy
The programme established in 2013 for selected New York households displaced by Hurricane Sandy.

The names are sometimes used inconsistently in reports. KDHAP and the Disaster Voucher Program preceded the fully developed FEMA–HUD DHAP model and served narrower or different populations.

What Was the Disaster Housing Assistance Program?

DHAP was a joint federal pilot that provided temporary rent subsidies to disaster-displaced households through HUD’s public housing agency network.

FEMA selected eligible households and financed the programme. HUD issued operating requirements, coordinated public housing agencies, managed housing-administration systems, and served as the federal housing expert.

Participating public housing agencies helped households locate rental units, inspected units, determined allowable rent subsidies, entered contracts with landlords, issued monthly housing payments, maintained records, and performed programme-specific transition duties.

DHAP was designed to fill the period between FEMA’s immediate disaster housing assistance and a stable permanent housing solution.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the DHAP model used after Katrina, Rita, Gustav, Ike, and Sandy. It covers the programme’s origins, federal authority, interagency structure, household referral, rent subsidies, landlord contracts, inspections, case management, rent transition, portability, programme termination, conversion vouchers, civil-rights obligations, costs, evaluations, limitations, and current inactive status.

It distinguishes DHAP from FEMA’s standard Rental Assistance, Direct Lease, Multifamily Lease and Repair, Transitional Sheltering Assistance, transportable temporary housing units, HUD Housing Choice Vouchers, Emergency Housing Vouchers, and Community Development Block Grant Disaster Recovery.

Because every DHAP activation used a distinct interagency agreement and operating requirements, individual rules differed. This page describes the shared model and identifies major differences where material.

The Disaster Housing Continuum

Disaster housing is commonly understood as a continuum.

Emergency sheltering
Short-term life-sustaining shelter in congregate facilities, hotels, motels, or other emergency settings.
Interim or temporary housing
Housing used while a household repairs, rebuilds, relocates, secures employment, resolves insurance, or pursues a permanent solution.
Permanent housing
Housing sustainable without continuing disaster-specific assistance.

FEMA is experienced in emergency response and short-term disaster assistance. HUD administers long-term rental subsidies, public housing, fair-housing law, housing quality standards, homelessness programmes, and local housing-agency relationships.

DHAP attempted to bridge the institutional gap between these two federal missions.

Hurricane Katrina and the Housing Crisis

Hurricane Katrina made landfall on August 29, 2005, followed by catastrophic flooding, infrastructure failure, and displacement across the Gulf Coast.

Hurricane Rita struck less than one month later, further damaging Louisiana and Texas.

Hundreds of thousands of households left their homes. Survivors moved into shelters, hotels, apartments, travel trailers, manufactured housing, relatives’ homes, and communities throughout the United States.

The scale and duration of displacement exceeded existing federal housing systems. FEMA’s short-term rent assistance and temporary units did not provide an adequate permanent transition for every household.

The crisis exposed fragmented responsibility among FEMA, HUD, states, housing agencies, voluntary organisations, and local governments.

Katrina Disaster Housing Assistance Program

HUD established the Katrina Disaster Housing Assistance Program in 2005 under a FEMA mission assignment.

KDHAP principally assisted households that had received HUD public housing, voucher, project-based rental assistance, or specified homeless and special-needs housing assistance before displacement.

Public housing agencies administered rent assistance for up to eighteen months and helped displaced families lease units in destination communities.

KDHAP was not identical to the later DHAP-Katrina programme. It was an early emergency response focused on restoring assistance to households already connected to HUD programmes.

Disaster Voucher Program

Congress and HUD also used the Disaster Voucher Program for HUD-assisted and selected special-needs families displaced by Hurricanes Katrina and Rita.

DVP relied more directly on Housing Choice Voucher administration and allowed displaced households to lease housing through participating agencies.

Some DVP households later received conversion vouchers or returned to ordinary HUD assistance.

DHAP-Katrina addressed a broader FEMA rental-assistance population that had not necessarily received HUD assistance before the disaster.

Creation of DHAP-Katrina

FEMA and HUD entered an interagency agreement in 2007 to transfer approximately 45,000 eligible Katrina and Rita households from FEMA rental assistance into a HUD-administered system.

FEMA acted under Stafford Act authority and provided grant funding. HUD acted as the servicing agency and used its public housing infrastructure.

DHAP-Katrina began transitioning households in late 2007, more than two years after Hurricane Katrina.

The delayed creation reflected the absence of a standing federal programme prepared to move mass disaster survivors from emergency assistance into medium-term rental housing.

Legal and Administrative Authority

FEMA financed DHAP under Section 408 of the Stafford Act, which authorises federal assistance to individuals and households after a major disaster.

DHAP was not separately enacted as a permanent programme in the United States Housing Act or the Stafford Act.

Each activation depended on:

  • a presidential major disaster declaration;
  • FEMA’s decision to use the DHAP model;
  • a FEMA–HUD interagency agreement;
  • HUD operating requirements;
  • public housing agency grant agreements;
  • FEMA referral of eligible households; and
  • available Disaster Relief Fund resources.

This temporary administrative foundation gave the agencies flexibility but prevented DHAP from becoming automatically available after later disasters.

FEMA–HUD Division of Responsibility

FEMA
Determined initial disaster eligibility, selected households for referral, financed subsidies and fees, set disaster-authority conditions, and retained programme oversight responsibility.
HUD
Developed housing operating procedures, recruited and supported public housing agencies, managed housing information and payment systems, and coordinated federal housing administration.
Public housing agencies
Worked directly with households and landlords, inspected units, established contracts, calculated payments, issued subsidies, maintained records, and implemented case-management or transition functions.
State and local partners
Supported recovery services, case management, housing identification, social services, and transition planning.

DHAP’s design recognised that no single agency possessed all the necessary legal authority, housing expertise, local relationships, and disaster funding.

Role of Public Housing Agencies

Public housing agencies are state or local entities administering public housing, Housing Choice Vouchers, and related federal housing programmes.

Under DHAP, participating agencies could serve referred households living in their jurisdiction, even when those families had been displaced from another state.

Agency duties included:

  • contacting referred families;
  • conducting programme briefings;
  • helping locate units;
  • determining household composition and unit size;
  • reviewing rent reasonableness;
  • inspecting housing;
  • executing landlord subsidy contracts;
  • making monthly payments;
  • recording programme data;
  • monitoring family obligations;
  • coordinating case management; and
  • terminating or closing assistance when required.

Household Eligibility

FEMA—not the public housing agency—made initial DHAP eligibility decisions.

Eligible households generally had to:

  • be displaced by the designated hurricane;
  • have received or qualified for specified FEMA temporary housing assistance;
  • remain unable to return to the pre-disaster primary residence;
  • need continued rental assistance;
  • be referred by FEMA to HUD; and
  • comply with household and programme obligations.

A person could not ordinarily apply directly to a housing agency and demand DHAP admission when FEMA had not referred the household.

Eligibility criteria changed among DHAP-Katrina, DHAP-Ike, and DHAP-Sandy.

Income and Disaster Eligibility

DHAP was created for disaster displacement rather than as an ordinary poverty programme.

Unlike regular Housing Choice Vouchers, initial admission did not always require the household to fall below HUD’s normal very-low-income limits.

Income information remained important for:

  • calculating household rent contribution;
  • determining transition affordability;
  • case-management planning;
  • identifying eligibility for permanent HUD programmes;
  • verifying continued need; and
  • detecting duplicate or inaccurate assistance.

A household that did not qualify for a permanent HUD voucher could still receive temporary DHAP assistance when FEMA referred it.

Housing Search and Placement

Households generally selected rental housing in the private market rather than being assigned to a federal housing project.

Public housing agencies helped identify landlords and available units, explain programme requirements, and arrange inspection and subsidy contracts.

DHAP-Sandy authorised placement fees and, when necessary, broker assistance because the New York metropolitan rental market was expensive and constrained.

Housing search remained difficult where disasters destroyed rental stock, increased rents, displaced large populations, or produced landlord reluctance.

Housing Quality and Inspection

Participating units had to meet programme inspection standards designed to ensure decent, safe, and sanitary housing.

Public housing agencies examined structural safety, utilities, sanitation, lead hazards, access, space, appliances, security, and other required conditions.

Landlords had to correct identified deficiencies before or during participation according to programme rules.

Inspection protected households but could delay placement when housing supply was scarce or agencies lacked sufficient inspectors.

Reasonable accommodation and accessible housing were required for households with disabilities.

Rent Reasonableness and Subsidy Standards

Public housing agencies reviewed requested rents against comparable unassisted units and programme limits.

Subsidy standards determined the appropriate number of bedrooms based principally on the disaster-displaced household composition recognised by FEMA.

Fair Market Rents and programme exceptions influenced the maximum subsidy.

DHAP-Sandy allowed additional flexibility in a high-cost market, including units above ordinary Fair Market Rent percentages under specified conditions.

Families could pay additional rent only within programme affordability restrictions.

Disaster Rent Subsidy Contract

The Disaster Rent Subsidy Contract was the agreement between a public housing agency and a participating landlord.

It identified the assisted unit, approved rent, subsidy amount, contract period, inspection obligations, landlord duties, and termination conditions.

The household entered a separate lease with the landlord and agreed to programme obligations.

The structure resembled Housing Choice Voucher administration but did not transform DHAP participants into permanent voucher holders.

Direct Payment to Landlords

Under DHAP, the public housing agency paid the federal rent subsidy directly to the landlord.

This differed from ordinary FEMA Rental Assistance, which generally provides money to a household that then leases housing and pays the landlord.

Direct landlord payment created a reliable payment stream, allowed agency monitoring, and used established housing-assistance controls.

It also increased administrative expense because agencies had to contract, inspect, calculate rent, process payments, and manage landlord relationships.

Security and Utility Deposits

Selected DHAP versions provided security-deposit and utility-deposit assistance to help households enter new leases.

Deposits are a major barrier for disaster survivors who have lost property, income, records, and savings.

Programme rules governed maximum amounts, payment recipients, return of deposits, damage deductions, and recordkeeping.

DHAP-Sandy expressly included rental subsidies, security-deposit assistance, and utility-deposit assistance for eligible New York households.

Case Management

DHAP was designed as more than a rent-payment programme.

Under DHAP-Katrina and DHAP-Ike, public housing agencies received monthly fees to provide case-management services.

Case management included:

  • a household needs assessment;
  • an individual or family development plan;
  • employment and income planning;
  • benefit and service referrals;
  • education and childcare coordination;
  • transportation support;
  • health and disability referrals;
  • credit or financial counselling;
  • landlord and housing problem resolution; and
  • transition to permanent housing.

Participation in case management was generally a condition of continuing assistance.

Case Management Under DHAP-Sandy

DHAP-Sandy required continuing disaster case management but did not assign that responsibility principally to public housing agencies.

State and local disaster case-management entities provided needs assessment and recovery planning through a separate FEMA-supported programme.

Housing agencies focused on rent subsidy, placement, inspection, contracts, payment, and programme compliance.

This division recognised that housing administration and social-service case management require different staff and institutional expertise.

Incremental Rent Transition

DHAP was temporary. Programme designers gradually increased household rent responsibility to reduce a sudden financial cliff at termination.

Under DHAP-Katrina and DHAP-Ike, household rent contributions generally began at $50 per month and increased by another $50 in later months.

DHAP-Sandy used a different formula tied to pre-disaster housing expense or post-disaster adjusted income, with contribution percentages increasing quarterly.

The phase-in was intended to prepare households for self-sufficiency or transition to another housing programme.

Critics argued that increasing contributions could burden households whose jobs, communities, homes, and wealth had not recovered.

Household Obligations

Participating families generally had to:

  • provide accurate household and income information;
  • permit inspections;
  • comply with the lease;
  • pay the required rent contribution;
  • avoid duplicate housing subsidy;
  • report moves and household changes;
  • participate in required case management;
  • use the assisted unit as the family’s residence;
  • avoid serious programme fraud or abuse; and
  • cooperate with transition planning.

Failure to meet obligations could lead to termination, subject to notice and review procedures.

Landlord Obligations

Participating landlords agreed to:

  • maintain the unit in required condition;
  • comply with the lease and subsidy contract;
  • accept the approved subsidy and family contribution;
  • avoid unauthorised side payments;
  • permit inspection;
  • report relevant tenancy changes;
  • comply with fair-housing and nondiscrimination law;
  • avoid fraud and duplicate claims; and
  • follow lawful eviction and termination procedures.

The programme could suspend or terminate subsidy payments when the owner failed to comply.

Interstate Displacement and Portability

Hurricanes Katrina and Rita dispersed households across the nation.

DHAP used public housing agencies in destination communities, allowing eligible families to receive assistance outside the original disaster area.

HUD described DHAP-Ike assistance as portable because participating families lived in multiple states.

National administration required data sharing, transfers, interagency communication, consistent household records, and coordination when families moved.

Portability offered choice and safety but could also make return to the home community more difficult as jobs, schools, and family ties shifted.

DHAP-Katrina Phases

DHAP-Katrina operated through several administrative phases.

Early activities included transferring FEMA households, briefing families and landlords, placing households under subsidy contracts, and initiating case management.

Later phases increased family rent contributions and concentrated on transition from disaster-specific assistance.

Congress appropriated funds for eligible households whose DHAP assistance ended but who remained unable to afford housing, allowing selected families to receive continuing HUD voucher assistance.

This transition demonstrated that some disaster displacement had become long-term poverty and housing instability rather than a temporary interruption.

DHAP-Ike

Hurricanes Gustav and Ike struck the Gulf Coast in 2008.

FEMA and HUD established DHAP-Ike rapidly, and assistance began in November 2008.

The programme served homeowners and renters who had received FEMA temporary housing assistance and remained displaced.

HUD and FEMA reported that approximately 26,300 families received DHAP-Ike assistance during the programme.

The programme was extended several times. Approximately 9,200 families remained when a 2010 extension lengthened assistance to October 31, 2010, and additional transition support continued for selected households.

DHAP-Sandy

Hurricane Sandy struck the northeastern United States in October 2012 and caused severe housing damage in New York and New Jersey.

FEMA and HUD executed the DHAP-Sandy interagency agreement on March 25, 2013.

DHAP-Sandy served selected New York State families referred by FEMA from Rental Assistance, hotels, shelters, or other emergency arrangements.

Public housing agencies provided monthly rent subsidies, deposits, placement, inspections, landlord payments, and programme administration for up to twelve months under initial contracts.

The programme formally terminated on December 31, 2014, with later closeout and transition activity.

Programme Duration and Extensions

DHAP duration varied substantially.

  • DHAP-Katrina began nearly two years after the 2005 storms and continued for more than two additional years;
  • DHAP-Ike began within approximately two months of the 2008 storms and continued, through extensions and transition measures, for more than three years; and
  • DHAP-Sandy began approximately five months after the 2012 storm and ended just over two years after the disaster.

Extensions responded to continuing housing need but also made programme closure uncertain for agencies, landlords, and families.

Transition to Permanent Housing

DHAP’s stated objective was not indefinite federal disaster subsidy. Households were expected to move toward:

  • return to a repaired or rebuilt home;
  • an unsubsidised private rental;
  • regular Housing Choice Voucher assistance;
  • public or project-based housing;
  • homeless or supportive-housing programmes;
  • family reunification or another stable arrangement; or
  • other state, local, charitable, or federal housing assistance.

Permanent programme capacity was insufficient for every household needing help after DHAP ended.

A successful disaster-housing programme therefore depends on the ordinary affordable-housing system available after the temporary programme expires.

Conversion and Transition Vouchers

Congress appropriated funds for continuing tenant-based rental assistance to selected DHAP-Katrina and DHAP-Ike households.

Housing agencies converted eligible families into special Housing Choice Voucher assistance when the disaster programme ended.

These vouchers were generally limited to the original assisted family and could not be reissued to another household when the family left the programme.

The conversion prevented homelessness and severe rent burden for selected households but did not create an equal transition for every former DHAP participant.

Programme Scale and Cost

Complete comparable cost and household data are limited.

GAO found:

  • the five largest DHAP-Katrina public housing agencies made approximately $240.6 million in subsidy payments, expressed in 2019 dollars, with an estimated average monthly subsidy of $808;
  • the five largest DHAP-Ike agencies made approximately $127.5 million in subsidy payments, expressed in 2019 dollars, with an estimated average monthly subsidy of $714; and
  • the DHAP-Sandy administering agency made approximately $5 million in subsidy payments, with an average monthly subsidy of approximately $1,860 in 2019 dollars.

These figures do not represent complete total programme cost. Administrative, placement, broker, case-management, federal personnel, information-system, and oversight costs were not consistently captured for comparison.

Public Housing Agency Fees

FEMA financed public housing agency duties through programme-specific fees.

Fee categories included:

  • case-management fees;
  • pre-transition activity fees;
  • one-time placement fees;
  • ongoing administrative fees;
  • broker fees under DHAP-Sandy; and
  • other approved programme expenses.

DHAP-Katrina case management was generally funded at $92 per participating household per month, while DHAP-Ike used $100 per household per month. DHAP-Sandy did not pay housing agencies to provide case management.

Fee design changed across activations, contributing to inconsistent cost measurement.

Relationship to FEMA Rental Assistance

FEMA’s Individuals and Households Program provides financial Rental Assistance directly to eligible households after a declared disaster.

The household generally finds a unit, receives FEMA funds, pays the landlord, and requests continued assistance when needed.

DHAP differed by:

  • using HUD and public housing agencies;
  • paying landlords directly;
  • conducting housing inspections;
  • reviewing rent reasonableness;
  • providing housing search support;
  • including structured case management or case-management coordination;
  • phasing in family rent contributions; and
  • operating under disaster-specific interagency agreements.

Relationship to IHP Financial and Time Limits

DHAP was authorised under Stafford Act Section 408 but was not always administered under the ordinary IHP financial-assistance maximum in the same way as direct household grants.

Congressional research found that DHAP could provide more assistance over a longer period than ordinary capped financial Rental Assistance.

Several activations continued beyond the standard eighteen-month Stafford Act housing period through programme timing, extensions, waivers, or separate transition appropriations.

This flexibility made DHAP useful for prolonged displacement but complicated cost control and comparisons with FEMA programmes.

Relationship to Direct Lease

Under Direct Lease, FEMA contracts directly with property owners for housing and places eligible survivors into units.

Under DHAP, the household generally identified a unit, while a public housing agency entered the subsidy contract and paid the owner.

Direct Lease can be activated without building a HUD–PHA grant network, which FEMA cited as an administrative advantage after the 2017 hurricanes.

DHAP offered local housing expertise, tenant-based choice, inspections, and transition support that ordinary Direct Lease did not always provide.

Relationship to Transitional Sheltering Assistance

Transitional Sheltering Assistance pays participating hotels or motels directly for eligible survivors leaving emergency shelters.

TSA is sheltering rather than a conventional lease-based rental subsidy.

It can be activated rapidly but does not provide the stability, cooking facilities, tenancy rights, or ordinary community setting of an apartment.

DHAP was intended for the medium-term phase when a hotel room was inadequate but the household still lacked permanent housing.

Relationship to the Housing Choice Voucher Program

DHAP borrowed many Housing Choice Voucher administrative tools:

  • local housing agencies;
  • tenant-selected private rental units;
  • landlord subsidy contracts;
  • rent reasonableness;
  • unit-size standards;
  • housing inspections;
  • direct landlord payments; and
  • family reporting obligations.

DHAP remained legally and financially distinct.

A DHAP household did not automatically gain a permanent voucher, and normal voucher income limits, waiting lists, preferences, portability rules, subsidy calculation, and reissuance policies did not apply identically.

Relationship to CDBG Disaster Recovery

Community Development Block Grant Disaster Recovery funds are specially appropriated by Congress to address long-term unmet recovery needs.

States and local governments can use CDBG-DR for housing repair, reconstruction, buyouts, infrastructure, economic recovery, and selected rental programmes.

DHAP provided household-level temporary rent assistance through FEMA and HUD rather than flexible long-term community-development grants.

The two programmes could operate sequentially or together but had separate eligibility, appropriation, planning, and duplication-of-benefits requirements.

Fair Housing and Nondiscrimination

Public housing agencies and participating landlords had to administer DHAP consistently with federal civil-rights law.

Applicable protections included:

  • Fair Housing Act;
  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
  • section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973;
  • Titles II and III of the Americans with Disabilities Act;
  • Age Discrimination Act;
  • Stafford Act nondiscrimination requirements;
  • language-access requirements; and
  • state and local housing law.

HUD investigated discrimination complaints involving housing advertisements, landlord refusals, race, disability, family status, and other protected characteristics following Gulf Coast displacement.

Disability Access

Displaced households with disabilities could require accessible units, additional bedrooms for equipment or attendants, assistance animals, communication aids, home modifications, proximity to medical providers, and reasonable accommodation.

Public housing agencies were responsible for accessible programme communication and reasonable modification of procedures where required.

A housing shortage could make accessible placement particularly difficult.

Disaster case management was important for coordinating housing with medical equipment, home health, transportation, benefits, and community services.

Language and Communication Access

Public housing agencies served survivors dispersed across unfamiliar jurisdictions and administrative systems.

Agencies had to communicate eligibility, landlord rules, inspections, rent increases, case management, appeals, and termination in understandable formats.

Language assistance was required for people with limited English proficiency, while accessible communication was necessary for people with hearing, vision, cognitive, or other disabilities.

Failure to understand an incremental rent increase or reporting duty could place a household at risk of termination.

Significance for African American Communities

Hurricane Katrina disproportionately displaced Black residents of New Orleans and surrounding Gulf Coast communities.

Segregated housing markets, redlining, poverty, weak flood protection, rental concentration, public housing redevelopment, and limited household wealth shaped exposure and recovery.

DHAP allowed many displaced families to obtain rental housing, but assistance often occurred far from their original neighbourhoods.

Displacement disrupted voting, schools, employment, churches, kinship, healthcare, culture, and political representation.

Rent subsidies did not guarantee a right to return, replacement of demolished public housing, affordable housing in New Orleans, or restoration of community institutions.

The programme illustrates the difference between housing an individual household and rebuilding a displaced Black community.

Right of Return and Permanent Relocation

Disaster housing policy confronted two legitimate but sometimes conflicting goals: enabling return and supporting safe permanent relocation.

Some households wanted to return to repaired homes, public housing, or neighbourhoods. Others established new employment, schools, healthcare, and family relationships in destination communities.

The pace of rebuilding, demolition of housing, insurance disputes, rent inflation, and public-service restoration constrained meaningful choice.

DHAP portability supported relocation but did not itself finance reconstruction of the community of origin.

Rental-Market Constraints

DHAP depended on private rental units.

The programme could not function effectively when:

  • rental stock had been destroyed;
  • vacancy was extremely low;
  • rents exceeded programme limits;
  • landlords refused participation;
  • units failed inspection;
  • accessible units were unavailable;
  • families lacked transportation to search;
  • employment was far from available housing; or
  • the household needed specialised supportive housing.

A rental subsidy increases purchasing power but does not create a unit.

Case-Management Limitations

Case management attempted to connect rent assistance with long-term recovery.

Problems included inconsistent service quality, varying caseloads, fragmented federal programmes, uncertain definitions of self-sufficiency, duplicated services, incomplete records, and changing programme deadlines.

GAO found overlapping case-management programmes after Katrina and Rita, administered by FEMA, HUD, HHS, states, and nonprofit organisations at different times.

Referral cannot solve a need when affordable housing, employment, disability services, childcare, transportation, or healthcare is unavailable.

Oversight and Programme Integrity

HUD, FEMA, public housing agencies, inspectors general, and auditors reviewed programme expenditure and administration.

Risks included:

  • unsupported subsidy payments;
  • duplicate federal housing assistance;
  • inaccurate household composition or income;
  • payments for ineligible units;
  • poor procurement;
  • unsupported case-management salaries;
  • insufficient inspection records;
  • owner fraud;
  • unauthorised tenant charges;
  • weak information systems; and
  • incomplete closeout.

Some housing agencies were required to document costs or repay unsupported amounts.

Notices, Termination, and Appeals

Households could face adverse decisions involving:

  • initial FEMA referral status;
  • household composition;
  • rent subsidy calculation;
  • unit eligibility;
  • family obligations;
  • nonpayment of the family share;
  • failure to participate in case management;
  • duplicate assistance;
  • programme fraud; or
  • continued eligibility.

Responsibility for review depended on the issue. FEMA controlled initial disaster eligibility, while public housing agencies or HUD handled many programme-administration determinations.

Written notice and an opportunity to challenge adverse decisions were essential because termination could lead rapidly to homelessness.

Government Accountability Office Findings

GAO examined DHAP repeatedly.

Reviews found that the model used experienced housing agencies and combined rental assistance with case management, but federal agencies lacked complete and comparable cost information.

In 2020, GAO concluded that FEMA did not activate DHAP after the 2017 and 2018 hurricanes and did not consider it a standard active programme.

FEMA stated that its own Rental Assistance and direct housing programmes could respond more quickly and efficiently.

GAO found that available data were insufficient to establish reliable comparisons of complete programme cost and cost-effectiveness.

Nonactivation After the 2017 Hurricanes

Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and Maria created extensive housing displacement in 2017.

Houston and Puerto Rico requested or supported use of DHAP. FEMA and HUD developed draft administrative material in at least one case.

FEMA declined to activate the programme and relied on IHP Rental Assistance, Direct Lease, Multifamily Lease and Repair, transportable housing units, and other programmes.

FEMA cited speed, administrative burden, direct control, existing alternatives, and cost-effectiveness concerns.

Housing advocates argued that HUD’s housing-agency network and case-management model were better suited to prolonged low-income displacement.

National Disaster Housing Strategy

Congress required development of a national disaster-housing strategy after Hurricane Katrina.

FEMA’s 2009 strategy described DHAP as a federal rental-assistance pilot and identified HUD’s public housing agency network as an important national housing resource.

The strategy recommended planning for a permanent DHAP-like capability that could be activated more predictably.

Congress did not establish DHAP as a fully permanent standard programme, and later activation remained dependent on FEMA–HUD agreement.

Current Status Through August 2026

DHAP was last activated for Hurricane Sandy in 2013.

GAO reported in 2020 that FEMA did not consider DHAP a standard post-disaster housing programme or an active programme.

Federal records continue to reference DHAP for accounting, archived grants, conversion vouchers, historical programme codes, and closeout activity.

No official FEMA or HUD announcement activating a new DHAP iteration was identified through August 2, 2026.

Current disaster survivors should not wait for DHAP. They must use assistance authorised for the current declaration, including FEMA IHP, state programmes, SBA loans, HUD-assisted housing portability, CDBG-DR programmes, legal aid, and voluntary-agency support.

Debate Over a Permanent DHAP

Housing advocates, emergency-management experts, and members of Congress have proposed establishing a permanent DHAP-like programme.

Arguments for permanence include:

  • pre-negotiated FEMA–HUD roles;
  • rapid activation;
  • use of housing agencies with rental expertise;
  • direct landlord payment;
  • housing inspections;
  • case management;
  • better transition to permanent HUD programmes;
  • support for low-income renters; and
  • reduced dependence on hotels and temporary units.

Concerns include administrative expense, local agency capacity, slow interagency start-up, dependence on rental-market supply, programme duplication, and uncertain cost-effectiveness.

Major Achievements

  • Created an interagency model combining disaster authority with housing expertise.
  • Provided medium-term rental assistance to households facing prolonged displacement.
  • Used public housing agencies across destination communities.
  • Paid subsidies directly to landlords.
  • Helped households locate and enter private rental housing.
  • Required housing inspections and rent-reasonableness review.
  • Included deposits in selected programme versions.
  • Combined rent support with case management or coordinated case management.
  • Supported interstate portability after mass displacement.
  • Created gradual rent transitions rather than immediate termination of full assistance.
  • Provided a pathway to permanent vouchers for selected households.
  • Demonstrated a potential federal model for medium-term disaster housing.
  • Served survivors of five major hurricanes through three formal activations.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • DHAP was not a permanent statutory entitlement.
  • Each activation required a new interagency agreement.
  • DHAP-Katrina began nearly two years after the disaster.
  • Initial eligibility depended on FEMA referral rather than open application.
  • Rules differed among activations.
  • Local housing-agency capacity varied.
  • Rental subsidies could not create units in damaged or high-cost markets.
  • Incremental rent increases burdened households with incomplete recovery.
  • Case-management quality and coordination varied.
  • The programme did not guarantee permanent housing assistance.
  • Households could face homelessness when temporary support ended.
  • Federal agencies did not maintain complete comparable cost data.
  • Administrative and case-management fees added cost.
  • Dispersed families could lose community ties and the practical ability to return.
  • Housing placement did not rebuild destroyed neighbourhoods or public housing.
  • FEMA declined to reactivate DHAP after major 2017 disasters.

Policy Lessons

DHAP demonstrates that emergency shelter, temporary rent assistance, and permanent affordable housing are different functions.

Effective disaster-housing policy requires:

  • pre-disaster interagency agreements;
  • clear activation criteria;
  • rapid household referral;
  • complete cost and outcome data;
  • adequate rental supply;
  • landlord recruitment;
  • fair-housing enforcement;
  • accessible units;
  • case-management capacity;
  • protection against abrupt subsidy cliffs;
  • permanent housing pathways;
  • support for return or voluntary relocation; and
  • coordination with community rebuilding.

A temporary housing programme cannot succeed when the permanent affordable-housing system is already inadequate before disaster.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

DHAP
The joint FEMA–HUD Disaster Housing Assistance Program.
Temporary housing
Housing bridging emergency shelter and a permanent housing solution sustainable without disaster-specific aid.
Public housing agency
A state or local entity administering HUD public housing, vouchers, or related programmes.
Rental subsidy
A payment covering part or all of the approved rent for an eligible household.
Disaster Rent Subsidy Contract
The programme agreement between a public housing agency and participating landlord.
Incremental rent transition
Scheduled increases in the household’s share of rent as temporary assistance approached termination.
Case management
Assessment, planning, referral, and coordination intended to help a household achieve recovery and permanent housing.
Fair Market Rent
A HUD estimate of rental cost used in federal housing programme administration.
Housing Quality Standards
Physical standards historically used to determine whether assisted units were decent, safe, and sanitary.
Rent reasonableness
Comparison of a proposed assisted rent with rents for comparable unassisted housing.
Conversion voucher
Continuing Housing Choice Voucher assistance authorised for selected households leaving a temporary disaster programme.
Portability
The ability to receive tenant-based assistance outside the original jurisdiction, subject to programme rules.
FEMA Rental Assistance
IHP financial assistance generally paid to a household to rent temporary housing.
Direct Lease
A FEMA programme under which the federal government leases properties and places eligible survivors.
Permanent housing
Housing that can be sustained without continuing disaster-related subsidy.

Summary

The Disaster Housing Assistance Program was a joint FEMA–HUD pilot designed to provide medium-term rental housing after catastrophic displacement.

FEMA used Stafford Act authority and Disaster Relief Fund resources, selected eligible disaster households, and referred them to HUD. HUD used public housing agencies to place families, inspect units, review rents, contract with landlords, and make monthly subsidy payments.

The programme was activated three times: DHAP-Katrina for Hurricanes Katrina and Rita, DHAP-Ike for Hurricanes Gustav and Ike, and DHAP-Sandy for Hurricane Sandy.

DHAP-Katrina and DHAP-Ike included case management provided through housing agencies. DHAP-Sandy coordinated required case management separately. Programme versions phased in household rent responsibility and sought to move participants toward permanent housing.

DHAP was modelled partly on Housing Choice Vouchers but did not create permanent vouchers or an open-ended federal entitlement. Selected households later received conversion or transition vouchers through separate congressional funding.

The programme’s principal advantage was combining FEMA’s disaster authority with HUD’s housing expertise. Its principal weakness was that it had to be recreated through a new interagency agreement after each disaster.

GAO found that FEMA did not maintain complete comparable cost data and did not activate DHAP after the major hurricanes of 2017 and 2018. FEMA did not consider it a standard active programme.

As of August 2, 2026, DHAP had not been reactivated since Hurricane Sandy. Its continuing importance lies in the unresolved question of which federal institution should lead medium-term rental recovery for low-income disaster survivors.
