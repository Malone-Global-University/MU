Byrne Justice Assistance Grant Program

The Edward Byrne Memorial formula grant created in fiscal year 2005 to provide flexible federal support for law enforcement, prosecution, courts, public defense, prevention, corrections, treatment, victims, mental health, technology, and criminal-justice planning.

What Is the Byrne Justice Assistance Grant Program?

Byrne JAG is a formula-based federal grant program supporting a wide range of state and local criminal-justice activities.

Unlike a narrowly targeted competitive grant, JAG gives recipients substantial discretion to select projects within federally authorized program areas and applicable award conditions.

The program can support personnel, equipment, supplies, contracts, training, technical assistance, information systems, and other allowable criminal-justice or civil-proceeding costs.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s origins, namesake, statutory purposes, formula, state and local allocation systems, pass-through rules, application process, strategic planning, public review, allowable and prohibited uses, reporting, civil-rights conditions, federal penalties, controversies, and current 2026 status.

It distinguishes formula JAG awards from Byrne Discretionary Community Project Grants, Byrne State Crisis Intervention Program awards, COPS grants, and other Justice Department assistance.

Geographic Scope

The state formula covers the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, Guam, the United States Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Direct local allocations are calculated for eligible counties, municipalities, townships, tribal governments, and other units meeting statutory definitions and the annual $10,000 threshold.

Historical Context

Byrne JAG developed from federal crime-control grants created during the War on Crime and expanded during the War on Drugs.

By the early 2000s, states and localities received flexible justice funding through two major systems: the Byrne Formula Grant Program and the Local Law Enforcement Block Grants Program.

Congress combined the programs to simplify administration and create a single broad formula grant beginning in fiscal year 2005.

Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968

The Safe Streets Act established the principal federal framework for state and local criminal-justice assistance.

It created federal grant institutions and encouraged statewide planning, data collection, law-enforcement modernization, court improvement, corrections, and crime prevention.

Byrne JAG remains part of this statutory architecture.

Law Enforcement Assistance Administration

The Law Enforcement Assistance Administration administered large block and categorical grants from the late 1960s through the 1970s.

LEAA’s history established enduring debates over federal influence, state planning, police equipment, crime-control priorities, and measurement of grant outcomes.

Congress phased out LEAA in the early 1980s, but later grant programs revived many of its functions under different structures.

Edward Byrne Memorial Formula Grant Program

The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988 consolidated federal drug-control assistance into the Edward Byrne Memorial State and Local Law Enforcement Assistance Program.

The Byrne Formula Grant Program funded multijurisdictional drug task forces, prosecution, courts, corrections, treatment, prevention, and other justice activities.

It became closely associated with state drug-control strategies during the 1990s.

Local Law Enforcement Block Grants Program

The Local Law Enforcement Block Grants Program began in fiscal year 1996.

It provided direct formula funding to local governments for law-enforcement personnel, equipment, prosecution, courts, corrections, crime prevention, and related purposes.

LLEBG used a violent-crime formula and a local direct-award structure later incorporated into JAG.

Creation of JAG in Fiscal Year 2005

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2005 merged the Byrne Formula Grant and Local Law Enforcement Block Grants programs.

The combined Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant Program preserved flexible uses while establishing one population-and-violent-crime formula and separate state and local application tracks.

Officer Edward Byrne

Edward R. Byrne was a twenty-two-year-old New York City police officer assigned to protect the home of a witness cooperating against a drug dealer in Queens.

Four men murdered him while he sat in a marked patrol car during the early morning of February 26, 1988.

Congress named federal state and local justice assistance in his memory later that year.

Federal Administration

The Bureau of Justice Assistance administers JAG within the Office of Justice Programs.

BJA issues notices of funding opportunity, awards grants, manages special conditions, provides guidance, reviews modifications, monitors compliance, and supports training and technical assistance.

The Office of the Chief Financial Officer, Office for Civil Rights, Bureau of Justice Statistics, SMART Office, PREA Management Office, and other DOJ components perform related functions.

Bureau of Justice Statistics

BJS calculates annual formula allocations using population estimates and FBI Uniform Crime Reporting data.

BJS publishes technical reports explaining the methodology, data years, minimum allocations, state-local division, and local eligibility.

State Administering Agency

Each state designates one State Administering Agency to apply for and manage the state JAG award.

The SAA develops the statewide strategy, administers subawards, calculates required pass-through, monitors recipients, reports performance, and coordinates with criminal-justice stakeholders.

Direct Local Awards

BJA awards funds directly to units of local government whose calculated allocation reaches the statutory minimum.

Direct awards reduce dependence on state grant decisions but create separate federal application, reporting, financial-management, and public-review obligations.

Tribal Participation

Tribes can qualify as units of local government when identified in annual allocation tables.

Tribal eligibility can change from year to year because the formula depends on reported violent crime and the national appropriation.

Tribes also receive justice assistance through separate programs, including the Coordinated Tribal Assistance Solicitation.

Formula Overview

Congress directs the Attorney General to allocate JAG formula funds using population and Part 1 violent-crime data.

The formula first determines state-level amounts, applies a minimum allocation, divides each amount between state and local shares, and then calculates eligible local awards.

Population Component

Fifty percent of the amount remaining after minimum allocations is distributed according to each state’s share of the national population.

Population data are supplied by the United States Census Bureau.

Violent-Crime Component

Fifty percent is distributed according to each state’s share of the national three-year average of Part 1 violent crimes reported through the FBI Uniform Crime Reporting Program.

The formula uses reported crime, making data completeness and reporting systems important to allocation accuracy.

Part 1 Violent Crime

For current JAG calculation purposes, Part 1 violent crime includes murder and nonnegligent manslaughter, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, and statutorily defined human trafficking.

The formula does not use every offense, arrest, call for service, or conviction.

Three-Year Average

BJS uses the three most recent calendar years of available reported violent-crime data.

A multiyear average reduces the effect of one-year fluctuation but can delay the effect of recent crime changes.

Minimum State Allocation

Each state receives at least 0.25 percent of the formula amount.

After minimum amounts are assigned, remaining funds are distributed among states above the minimum according to population and violent crime.

The minimum protects small states and territories but reduces the amount available for purely proportional distribution.

Sixty-Forty State-Local Split

For each state allocation, 60 percent is assigned initially to the state portion and 40 percent to direct local-government allocations.

The District of Columbia and territories are treated differently because they do not have the same state-local governmental structure.

Local Allocation Formula

Each eligible local government’s share is based on its proportion of the state’s three-year average Part 1 violent crime.

Population does not independently determine the local formula after the state amount has been calculated.

The $10,000 Direct-Award Threshold

A local government with a calculated allocation of at least $10,000 can apply directly to BJA.

Amounts calculated below $10,000 are added to the state award and must be passed through for local use or to the state police according to current rules.

Joint and Disparate Jurisdictions

Some adjoining or overlapping local jurisdictions receive a joint allocation and must submit one joint application.

A memorandum of understanding identifies the fiscal agent, distribution, project responsibilities, and participating governments.

Joint arrangements are intended to prevent fragmented awards and account for shared crime and service patterns.

Variable Pass-Through Requirement

A state cannot retain the entire 60-percent state share.

The variable pass-through percentage reflects the local share of combined state and local criminal-justice expenditures.

BJA publishes each state’s required percentage and dollar amount annually.

Less-Than-$10,000 Pass-Through

The state award includes funds associated with local jurisdictions whose formula amounts were below the direct-award threshold.

States must pass through 100 percent of those funds to otherwise ineligible local jurisdictions and/or the state police under applicable guidance.

Purpose of Pass-Through Rules

Pass-through prevents the federal formula from concentrating funds entirely at the state level when local governments bear substantial criminal-justice costs.

It also ensures that small jurisdictions benefit even when their individual calculated awards are too small for direct federal administration.

Fiscal Year 2026 Byrne-JAG Appropriation Structure

DOJ fiscal year 2027 budget materials report $964 million in fiscal year 2026 Byrne-JAG funding and carveouts.

Approximately $537.9 million consisted of Byrne Discretionary Community Project Grants—congressionally designated projects.

The remaining amount included formula JAG and multiple programmatic carveouts. The $964 million figure should not be described as the formula allocation available to states and local governments.

Byrne Discretionary Community Project Grants

Byrne Discretionary Community Project Grants are congressionally designated projects funded within or alongside the Byrne-JAG appropriations structure.

They are noncompetitive invited awards for named recipients and purposes.

They do not use the JAG population-and-violent-crime formula and should not be confused with state or local formula awards.

Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Proposal

The administration proposed reducing the Byrne-JAG line and carveouts from $964 million to $426 million by eliminating community-project grants and several other carveouts.

As of August 2, 2026, the proposal had not been enacted and did not alter fiscal year 2026 awards or the underlying JAG statute.

Authorized Program Areas

Current federal law permits JAG funding for personnel, equipment, supplies, contracts, training, technical assistance, and information systems within the authorized program areas.

A project must also comply with appropriations restrictions, DOJ financial rules, civil-rights requirements, environmental review, award conditions, and state or local law.

Law-Enforcement Programs

Law-enforcement uses can include personnel, patrol, investigations, crime analysis, communications, officer safety, training, evidence systems, community policing, forensic support, and multijurisdictional operations.

The breadth of this category makes law enforcement historically one of the largest JAG spending areas.

Prosecution and Court Programs

JAG can support prosecutors, public defenders, courts, pretrial services, specialty courts, case management, technology, training, witness support, and backlog reduction.

BJA has expressly recognized indigent-defense representation as an allowable JAG use.

Prevention and Education Programs

Prevention uses can include youth programs, violence prevention, public education, school and community partnerships, crime-prevention planning, restorative practices, and targeted interventions.

Prevention projects should identify measurable risk, protective factors, and outcomes rather than relying only on attendance counts.

Corrections and Community Corrections

JAG can support jails, prisons, probation, parole, community supervision, reentry, alternatives to incarceration, electronic systems, training, and correctional safety.

Construction is generally restricted except for penal or correctional institutions and remains subject to waiver, environmental, and statutory requirements.

Drug Treatment and Enforcement

JAG can support drug task forces, investigations, prosecution, treatment, recovery support, diversion, overdose response, intelligence, drug courts, and community supervision.

The combined category reflects the program’s roots in federal drug-control policy while permitting treatment and public-health-related strategies.

Planning, Evaluation, and Technology

Allowable uses include statewide planning, research, evaluation, records systems, crime analysis, information sharing, cybersecurity, digital evidence, data quality, and technology implementation.

Technology purchases create continuing maintenance, privacy, training, interoperability, and replacement costs.

Crime Victim and Witness Programs

JAG can support victim and witness services other than direct compensation.

Projects can include advocacy, notification, witness protection support, language access, trauma-informed services, court accompaniment, and system coordination.

Victim compensation is funded principally through separate Victims of Crime Act systems.

Mental-Health and Related Programs

JAG can support behavioural-health programs, crisis-intervention teams, co-responder models, mental-health courts, correctional treatment, diversion, training, and service coordination.

Effective projects require sustainable partnerships with health-care and community providers.

State Crisis-Intervention Court Proceedings

Current law authorizes implementation of state crisis-intervention court proceedings and related programs.

Examples include mental-health courts, drug courts, veterans treatment courts, and extreme-risk-protection-order programs that satisfy statutory constitutional safeguards.

Unmanned-Aircraft Systems

JAG can support purchase and operation of unmanned-aircraft systems for public-safety purposes.

Recipients must obtain prior express BJA approval and comply with aviation, privacy, cybersecurity, procurement, data-retention, and state-law requirements.

Counter-Unmanned-Aircraft Systems

Current statute permits funding for specified counter-UAS technologies when recipients possess lawful federal authority and use approved technology.

Most state and local agencies do not possess independent authority to disrupt aircraft or radio signals merely because they receive JAG funding.

Personnel Costs

JAG can fund salaries, overtime, benefits, contracts, and temporary personnel when connected to an authorized project.

Recipients must maintain time-and-effort documentation and avoid supplanting where award conditions or local representations require additional capacity.

Equipment and Supplies

Allowable purchases can include communications, computers, forensic equipment, protective equipment, evidence storage, training equipment, police cruisers, correctional equipment, and other approved items.

Property records, inventory, maintenance, disposition, procurement, and environmental requirements can apply.

Contractual Support

Recipients can contract for services, technology, evaluation, training, treatment, legal representation, consulting, and other approved activities.

Federal procurement standards require competition, cost reasonableness, conflict controls, contract oversight, and documentation.

Training and Technical Assistance

JAG can support academy instruction, in-service training, leadership development, de-escalation, crisis response, prosecution, courts, corrections, technology, data, and other justice topics.

Training should be connected to policy, supervision, evaluation, and operational change.

Prohibited Expenditures

Federal statute and current BJA guidance divide restrictions into strictly prohibited activity and expenditures requiring extraordinary-and-exigent-circumstances certification.

A recipient cannot assume that a purchase is allowable merely because it serves a police or public-safety agency.

Strictly Prohibited Use

JAG funds cannot directly or indirectly provide security enhancements or equipment to a nongovernmental entity that is not engaged in criminal justice or public safety.

BJA does not permit a waiver for this category.

Expenditures Requiring Federal Certification

Unless DOJ first certifies extraordinary and exigent circumstances essential to public safety and good order, JAG funds cannot purchase:

  • vehicles other than police cruisers;
  • vessels other than police boats;
  • aircraft other than police helicopters;
  • luxury items;
  • real estate;
  • construction other than penal or correctional institutions; or
  • similar matters.

Vehicles

Police cruisers used in ordinary routine patrol can be purchased without the extraordinary-circumstances certification when otherwise allowable.

Other trucks, vans, buses, recreational vehicles, motorcycles, sport-utility vehicles, all-terrain vehicles, and similar registered vehicles generally require prior certification unless they qualify as ordinary police cruisers under BJA guidance.

Aircraft and Boats

Police helicopters and police boats used in ordinary law-enforcement operations are excluded from the statutory waiver list when otherwise allowable.

Other aircraft and vessels require DOJ certification.

Unmanned-aircraft systems have separate statutory authority and prior-approval requirements.

Real Estate and Construction

Real-estate purchases ordinarily require extraordinary-and-exigent-circumstances certification.

Construction is similarly restricted except for penal or correctional institutions.

Construction and renovation can also trigger National Environmental Policy Act review, historic-preservation review, and long-term real-property reporting.

Administrative Costs

A recipient can use up to 10 percent of the award for administration.

Administrative costs can include grant staff, accounting, monitoring, reporting, procurement, data systems, and subrecipient oversight.

The cap does not make every administrative charge allowable; each cost must remain necessary, reasonable, allocable, and documented.

No Match Requirement

Formula JAG does not ordinarily require a state or local cash or in-kind match.

Recipients remain responsible for costs exceeding the award and for sustaining activities after federal funds end.

Supplanting and Additionality

The JAG statute is more flexible than the COPS Hiring Program’s explicit nonsupplanting system.

Recipients nevertheless must use funds for approved purposes, comply with representations in the application, and avoid using federal money to evade legal obligations or duplicate another federal reimbursement.

Statewide Strategic Plan

States must submit a comprehensive statewide strategic plan with the JAG application.

In years when the plan is not fully updated, the state submits an annual report describing changes and implementation.

The plan should address the entire criminal-justice system rather than one agency or funding category.

Stakeholder Planning

BJA encourages participation by law enforcement, prosecutors, courts, public defenders, corrections, community supervision, victim services, juvenile justice, treatment, reentry providers, local governments, tribes, researchers, and community organizations.

Meaningful planning requires disclosure of proposed allocations and a way for stakeholders to influence priorities.

Local Application

A directly eligible local government submits the federal application through Grants.gov and JustGrants.

The application identifies the project, budget, governing-body review, public-comment process, financial controls, civil-rights assurances, and required certifications.

Governing-Body Review

The application must be made available for review by the governing body of the applicant or by an organization designated by that governing body.

Review promotes legislative awareness of grant-funded policing, prosecution, courts, technology, and corrections projects.

Public Comment

Applicants must provide an opportunity for public comment on the proposed JAG application.

Public posting should occur early enough for comments to influence the project rather than functioning as notice after all decisions have been made.

Grants.gov and JustGrants

Applicants submit the initial federal form through Grants.gov and complete the full application through DOJ’s JustGrants system.

Active SAM.gov registration and a Unique Entity Identifier are required.

Award Period

Awards typically begin October 1 of the fiscal year identified in the solicitation.

Awards of $25,000 or more ordinarily have four-year project periods.

Awards below $25,000 ordinarily have two-year periods and can receive automatic extensions up to four years under current guidance.

Financial Management

Recipients must maintain internal controls, accounting records, source documentation, procurement files, subaward records, equipment inventories, and audit trails.

Federal funds must be drawn only for immediate allowable needs and cannot be treated as unrestricted local revenue.

Subawards

States and eligible local recipients can make subawards when authorized.

The primary recipient remains responsible for risk assessment, written agreements, monitoring, performance, financial review, civil rights, and corrective action.

Performance Reporting

Recipients report quarterly performance measures through the Performance Measurement Tool.

Awards of $25,000 or more generally submit semiannual performance reports in JustGrants; smaller awards generally report annually.

Quarterly Federal Financial Reports are also required.

Performance Measures

Measures vary by project area and can include personnel, activities, participants, cases, arrests, prosecutions, court operations, treatment, recidivism, victim services, technology deployment, training, and organizational change.

Output reporting does not by itself establish effectiveness, fairness, or causal impact.

Death in Custody Reporting Act

States must report information concerning deaths occurring during arrest, transport, detention, incarceration, and specified correctional custody.

Beginning with fiscal year 2023 awards, states submit a DCRA implementation plan and annual updates.

The Attorney General can reduce a noncompliant state’s JAG allocation by up to 10 percent.

SORNA Reduction

A state that has not substantially implemented the Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act is subject to a 10-percent reduction in the JAG award.

A state can receive the reduced amount for use solely to achieve SORNA compliance under applicable law and guidance.

PREA Reduction

A governor who does not certify full compliance with the Prison Rape Elimination Act standards can subject the state to loss of 5 percent of specified DOJ grant funds, including JAG.

The state can avoid loss by assuring that the amount will be used to achieve compliance.

Crime-Data Reporting

JAG formula calculations depend on crime data reported to the FBI.

Agencies reporting through either the National Incident-Based Reporting System or the Summary Reporting System can contribute eligible data under current guidance.

Missing or incomplete data can affect local eligibility and allocation.

NIBRS Transition

NIBRS became the national standard for law-enforcement crime reporting in 2021.

Because not all agencies transitioned on schedule, the FBI resumed accepting Summary Reporting System data in 2022 to improve coverage.

The two systems count offenses differently, creating technical issues for allocation comparability.

Civil-Rights Compliance

JAG recipients and subrecipients must comply with federal civil-rights laws, DOJ regulations, and award conditions.

These requirements apply to employment, services, enforcement, detention, court access, contracting, and grant administration.

Title VI and Language Access

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, colour, or national origin in federally assisted programs.

Meaningful access for people with limited English proficiency can require interpretation, translation, accessible notices, and language-qualified staff.

Disability Access

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act and the Americans with Disabilities Act require nondiscrimination, effective communication, and reasonable modifications.

Requirements apply to police interaction, courts, detention, treatment, victim services, technology, facilities, and grant activities.

Equal Employment Opportunity

Recipients can be required to submit or certify compliance with an Equal Employment Opportunity Plan.

Employment discrimination can lead to corrective action, civil-rights enforcement, or grant consequences.

Immigration-Related Award Conditions

Successive administrations have attached immigration-information and cooperation conditions to JAG awards.

Several conditions produced extensive federal litigation concerning statutory authority, federalism, separation of powers, and nationwide injunctions.

Current applicants must review the conditions attached to their specific award year rather than relying on older litigation summaries.

Environmental and Historic-Preservation Review

Construction, renovation, communications towers, firing ranges, equipment installation, drones, and other physical projects can require environmental review.

Recipients should not begin restricted activity before BJA completes required review or issues approval.

Procurement

Purchases and contracts must comply with federal procurement standards and applicable state or local law.

Requirements include competition, conflict controls, cost reasonableness, contract oversight, suspension-and-debarment checks, domestic preferences where applicable, and documentation.

Information Technology and Privacy

JAG frequently supports records systems, data integration, cameras, surveillance tools, analytics, and digital evidence.

Projects should address privacy, security, access control, retention, accuracy, bias, public records, vendor ownership, interoperability, and long-term cost.

Body-Worn Cameras

JAG can support body-worn cameras when allowable under the award and approved plan.

Successful implementation requires policy, storage, disclosure, redaction, supervision, training, maintenance, and evaluation—not merely camera purchase.

Multijurisdictional Task Forces

Byrne funding has historically supported drug, gang, violent-crime, fraud, trafficking, and intelligence task forces.

Task forces can pool expertise across boundaries but create accountability challenges involving command, use of force, forfeiture, data, prosecution, and community oversight.

Indigent Defense

BJA has confirmed that JAG may support constitutionally required indigent-defense representation.

Funding can address staffing, workload, training, investigators, experts, technology, pretrial representation, and system planning.

Temporary grant funding does not eliminate the government’s continuing constitutional obligation to provide counsel.

Protection of Election Workers

BJA has advised that JAG can support lawful efforts to investigate and prevent threats against election workers and officials.

Projects remain subject to constitutional protections, election law, threat-assessment standards, and neutral administration.

Hate-Crime Response

JAG can support hate-crime investigation, prosecution, reporting, victim services, training, outreach, and data improvement.

Assistance should distinguish protected speech from criminal conduct and address barriers to reporting.

Reentry and Recidivism Reduction

JAG can support reentry planning, supervision, identification documents, housing coordination, employment services, treatment, transportation, and data systems.

Projects can complement but should not duplicate Second Chance Act awards.

Justice Reinvestment

States have used Byrne-related funding to analyse corrections growth, supervision, sentencing, behavioural health, and reinvestment strategies.

Justice Reinvestment Initiative funds have often been provided as a carveout within broader Byrne-JAG appropriations rather than through the ordinary formula.

Program Integrity

Program integrity includes accurate allocation, transparent planning, lawful procurement, allowable costs, reliable performance data, pass-through compliance, civil-rights protection, and correction of underuse or misuse.

Integrity is broader than criminal fraud.

Monitoring

BJA and primary recipients use desk reviews, site visits, financial analysis, risk assessment, data validation, subrecipient monitoring, special conditions, and audit findings.

Monitoring should examine whether projects match the approved purpose and produce credible benefits.

Audit and Inspector-General Oversight

JAG awards can be reviewed through Single Audits, DOJ Office of the Inspector General audits, state audits, legislative audits, and program monitoring.

Common findings include unsupported costs, inadequate procurement, weak subrecipient monitoring, late reports, property-control failures, and unspent balances.

Fraud, Waste, and Misuse

Intentional misconduct can include falsified invoices, conflicts of interest, personal use of equipment, duplicate reimbursement, sham contracts, false performance reports, and diversion of subawards.

Remedies can include repayment, suspension, termination, debarment, civil action, and criminal prosecution.

Unspent and Delayed Funds

Long award periods provide flexibility but can permit money to remain unobligated while needs change.

Delays can result from procurement, vacancies, litigation, technology planning, subaward competitions, environmental review, or weak administration.

Recipients should modify or reprogram projects rather than preserve obsolete plans.

Performance-Measurement Challenges

JAG funds thousands of different projects, making one national outcome measure impractical.

Formula allocation is not based on project quality, and flexible spending can produce strong local fit but weak comparability.

Crime changes can rarely be attributed to one grant without careful evaluation.

Federalism

JAG combines federal money with broad state and local discretion.

Supporters view the formula as respect for local knowledge; critics argue that federal funding can subsidize policies without adequate national standards.

Conditions involving immigration, detention, firearms, and civil rights have repeatedly tested the boundary of federal grant authority.

Regional and Jurisdictional Equity

The formula combines population, reported violent crime, minimum allocations, and local thresholds.

Small states benefit from the minimum, large jurisdictions receive larger proportional shares, and small local governments often depend on state pass-through.

Formula fairness depends on data quality and does not directly measure poverty, fiscal capacity, court backlog, incarceration, or victimization not reported to police.

Race and Criminal-Justice Equity

Byrne funding developed within the War on Drugs and historically supported drug task forces and enforcement systems associated with racial disparities.

Modern JAG can also support public defense, diversion, treatment, violence intervention, reentry, data transparency, and civil-rights improvement.

Equity depends on state and local choices, stakeholder participation, enforcement patterns, and outcome measurement.

Law-Enforcement Spending Share

Law enforcement has historically received a substantial portion of JAG expenditures.

Flexible planning can direct funds to courts, defense, treatment, victims, corrections, prevention, and mental health, but institutional capacity often gives police agencies a strong role in funding decisions.

Community Participation

Governing-body review and public comment create a minimum democratic process.

Stronger practice includes publishing proposed budgets, consulting affected communities, reporting subawards, disclosing technology impacts, and evaluating outcomes by race, geography, age, sex, disability, and income where lawful and appropriate.

Formula Grants and Competitive Grants

Formula JAG awards are calculated by statute and do not undergo ordinary peer review for whether one state deserves more than another.

Competitive BJA programs rank proposals by merit and priority.

Byrne discretionary projects are congressionally designated and use neither the formula nor ordinary open competition.

Byrne JAG and COPS Grants

COPS grants are administered by the Office of Community Oriented Policing Services and often focus on officer hiring, community policing, school safety, technology, or training.

JAG is administered by BJA and provides broader formula flexibility across the entire justice system.

Recipients can receive both but must prevent duplicate charging.

Byrne JAG and Byrne State Crisis Intervention Program

The Byrne State Crisis Intervention Program was created by the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act and uses a separate appropriation and formula based on JAG.

Byrne SCIP supports crisis-intervention court proceedings and related firearm-crisis programs.

It is not part of the ordinary annual JAG formula award, even though the programs share terminology and administrative systems.

Formula JAG and Community Project Grants

Formula JAG uses statutory population and crime calculations.

Byrne Discretionary Community Project Grants fund named projects listed through congressional appropriations.

Reporting the two together without explanation overstates the amount distributed through the JAG formula.

Major Achievements

  • Created one broad formula grant replacing two overlapping systems.
  • Provides predictable annual justice funding to every state and territory.
  • Supplies direct awards to more than one thousand local jurisdictions annually.
  • Supports all major components of the criminal-justice system.
  • Requires statewide strategic planning and local pass-through.
  • Permits funding for indigent defense, treatment, prevention, victims, and mental health as well as law enforcement.
  • Offers flexibility to address emerging threats and local conditions.
  • Requires public review, performance reporting, financial controls, and civil-rights compliance.
  • Has produced nearly 26,000 direct awards exceeding $8.2 billion since fiscal year 2005.
  • Provides a formula foundation used by related programs such as Byrne SCIP.

Limitations and Failures

  • Annual appropriations fluctuate and can be reduced by carveouts.
  • Formula awards are not based on project quality or demonstrated effectiveness.
  • Reported crime data can be incomplete or inconsistent.
  • Violent-crime and population measures do not capture every justice need.
  • Broad flexibility can preserve outdated or punitive programs.
  • Law-enforcement institutions can dominate state planning.
  • Small local governments depend on state pass-through and subaward processes.
  • Public-comment procedures can become perfunctory.
  • Thousands of varied projects are difficult to evaluate nationally.
  • Technology purchases can create privacy and continuing-cost burdens.
  • Penalty systems can reduce flexible justice funding while attempting to enforce unrelated federal mandates.
  • Long award periods can contribute to delayed or unspent funds.

Major Controversies

War on Drugs legacy
Byrne funding helped build multijurisdictional drug-enforcement systems associated with aggressive policing and racial disparities.
Formula design
Population and reported violent crime provide objectivity but do not measure all need or fiscal capacity.
Police dominance
Flexible justice funds can be concentrated in law enforcement rather than defense, treatment, victims, prevention, or courts.
Federal conditions
Immigration, sex-offender registration, prison standards, and death-reporting conditions create federalism and policy disputes.
Community-project grants
Congressionally designated projects can be labelled Byrne funding despite operating outside the formula.
Technology
Grants can finance surveillance and data systems without sufficient privacy review.
Evidence standards
Formula recipients can fund locally popular projects without demonstrating comparative effectiveness.
Transparency
Federal award records do not always reveal state subawards, actual spending, deployment, or local outcomes.

How Byrne JAG Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the fiscal year, formula or discretionary status, recipient, award amount, pass-through requirement, subrecipient, program area, project period, expenditure, performance measure, and current status.

Important distinctions include:

  • formula JAG versus Byrne discretionary projects;
  • state awards versus direct local awards;
  • state retention versus required pass-through;
  • allocation amounts versus actual expenditures;
  • reported crime versus victimization;
  • law-enforcement spending versus whole-system planning;
  • award outputs versus public-safety outcomes;
  • allowable use versus effective use;
  • formula year versus application-posting year;
  • appropriations carveouts versus formula availability;
  • JAG penalties versus underlying program performance; and
  • budget proposals versus enacted law.

Why the Byrne Justice Assistance Grant Program Matters

Byrne JAG is the federal government’s broadest recurring formula grant for state and local criminal justice.

Its flexibility permits jurisdictions to address needs across policing, prosecution, defense, courts, corrections, treatment, victims, prevention, mental health, technology, and planning.

The same flexibility makes the program politically and administratively important: state and local decisions determine whether funding reinforces traditional enforcement or supports reform, treatment, defense, prevention, and community-based strategies.

Because allocations depend on population and reported violent crime rather than project merit, accountability must come through planning, public review, financial controls, civil-rights enforcement, transparent subawards, and credible outcome evaluation.

Summary

The Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant Program is the leading flexible federal formula grant for state and local criminal justice.

Congress created JAG for fiscal year 2005 by merging the Edward Byrne Memorial Formula Grant Program with the Local Law Enforcement Block Grants Program.

BJA administers the program, and BJS calculates allocations using a minimum state amount, population, and a three-year average of reported Part 1 violent crime.

Sixty percent of each state allocation is initially assigned to the state share and forty percent to eligible direct local awards. States must also pass through a variable share and funds associated with local allocations below $10,000.

JAG can support law enforcement, prosecution, courts, public defense, prevention, corrections, drug treatment and enforcement, planning, technology, victims, mental health, crisis-intervention courts, drones, and authorized counter-drone systems.

Formula awards require no ordinary local match, allow up to 10 percent for administration, and generally provide two- or four-year project periods.

Fiscal year 2025—the latest published formula year as of August 2, 2026—provided approximately $295.6 million through 56 state and territorial awards and 1,230 direct local awards.

Fiscal year 2026 Justice Department documents report a broader $964 million Byrne-JAG line and carveouts, including approximately $537.9 million in congressionally designated community-project grants. That broader amount should not be confused with formula JAG allocations.

The program’s flexibility is both its greatest strength and central weakness. It allows locally responsive investment across the justice system, but formula funding and broad discretion do not guarantee effective, equitable, transparent, or evidence-based use.
