Federal Crop Insurance Program

The federally subsidised public-private insurance system that protects agricultural producers against losses in yield, revenue, price, rainfall, livestock margins, and other insured risks.

What Is the Federal Crop Insurance Program?

The Federal Crop Insurance Program is a national system of agricultural insurance authorised, subsidised, regulated, and reinsured by the federal government but sold and serviced by private insurance companies.

Producers choose among available products and coverage levels before an applicable sales closing date. The insurance guarantee is established from expected yield, price, revenue, area results, rainfall, livestock value, or another approved measure.

When an insured loss occurs, the producer reports it to the insurance provider. A loss adjuster verifies acreage, production, price, cause, farming practice, ownership share, and compliance. The company pays an indemnity if the policy formula produces a covered loss.

The federal government lowers producer cost through premium subsidies and supports private delivery through reinsurance and administrative-and-operating expense payments.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines programme origins, federal and private roles, product types, producer eligibility, crop-insurance units, coverage levels, premium subsidies, 2026 reforms, Actual Production History, revenue pricing, prevented planting, replanting, good farming practices, conservation compliance, insurance deadlines, loss adjustment, indemnities, reinsurance, company compensation, fraud, appeals, disaster interaction, climate risk, specialty and livestock products, racial and regional significance, achievements, limitations, and current policy issues through August 3, 2026.

It does not determine whether a specific farm, commodity, cause of loss, acreage, production method, or claim is insurable. The policy, crop provisions, actuarial documents, special provisions, endorsements, handbooks, and written determinations control.

Producers should obtain current information from a licensed crop-insurance agent before the applicable sales closing date.

Great Depression and Dust Bowl Conditions

The programme developed during a national agricultural crisis.

Farmers faced:

  • severe drought;
  • wind erosion and dust storms;
  • crop failure;
  • falling commodity prices;
  • foreclosure;
  • farm tenancy and displacement;
  • bank failure;
  • limited private insurance; and
  • unstable federal disaster policy.

Private insurers were reluctant to cover widespread drought or price-related agricultural losses because catastrophic events affect many policyholders simultaneously.

Federal participation made geographic pooling, premium subsidy, national data collection, and catastrophic risk sharing possible.

Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1938

Congress created the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation in 1938.

The original programme was experimental, limited geographically, and focused on selected major crops.

Early coverage relied heavily on federal administration because a private crop-insurance market did not exist at national scale.

Participation remained limited for decades because products were narrow, rates were difficult to establish, coverage was unavailable in many regions, and ad hoc federal disaster aid could substitute for insurance.

Federal Crop Insurance Corporation

FCIC is a wholly owned government corporation within USDA.

Its statutory purpose is to promote agricultural economic stability through a sound crop-insurance system and research supporting agricultural insurance.

The FCIC Board of Directors:

  • approves new policies and major product modifications;
  • reviews private-sector product submissions;
  • authorises pilot programmes;
  • approves reimbursement of authorised research and development expenses;
  • oversees actuarial soundness and fairness;
  • manages corporate authority subject to the Secretary of Agriculture; and
  • delegates operational responsibilities to the FCIC manager and RMA.

Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1980

The 1980 law attempted to transform crop insurance from a limited experiment into the primary national protection against crop disaster.

It:

  • expanded eligible crops and geographic areas;
  • authorised private insurance companies to sell and service federal policies;
  • increased federal premium subsidy;
  • provided federal reinsurance;
  • expanded programme research; and
  • sought to reduce reliance on free ad hoc disaster payments.

The original 1980 subsidy equalled thirty percent of premium, limited by the amount associated with sixty-five-percent coverage.

Ad Hoc Disaster Assistance

Participation after 1980 did not immediately reach congressional expectations.

Severe drought, flood, and weather disasters led Congress to enact emergency crop-loss payments in 1988, 1989, 1990–1992, and 1993.

Repeated disaster bills weakened incentives to purchase insurance because some producers expected federal relief after catastrophic loss.

The conflict between pre-purchased insurance and post-disaster assistance drove the major 1994 reform.

Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994

The 1994 law greatly expanded participation.

It:

  • created Catastrophic Risk Protection;
  • fully subsidised the CAT premium;
  • increased subsidies for buy-up coverage;
  • linked crop-insurance participation to eligibility for selected farm-programme benefits;
  • strengthened data, compliance, and delivery systems; and
  • made crop insurance the central farm disaster safety net.

CAT originally covered losses beyond fifty percent of average yield at sixty percent of the established price. Later law changed the price percentage to fifty-five percent.

Creation of the Risk Management Agency

USDA created the Risk Management Agency in 1996 to administer FCIC and broader agricultural risk-management functions.

RMA combined policy administration, actuarial work, product development, compliance, insurance services, and producer education in one agency.

The mandatory purchase rule was repealed, but producers accepting certain farm benefits remained subject to crop-insurance or waiver requirements connected to later disaster eligibility.

Insured acreage increased rapidly after the 1994 reform and private delivery expanded.

Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000

The Agricultural Risk Protection Act increased premium subsidies and expanded product development.

It:

  • raised subsidy rates for buy-up coverage;
  • encouraged revenue products;
  • authorised private-sector product submissions under Section 508(h);
  • expanded fraud-prevention and data authorities;
  • supported research, education, and outreach; and
  • strengthened protection for specialty crops and underserved producers.

The law helped establish the premium-subsidy structure that drove high participation in major row crops.

Agricultural Act of 2014

The 2014 Farm Bill expanded area-based supplemental coverage and tied premium subsidy to conservation compliance.

Major changes included:

  • Supplemental Coverage Option;
  • Stacked Income Protection Plan for upland cotton;
  • beginning-farmer and veteran-farmer benefits;
  • Whole-Farm Revenue Protection development;
  • greater specialty-crop attention;
  • organic price elections; and
  • highly erodible land and wetland conservation requirements.

Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018

The 2018 Farm Bill continued the programme and expanded flexibility.

It:

  • expanded livestock insurance support;
  • increased the CAT administrative fee to $655 per crop per county;
  • modified enterprise-unit and specialty-crop provisions;
  • expanded veteran and beginning-farmer benefits;
  • supported industrial hemp insurance development;
  • authorised new product research; and
  • continued conservation-compliance requirements.

2025 Statutory Expansion

Public Law 119-21 made major crop-insurance changes beginning with 2026 implementation.

It:

  • increased premium subsidies for basic, optional, and enterprise units;
  • raised SCO and similar endorsement subsidies from sixty-five to eighty percent;
  • expanded the interaction between SCO and Agriculture Risk Coverage elections;
  • raised available area-based coverage;
  • authorised ninety-percent Whole-Farm Revenue Protection coverage;
  • extended beginning-farmer status from five to ten crop years; and
  • added graduated beginning-farmer premium support during the earliest crop years.

Public-Private Delivery Partnership

Federal crop insurance is neither a conventional private insurance market nor a programme delivered entirely by government employees.

USDA determines the authorised policy framework and provides public subsidy and reinsurance. Private companies market and service policies.

The arrangement uses:

  • private agent networks;
  • private underwriting and claims staff;
  • federal actuarial and policy standards;
  • federal producer premium subsidies;
  • federal administrative-and-operating reimbursements;
  • shared underwriting gains and losses;
  • federal compliance review; and
  • publicly enforceable policy terms.

Approved Insurance Providers

An AIP must enter a Standard Reinsurance Agreement or another authorised agreement with FCIC.

AIPs:

  • appoint and supervise agents;
  • accept eligible applications;
  • process policy changes;
  • collect producer premiums;
  • maintain underwriting files;
  • train loss adjusters;
  • pay indemnities;
  • report data to RMA;
  • conduct quality control;
  • investigate irregularities;
  • comply with civil-rights and outreach duties; and
  • share risk under the reinsurance agreement.

A producer generally purchases federal crop insurance through an agent representing an AIP rather than directly from RMA.

Crop-Insurance Agents

Agents explain available products, deadlines, unit structures, price elections, subsidy rates, and reporting obligations.

An agent does not ordinarily have authority to alter federal policy terms or guarantee that a disputed loss will be paid.

Producers should:

  • review all documents before signing;
  • retain copies of applications and reports;
  • confirm deadlines in writing;
  • disclose every farming interest accurately;
  • ask whether coverage is individual, area, or index based;
  • understand exclusions and unit structure; and
  • report mistakes promptly.

Standard Reinsurance Agreement

The Standard Reinsurance Agreement governs the relationship between FCIC and participating private companies for eligible crop-insurance contracts.

It establishes:

  • risk-sharing funds;
  • company retention of risk;
  • federal assumption of selected catastrophic risk;
  • underwriting gain and loss formulas;
  • administrative-and-operating expense reimbursement;
  • data reporting;
  • company performance;
  • quality control;
  • sanctions and default;
  • financial standards; and
  • programme integrity requirements.

AIPs can assign policies to different reinsurance funds before loss experience is known, subject to agreement rules.

Annual Insurance Cycle

  1. USDA publishes policy, actuarial, price, and special-provision information.
  2. The producer meets with an agent before the sales closing date.
  3. The producer applies or changes coverage.
  4. The AIP accepts the policy subject to eligibility and underwriting.
  5. The producer reports production history and other underwriting information.
  6. Insurance attaches under the policy.
  7. The producer reports planted and prevented acres by the acreage reporting date.
  8. The AIP issues a summary of coverage.
  9. The producer receives premium billing and pays the producer share and fees.
  10. The producer reports damage or loss within the required period.
  11. A loss adjuster inspects and calculates covered loss.
  12. The AIP pays an indemnity when due.
  13. The producer reports final production for future yield history.

Key Deadlines

Sales closing date
Final date to apply for or change coverage for the crop year.
Cancellation date
Date by which the producer or insurer can cancel continuing coverage, often the same as the sales closing date.
Production reporting date
Deadline to report historical production used in underwriting and approved yield.
Final planting date
Last date to plant and receive the full production guarantee, subject to crop provisions.
Late planting period
Period after the final planting date during which planting can remain insured with reduced guarantee.
Acreage reporting date
Deadline to report planted and prevented acreage, share, practice, type, and location.
Premium billing date
Earliest date the insurer can bill the premium under the actuarial documents.
Notice of loss
Generally within seventy-two hours after initial discovery of damage or loss of production and no later than fifteen days after the insurance period ends, unless policy terms specify otherwise.
End of insurance period
Policy-specific date ending coverage, often harvest, abandonment, destruction, or a calendar date.

Insurable Interest and Share

A producer must have an economic interest in the insured crop or commodity.

Eligible interests can arise through:

  • owner-operation;
  • cash lease;
  • crop-share lease;
  • sharecropping;
  • landlord share;
  • tenant share;
  • partnership or entity ownership;
  • contract production;
  • livestock ownership; or
  • another policy-recognised arrangement.

Insurance liability, premium, and indemnity are multiplied by the producer’s insured share.

Undisclosed or incorrectly reported ownership interests can lead to reduced coverage, revised units, overpayments, or fraud findings.

Persons, Entities, and Substantial Beneficial Interests

Policies can be issued to individuals, partnerships, corporations, estates, trusts, associations, and other recognised legal entities.

Applicants must disclose persons with substantial beneficial interests according to policy and reporting rules.

Identification is used to:

  • verify eligibility;
  • prevent duplicate insurance;
  • apply sanctions and debt offsets;
  • determine beginning or veteran status;
  • coordinate conservation compliance;
  • identify common ownership; and
  • support programme-integrity review.

Insurance Units

A unit is the acreage or commodity grouping for which premium, guarantee, production, and indemnity are calculated.

Basic unit
Generally combines all insurable acreage of a crop in a county in which the producer has the same share arrangement with the same owner.
Optional unit
Separates qualifying acreage by section, farm serial number, irrigated practice, nonirrigated practice, type, or another authorised distinction.
Enterprise unit
Combines all qualifying acreage of the crop in the county, or authorised multicounty area, into a larger diversified unit.
Whole-farm unit
Combines qualifying crops or commodities under authorised whole-farm structures.

Larger units pool losses across more acreage, often lowering premium and increasing federal subsidy but requiring the combined unit—not an individual field—to experience a sufficient loss.

Coverage Level and Deductible

A producer selects the percentage of expected yield, revenue, or value to insure within the range offered.

The uninsured portion is the deductible.

For example, seventy-five-percent individual revenue coverage ordinarily leaves a twenty-five-percent deductible before an indemnity is triggered.

Higher coverage:

  • raises liability;
  • reduces the deductible;
  • increases total premium;
  • can change the federal subsidy percentage; and
  • can make supplemental endorsements available over remaining deductible bands.

Catastrophic Risk Protection

CAT is the lowest-cost federal crop-insurance option for eligible yield-based crops.

It generally:

  • uses fifty-percent yield coverage;
  • values the loss at fifty-five percent of the established price;
  • has a fully subsidised premium;
  • requires a $655 administrative fee per crop per county unless waived;
  • does not offer the same revenue protection as buy-up policies; and
  • pays only after a severe loss exceeds the fifty-percent yield deductible.

CAT can preserve access to selected disaster programmes but may provide insufficient protection for operating loans, land payments, or high-cost production.

Buy-Up Coverage

Buy-up coverage protects more than CAT and generally allows the producer to select higher yield or revenue guarantees and full authorised price elections.

The producer pays a portion of actuarially determined premium after federal subsidy.

Buy-up coverage can include:

  • individual yield protection;
  • individual revenue protection;
  • area yield or revenue protection;
  • supplemental area coverage;
  • whole-farm revenue;
  • rainfall or weather indexes;
  • livestock price and margin products; and
  • crop-specific endorsements.

Actual Production History

APH uses the producer’s historical yields to estimate expected production.

The database generally contains four to ten years, but policy provisions can use:

  • actual farm yields;
  • transitional yields when records are insufficient;
  • yield substitutions for exceptionally low years;
  • yield floors;
  • yield cups limiting annual decline;
  • trend adjustments;
  • county or area yields;
  • new-producer procedures; and
  • assigned yields after reporting failure.

Accurate production records are essential because the approved yield determines guarantee and premium.

Yield Protection

Yield Protection insures against a production shortfall caused by an insured event.

The guarantee generally equals:

approved yield × coverage level × insured acres × insured share.

An indemnity is based on the amount by which actual production falls below the guarantee, multiplied by the selected price and share.

Yield Protection does not ordinarily pay solely because the market price falls.

Revenue Protection

Revenue Protection covers revenue loss caused by lower yield, lower price, or a combination.

RMA establishes a projected price before planting and a harvest price later through commodity-market discovery.

Standard Revenue Protection generally uses the higher of projected or harvest price to establish the guarantee, protecting a producer whose yield loss occurs while market prices rise.

Actual revenue is based on harvested production multiplied by the harvest price, subject to policy adjustments.

Revenue Protection with Harvest Price Exclusion

RP-HPE protects against yield and price decline but does not increase the guarantee when harvest price exceeds projected price.

The product generally costs less than standard Revenue Protection.

A producer with forward contracts or marketing arrangements must consider whether loss of the harvest-price increase would create a gap after a yield loss.

Area Risk Protection Insurance

ARPI bases indemnities on area results, generally county yield or revenue, rather than the individual farm’s loss.

A producer can suffer a serious farm loss and receive no payment if the county does not trigger.

Conversely, a producer can receive payment when individual results are strong but the area result falls below the trigger.

Area plans can reduce recordkeeping and moral-hazard concerns but introduce basis risk between farm and county outcomes.

Statutory changes expanded available area protection to as much as ninety percent of expected area yield or revenue.

Supplemental Coverage Option

SCO is an area-based endorsement that covers part of the deductible above an underlying individual policy.

The endorsement ordinarily begins at eighty-six percent and ends at the underlying policy coverage level.

Beginning with 2026:

  • the federal premium subsidy is eighty percent;
  • producers can purchase SCO regardless of Agriculture Risk Coverage election under the revised rules; and
  • ECO can functionally protect the additional band through ninety percent until SCO’s statutory maximum is implemented at ninety percent for 2027.

SCO pays only when the area trigger is met.

Enhanced Coverage Option

ECO provides area-based protection at the top of the producer’s deductible, commonly with a ninety or ninety-five-percent trigger.

It supplements an underlying individual policy but does not convert an individual farm loss into an area payment.

Beginning with 2026, the federal premium subsidy is eighty percent.

ECO can be combined with SCO under authorised structures but not with every other area or index product on the same acreage.

Stacked Income Protection Plan

STAX provides area revenue protection for upland cotton.

Coverage can protect a band of expected revenue above the deductible of another policy or operate as a stand-alone product where authorised.

Federal premium subsidy is high because Congress designed STAX as part of the transition from traditional cotton commodity payments.

Eligibility interacts with seed-cotton base enrolled in Agriculture Risk Coverage or Price Loss Coverage.

Margin Coverage

Margin Protection and Margin Coverage Option address the difference between expected revenue and selected input costs rather than gross revenue alone.

The products can use county yields, projected prices, harvest prices, and nationally determined input prices.

MCO is an endorsement to an eligible underlying individual policy.

Beginning with 2026, MCO receives an eighty-percent federal premium subsidy.

Pasture, Rangeland, and Forage Insurance

PRF protects grazing or haying operations against below-normal precipitation using a Rainfall Index.

Producers select:

  • eligible grid acreage;
  • intended use;
  • coverage level;
  • productivity factor;
  • two-month index intervals; and
  • percentage of value allocated to each interval.

NOAA precipitation data determine the grid index. The producer does not need to prove individual forage loss.

PRF accounted for a large share of the rapid growth in total insured acres because one policy can cover extensive rangeland.

Apiculture Rainfall Index

Apiculture insurance protects beekeeping operations against below-normal rainfall affecting floral resources and forage conditions.

Like PRF, it uses NOAA grid data rather than direct measurement of hive or honey loss.

Producers select index intervals and coverage allocations based on expected forage needs.

Basis risk remains because rainfall within a grid may not match conditions at a particular apiary.

Hurricane Insurance Protection–Wind Index

HIP-WI covers part of the deductible under an underlying crop policy when an insured county or adjacent county experiences sustained hurricane-force winds from a named hurricane.

The Tropical Storm Option can add coverage when specified wind and rainfall triggers are met.

The endorsement is available for many crops in hurricane-prone areas near the Gulf of Mexico, Atlantic Coast, and Hawaii.

Beginning with 2026, federal premium subsidy is eighty percent.

Fire Insurance Protection–Smoke Index

FIP-SI protects eligible grape producers against smoke exposure associated with qualifying wildfire smoke events.

The index determines whether the county experiences the required number of smoke events rather than requiring each producer to prove direct smoke measurements for the trigger.

The pilot began in selected California counties and expanded to Idaho, Oregon, and Washington for 2026.

Federal premium subsidy is eighty percent under the 2026 statutory change.

Whole-Farm Revenue Protection

WFRP insures revenue from multiple farm commodities under one policy.

It is designed for diversified operations, specialty-crop farms, organic farms, livestock and crop combinations, direct-market farms, and producers whose commodities lack separate policies.

Current guidance permits up to $17 million in insured revenue.

Beginning with 2026, coverage can reach ninety percent, with the highest coverage subject to diversification and policy requirements.

WFRP relies heavily on farm tax records, expected revenue, expense information, inventory, contracts, and commodity diversification.

Micro Farm

Micro Farm is a simplified whole-farm product for small and diversified operations.

For 2026, it is tailored for farms with approved revenue up to $350,000.

It can cover:

  • specialty crops;
  • organic commodities;
  • livestock and animal products;
  • direct-market sales;
  • local and regional markets;
  • value-added products within policy limits; and
  • farm-identity-preserved commodities.

Simplification improves access but tax-record and revenue-verification requirements can still burden new or informal small farms.

Livestock Insurance

Federal crop insurance increasingly covers livestock and animal-product revenue.

Livestock Risk Protection
Protects against decline in market price for eligible cattle and swine during a selected endorsement period.
Livestock Gross Margin
Protects the expected margin between livestock or milk value and specified feed costs.
Dairy Revenue Protection
Protects quarterly milk revenue based on price and production factors.
Weaned Calf Risk Protection
Pilot protection for cow-calf producers against reduced weaning weight and revenue in specified states.

Livestock plans represented approximately twenty percent of FCIP liability in 2025.

Specialty Crops

Specialty crops include fruits, vegetables, tree nuts, nursery crops, floriculture, and other high-value commodities.

Insurance challenges include:

  • many varieties and grades;
  • high production cost;
  • quality loss;
  • perennial plants;
  • regional concentration;
  • contract pricing;
  • limited production data;
  • organic and conventional price differences;
  • labour dependence; and
  • rapidly changing markets.

RMA has expanded enterprise units, organic practice options, contract-price authority, tree and vine products, and pilot insurance.

Organic Production

Organic producers can insure eligible organic crops where actuarial documents provide an organic practice or written agreement permits coverage.

Organic price elections can reflect higher market value when sufficient data exist.

The producer must follow recognised organic practices, certification rules, transition requirements, and policy provisions.

Whole-farm and Micro Farm products can provide alternatives when individual organic crop products are unavailable or incomplete.

Beginning Farmer and Rancher Benefits

Beginning-farmer status is intended to reduce barriers caused by limited production history, capital, and insurance experience.

Beginning with 2026 implementation, qualifying individuals can receive benefits for up to ten crop years of actively operating and managing a farm or ranch.

Benefits include:

  • ordinary additional ten percentage points of premium subsidy;
  • five additional points in crop years one and two;
  • three additional points in crop year three;
  • one additional point in crop year four;
  • waiver of CAT and selected administrative fees;
  • use of a higher percentage of transitional yield in selected APH calculations;
  • ability to use production history from farms previously involved with the producer; and
  • special procedures for entities containing beginning farmers.

Veteran Farmer and Rancher Benefits

A qualifying veteran farmer or rancher generally receives:

  • ten additional percentage points of premium subsidy;
  • waiver of CAT and selected administrative fees;
  • production-history benefits similar to those for beginning farmers; and
  • special entity procedures.

The 2025 legislation did not change the veteran definition or benefit schedule.

When a producer qualifies as both beginning and veteran, the higher applicable benefit governs rather than stacking both full benefits.

Limited-Resource Producers

Limited-resource farmers can receive waivers of selected administrative fees when they satisfy current USDA criteria and certification procedures.

Premium subsidy remains essential because small farms can be unable to purchase actuarially priced coverage without federal support.

Access barriers include:

  • minimum premium and paperwork costs;
  • limited agent availability;
  • lack of production records;
  • small acreage;
  • diverse commodities;
  • informal markets;
  • land tenure;
  • language access; and
  • mistrust arising from historical discrimination.

Prevented Planting

Prevented planting can pay when an insured cause prevents planting by the final planting date or during the late planting period.

Eligibility generally requires:

  • an insured cause affecting the area;
  • reasonable expectation that the acreage could be planted under normal conditions;
  • eligible crop and acreage history;
  • sufficient prevented-planting eligible acreage;
  • timely notice and acreage reporting;
  • compliance with planting and farming-practice rules;
  • no excluded cause such as lack of inputs or management choice; and
  • appropriate treatment of any second crop.

Prevented-Planting Payment

The payment is generally a percentage of the production guarantee for the insured crop.

Crop provisions establish the ordinary prevented-planting factor, and additional buy-up options can be available for selected crops.

Planting a second crop can reduce the prevented-planting payment to thirty-five percent and can affect premium, production history, and eligibility, subject to policy exceptions.

Leaving acreage idle, planting a cover crop, haying, grazing, or planting another crop can produce different outcomes depending on dates and rules.

Late Planting

A producer who plants after the final planting date can retain insurance during the late planting period with a daily reduction in guarantee.

After the late planting period, coverage can remain available at a reduced prevented-planting-based guarantee or under other crop-specific rules.

The producer must compare:

  • expected late-planted yield;
  • reduced guarantee;
  • prevented-planting payment;
  • input cost;
  • second-crop options;
  • contract obligations;
  • cover-crop rules; and
  • future APH effect.

Replanting Payments

Selected crop policies provide a payment when an insured cause damages the original planting and replanting is practical.

The producer must generally:

  • notify the insurer before replanting;
  • permit inspection;
  • meet minimum acreage or loss thresholds;
  • replant to the same insured crop unless otherwise authorised;
  • replant within reasonable dates; and
  • follow the adjuster’s instructions.

Replanting without notice can destroy evidence and jeopardise payment.

Good Farming Practices

Federal crop insurance covers unavoidable insured loss, not loss caused by inadequate farming.

Producers must use production methods generally recognised by agricultural experts for the crop, area, and practice to allow the crop to make normal progress toward maturity.

Relevant practices include:

  • seed selection;
  • planting date and rate;
  • fertility;
  • irrigation;
  • pest and disease control;
  • weed management;
  • harvest timing;
  • organic methods;
  • soil preparation; and
  • weather-responsive management.

A producer can request review of an adverse good-farming-practice determination under specialised federal procedures.

Conservation Compliance

Federal premium subsidy is tied to compliance with highly erodible land and wetland conservation requirements.

A producer generally must:

  • file Form AD-1026 with the Farm Service Agency by the applicable deadline;
  • avoid producing agricultural commodities on highly erodible land without an approved conservation system;
  • avoid converting wetlands for crop production;
  • provide information necessary for USDA determinations; and
  • seek timely relief when failure results from circumstances beyond control.

Noncompliance can make the producer ineligible for federal premium subsidy across policies even when insurance coverage otherwise exists.

Notice of Loss

A producer should notify the crop-insurance agent immediately after discovering damage or potential loss.

The Common Crop Insurance Policy generally requires notice:

  • within seventy-two hours after initial discovery of damage or loss of production; and
  • no later than fifteen days after the end of the insurance period.

Special deadlines apply to prevented planting, revenue claims, quality adjustment, livestock endorsements, nursery value, rainfall indexes, and other products.

The producer should not destroy, abandon, harvest, replant, graze, or put damaged production to another use without instructions when doing so would prevent accurate adjustment.

Loss Adjustment

A loss adjuster verifies the claim under FCIC-approved procedures.

The review can include:

  • insured acreage and share;
  • planting dates;
  • production records;
  • harvested and unharvested production;
  • appraisals;
  • quality tests;
  • market prices;
  • cause and timing of loss;
  • farming practices;
  • weather data;
  • input records;
  • storage and disposition;
  • other insurance; and
  • policyholder eligibility.

Quality Adjustment

Production can be reduced for claim purposes when an insured cause lowers quality below policy standards.

Quality factors can include:

  • test weight;
  • kernel damage;
  • toxins;
  • mould;
  • sprouting;
  • grade;
  • market rejection;
  • processing suitability;
  • smoke exposure; and
  • other crop-specific defects.

Samples, tests, sale records, and approved laboratories can be required. Failure to preserve representative samples can prevent adjustment.

Indemnity Payment

An indemnity is due when the verified covered result falls below the policy guarantee or meets an authorised index trigger.

The insurer issues a claim statement showing:

  • guarantee or insured value;
  • actual production or revenue;
  • coverage level;
  • price or index;
  • insured acreage and share;
  • quality adjustment;
  • uninsured-cause appraisal;
  • premium or fee offset;
  • prior payments; and
  • net indemnity.

Crop-insurance indemnities are generally taxable farm income under federal tax rules, subject to special deferral provisions for qualifying crop proceeds.

Premium Debt and Offsets

Unpaid premium, administrative fees, interest, or other programme debt can be offset from an indemnity.

Delinquent debt can affect eligibility for future insurance and other federal benefits.

Producers should review billing promptly because incorrect acreage, share, units, or subsidy status can alter premium substantially.

Dispute Resolution

The correct dispute route depends on who made the determination and what issue is contested.

AIP determinations
Many disputes over policy administration, claim amount, acreage, production, or coverage proceed through mediation or arbitration under the policy.
RMA agency decisions
Adverse agency decisions can generally be appealed to USDA’s National Appeals Division unless a statute or regulation provides otherwise.
Good farming practices
Use a specialised reconsideration and judicial-review pathway rather than ordinary policy arbitration.
Policy interpretation
A binding Final Agency Determination can be requested when interpretation is needed in a dispute.
Judicial review
Federal district-court review can be available after exhaustion and within strict statutory time limits.

Mediation and Arbitration

Mediation allows the producer and insurer to seek a voluntary resolution within federal policy terms.

If unresolved, many policy disputes proceed to binding arbitration under applicable rules.

An arbitrator cannot rewrite federal policy, expand coverage through equitable estoppel, or disregard binding FCIC interpretations.

Deadlines are strict, and failure to begin arbitration or litigation on time can bar the claim.

Fraud and False Statements

Programme fraud can include:

  • false acreage reports;
  • fabricated production records;
  • concealed production;
  • false ownership shares;
  • duplicate insurance;
  • misstated planting dates;
  • staged or intentional crop destruction;
  • false prevented-planting claims;
  • misreported farming practices;
  • agent or adjuster collusion;
  • identity or entity manipulation; and
  • false eligibility certifications.

Consequences can include indemnity denial, repayment, civil penalties, disqualification, debarment, administrative sanctions, False Claims Act liability, and criminal prosecution.

Programme Integrity and Compliance

RMA and AIPs use:

  • data mining;
  • producer and agent anomaly detection;
  • remote sensing and satellite imagery;
  • Farm Service Agency acreage data;
  • production-record review;
  • weather and market data;
  • quality-control reviews;
  • spot checks;
  • special investigations;
  • USDA Office of Inspector General referrals;
  • Department of Justice prosecution; and
  • company sanctions.

Integrity systems must distinguish fraud from legitimate differences in yield, management, soil, weather, irrigation, and local conditions.

Federal Programme Cost

Federal cost includes more than indemnities.

Major components include:

  • producer premium subsidy;
  • administrative-and-operating expense reimbursement;
  • underwriting gains paid to AIPs;
  • federal underwriting losses;
  • product research and maintenance;
  • RMA administration;
  • compliance and data systems;
  • education and outreach; and
  • interest and accounting effects.

Premium subsidies were approximately $10.4 billion in 2024 and remain the largest regular cost component.

Actuarial Soundness

The Federal Crop Insurance Act directs FCIC to establish premium rates sufficient to cover anticipated losses and a reasonable reserve over time.

Actuarial rates consider:

  • historical yields;
  • loss experience;
  • commodity prices;
  • volatility;
  • soil and climate;
  • irrigation;
  • county and regional risk;
  • unit structure;
  • coverage level;
  • farming practice;
  • product design; and
  • catastrophic correlation.

Premium subsidy reduces the producer’s price but does not alter the total actuarial premium recorded for the policy.

Loss Ratio

Loss ratio equals indemnities divided by total premium.

A ratio:

  • below 1.0 means premium exceeded indemnities;
  • equal to 1.0 means premium equalled indemnities; and
  • above 1.0 means indemnities exceeded premium.

A single-year ratio does not establish long-run actuarial adequacy because agriculture experiences highly variable weather and price cycles.

Subsidy means producers as a group pay less than total premium, so producer-paid premium should not be used as the denominator for programme loss ratio.

Relationship to Agricultural Disaster Assistance

Crop insurance is intended to provide pre-disaster protection, but Congress continues to enact supplemental disaster programmes after severe events.

Programmes can address:

  • uninsured or underinsured losses;
  • shallow losses below the deductible;
  • quality or tree losses not fully covered;
  • livestock death;
  • forage loss;
  • emergency conservation;
  • orchard restoration;
  • producer expenses; and
  • disasters not covered by available policies.

Disaster payments commonly deduct crop-insurance indemnities and require future insurance or Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program coverage to prevent duplication and encourage participation.

Relationship to the Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program

NAP is administered by the Farm Service Agency for crops lacking an available federal crop-insurance policy.

NAP and FCIP are separate programmes.

A producer generally cannot obtain duplicate protection for the same crop and acreage.

New products can move commodities from NAP toward crop insurance when adequate data and actuarial methods become available.

Relationship to ARC and PLC

Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage are commodity programmes administered by the Farm Service Agency.

They are not crop-insurance policies and use different farm, base-acre, price, and revenue formulas.

Producers can combine eligible underlying crop insurance with commodity programmes, subject to product interactions.

The 2025 legislation removed the prior rule preventing SCO purchase on acreage enrolled in ARC, while STAX remains restricted on certain seed-cotton base.

Agricultural Lending

Lenders frequently require crop insurance as a condition of operating credit.

Insurance can:

  • protect loan repayment;
  • support collateral value;
  • stabilise cash flow;
  • reduce default risk;
  • permit higher operating loans;
  • support forward contracting; and
  • encourage investment in inputs and equipment.

An indemnity assignment can direct payment to a creditor, but assignment does not change policy eligibility or create coverage.

Climate and Catastrophic Risk

Agricultural risk is changing as producers face drought, heat, wildfire, intense rainfall, flood, hurricane, changing pest ranges, water scarcity, and production volatility.

Crop insurance can help farms survive individual disasters, but rising correlated losses can increase:

  • actuarial premium;
  • federal subsidy cost;
  • reinsurance losses;
  • regional concentration;
  • pressure for ad hoc aid;
  • difficulty insuring marginal land; and
  • debate over adaptation and conservation conditions.

Historical yield methods can adapt gradually as recent losses enter APH, but repeated disasters can reduce guarantees and future protection.

Conservation Incentives and Moral Hazard

Premium subsidy can encourage participation and risk management, but critics argue that it can also reduce the private cost of planting risky land or maintaining risk-intensive practices.

Potential reforms include:

  • greater subsidy for soil-health practices;
  • premium recognition of proven conservation risk reduction;
  • stronger highly erodible land enforcement;
  • cover-crop flexibility;
  • water-use and drought planning;
  • limits on repeated losses;
  • climate-adjusted rating; and
  • expanded data on conservation performance.

Insurance cannot require a practice unless authorised by law and policy, and conservation changes must preserve actuarial and producer equity.

Significance for African American Farmers

Black farmers have experienced land loss, discriminatory lending, exclusion from USDA programmes, unequal county administration, heirs’ property, limited credit, small acreage, and weak access to insurance agents and technical assistance.

Crop insurance can protect farm income and support operating credit, but access can be limited when:

  • the crop lacks an available policy;
  • the farm is too small or diversified for conventional products;
  • production records are incomplete;
  • land title or lease arrangements are informal;
  • the producer lacks an established agent relationship;
  • premium and fees remain unaffordable;
  • county actuarial data undervalue local production;
  • specialty or direct-market prices are not recognised; or
  • historical discrimination creates distrust.

Whole-farm products, beginning-farmer benefits, fee waivers, outreach partnerships, and heirs’ property reforms can improve access but do not erase structural inequality.

Heirs’ Property and Land Tenure

Heirs’ property is land inherited by multiple relatives without a clear partition or completed probate.

Crop-insurance participation can require proof of:

  • legal entity identity;
  • insurable share;
  • authority to sign;
  • lease or occupancy;
  • acreage control;
  • production ownership; and
  • substantial beneficial interests.

Flexible documentation and Farm Service Agency records can help, but unclear title can delay credit, insurance, disaster aid, and conservation participation.

Women Farmers

Women operate farms across commodity, livestock, specialty, direct-market, and urban agriculture sectors.

Barriers can include:

  • smaller farms;
  • less land ownership;
  • limited credit history;
  • informal management roles not reflected in records;
  • lack of agent outreach;
  • diverse commodities;
  • caregiving demands; and
  • assumptions that a male relative is the principal operator.

Accurate entity and production reporting should recognise the actual operator and insurable interest.

Tribal and Native Producers

Tribal producers can face trust-land leasing, fractional interests, remote geography, limited agent networks, livestock concentration, drought, forage risk, and federal land-management requirements.

Relevant products include:

  • Pasture, Rangeland, Forage;
  • livestock price and margin insurance;
  • whole-farm products;
  • individual crop policies;
  • apiculture;
  • nursery and specialty products; and
  • private-sector developed pilots.

Outreach should respect tribal sovereignty, local production systems, data governance, and traditional agricultural practices.

Hispanic, Immigrant, and Limited-English-Proficient Producers

Producers can face language barriers, unfamiliar contracts, complex entity rules, limited access to licensed agents, and fear arising from immigration or labour enforcement.

AIPs and RMA must provide nondiscriminatory service and meaningful access consistent with federal law and programme obligations.

Translation must explain deadlines, exclusions, reporting, conservation compliance, claim duties, and dispute rights—not merely advertise the programme.

Small and Diversified Farms

Traditional crop insurance developed around large-acreage commodity production with standardised yields and markets.

Small and diversified farms can sell many commodities through farmers markets, subscriptions, restaurants, institutions, local wholesalers, and direct retail.

Challenges include:

  • multiple commodities on few acres;
  • high value per acre;
  • limited county data;
  • specialty pricing;
  • value-added products;
  • informal or cash sales;
  • rapid crop rotation;
  • urban land tenure; and
  • high paperwork relative to insured value.

WFRP and Micro Farm are designed to address several of these problems.

Regional and Commodity Concentration

Programme benefits historically concentrate in major producing regions and crops because liability follows acreage, value, and participation.

Corn, soybeans, wheat, cotton, and other row crops account for a large share of insured liability.

Revenue products dominate liability, while PRF dominates insured acreage and livestock products have expanded rapidly.

Concentration can reflect agricultural production but also raises questions about subsidy distribution among farm sizes, commodities, states, and income levels.

Payment Limits and Income Tests

Federal premium subsidies and indemnities generally are not subject to the same payment limits and adjusted-gross-income tests that govern many Farm Service Agency commodity and conservation payments.

Supporters argue that actuarial insurance must cover the full insurable value and that limiting subsidy could reduce participation among large operations.

Critics argue that unlimited premium subsidy directs large public benefits to the largest farms and landowners.

Proposals include subsidy caps, income tests, reduced subsidy for very high coverage, and public disclosure of recipient benefits.

Transparency and Privacy

RMA publishes extensive aggregate data on acres, policies, liability, premium, subsidy, indemnities, crops, plans, states, counties, and loss causes.

Individual producer and policy information is protected by privacy, tax, trade-secret, and programme statutes.

Debate continues over how much information should be disclosed concerning:

  • large premium subsidies;
  • company compensation;
  • underwriting gains;
  • agent commissions;
  • repeat-loss policies;
  • conservation performance;
  • beneficial ownership; and
  • racial and demographic participation.

Major Achievements

  • Created a national agricultural insurance system after the Dust Bowl.
  • Replaced much of the reliance on direct federal crop-loss compensation.
  • Provides pre-enrolled protection before disasters occur.
  • Insures more than one hundred crops and numerous livestock interests.
  • Protected approximately 543 million acres and more than $192 billion in liability in 2024.
  • Supports farm operating credit and rural financial stability.
  • Provides yield and revenue protection against multiple natural hazards.
  • Protects against market-price decline through revenue and livestock products.
  • Provides prevented-planting and replanting protection.
  • Uses private agents and insurers to deliver national coverage.
  • Shares catastrophic risk between private companies and the federal government.
  • Offers whole-farm products for diversified, organic, and direct-market farms.
  • Provides rainfall-index protection for rangeland and apiculture.
  • Expanded livestock insurance substantially after 2018.
  • Provides special premium support for beginning and veteran producers.
  • Links premium subsidies to conservation compliance.
  • Develops new products through public and private research.
  • Can pay claims without waiting for new congressional disaster legislation.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • The programme costs the federal government billions of dollars annually.
  • Premium subsidies can disproportionately benefit large farms.
  • There is generally no subsidy payment cap or broad income test.
  • Private insurer compensation and underwriting gains are controversial.
  • Policies and deadlines are highly complex.
  • Coverage is uneven across crops and counties.
  • Small and diversified farms can face disproportionate paperwork.
  • Area and index products create basis risk.
  • APH can reduce future guarantees after repeated disaster.
  • CAT provides limited protection and charges a substantial fee.
  • High deductibles leave shallow losses uncovered.
  • Ad hoc disaster programmes continue despite insurance expansion.
  • Subsidies can influence planting and land-use decisions.
  • Climate change can raise correlated losses and federal cost.
  • Conservation incentives may be too weak or poorly measured.
  • Historically underserved farmers face access and trust barriers.
  • Dispute procedures can require costly arbitration or federal litigation.
  • Agent errors do not necessarily expand coverage beyond federal policy.
  • Fraud and data manipulation remain programme-integrity risks.
  • Public data are limited for evaluating demographic equity and individual subsidy concentration.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Implementation of 2026 Subsidy Increases

RMA and AIPs are implementing higher subsidies for common-policy unit structures, SCO, ECO, MCO, HIP-WI, FIP-SI, and related coverage.

Beginning-Farmer Expansion

Agents and insurers are applying the ten-year definition and graduated first-four-year premium enhancements to eligible producers and entities.

Whole-Farm Ninety-Percent Coverage

WFRP now offers a higher maximum coverage option while retaining diversification, revenue, tax-record, and commodity requirements.

SCO and ARC Interaction

Producers can purchase SCO regardless of ARC election under the revised rules, requiring new coordination among RMA, AIPs, and the Farm Service Agency.

2027 Product Changes

RMA issued 2026 policy bulletins implementing additional changes for 2027 and later crop years, including forage and crop-specific revisions.

Forage and Livestock Expansion

USDA continues adding revenue, yield, rainfall, livestock, and margin options for operations historically underserved by crop insurance.

Climate Adaptation

Policymakers are debating how rates, yield history, conservation, repeat losses, water scarcity, and new indexes should respond to changing climate conditions.

Programme Cost

Higher subsidy rates and rising insured values increase mandatory federal spending, with crop-insurance outlays projected near $156 billion over 2027–2036.

Insurer Compensation

Congress and USDA continue debating administrative reimbursement, reinsurance terms, underwriting gains, agent commissions, and delivery cost.

Underserved Producers

Outreach remains focused on Black, Native, Hispanic, women, limited-resource, beginning, veteran, specialty-crop, organic, urban, and direct-market producers.

Disaster Programme Coordination

Supplemental disaster assistance continues addressing uncovered and shallow losses while requiring coordination with insurance indemnities and future coverage.

Data and Technology

Remote sensing, acreage reporting, digital records, price data, and analytics are improving adjustment and fraud prevention while raising privacy and due-process concerns.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Approved Insurance Provider
A private insurer authorised to sell and service federally reinsured crop-insurance policies.
Actuarial documents
County- and crop-specific official data establishing availability, rates, dates, prices, practices, types, and special provisions.
Liability
The maximum amount of insurance protection before a loss is calculated.
Total premium
The actuarially determined cost of insurance before federal subsidy.
Premium subsidy
The federal portion of premium paid on behalf of the producer.
Producer premium
The portion of premium owed by the policyholder after subsidy.
Indemnity
Payment for a verified covered loss.
Loss ratio
Indemnities divided by total premium.
Approved yield
The yield used to establish insurance guarantee under APH or another approved method.
Projected price
The pre-plant price established through RMA price discovery for an eligible revenue policy.
Harvest price
The later market price used to calculate revenue loss under eligible policies.
Insurance unit
The grouping of acreage or commodities used to calculate premium, guarantee, production, and indemnity.
Catastrophic Risk Protection
Low-level coverage with fully subsidised premium and an administrative fee.
Buy-up coverage
Coverage above CAT for which the producer pays a subsidised premium share.
Prevented planting
Failure to plant by required dates because of an insured cause under policy conditions.
Replanting payment
Policy payment supporting required replanting after insured damage.
Good farming practices
Recognised production methods used to allow the crop to make normal progress toward maturity.
Basis risk
The possibility that an area or index result differs from the individual producer’s actual loss.
Underwriting gain
The positive difference allocated under the reinsurance agreement when retained premium and related amounts exceed allocated losses and expenses.
Reinsurance
Federal sharing of insurance risk with Approved Insurance Providers.

Summary

The Federal Crop Insurance Program is the principal United States insurance system protecting agricultural producers against yield, revenue, price, rainfall, livestock, and other insured risks.

Congress created FCIC in 1938 during the Great Depression and Dust Bowl. The modern programme developed through the 1980 expansion, the 1994 creation of catastrophic coverage, the 1996 creation of RMA, the 2000 subsidy expansion, and later Farm Bills.

USDA establishes policy, prices, rates, subsidies, product rules, compliance standards, and reinsurance. Approved private insurers sell and service coverage through agents and loss adjusters.

Producers can purchase individual yield or revenue policies, area protection, rainfall and weather indexes, whole-farm coverage, specialty-crop policies, and livestock price or margin insurance.

The federal government pays the CAT premium and subsidises a large share of buy-up premium. For 2026, enterprise-unit subsidies reach eighty percent through seventy-five-percent coverage, and SCO and several related endorsements receive an eighty-percent subsidy.

Beginning-farmer eligibility now extends for up to ten crop years, with additional graduated subsidy in the first four years. WFRP now offers coverage as high as ninety percent.

Producers must meet strict application, acreage, production, conservation, good-farming-practice, notice-of-loss, and claim-documentation requirements.

Approximately 543 million farm and ranch acres and more than $192 billion in liability were insured in 2024. Premium subsidies totalled approximately $10.4 billion.

The programme’s principal strength is predictable protection paid under a pre-existing contract rather than discretionary relief enacted after disaster.

Its principal policy challenges are federal cost, subsidy concentration, insurer compensation, unequal access, coverage gaps, repeated disaster aid, conservation incentives, and rising climate-related risk.
