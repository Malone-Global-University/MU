Talent Search Program

The broad-reach Federal TRIO outreach and counseling program helping eligible students and educational reentry participants complete secondary school, obtain financial-aid and admissions assistance, and enter and complete postsecondary education or training.

Overview

The Talent Search Program is a federal college-access, educational-outreach, and reentry program serving individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds who have the potential to succeed in postsecondary education. It helps participants remain in or return to school, complete secondary education, understand college and training options, apply for admission and financial aid, enroll in postsecondary education, and progress toward completion.

Talent Search is one of the Federal TRIO Programs authorized under the Higher Education Act of 1965. It was created by the original Higher Education Act and became the second program in the sequence later called TRIO, following Upward Bound in 1964. Student Support Services joined the system in 1968, producing the original group of three.

The program’s statutory purposes are to identify qualified young people with postsecondary potential and encourage them to complete secondary school and undertake postsecondary education; publicize and facilitate applications for student financial aid; and encourage individuals who did not complete secondary or postsecondary education to enter, reenter, and complete those programs.

Talent Search is a project-grant program rather than a direct federal benefit. The Department of Education awards competitive grants to colleges, universities, public agencies, nonprofit organizations, community-based organizations, secondary schools, and combinations of eligible entities. Funded projects serve approved target schools, target areas, or specially designated populations.

An eligible individual does not apply to the federal government for a personal Talent Search grant. The individual seeks services from a funded local project. Eligibility does not create an entitlement because project capacity, target boundaries, staff, grant objectives, and local selection procedures limit the number of people served.

Talent Search generally serves individuals who have completed five years of elementary education or are at least eleven but not more than twenty-seven years old. Eligible educational statuses include enrollment in or dropout from grades six through twelve, secondary-school graduation, or prior postsecondary enrollment followed by nonenrollment. Veterans can be served regardless of age, and some individuals older than twenty-seven may participate when an Educational Opportunity Center cannot serve them and their participation does not dilute services to the ordinary age group.

At least two-thirds of each project’s participants must be both low-income individuals and potential first-generation college students. The remaining participants can qualify under the project’s authorized selection framework and must still have a documented need for project services.

A low-income individual generally comes from a family whose taxable income for the preceding calendar year did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level. The Department publishes annual TRIO low-income levels. For 2026, the threshold for a family of four is $49,500 in the contiguous states and District of Columbia, $61,875 in Alaska, and $56,925 in Hawaii.

A potential first-generation college student generally is an individual whose natural or adoptive parents did not complete a bachelor’s degree. Federal rules also cover individuals who lived with and received support from only one parent who lacked a bachelor’s degree and individuals who did not regularly reside with or receive support from a natural or adoptive parent before age eighteen.

Talent Search differs from Upward Bound in intensity and scale. Upward Bound generally provides intensive, repeated instruction and summer experiences to relatively small cohorts. Talent Search generally serves larger numbers through advising, tutoring connections, application assistance, workshops, mentoring, college exposure, financial literacy, and educational reentry services.

Required Talent Search services include connections to high-quality academic tutoring; advice concerning secondary and initial postsecondary course selection; college entrance examination and admissions assistance; information concerning federal student aid and scholarships; FAFSA assistance; guidance concerning secondary reentry, alternative education, high-school equivalency, and postsecondary entry; and connections to financial and economic literacy services.

Projects may directly provide tutoring and can offer career exploration, aptitude assessment, personal and career counseling, campus visits, cultural exposure, family workshops, mentoring, and specially designed services for individuals with disabilities, limited English proficiency, homelessness, foster-care experience, traditional underrepresentation, or other forms of disconnection.

Talent Search can serve middle-school students, secondary-school students, school dropouts, recent graduates, veterans, and individuals who began postsecondary education but stopped attending. This broad eligibility distinguishes it from programs restricted to enrolled high-school students or current college students.

The program does not award secondary diplomas, college admission, Pell Grants, scholarships, or guaranteed employment. It helps participants navigate institutions and complete the actions needed to pursue those outcomes.

Talent Search projects generally operate for five-year project periods and require no cost sharing or matching. The Department uses peer review, statutory selection criteria, competitive priorities, prior-experience points for qualifying incumbents, and available appropriations to make awards.

Talent Search served the largest funded participant population among the Federal TRIO Programs in fiscal year 2024. The Department allocated approximately $196.17 million to 545 projects funded to serve 341,744 participants. The average project award was approximately $359,938, the average funded project size was 627 participants, and the federal cost per funded participant was approximately $574.

Fiscal year 2025 continuation funding was approximately $185.676 million, and the Department listed 517 projects funded for the 2025–2026 period.

The fiscal year 2026 competition opened March 17 and closed May 1, 2026. The government estimated $175,152,359 in available funds, approximately 175 awards, and five-year project periods. Ordinary applicants were required to propose serving at least 500 participants annually at no more than $500 per participant and could request up to $1 million per year. A governor-designated state-level applicant or qualifying Indian Tribe could request up to $10 million annually under the competition rules.

The 2026 competition was administered by the Department of Labor’s Employment and Training Administration on behalf of the Department of Education under an interagency partnership. It required applicants to address an absolute priority involving accelerated learning, distance education, pre-apprenticeship or Registered Apprenticeship, or programs leading to in-demand industry-recognized credentials.

The 2026 competition also offered competitive-preference points for qualifying state and Tribal applicants and included an invitational priority concerning talent marketplaces, learning and employment records, learner wallets, credential registries, and skills-based connections among participants, education providers, and employers.

As of August 2, 2026, the Department’s public Talent Search page listed the competition as closed but had not posted a fiscal year 2026 award list. Existing projects continued operating while the new competition was under review.

The Department reported that 71.5 percent of the defined 2024 Talent Search “college-ready” cohort enrolled in postsecondary education during the reporting period or the following fall. The federal target was 81 percent. College-ready participants in this measure had received a regular secondary-school diploma or an alternative award such as a high-school equivalency certificate.

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 budget request proposed eliminating funding for all Federal TRIO Programs. A presidential budget request is not enacted law. Congress retains authority over appropriations, and Talent Search remained authorized and operational as of August 2026.

Talent Search developed during the Great Society and Civil Rights eras in response to the recognition that tuition aid alone could not overcome unequal preparation, limited counseling, discriminatory institutions, poverty, segregated schools, and lack of information about college. The program remains especially important to low-income communities, first-generation families, HBCUs, community colleges, rural areas, veterans, foster youth, homeless students, and individuals returning to education.

Definitions

Talent Search
The Federal TRIO outreach and counseling program helping eligible individuals complete or reenter secondary and postsecondary education and obtain admissions and financial-aid assistance.
Federal TRIO Programs
The federal college-access and student-support programs serving low-income, first-generation, disabled, veteran, and other eligible populations across the educational pipeline.
Low-income individual
An individual whose family taxable income in the calendar year preceding initial project participation did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level.
Potential first-generation college student
An individual whose parents did not complete a bachelor’s degree, subject to federal rules for one-parent support and individuals who did not regularly live with or receive support from a natural or adoptive parent.
Participant
An eligible individual formally selected by a project who receives services designed for the person’s age or educational stage.
Potential participant
An individual recruited, screened, contacted, or considered by a project who has not necessarily been selected and served as an official participant.
Target area
The approved geographic community, school system, service region, or other area from which a project recruits and serves participants.
Target school
A middle school, secondary school, alternative school, or other approved educational site identified in the funded application.
Educational reentry
The process through which a person who left secondary or postsecondary education returns to complete a diploma, equivalency, certificate, degree, or other credential.
College-ready participant
For the federal enrollment performance measure, a Talent Search participant who has received a regular secondary-school diploma or an alternative award such as a high-school equivalency certificate.
Rigorous secondary school program of study
A recognized challenging course sequence preparing students for postsecondary education and satisfying federal or state criteria.
Secondary-school persistence
Continued enrollment and advancement in secondary education from one reporting period or grade level to the next.
Regular secondary-school diploma
A diploma awarded to students meeting or exceeding the state’s coursework and performance standards for high-school completion.
Alternative education program
A program providing a pathway for a student who left or is not succeeding in a conventional school setting to complete secondary education.
High-school equivalency
A state-recognized alternative credential based on an authorized examination or completion process, commonly associated with GED or similar programs.
Postsecondary education
Education beyond secondary school, including colleges, universities, community colleges, eligible career schools, apprenticeships, and qualifying training pathways.
College access
The ability to prepare for, apply to, finance, enter, and participate in postsecondary education.
Financial and economic literacy
Knowledge and skills concerning college costs, grants, loans, budgeting, banking, credit, debt, financial planning, and economic decision-making.
Prior experience
Competitive points available to an incumbent grantee based on documented performance during specified project years.
Annual Performance Report
The federal report documenting participant characteristics, services, educational status, project objectives, outcomes, and grant performance. The abbreviation APR is commonly used.
Standard objectives
Federally approved commitments involving participant numbers, persistence, graduation, rigorous coursework, postsecondary enrollment, and postsecondary completion.
Absolute priority
A competition requirement that every eligible application must address.
Competitive preference priority
A competition priority for which an applicant can receive additional points.
Invitational priority
A policy area the Department encourages applicants to address without awarding preference points or requiring participation.
Talent marketplace
A digital or institutional system connecting education, skills, credentials, employment records, training providers, and employers.
Learning and employment record
A digital record describing verified education, training, credentials, skills, and work experience. The abbreviation LER is commonly used.
Disconnected participant
An individual facing homelessness, foster care, disability, limited English proficiency, educational interruption, justice-system involvement, or another condition separating the person from stable educational opportunity.

Historical Origins

Great Society

Talent Search emerged from the Johnson administration’s expansion of federal responsibility for educational opportunity. Policymakers increasingly viewed secondary completion and college access as national concerns connected to poverty, civil rights, economic growth, and democratic participation.

Information Barrier

Federal student grants and loans could not benefit individuals who did not know the programs existed, did not complete applications, lacked counseling, left school early, or believed college was inaccessible.

College Outreach

Talent Search created a structured federal mechanism for colleges and public or nonprofit agencies to enter schools and communities, identify students, explain postsecondary options, and assist with applications.

Civil Rights Era

The program developed while African American students and civil-rights organizations challenged segregated schools, discriminatory admissions, unequal guidance systems, and exclusion from public and private colleges.

Educational Reentry

Congress did not restrict Talent Search to students continuously enrolled in school. The statute also addressed dropouts and individuals who began but did not complete postsecondary education.

Formation of TRIO

Upward Bound, Talent Search, and Special Services for Disadvantaged Students became the original three programs known collectively as TRIO.

Expansion Beyond College Admission

Later amendments added financial literacy, rigorous-coursework support, disability and disconnected-youth services, completion measures, mentoring, family workshops, and stronger accountability.

Program Purpose and Theory

Identify Potential

Talent Search assumes that many students and adults capable of postsecondary success are not identified or encouraged by ordinary educational systems.

Prevent Educational Exit

Early advising, course planning, tutoring connections, and mentoring can reduce the likelihood that a student leaves secondary school or closes off college options.

Provide College Knowledge

Participants learn how admissions, financial aid, credentials, majors, transfer, apprenticeships, training, and campus systems work.

Complete Applications

The program turns general aspiration into concrete actions: registering for examinations, completing applications, filing the FAFSA, requesting records, comparing offers, and meeting deadlines.

Reconnect Dropouts and Stopouts

Participants who left secondary or postsecondary education receive guidance concerning reentry, equivalency, alternative education, transfer, and credential completion.

Support Family Decision-Making

Workshops help families understand costs, aid, borrowing, institutional differences, safety, housing, and academic expectations.

Increase Completion

Federal objectives extend beyond initial enrollment to postsecondary persistence and degree or credential attainment.

Serve Large Populations Efficiently

Talent Search uses a broad-reach model with lower per-participant costs than intensive residential or research programs.

Relationship to the Federal TRIO Programs

Original Three

Talent Search was one of the three programs that produced the historical TRIO name.

Upward Bound

Upward Bound generally provides intensive instruction, repeated contact, and summer programming to smaller cohorts. Talent Search generally provides broad counseling, information, referrals, application assistance, and outreach to larger populations.

Student Support Services

Student Support Services serves students already enrolled at the grantee college. Talent Search prepares individuals to enter, reenter, or continue postsecondary education.

Educational Opportunity Centers

EOC focuses principally on adults nineteen and older and educational reentry. Talent Search ordinarily serves ages eleven through twenty-seven and can include younger students, dropouts, graduates, and veterans.

Veterans Upward Bound

VUB provides intensive academic preparation specifically for veterans. Talent Search may serve eligible veterans of any age but generally offers a broader counseling and referral model.

McNair

McNair serves undergraduates preparing for doctoral education, a later point in the educational pipeline.

Coordination Requirement

Talent Search applicants must assure collaboration with TRIO, GEAR UP, and similar programs serving the same schools or areas to reduce duplication and expand access.

No Institutional Recruitment Use

An institution of higher education operating Talent Search must assure that it will not use the project as part of its own student-recruitment program.

Eligible Grant Applicants

Federal regulations permit grants to:

  • Institutions of higher education
  • Public agencies and organizations
  • Private nonprofit agencies and organizations
  • Community-based organizations experienced in serving disadvantaged youth
  • Secondary schools
  • Consortia or combinations of eligible entities

Colleges and Universities

Institutions of higher education can provide admissions expertise, financial-aid knowledge, campus exposure, faculty, student mentors, technology, and pathways into college.

Community Colleges

Community colleges often operate projects closely connected to local school districts, adult reentry, career pathways, transfer, and regional labor markets.

Nonprofit Organizations

Nonprofits can bring community trust, culturally responsive outreach, service coordination, and experience with foster youth, homelessness, disability, or educational reentry.

Public Agencies

State, local, Tribal, educational, workforce, and other public agencies can apply when they satisfy competition requirements.

Secondary Schools

School systems and qualifying secondary schools, including charter schools under the 2026 competition guidance, can apply directly when consistent with program purposes.

Multiple Applications

An eligible applicant may submit more than one application when each proposed project serves a different target area, target schools, or designated population.

No Match

Talent Search does not require cost sharing or matching, although reviewers evaluate applicant and community support.

Participant Eligibility

Citizenship or Qualifying Status

An individual generally must be a United States citizen or national, permanent resident, qualifying nontemporary resident intending permanent residence, permanent resident of Guam or the Northern Mariana Islands, or resident of a Freely Associated State under the regulatory provisions.

Age and Elementary Education

The ordinary participant has completed five years of elementary education or is at least eleven and not more than twenty-seven years old.

Secondary-School Status

Eligible individuals include students enrolled in or dropped out of grades six through twelve and individuals who have graduated from secondary school.

Postsecondary Stopouts

An individual who undertook but is not currently enrolled in postsecondary education can participate.

Veterans

A veteran meeting the other applicable requirements can participate regardless of age.

Individuals Older Than Twenty-Seven

A project may serve an older individual when an Educational Opportunity Center cannot appropriately serve the person and participation does not dilute services to the ordinary Talent Search population.

Two-Thirds Group

At least two-thirds of participants must be both low income and potential first-generation college students.

Need for Services

The project performs and documents a needs assessment so services correspond to the participant’s educational stage and barriers.

No Automatic Right to Service

A federally eligible person can be outside the target area, unable to enter a full project, or better served by another program.

2026 Low-Income Levels

The current TRIO low-income levels became effective January 13, 2026 and remain in effect until replaced. The figures equal 150 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Target Areas and Target Schools

Documented Need

Applicants document conditions showing why the proposed schools and communities need a Talent Search project.

Federal Need Factors

  • High numbers or percentages of low-income families
  • High free- or reduced-price school-meal eligibility
  • Low secondary-school persistence
  • Low on-time graduation
  • Low postsecondary enrollment and completion
  • Low adult bachelor’s-degree attainment
  • Limited rigorous coursework
  • Low academic achievement or test scores
  • High student-to-counselor ratios
  • Unaddressed needs involving foster youth, homelessness, disability, or other socioeconomic barriers

School Partnerships

Projects coordinate recruitment, student records, counseling, workshops, tutoring referrals, FAFSA events, college visits, and educational planning with target schools.

Accessible Settings

Applicants must assure that project services will be located in settings accessible to the individuals proposed to be served.

Geographic Gaps

Some high-need schools remain outside any funded target area because federal grants are limited and awards depend on applications and competition scores.

Changing Boundaries

School closures, district reorganization, charter enrollment, migration, and demographic change can affect target-school relationships during a five-year grant.

Recruitment and Selection

Recruitment Sources

Projects recruit through counselors, teachers, classrooms, family events, community organizations, youth programs, alternative schools, veterans’ services, social media, alumni, and direct outreach.

Application Information

Local applications can request family income, parental education, educational status, academic goals, transcripts, counselor information, veteran status, disability or accommodation needs, and consent for records.

Needs Assessment

Projects identify the services each participant needs rather than providing the same intervention to every person.

Broad Reach

A project can serve hundreds or thousands of participants through group workshops, school visits, individual counseling, FAFSA events, campus trips, and digital advising.

Priority and Capacity

Projects can establish lawful selection procedures reflecting target schools, educational stage, need, available capacity, and ability to benefit.

Participant Definition

A contact or one-time public event does not automatically make every attendee an official participant. The grantee must determine eligibility and provide age- or grade-appropriate project services.

Equal Access

Selection and service delivery must comply with federal civil-rights and nondiscrimination requirements.

Required Services

Connections to Tutoring

Projects must connect participants to high-quality academic tutoring services enabling completion of secondary or postsecondary courses.

Course Selection

Participants receive advice concerning secondary-school courses and, when appropriate, initial postsecondary course selection.

Entrance Examinations

Projects assist participants preparing for authorized college entrance examinations and other relevant admissions assessments.

College Applications

Staff help participants complete postsecondary admissions applications and required supporting documents.

Federal Student Aid

Projects provide information concerning Pell Grants, federal student loans, Federal Work-Study, loan forgiveness, veterans’ benefits, and other federal resources.

Scholarships

Participants learn how to locate public and private scholarships and evaluate eligibility and application requirements.

FAFSA Assistance

Projects help participants and contributors understand and complete the Free Application for Federal Student Aid.

Secondary Reentry

Projects guide school dropouts toward secondary reentry, alternative education, or high-school equivalency pathways.

Postsecondary Entry or Reentry

Individuals who completed secondary school or stopped out of college receive assistance entering or returning to postsecondary education.

Financial and Economic Literacy

Projects connect participants or their parents with services addressing college financial planning, budgeting, borrowing, debt, banking, credit, and economic decision-making.

Additional Authorized Services

Direct Academic Tutoring

Projects may provide instruction in reading, writing, study skills, mathematics, science, and other subjects.

Personal and Career Counseling

Counseling can address educational goals, motivation, career interests, family responsibilities, reentry, time management, and referral needs.

Career Exploration

Participants can complete aptitude or interest assessments and explore occupations, credentials, earnings, labor-market demand, and education requirements.

Campus Exposure

Projects can organize college visits, classroom observations, student panels, admissions sessions, residence-hall tours, and cultural activities.

Family Workshops

Families can receive information concerning course planning, admission, financial aid, college costs, safety, housing, careers, and expectations.

Mentoring

Teachers, counselors, faculty, college students, professionals, alumni, veterans, and community members can mentor participants.

Specially Designed Services

Projects can tailor services for individuals with disabilities, limited English proficiency, homelessness, foster-care involvement, traditional underrepresentation, or other forms of disconnection.

Special Middle-School Activities

Talent Search can provide age-appropriate activities for sixth-, seventh-, and eighth-grade students before college applications become immediate.

Tutoring and Course Selection

Tutoring Connections

The project can connect participants with school tutoring, college tutors, nonprofit programs, online platforms, peer tutoring, libraries, and community providers.

Direct Tutoring

A project may operate its own tutoring services when consistent with the approved grant and participant needs.

Graduation Requirements

Staff help students understand state and district diploma requirements, credit accumulation, attendance, examinations, and deadlines.

College-Preparatory Courses

Participants receive advice concerning algebra, geometry, advanced mathematics, laboratory science, English, foreign language, social studies, career education, and electives.

Advanced Coursework

Projects can help students access honors, advanced placement, International Baccalaureate, dual enrollment, early college, and other rigorous courses.

Initial College Course Selection

Recent graduates and reentry participants can receive help interpreting placement, prerequisites, developmental education, general education, majors, and registration.

Tuition for Unavailable Rigorous Courses

Federal funds may pay for no more than the equivalent of two rigorous secondary courses per participant per school year when the course is unavailable in the district, a waiver is unavailable, the course is offered by an accredited institution, and the secondary school accepts it toward graduation.

Admissions and Financial-Aid Assistance

Postsecondary Search

Participants compare colleges, universities, community colleges, career schools, apprenticeships, online options, program quality, cost, accreditation, transfer, graduation, and employment outcomes.

Application Planning

Projects help participants organize deadlines, essays, recommendations, transcripts, portfolios, examinations, fees, and admissions requirements.

Application Fees

Grant funds can pay college-admission, entrance-examination, or alternative-education examination fees when a waiver is unavailable and payment is made directly to a third party.

FAFSA Preparation

Participants and contributors receive help gathering information, creating StudentAid.gov accounts, providing consent, completing the form, reviewing the submission summary, and correcting errors.

Aid Programs

Projects explain Pell Grants, FSEOG, Federal Work-Study, federal loans, state grants, institutional aid, scholarships, veterans’ benefits, loan forgiveness, and other resources.

Aid-Offer Comparison

Participants learn to distinguish grants, loans, work-study, net price, unmet need, deposits, renewable aid, housing, food, transportation, and indirect expenses.

Verification and Follow-Up

Staff can help participants respond to verification, identity, citizenship, tax, dependency, and other institutional requests without making financial-aid decisions for the school.

Enrollment Tasks

Projects assist with placement, orientation, housing, health records, disability services, registration, transcripts, and final enrollment steps.

Secondary-School Reentry and Equivalency

School Dropouts

Talent Search is expressly authorized to serve individuals who dropped out of grades six through twelve.

Reentry Options

Projects identify district reentry, alternative schools, adult education, credit recovery, online programs, evening school, competency-based education, and other lawful pathways.

Regular Diploma Priority

Federal services emphasize reentry programs leading to a regular secondary-school diploma when possible.

High-School Equivalency

Participants can receive guidance concerning GED and other state-recognized equivalency examinations and preparation programs.

Barrier Assessment

Reentry counseling can address childcare, work, transportation, homelessness, disability, safety, court involvement, health, documentation, and prior school conflict.

Postsecondary Transition

Completing an equivalency credential is connected to admissions, placement, financial aid, apprenticeships, career training, and longer-term educational planning.

No Diploma Authority

Talent Search does not itself award a secondary diploma or equivalency credential.

Postsecondary Reentry and Completion

Stopout Population

An individual who began postsecondary education but is not currently enrolled can qualify for Talent Search within the ordinary eligibility framework.

Transcript Review

Participants can obtain transcripts and learn how prior credits may apply to a new or returning program.

Academic Standing

Projects help participants understand academic probation, satisfactory academic progress, readmission, debt holds, and institutional appeal procedures.

Financial-Aid Restoration

Participants can receive guidance concerning FAFSA renewal, loan default resolution, grant overpayments, lifetime aid limits, and institutional requirements.

Transfer

Staff can help compare transfer agreements, program requirements, accreditation, credit loss, cost, and completion time.

Career and Training Alternatives

Reentry planning can include degrees, certificates, industry credentials, Registered Apprenticeships, and other high-quality pathways.

Referral to EOC or SSS

Adults may be referred to an Educational Opportunity Center, and enrolled students may be connected to Student Support Services or campus advising.

Career and Workforce Pathways

Career Exploration

Projects introduce occupations, industries, credentials, education requirements, earnings, working conditions, and advancement pathways.

Aptitude and Interest Assessment

Assessments can support discussion but should not permanently restrict students to predicted occupations or tracks.

College and Training

Talent Search includes traditional college pathways and can also connect participants to high-quality career education, pre-apprenticeship, Registered Apprenticeship, and industry credentials.

2026 Absolute Priority

The fiscal year 2026 competition required proposed projects to expand access to at least one of the following: accelerated learning such as high-impact tutoring; distance education, pre-apprenticeship, or Registered Apprenticeship; or programs and coursework leading to in-demand industry-recognized credentials.

Workforce Alignment

The 2026 Education–Labor partnership placed greater emphasis on employment and earnings outcomes and coordination with state workforce systems.

Educational Choice

Career information should preserve informed choice and explain differences among degrees, certificates, apprenticeships, transfer programs, and short-term training.

Quality Protection

Participants need information concerning accreditation, licensing, transferability, completion, debt, earnings, employer recognition, and program closure before choosing a pathway.

Family Workshops and Engagement

First-Generation Navigation

Families without direct college experience can receive explanations of admissions language, financial aid, credentials, academic expectations, and institutional systems.

Course Planning

Families learn how secondary-school course choices affect graduation, admission, placement, majors, and scholarship eligibility.

College Cost

Workshops explain tuition, fees, housing, food, books, transportation, grants, loans, work-study, scholarships, and net price.

FAFSA Contributors

Parents and spouses learn their contributor responsibilities, consent requirements, privacy protections, and the distinction between providing FAFSA information and assuming college debt.

Family Expectations

Projects discuss work, caregiving, transportation, housing, safety, communication, and support during educational transition.

Language and Accessibility

Family information should be understandable, accessible, and available through appropriate language support.

Adult Participants

Family engagement can include spouses, partners, children, or caregivers when adult participants return to education.

Disconnected and Special Populations

Students with Disabilities

Projects can provide accessible materials, accommodations, disability-service referrals, self-advocacy support, and postsecondary transition information.

Limited English Proficiency

Services can include language-access support, interpretation, bilingual outreach, and information concerning English-language and academic programs.

Homeless Youth

Participants experiencing homelessness may need assistance with identification, FAFSA dependency status, transportation, school continuity, housing referrals, and application follow-up.

Foster Youth

Projects can help current and former foster youth navigate records, independent status, tuition benefits, housing, mentoring, and campus supports.

Veterans

Eligible veterans can receive Talent Search services regardless of age, including educational reentry, admissions, financial aid, and referrals to veteran-specific programs.

Justice-System Involvement

Projects can assist eligible individuals returning from detention, incarceration, or court involvement, subject to target-area and project eligibility.

Rural Isolation

Remote participants can face transportation, broadband, limited course offerings, few counselors, and long distances to colleges or training providers.

Trauma-Informed Practice

Effective projects recognize that instability, violence, loss, family separation, and institutional mistrust can affect participation and educational planning.

Allowable Program Costs

Costs must be reasonable, necessary, allocable, documented, and related to approved project objectives.

Transportation, Meals, and Lodging

Funds can support participant and staff travel for college visits, College Day activities, career field trips, and instruction connected to a rigorous secondary-school program.

Testing and Preparation

Projects can purchase testing materials and test-preparation programs.

Application and Examination Fees

Funds can pay admissions, entrance-examination, and alternative-education examination fees when waivers are unavailable and payment is made directly to a third party.

Staff Training

In-service training can strengthen counseling, financial aid, data, disability access, career guidance, grant compliance, and other project capacities.

Space

Projects can rent space when suitable space is unavailable at the grantee and the rented property is not owned by the grantee.

Technology

Funds can purchase, lease, or rent hardware, software, equipment, service agreements, and supplies supporting participant services, project administration, and recordkeeping.

Rigorous-Course Tuition

Funds can pay qualifying tuition when a rigorous course is unavailable within the district, the school accepts the course toward a diploma, the provider is accredited, a waiver is unavailable, and other regulatory conditions are met.

Two-Course Limit

A project can pay for no more than the equivalent of two rigorous courses for one participant during a school year.

Uniform Guidance

Federal cost principles, procurement, internal controls, equipment, records, audit, and subaward requirements apply under 2 C.F.R. part 200 and Department regulations.

Grant Competition and Selection

Competitive Application

Eligible organizations submit proposals during a federal notice inviting applications.

Selection Criteria

The regulations establish a 100-point peer-review structure:

  • Need for the project—24 points
  • Objectives—8 points
  • Plan of operation—30 points
  • Applicant and community support—16 points
  • Quality of personnel—9 points
  • Budget—5 points
  • Evaluation plan—8 points

Prior Experience

An incumbent applicant can receive up to fifteen additional points based on high-quality service and performance during specified project years.

Rank Order

New grants are generally made in rank order based on peer-review and prior-experience scores, subject to available funds and competition rules.

Tie Breaking

When tied applications cannot all be funded, the Department can prioritize underserved geographic areas and populations.

Fraud Restriction

The Department does not make a new grant to an applicant whose prior project involved fraudulent use of program funds.

Five-Year Period

A Talent Search project period is five years, with annual continuation funding subject to appropriations, substantial progress, and compliance.

No Match

Applicants are not required to provide a formal nonfederal match.

Fiscal Year 2026 Competition

Interagency Administration

The Department of Labor’s Employment and Training Administration managed competition activities on behalf of the Department of Education.

Application Period

The competition opened March 17, 2026 and closed May 1, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time.

Estimated Funding

The Department listed $175,152,359 in estimated available funds and approximately 175 expected awards.

Project Period

Successful applicants were expected to receive five-year, sixty-month project periods.

Ordinary Minimum Project Size

New and incumbent applicants generally had to propose serving at least 500 participants annually at a cost not exceeding $500 per participant.

Ordinary Award Ceiling

An applicant not designated as the state-level applicant could request no more than $1 million annually.

State-Level and Tribal Ceiling

One governor-designated state-level applicant and qualifying Indian Tribes receiving competitive-priority points could request up to $10 million annually.

Absolute Priority

Every application had to expand access to one or more of the following:

  • Accelerated learning such as high-impact tutoring
  • Distance education
  • Pre-apprenticeship
  • Registered Apprenticeship, including pathways for in-school or out-of-school youth
  • Programs or coursework leading to in-demand industry-recognized credentials

Competitive Preference

Five additional points were available for projects carried out by qualifying state educational, workforce, or higher-education agencies or by qualifying Tribal governments and organizations.

Invitational Priority

The government encouraged—but did not award preference points for—projects using learning and employment records, learner wallets, credential registries, skills-based job descriptions, and interoperable talent marketplaces.

Status

As of August 2, 2026, the public application page listed the competition as closed. A fiscal year 2026 awards list had not been posted on the Department’s Talent Search page.

Prior-Experience Points

Maximum Score

An incumbent applicant can receive up to fifteen prior-experience points.

Three-Year Average

The Department evaluates specified prior project years and averages the scores used in the competition.

Performance Categories

  • Serving the funded number of participants and satisfying the two-thirds requirement
  • Secondary-school persistence or promotion
  • Graduation with a regular diploma
  • Completion of a rigorous secondary-school program
  • Postsecondary enrollment
  • Postsecondary attainment

Continuity Benefit

Prior experience rewards projects that maintain participant volume, comply with eligibility rules, and meet approved objectives.

New-Applicant Disadvantage

A new applicant begins without prior-experience points and may compete against incumbents with up to fifteen additional points.

Policy Debate

Supporters view the system as performance accountability and continuity protection. Critics argue that it can entrench existing grantees and leave new high-need areas unserved.

Project Administration

Project Director

A grantee generally must employ a full-time project director unless the director administers a limited number of related programs or the Department grants a waiver.

Authority

The grantee must give the project director sufficient authority to administer services, personnel, partnerships, records, budget, and federal requirements effectively.

Participant Number

Each project must serve at least the number of participants established in the competition notice and funded application.

Eligibility Records

The project maintains documentation supporting each participant’s citizenship or qualifying status, age or education, educational status, low-income and first-generation status when applicable, and selection.

Needs Assessment

The project records the participant’s needs and connects services to those needs.

Service Records

Projects document tutoring connections, counseling, applications, financial aid, workshops, campus visits, mentoring, reentry, and other services.

Educational Progress

Records track persistence, promotion, graduation, rigorous coursework, postsecondary enrollment, and completion when available.

Coordination Records

Projects document services participants receive from other TRIO or similar federally funded programs when practicable.

Annual Performance Report

Every grantee submits an APR documenting progress toward approved objectives.

Performance Measures

Participant Number

Projects are evaluated on whether they serve at least the approved number of participants.

Secondary Persistence

The project measures continued enrollment and advancement in secondary education.

Regular Diploma

Projects track graduation with a regular secondary-school diploma within the standard period.

Rigorous Program of Study

Projects report participants completing recognized rigorous secondary coursework.

Postsecondary Enrollment

The Department measures whether college-ready participants enroll in postsecondary education during the reporting period or following fall.

Postsecondary Completion

Projects attempt to track degrees and credentials after participant enrollment.

Financial-Aid Application

Talent Search GPRA reports also examine completion of financial-aid applications among relevant participants.

2024 Enrollment Outcome

The Department reported a 71.5-percent postsecondary enrollment rate against an 81-percent target for the defined 2024 college-ready cohort.

Interpretive Limits

The measure is not the share of every Talent Search participant entering college in one year. It applies to a defined cohort that completed secondary education or an alternative credential.

Evaluation and Evidence

Project-Level Reporting

Annual reports provide the principal recurring source of national information concerning participants, services, and objectives.

Past National Studies

Federal and independent studies have examined college information, application behavior, financial-aid filing, secondary completion, postsecondary enrollment, and completion.

Mixed Findings

Research findings vary according to program period, service intensity, participant group, comparison design, outcome definition, and local implementation.

Selection Problem

Participants are selected because they need and can benefit from services. Simple comparisons with nonparticipants can reflect preexisting motivation, school conditions, and access to other programs.

Service Heterogeneity

One project may emphasize FAFSA workshops and school counseling, while another provides tutoring, mentoring, campus exposure, reentry, and career services. National averages can conceal these differences.

Long-Term Tracking

Following participants across schools, colleges, states, name changes, and educational interruptions is difficult and depends on accurate records and data matching.

Current Policy Dispute

The fiscal year 2027 budget justification cites missed performance targets and studies it interprets as showing insufficient enrollment effects. Program advocates emphasize the broad participant population, application and financial-aid assistance, local outcomes, and the need for stronger rather than eliminated evaluation.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Civil Rights Era Origins

Talent Search was created as African American students and civil-rights organizations were challenging segregated schools, discriminatory colleges, unequal counseling, and exclusion from public opportunity.

School Segregation and Poverty

Black students remain disproportionately represented in high-poverty and racially isolated schools with high counselor caseloads, limited advanced courses, fewer college visits, and weaker access to admissions and financial-aid expertise.

Income and Wealth

Racial disparities in household income, homeownership, savings, inheritance, and family wealth affect the ability to pay for applications, testing, travel, deposits, technology, and unpaid educational experiences.

First-Generation Navigation

Historical exclusion from higher education affects whether families possess direct knowledge of admissions, majors, transfer, financial aid, campus offices, and graduate education.

Historically Black Colleges and Universities

HBCUs operate Talent Search projects, host college visits, employ counselors and mentors, partner with schools, and serve as postsecondary destinations.

Community Colleges

Community-college projects can connect Black students and adults with local entry, transfer, career programs, apprenticeships, and educational reentry.

Dropout and Reentry

Talent Search’s authority to serve dropouts and stopouts is significant in communities affected by school exclusion, economic disruption, housing instability, incarceration, caregiving, and employment demands.

Career Tracking Risk

Workforce guidance must avoid reproducing racial occupational tracking by steering Black participants away from selective colleges, advanced academic fields, or professional pathways without informed choice.

Equity Measures

Meaningful evaluation examines recruitment, service intensity, rigorous-course access, FAFSA completion, institutional quality, college enrollment, completion, debt, transfer, credentials, and employment—not only total participation.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Early Outreach

Talent Search reaches students before secondary course choices, dropout, and missed deadlines close educational options.

Large-Scale Service

The program can serve hundreds of thousands of individuals at a lower per-participant cost than intensive residential programs.

Admissions Navigation

Staff help participants complete complex applications and institutional requirements.

Financial-Aid Access

FAFSA and scholarship assistance can convert financial eligibility into actual aid receipt.

Educational Reentry

Dropouts, graduates, veterans, and former college students receive pathways back into education.

Family Knowledge

Workshops extend college and financial information beyond the individual participant.

Career Awareness

Participants learn about occupations, credentials, apprenticeships, college majors, and educational requirements.

Community Partnerships

Grants connect colleges, schools, public agencies, nonprofits, families, and workforce systems.

Reduced Administrative Exclusion

Assistance with forms, deadlines, documentation, and follow-up can prevent capable individuals from losing opportunity through procedural barriers.

Limitations and Risks

Limited Coverage

Many eligible individuals live outside funded target areas or attend schools without a project.

Uneven Service Intensity

A participant may receive sustained counseling and tutoring or only limited group services, depending on project design and need.

Large Caseloads

Broad project sizes can limit individualized follow-up.

Grant Instability

Projects can lose funding after a competition, disrupting staff, school relationships, and participant continuity.

Prior-Experience Barrier

New applicants compete against incumbents eligible for additional points.

Dependence on Schools

Projects need access to students, records, counselors, classrooms, and family events. Weak school cooperation can reduce effectiveness.

College Affordability

Counseling and FAFSA assistance do not guarantee that available aid will make a chosen institution affordable.

Institutional Quality

Helping a participant enroll does not ensure the selected college, training program, or credential offers strong completion and employment outcomes.

Long-Term Tracking

Projects can lose contact after high school, transfer, relocation, name change, military service, or stopout, complicating completion reporting.

Major Policy Debates

Expansion or Elimination

Supporters seek broader coverage and higher funding. The fiscal year 2027 administration request proposes ending the federal program.

College Access or Workforce Alignment

Traditional Talent Search emphasizes secondary completion and postsecondary education. The 2026 competition places greater emphasis on apprenticeships, industry credentials, distance education, and employment outcomes.

Breadth or Depth

Serving large populations expands reach but can reduce individualized service. More intensive projects cost more and serve fewer people.

State-Level Delivery

The 2026 competition allowed large state-level and Tribal proposals. Supporters argue that statewide coordination can reduce fragmentation; critics worry about concentration, bureaucracy, and loss of local relationships.

Technology and Talent Marketplaces

Digital records and learner wallets can improve portability and workforce connections. They also raise privacy, surveillance, accuracy, interoperability, and algorithmic-bias concerns.

Prior Experience

Performance points protect continuity but can make it difficult for new organizations and newly underserved regions to win grants.

Outcome Measurement

Federal targets emphasize persistence, graduation, enrollment, and completion. Projects also provide benefits—such as family knowledge and application assistance—that are difficult to capture in one outcome rate.

Federal or Local Responsibility

Supporters describe college access as a national civil-rights and economic-mobility responsibility. Critics favor state, institutional, and community control without federal grant administration.

Key Distinctions

Talent Search and TRIO

Talent Search is one program within the broader Federal TRIO system.

Talent Search and Upward Bound

Talent Search generally provides broad outreach and counseling to larger populations. Upward Bound generally provides more intensive instruction and summer programming to smaller cohorts.

Talent Search and Educational Opportunity Centers

Talent Search ordinarily serves ages eleven through twenty-seven and includes secondary students. EOC focuses principally on adults nineteen and older, although the programs can overlap.

Talent Search and Student Support Services

Talent Search prepares individuals to enter or return to postsecondary education. SSS serves students currently enrolled at the grantee institution.

Talent Search and GEAR UP

Talent Search serves selected eligible individuals across multiple educational statuses. GEAR UP commonly serves cohorts or priority students beginning no later than middle school.

Eligibility and Entitlement

Federal eligibility permits selection by a funded project. It does not guarantee service.

Tutoring Connection and Direct Tutoring

A project must connect participants to high-quality tutoring and may also provide tutoring directly.

Talent Search and Financial Aid

Talent Search provides information and application assistance. Pell Grants, work-study, loans, and scholarships provide financial resources under separate programs.

Postsecondary Enrollment and Completion

Enrollment means entering an education or training program. Completion requires earning the intended degree, certificate, or credential.

Career Counseling and Job Placement

Talent Search provides career information and pathway assistance but is not primarily an employment-placement program.

Geographic Scope

Talent Search operates across the United States, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, participating territories, and eligible freely associated jurisdictions under federal participant rules.

Projects serve urban school systems, rural regions, Tribal communities, migrant populations, border areas, suburbs, small towns, alternative schools, adult-education sites, and postsecondary stopouts.

Federal availability is uneven. Some communities contain multiple projects serving different schools or populations, while other high-need areas have no local project.

Rural projects face long travel distances, limited broadband, few colleges, transportation barriers, and counselor shortages. Urban projects can face large caseloads, fragmented schools, housing instability, and demand exceeding project capacity.

The 2026 competition created a pathway for governor-designated statewide projects and qualifying Tribal applicants with larger annual award ceilings.

Alaska and Hawaii use higher low-income thresholds reflecting separate federal poverty guidelines.

Legal Context

Higher Education Act of 1965

The Act created Talent Search and the modern federal framework for postsecondary access, institutional support, and student aid.

Higher Education Amendments of 1968

The amendments created Special Services for Disadvantaged Students, completing the original TRIO group.

Education Amendments of 1972

The amendments reorganized federal postsecondary policy and created Educational Opportunity Centers.

Higher Education Amendments of 1980

The law revised federal access programs, eligibility, services, and administration.

Higher Education Amendments of 1992

The law established major modern TRIO competition, prior-experience, participant, and service provisions.

Higher Education Amendments of 1998

The law strengthened financial-aid assistance, rigorous coursework, disconnected-youth services, and accountability.

Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008

The reauthorization revised participant definitions, project services, competitions, performance measures, and grant administration.

20 U.S.C. § 1070a-11

The general TRIO section governs grant authority, applicant eligibility, low-income and first-generation definitions, competition, prior experience, coordination, and common requirements.

20 U.S.C. § 1070a-12

The Talent Search section defines the program’s purpose, required services, permissible services, and participant priorities.

34 C.F.R. Part 643

The principal regulations govern grants, participant eligibility, services, target areas, selection criteria, prior experience, allowable costs, project periods, records, and project directors.

2 C.F.R. Part 200

The Uniform Guidance governs federal grant costs, procurement, internal controls, property, records, audits, and remedies.

Civil-Rights Laws

Title VI, Title IX, Section 504, the Americans with Disabilities Act, and other federal and state laws apply according to their terms.

Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act

FERPA governs access to and disclosure of educational records used in participant selection, counseling, progress monitoring, and reporting.

Summary

Talent Search is the broad-reach Federal TRIO outreach and counseling program helping eligible individuals complete secondary education, enter or return to postsecondary education, apply for financial aid, and pursue educational completion.

Congress created Talent Search through the Higher Education Act of 1965. It became the second program in the original TRIO sequence, following Upward Bound and preceding Student Support Services.

The program serves individuals who generally have completed five years of elementary education or are ages eleven through twenty-seven. Participants can be enrolled in grades six through twelve, secondary-school dropouts, high-school graduates, or former postsecondary students not currently enrolled.

Veterans can participate regardless of age. Individuals older than twenty-seven can be served in limited circumstances when an Educational Opportunity Center cannot appropriately serve them.

At least two-thirds of project participants must be both low income and potential first-generation college students. The low-income ceiling generally equals 150 percent of the federal poverty level.

Required services include tutoring connections, course-selection advice, college entrance and admissions assistance, federal student-aid and scholarship information, FAFSA assistance, secondary and postsecondary reentry guidance, and financial-literacy connections.

Projects may also provide tutoring, career and personal counseling, aptitude assessment, campus visits, cultural exposure, family workshops, mentoring, and specially designed services for disconnected participants.

Talent Search is a project grant, not an individual entitlement. Eligible students and adults seek services from funded local projects serving approved target schools and areas.

Projects generally operate for five years and require no cost sharing or matching. Incumbent grantees can receive prior-experience points based on participant volume and educational outcomes.

In fiscal year 2024, Talent Search received approximately $196.17 million for 545 projects funded to serve 341,744 participants. It served the largest participant population among the Federal TRIO Programs.

Fiscal year 2025 continuation funding was approximately $185.676 million, and the Department listed 517 projects for 2025–2026.

The fiscal year 2026 competition made approximately $175.15 million available, estimated 175 awards, required five-year projects, and emphasized accelerated learning, distance education, apprenticeships, industry credentials, state and Tribal participation, and workforce-connected data systems.

As of August 2, 2026, the competition was closed and under review, and the Department had not posted the new award list.

The Department reported that 71.5 percent of the defined 2024 college-ready Talent Search cohort enrolled in postsecondary education, below the federal target of 81 percent.

Talent Search has particular significance for African American communities, HBCUs, community colleges, rural areas, foster youth, homeless students, veterans, and first-generation families because it addresses unequal access to counseling, advanced coursework, applications, financial aid, campuses, and educational networks.

The administration has proposed eliminating TRIO funding in fiscal year 2027, but Congress has not enacted that proposal. Talent Search remains authorized and active.

The program’s effectiveness depends on stable funding, accurate participant selection, strong school and community partnerships, individualized follow-up, trustworthy career information, financial-aid expertise, accessible technology, and successful transition from application to completion.
