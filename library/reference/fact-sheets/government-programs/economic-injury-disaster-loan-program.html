Economic Injury Disaster Loan Program

The Small Business Administration direct-loan program providing working capital to eligible small businesses, agricultural cooperatives, aquaculture enterprises, nurseries, and private nonprofit organisations unable to meet ordinary financial obligations because of a declared disaster.

Terminology Note

The official program name is the Economic Injury Disaster Loan Program, commonly abbreviated EIDL.

Traditional EIDL is part of the Small Business Administration Disaster Loan Program. It should be distinguished from SBA’s ordinary 7(a), 504, Microloan, and investment programs.

EIDL is ordinarily a direct federal loan made and serviced by SBA. It is not normally a lender-funded loan with an SBA guaranty.

The separate Immediate Disaster Assistance Program authorises interim lender-originated guaranteed loans, but it is not the standard EIDL delivery system.

An EIDL is not a grant and ordinarily is not forgivable. The pandemic-era EIDL Advance, Targeted EIDL Advance, and Supplemental Targeted Advance were grants or grant-like advances created by separate temporary laws and should not be confused with the repayable COVID-19 EIDL loan.

The phrase substantial economic injury has a specific regulatory meaning: the business cannot meet obligations as they mature or pay ordinary and necessary operating expenses. A decline in sales or loss of expected profit alone does not establish eligibility.

The phrase credit elsewhere means that SBA believes the applicant can obtain adequate nonfederal financing on reasonable terms after the disaster. Traditional EIDL is generally limited to applicants that cannot obtain such credit.

COVID-19 EIDL was a temporary nationwide variation of the program created during the pandemic. New applications ended in 2021, processing ended in 2022, and the remaining portfolio is now principally a servicing, repayment, hardship, collection, fraud-review, and enforcement matter.

Overview

EIDL provides working capital to eligible businesses and private nonprofits whose cash flow has been impaired by a declared disaster.

The program is intended to preserve an otherwise viable organisation until ordinary operations can resume.

A business may suffer economic injury because customers cannot reach it, suppliers cannot deliver, employees are displaced, tourism declines, roads close, drought damages the surrounding economy, fishing waters close, public utilities fail, a major employer or military installation changes operations, or another declared event interrupts normal commerce.

EIDL does not compensate the borrower for all lost sales or expected profit. SBA estimates the working capital needed to pay obligations and necessary expenses during the recovery period.

The program can operate alongside a business physical-disaster loan. Physical loans repair or replace damaged property; EIDL covers financial obligations and operating expenses.

The combined ordinary cap is $2 million for the borrower and affiliates arising from one disaster.

Applicants borrow directly from the federal government and must repay the debt according to a loan authorisation, note, security agreement, and other closing documents.

SBA evaluates disaster causation, small-business status, ownership, affiliates, available resources, credit elsewhere, character, repayment ability, eligible needs, collateral, insurance, other recoveries, and federal debt status.

Classification and Scope

This page belongs under Government Programs because EIDL is a continuing federal lending program with statutory eligibility, declarations, underwriting, loan terms, permitted uses, servicing, appeals, and enforcement.

The Small Business Administration belongs under Institutions. Section 7(b) of the Small Business Act and major disaster-loan amendments belong under Laws and Legislation.

Business Physical Disaster Loans, Home and Personal Property Disaster Loans, Military Reservist EIDL, Immediate Disaster Assistance, and COVID-19 EIDL warrant separate entries.

FEMA grants, Agriculture Department emergency loans, Treasury pandemic programs, state bridge loans, insurance, and private business-interruption coverage are related but separate systems.

Historical Context

Small businesses are especially vulnerable to disasters because they often have limited cash reserves, concentrated customers, a single location, few suppliers, and restricted access to long-term credit.

Property insurance may repair a building or replace equipment without covering months of payroll, rent, supplier obligations, debt service, and reduced revenue.

Private business-interruption insurance is not universal and may contain waiting periods, exclusions, coverage limits, or causation disputes.

Congress placed disaster lending within SBA’s original 1953 mission to preserve small businesses and assist recovery.

Over time, federal law expanded disaster lending beyond physical destruction to cover substantial economic injury caused by drought, disruptions, military call-ups, terrorism, public-health emergencies, and other authorised events.

Statutory Foundation

Section 7(b) of the Small Business Act authorises SBA disaster loans.

The statute allows direct loans and, in limited circumstances, participation with financial institutions.

Economic injury authority permits assistance to eligible small concerns, cooperatives, and private nonprofit organisations suffering substantial injury because of a disaster.

The law establishes interest-rate formulas, maximum amounts, credit-elsewhere requirements, agricultural limitations, declarations, military-reservist assistance, penalties for misapplication, and other program controls.

Congress appropriates subsidy and administrative resources because the program offers long maturities and below-market rates to borrowers experiencing emergency conditions.

Direct Federal Loan Model

Traditional EIDL differs from SBA’s 7(a) Program.

In a 7(a) loan, a private lender ordinarily provides the funds and SBA guarantees part of the lender’s loss.

In traditional EIDL, SBA generally receives the application, underwrites the credit, approves the amount, closes the loan, disburses federal funds, services the account, and collects repayment.

Section 7(b) permits participation with a financial institution, but this is not the ordinary modern application channel.

Immediate Disaster Assistance loans are separate interim guaranteed loans made through approved lenders.

Disaster Declarations

EIDL becomes available only after an authorised declaration establishes the event, incident period, eligible geographic area, loan types, rates, and filing deadlines.

Declarations can arise through:

  • a presidential major-disaster declaration including appropriate federal assistance;
  • a presidential emergency involving primary federal responsibility;
  • an SBA physical-disaster declaration;
  • an SBA economic-injury-only declaration based on a governor’s certification;
  • a determination by the Secretary of Agriculture concerning drought or another authorised natural event;
  • a rural disaster declaration requested by a governor or Tribal chief executive; or
  • another statutory declaration mechanism.

SBA publishes declarations in the Federal Register and uses local media, online notices, field centres, and partner outreach.

Presidential Major-Disaster and Emergency Declarations

The President may declare a major disaster under the Stafford Act after a governor or Tribal chief executive requests federal assistance.

FEMA identifies designated counties and categories of assistance.

SBA may offer EIDL in primary and qualifying contiguous counties, depending on the form of the declaration.

A public-assistance-only declaration does not always produce EIDL eligibility in contiguous counties.

A presidential emergency involving primary federal responsibility can support an SBA economic-injury declaration covering the states or subdivisions identified in the emergency determination.

SBA Economic-Injury-Only Declarations

A governor may certify that at least five small businesses in an area suffered substantial economic injury and need financing not otherwise available on reasonable terms.

The certification identifies the affected county or political subdivision and includes supporting evidence.

SBA evaluates the request and issues a written decision.

EIDL-only declarations provide working-capital assistance without requiring the physical-damage thresholds used for an SBA physical-disaster declaration.

Agriculture Department Declarations

The Secretary of Agriculture may designate a natural disaster that causes economic injury to eligible nonfarm small businesses, cooperatives, nurseries, aquaculture enterprises, and private nonprofits.

Drought declarations are a common example.

SBA loans can address economic effects on merchants, service firms, tourism, equipment providers, processors, and other enterprises connected to the affected agricultural economy.

Ordinary agricultural producers, farmers, and ranchers generally obtain disaster credit through Agriculture Department programs rather than SBA EIDL.

Economic-Injury Events without Direct Property Damage

The regulations recognise that a disaster can cause severe economic injury without physically damaging the applicant’s property.

Examples can include:

  • drought;
  • below-average water levels affecting commerce;
  • toxic algal blooms;
  • closure or displacement of fishing waters;
  • unforeseeable contamination of food or consumer products;
  • industrial accidents;
  • riots or civil disorder;
  • terrorist attacks;
  • public-health emergencies under special legislation;
  • transportation closure;
  • utility interruption under authorised circumstances; and
  • other sudden events causing substantial economic injury.

Geographic Eligibility

The applicant must have been physically located in the declared area when the disaster occurred.

Eligibility can extend from primary counties to designated contiguous counties under the declaration rules.

An owner’s residence, customer location, or supplier location does not ordinarily substitute for the business’s own eligible location.

A business with several locations may qualify based on injury to eligible operations, but SBA evaluates affiliates, consolidated resources, and the specific business concern.

Voluntary relocation outside the disaster area can affect eligibility or servicing, although compelling disaster-related relocation may receive different treatment.

Substantial Economic Injury

Substantial economic injury exists when the applicant cannot meet obligations as they mature or pay ordinary and necessary operating expenses.

SBA examines whether the disaster created a genuine working-capital shortfall rather than a temporary decline that the business can absorb.

Lost expected profits, lower sales, market uncertainty, or reduced growth do not alone establish the regulatory injury.

The applicant must show that the disaster directly caused the inability to pay eligible obligations.

A business already insolvent or unable to operate before the disaster may not qualify merely because the disaster worsened an existing condition.

Direct Causation

SBA distinguishes disaster-caused losses from ordinary business risk.

Evidence can include:

  • pre- and post-disaster sales comparisons;
  • customer cancellations linked to the event;
  • road, facility, port, fishing-area, or government closure;
  • supply interruption;
  • employee displacement;
  • loss of utilities;
  • tourism or visitor interruption;
  • damage to the surrounding commercial area;
  • contracts delayed or cancelled because of the disaster;
  • business-interruption insurance records; and
  • cash-flow projections isolating the disaster effect.

General economic weakness, poor management, competition, or a decline beginning before the event may reduce or eliminate eligibility.

Eligible Business Applicants

The principal applicant is a small business concern that operated in the declared area when the disaster commenced.

Eligible legal forms can include sole proprietorships, partnerships, corporations, limited-liability companies, cooperatives, and other lawful business entities.

The applicant must meet applicable size standards with its affiliates.

SBA examines ownership, business activity, location, formation date, operating history, federal obligations, and the conduct of principals.

Private Nonprofit Organisations

Most nongovernmental private nonprofits may apply for EIDL.

Applicants can include educational, charitable, cultural, community-service, health, social-service, membership, and faith-based organisations.

The applicant generally provides an Internal Revenue Service determination or satisfactory state evidence of nonprofit status.

Current disaster notices commonly state that faith-based organisations can apply, but regulatory restrictions remain for organisations principally engaged in teaching, instructing, counselling, or indoctrinating religious beliefs.

SBA evaluates the legal entity, activities, operating expenses, revenue loss, available resources, and repayment capacity.

Agricultural Exclusions and Exceptions

SBA disaster credit generally excludes agricultural enterprises primarily producing food, fibre, livestock, or other farm products.

Traditional EIDL nevertheless includes:

  • small agricultural cooperatives;
  • producer cooperatives;
  • small aquaculture enterprises; and
  • qualifying nurseries affected by a designated drought.

A business entity with both agricultural and nonagricultural divisions may receive assistance only for the eligible operations.

Farmers and ranchers should distinguish SBA EIDL from Agriculture Department emergency, operating, and disaster programs.

Small-Business Size Standards

The applicant ordinarily must have qualified as small when the declared disaster began.

SBA uses revenue or employee standards associated with the applicant’s industry and counts affiliates under Part 121.

Special disaster size provisions may apply to certain accommodation, entertainment, education, transportation, publishing, rental, retail, and service businesses with limited locations.

A size decline is appealed through SBA’s Office of Hearings and Appeals procedures rather than ordinary disaster-loan reconsideration.

Affiliation and Corporate Groups

SBA may combine related businesses when determining size, available resources, disaster injury, loan limits, and repayment capacity.

Affiliation can arise through:

  • common ownership;
  • common management;
  • identity of interest;
  • family relationships;
  • economic dependence;
  • options or conversion rights;
  • contractual control;
  • merger agreements;
  • franchises; and
  • other power to control.

The ordinary $2 million disaster-business cap applies to the borrower together with affiliates for the same disaster.

COVID-19 EIDL later imposed a separate $10 million aggregate cap on entities within one majority-owned corporate group.

Credit Elsewhere

Traditional EIDL is designed for applicants that cannot obtain adequate financing from nongovernment sources on reasonable terms.

SBA can consider commercial credit availability, liquidity, credit score, collateral, income, cash flow, affiliated resources, and the financial capacity of principal owners.

The test does not require an applicant to obtain unaffordable, predatory, or impractical financing.

It does require SBA to avoid substituting subsidised federal credit for private financing that is reasonably available.

The COVID-19 program temporarily modified or waived portions of the ordinary credit-elsewhere framework under pandemic legislation.

Reasonably Available Business and Owner Funds

SBA may require the applicant, affiliates, and principal owners with at least 20 percent ownership to use reasonably available funds before receiving EIDL.

The purpose is to reserve federal disaster credit for genuine unmet need.

SBA considers liquidity, personal and business assets, operating requirements, taxes, family needs, retirement assets, other obligations, and whether using the funds would create undue hardship.

The rule does not necessarily require liquidation of every asset or destruction of the business’s remaining operating capacity.

Ability to Repay

A disaster loan must provide reasonable assurance of repayment.

SBA considers historical operations, tax returns, debt schedules, credit, projected recovery, management, cash flow, collateral, and the proposed loan payment.

SBA cannot approve a loan where repayment depends principally on foreclosing and selling collateral.

A business can demonstrate substantial economic injury yet still be declined if it cannot reasonably repay additional debt.

The thirty-year maximum allows SBA to reduce the scheduled payment for borrowers without credit elsewhere, but maturity is tailored to the borrower’s condition.

Character, Eligibility, and Federal Obligations

SBA evaluates satisfactory character and legal eligibility.

Fraud, false statements, delinquent federal obligations, debarment, illegal activity, certain criminal conduct, and failure to pay child support or taxes can affect eligibility under applicable law and policy.

Current rules and forms determine what criminal-history, citizenship, ownership, and federal-debt information is required.

A borrower must remain truthful throughout application, closing, disbursement, servicing, and audit.

Commonly Ineligible Businesses

Traditional EIDL excludes or restricts businesses:

  • engaged in lending;
  • engaged in speculation or investment, with a limited rental-property exception;
  • using multilevel sales distribution;
  • deriving more than one-third of annual revenue from legal gambling;
  • earning more than one-third of revenue from packaging SBA loans;
  • principally engaged in religious instruction or indoctrination;
  • primarily engaged in political or lobbying activity;
  • engaged in illegal activity;
  • government owned, except for qualifying Tribal ownership;
  • associated with obscene products or services under statutory standards;
  • outside the applicable size standard; or
  • otherwise excluded by section 7(b), Part 123, or the declaration.

Working-Capital Purpose

EIDL replaces working capital the applicant would have had if the disaster had not occurred.

It is not intended to leave the business in a better competitive or financial position than before the event.

SBA estimates the injury period and calculates an eligible shortfall based on financial records, available cash, other recoveries, owner resources, and expected resumption of operations.

The loan may be disbursed in one or more instalments subject to conditions and continuing need.

Permitted Uses

EIDL proceeds may be used for eligible ordinary and necessary expenses such as:

  • payroll and payroll taxes;
  • employee health and other benefits;
  • rent or lease payments;
  • utilities;
  • accounts payable;
  • ordinary inventory and supply needs;
  • insurance;
  • fixed debt payments where authorised;
  • essential operating contracts;
  • necessary owner compensation for actual services;
  • other expenses required to carry the organisation through recovery; and
  • specific expenditures approved in the loan authorisation.

The loan authorisation and current SBA guidance control the borrower’s actual use.

Prohibited Uses

Traditional EIDL generally may not be used to:

  • expand facilities or operations;
  • purchase unrelated new fixed assets;
  • repair or replace physical disaster damage;
  • refinance pre-disaster debt;
  • repay federal loans or SBIC obligations except where a special rule applies;
  • pay dividends or distributions to owners;
  • repay owner or shareholder loans;
  • pay excessive owner compensation;
  • pay fines, criminal penalties, or fraud-related tax penalties;
  • finance a change in ownership;
  • relocate voluntarily outside the disaster area;
  • fund speculative investment; or
  • provide working capital beyond the injury the business could have covered absent the disaster.

Relationship to Business Physical Disaster Loans

A Business Physical Disaster Loan repairs or replaces disaster-damaged real estate, machinery, equipment, inventory, and other business property.

EIDL covers working capital and operating expenses.

A business can apply for both if it suffered physical and economic loss.

The combined ordinary maximum for the borrower and affiliates is $2 million.

Insurance and other compensation reduce physical-loss eligibility, while available cash and recoveries also affect the economic-injury calculation.

Loan Amount Determination

SBA does not automatically approve the statutory maximum.

The loan amount is limited to the verified economic injury and necessary working capital.

SBA can consider:

  • historical financial statements;
  • tax returns;
  • monthly sales and expenses;
  • accounts receivable and payable;
  • inventory;
  • cash and liquid assets;
  • affiliate and owner resources;
  • insurance and business-interruption proceeds;
  • grants and other recoveries;
  • existing debt;
  • the expected recovery period;
  • seasonality;
  • repayment capacity; and
  • the combined disaster-loan cap.

Two-Million-Dollar Limit

Physical and economic-injury disaster loans to the same borrower and affiliates generally cannot exceed the lesser of uncompensated loss and injury or $2 million.

The limit applies per disaster rather than as a lifetime maximum.

The SBA Administrator can increase the cap for an individual declaration based on regional economic indicators and must publish the increase.

COVID-19 EIDL eventually used the $2 million individual-borrower cap while imposing a $10 million aggregate cap for one majority-owned corporate group.

Major Source of Employment Waiver

SBA may waive the $2 million cap for a business that is a major source of employment.

Regulatory tests consider whether the business employs a significant share of a local workforce or industry or employs at least 250 people in the disaster area.

The business must be closed or in imminent danger of closing because of the disaster.

More than $2 million must be necessary to preserve employment, and the business, affiliates, and principal owners must use available funds and credit elsewhere.

A waiver is discretionary and intended for exceptional employment consequences.

Interest Rates

Traditional EIDL bears a fixed rate of 4 percent per year or less.

SBA announces the exact rate with each declaration.

Private nonprofit rates can differ from business rates and have historically been lower.

During 2026, many SBA declarations announced rates of 4 percent for businesses and 3.625 percent for private nonprofits.

The fixed rate applies for the life of the loan unless a lawful modification changes the obligation.

Repayment Terms

The maximum term is thirty years.

SBA chooses the maturity based on the borrower’s ability to repay.

Current policy provides twelve months after the first disbursement before the first payment and no interest accrual during that initial period.

Payments are ordinarily monthly principal and interest.

SBA may consider seasonal or fluctuating income when structuring payments.

There is no penalty for prepayment.

Additional Payments Based on Earnings

Part 123 permits SBA to require an additional payment based on a percentage of net earnings for certain large disaster business physical and economic-injury loans.

The additional amount reduces principal and does not begin until five years after repayment starts.

The requirement appears in the loan documents where applicable and should not be assumed for every EIDL.

Fees and Closing Costs

SBA does not charge points, loan-origination fees, closing fees, or servicing fees for section 7(b) disaster loans.

Borrowers can remain responsible for third-party expenses including:

  • recording fees;
  • lien searches;
  • title insurance;
  • appraisals where required;
  • legal or organisational documents;
  • hazard or flood insurance; and
  • other external closing costs.

Loan packagers and private advisers are not SBA employees. Their fees and representations should be reviewed carefully.

Collateral

SBA generally does not require collateral for an EIDL of $50,000 or less.

Above that threshold, the borrower must pledge available collateral determined by SBA.

Collateral can include business assets, receivables, inventory, equipment, and real estate.

SBA commonly takes a broad security interest in business assets.

Real estate is preferred where available and appropriate.

SBA aggregates economic-injury loans associated with the same disaster when applying the unsecured threshold.

Insufficient Collateral

EIDL is not an asset-based loan in which collateral liquidation is the expected source of repayment.

SBA generally will not decline solely because the applicant lacks a particular collateral value if repayment is reasonably assured.

The borrower must nevertheless pledge collateral that is available.

Refusal to provide an available lien or security interest can cause decline, cancellation, or default.

Personal Guaranties

SBA may require personal guaranties from owners according to the loan amount, ownership percentage, legal authority, and current disaster-loan policy.

A guaranty creates personal liability independent of the business entity.

COVID-19 EIDL used special statutory and administrative guaranty thresholds that changed during the pandemic.

Applicants should rely on the actual loan authorisation and guaranty documents rather than general descriptions.

Insurance and Other Recoveries

SBA coordinates disaster credit with insurance, grants, legal claims, gifts, condemnation awards, and other compensation.

Business-interruption insurance can reduce the economic-injury need.

Duplicate federal benefits are prohibited.

Borrowers must disclose recoveries received after approval and may be required to apply them to the loan or eligible expenses.

Fraudulent concealment of insurance or grant proceeds can create default and civil or criminal liability.

Application Process

  1. Confirm the declaration. The applicant verifies that its location and organisation type are eligible.
  2. Identify the economic injury. The business documents obligations and expenses it cannot meet because of the disaster.
  3. Apply through SBA. The applicant uses the federal disaster-loan portal or another announced application method.
  4. Submit financial information. SBA may request tax transcripts, returns, debt schedules, sales records, financial statements, ownership, affiliates, and authorisations.
  5. Complete identity and fraud checks. SBA verifies applicants, owners, banking information, federal debts, and other records.
  6. Undergo credit analysis. SBA determines repayment ability, credit elsewhere, character, size, injury, and eligible uses.
  7. Receive approval or decline. A written decision states the approved amount and conditions or the reason for denial.
  8. Execute closing documents. Borrowers sign the note, authorisation, security instruments, guaranties, and certifications.
  9. Receive disbursement. SBA transfers funds subject to conditions and possible instalments.
  10. Use and document proceeds. The borrower maintains records and seeks SBA approval before unauthorised changes.

Common Application Documents

  • Federal disaster-loan application.
  • Tax-information authorisation.
  • Business and personal tax returns or transcripts.
  • Profit-and-loss statements.
  • Balance sheets.
  • Monthly sales figures.
  • Schedule of liabilities.
  • Ownership and affiliate information.
  • Personal financial statements where required.
  • Formation and governance records.
  • Lease or location evidence.
  • Insurance and recovery information.
  • Bank-account verification.
  • Nonprofit determination documents.
  • Economic-injury explanation and projections.
  • Other documents requested by SBA.

Requirements can vary according to loan amount, applicant type, disaster, risk, and available electronic verification.

Application Deadlines

Every declaration states a deadline for economic-injury applications.

EIDL deadlines are generally later than deadlines for property-damage loans because economic injury develops over time.

SBA may amend a declaration and extend a deadline.

Recent public notices have stated that SBA will accept applications during an additional sixty-day grace period after certain announced deadlines.

The regulation permits late filing when substantial causes beyond the applicant’s control explain the delay.

Applicants should not rely on an assumed grace period and should file by the published date whenever possible.

Approval and Loan Authorisation

Approval states the maximum loan amount, interest rate, maturity, payment schedule, collateral, guaranties, permitted uses, insurance obligations, disbursement conditions, and reporting requirements.

The borrower can accept less than the approved amount.

An approval is not final funding until closing conditions are completed and SBA disburses the proceeds.

Material misrepresentation discovered before or after closing can lead to cancellation, acceleration, recovery, or prosecution.

Reconsideration and Appeal

SBA must provide written reasons when it declines an EIDL application.

An applicant declined for a reason other than size may submit significant new information addressing the stated grounds.

The reconsideration request must generally be received within six months of the decline.

After a second decline, the applicant may appeal in writing to the Director of the Disaster Assistance Processing and Disbursement Center or a designee.

The appeal must generally arrive within thirty days and explain specifically why the decision should be reversed.

A size decline follows Part 121 appeal procedures.

Loan-Increase Requests

A borrower may ask SBA to increase an economic-injury loan when new events beyond the borrower’s control make additional funds essential to continued operation.

Examples include unexpected recovery delays or extended interruption after original approval.

SBA reassesses verified need, loan limits, credit, repayment, collateral, other recoveries, and the remaining disaster period.

The request is not an opportunity to finance expansion or correct a pre-disaster capital shortage.

Disbursement

SBA may disburse approved funds in one payment or in instalments.

Conditions can include execution of security documents, verification of insurance, payment instructions, bank-account validation, entity resolutions, and evidence of continued need.

Undisbursed funds can be cancelled if the borrower no longer qualifies, refuses conditions, misrepresents information, or no longer needs the proceeds.

The borrower should not commit unauthorised uses based only on an approval notice.

Books, Records, and Audit

Borrowers must retain complete records of transactions financed with loan proceeds for three years after final disbursement.

Business physical and economic-injury borrowers must maintain current and accurate books, financial statements, insurance policies, and tax returns.

Applicable records must also be retained for the period extending to three years after maturity or until full payment, as specified by the regulation.

SBA and authorised government personnel may inspect, audit, reproduce records, and appraise assets.

Loan Servicing

Servicing continues throughout the loan term.

Borrowers may need SBA approval for:

  • change in ownership;
  • assumption of the debt;
  • business relocation;
  • subordination of SBA liens;
  • release or substitution of collateral;
  • sale or trade of secured equipment;
  • additional senior financing;
  • release of a guarantor;
  • business closure or liquidation;
  • payment modification; and
  • other actions affecting repayment or security.

Completing a private transaction without required SBA approval can create default.

Payment Difficulty

Borrowers experiencing temporary difficulty should contact SBA before falling seriously delinquent.

Available relief depends on the loan type, current servicing policy, financial condition, collateral, and whether the business remains operating.

COVID-19 EIDL borrowers currently may seek a six-month payment reduction of 50 percent when eligible, generally once every five years.

Interest continues to accrue during that COVID-19 payment-assistance period and can produce a larger final payment.

Traditional disaster-loan servicing options can differ and are not guaranteed.

Delinquency and Default

Failure to make payments or comply with loan covenants can place the account in default.

Consequences can include:

  • late charges or accrued interest;
  • acceleration of the full debt;
  • liquidation of collateral;
  • enforcement against guarantors;
  • administrative offset;
  • federal salary or benefit offset where lawful;
  • referral to Treasury cross-servicing;
  • collection fees and penalties;
  • credit reporting;
  • litigation;
  • compromise or write-off under federal claims rules; and
  • future federal-credit consequences.

Treasury Offset and Cross-Servicing

Delinquent federal disaster loans can be referred to the Treasury Department.

Treasury’s Offset Program may collect eligible debts from federal payments.

Cross-servicing uses federal and private collection tools after SBA transfers an eligible delinquent account.

Once transferred, SBA may have limited authority to provide ordinary servicing assistance.

Current COVID-19 EIDL guidance warns that loans can be referred for offset after 120 days of delinquency and transferred to cross-servicing when eligibility conditions are met.

Bankruptcy

EIDL debt may be addressed in a business or personal bankruptcy depending on the borrower, guaranties, collateral, and chapter.

Federal law can make debt nondischargeable when obtained by fraud, false pretence, false statement, embezzlement, larceny, or wrongful misapplication.

SBA retains liens unless they are released, avoided, satisfied, or otherwise treated under applicable law.

Bankruptcy automatically changes collection and servicing procedures and requires coordination with SBA, Treasury, the Justice Department, and the bankruptcy court.

Wrongful Misapplication of Proceeds

Willful use of EIDL proceeds contrary to the loan authorisation is wrongful misapplication.

Failure to use disbursed proceeds for authorised purposes for sixty days or more can also be treated as misapplication.

A borrower found responsible can owe one and one-half times the amount disbursed as of the date SBA learns of the misuse.

SBA can cancel undisbursed funds, accelerate the debt, collect the civil penalty, foreclose collateral, and refer the matter for administrative, civil, or criminal enforcement.

False Statements and Fraud

Fraud schemes can include:

  • invented businesses;
  • stolen identities;
  • fabricated tax records;
  • false employees or payroll;
  • inflated revenue or cost of goods;
  • false disaster location;
  • concealed affiliates;
  • duplicate applications;
  • fraudulent bank accounts;
  • undisclosed insurance or grants;
  • owner distributions or luxury purchases;
  • money laundering;
  • bribery or insider assistance;
  • false nonprofit status;
  • shell companies; and
  • misuse of proceeds after disbursement.

Potential consequences include wire fraud, bank fraud, false statements, money laundering, identity theft, False Claims Act liability, restitution, forfeiture, imprisonment, and federal-program exclusion.

Military Reservist Economic Injury Disaster Loan

MREIDL assists a small business that suffers substantial economic injury because an essential employee is ordered to active military service.

Congress created the program through the Veterans Entrepreneurship and Small Business Development Act of 1999.

No geographic disaster declaration is required.

The employee must be critical to the business’s daily operations and generally must be called to active service for more than thirty consecutive days.

The filing period begins when the employee receives notice of expected call-up and ends one year after discharge or release.

MREIDL Eligibility and Terms

MREIDL provides the working capital the business could have met had the essential employee remained.

The ordinary maximum is $2 million, with a major-source-of-employment waiver available.

The fixed interest rate is 4 percent or less, published quarterly.

Terms can extend to thirty years.

Collateral is generally required above $50,000, but a loan is not automatically declined solely for insufficient collateral.

SBA ordinarily disburses MREIDL in quarterly instalments after reviewing continuing need.

COVID-19 Economic Injury Disaster Loan Program

The COVID-19 pandemic produced the largest use of EIDL in SBA history.

The CARES Act and later laws expanded eligibility, created emergency advances, modified ordinary rules, and authorised a nationwide economic-injury response.

SBA accepted COVID-19 EIDL applications beginning in March 2020.

Unlike Paycheck Protection Program loans, COVID-19 EIDL loans were direct repayable federal debt and were not designed for forgiveness.

The program initially used rapid self-certification and automated processing to respond to extraordinary demand.

COVID-19 EIDL Terms and Expansion

COVID-19 EIDL loans generally carried fixed rates of 3.75 percent for businesses and 2.75 percent for private nonprofits.

Maturities extended to thirty years.

SBA initially limited loan amounts administratively and later increased the maximum to $500,000 and then $2 million.

A 2021 rule allowed proceeds to make a broader range of business debt payments and prepayments, excluding specified federal and SBIC debts.

A single corporate group could receive no more than $10 million in aggregate COVID-19 EIDL loans.

Payment deferments were repeatedly extended during the pandemic, but interest accrued under the original COVID-19 terms.

EIDL Advances

The CARES Act created an emergency advance of up to $10,000 for applicants awaiting an EIDL decision.

The advance did not have to be repaid even when the loan was later denied.

SBA initially calculated the advance using the applicant’s employee count.

Later legislation created Targeted EIDL Advances and Supplemental Targeted Advances for qualifying businesses in low-income communities with specified revenue losses and employee limits.

The advances were temporary grants and no longer accept new applications.

Closure of COVID-19 EIDL Applications

SBA stopped accepting new COVID-19 EIDL loan and advance applications on January 1, 2022.

On May 6, 2022, SBA stopped processing new loan-increase and reconsideration requests because available program funds were exhausted.

The remaining portfolio consists of outstanding loans, servicing requests, payments, payment assistance, collateral actions, collections, audits, fraud investigations, and litigation.

COVID-19 EIDL Payment Assistance

SBA currently permits qualifying COVID-19 EIDL borrowers to reduce scheduled payments by 50 percent for six months.

Eligibility generally requires an active business, temporary rather than permanent financial difficulty, no active bankruptcy, and a loan that is not charged off or uncollectible.

The borrower generally can use the assistance once every five years.

Interest continues to accrue, increasing the amount due later.

Borrowers must resume full payments after the assistance period.

COVID-19 Fraud and Oversight

SBA approved and disbursed COVID-19 EIDL at unprecedented scale before the full antifraud system was in operation.

GAO reported that more than $210 billion of the eventual $385 billion portfolio had been disbursed before SBA fully implemented its four-step fraud process.

The process used automated screening, data analytics, human review, and Office of Inspector General referrals.

SBA submitted nearly three million potential-fraud referrals, but the Inspector General reported that about two million were not actionable because of missing, incorrect, or duplicate data.

SBA and its Inspector General continued working on a revised referral process in 2026.

Access for Underserved Communities

Businesses in low-income, rural, Native, immigrant, Black, Latino, and other underserved communities may have less insurance, weaker banking relationships, limited collateral, lower credit scores, and fewer financial records.

These conditions can increase disaster vulnerability and reduce approval rates.

SBA provides field outreach, disaster recovery centres, call centres, language access, resource-partner assistance, and online applications.

GAO has recommended stronger analysis of application and approval outcomes for underserved groups and more consistent outreach.

Equity analysis must distinguish lawful outreach and technical assistance from race-based loan approval standards.

Rural and Tribal Communities

Rural businesses can face long distances, weak broadband, small customer bases, agricultural dependence, bank closures, and limited professional assistance.

Tribal communities can face geographic isolation, infrastructure deficiencies, land-status complications, and limited access to mainstream credit.

Current regulations allow rural disaster declarations and requests by Tribal chief executives under specified conditions.

Tribal-owned businesses are not excluded merely because they are government owned.

SBA’s field response and communication can determine whether eligible applicants learn of the program before deadlines expire.

Program Benefits

  • Provides working capital without requiring physical property damage.
  • Offers long maturities and fixed subsidised interest rates.
  • Can preserve payroll, suppliers, rent, and essential services.
  • Serves businesses unable to obtain reasonable private credit.
  • Includes most private nonprofit organisations.
  • Supports drought-affected cooperatives, aquaculture enterprises, and nurseries.
  • Can operate in contiguous counties affected economically by a disaster.
  • Can be combined with a physical-disaster business loan.
  • Does not require an applicant to have adequate collateral when repayment is otherwise assured.
  • Charges no SBA points, closing, or servicing fees.
  • Provides reconsideration and appeal rights.
  • Includes a major-employer exception to protect local jobs.
  • Provides a separate military-reservist working-capital program.
  • Created a national infrastructure used rapidly during the pandemic.

Limitations and Failures

  • EIDL is debt and must generally be repaid.
  • A business can be injured yet fail the ability-to-repay test.
  • The credit-elsewhere and owner-resource tests can be burdensome.
  • The ordinary $2 million cap may be insufficient for large employers.
  • A loan cannot replace all lost revenue or profit.
  • Expansion and most fixed-asset purchases are prohibited.
  • Applications require financial and tax documentation during a crisis.
  • Deadlines can expire before the full injury is known.
  • Processing delays can reduce the usefulness of emergency working capital.
  • Collateral and guaranties can expose business and personal assets.
  • Long terms reduce payments but increase total interest.
  • Borrowers can become overleveraged during an uncertain recovery.
  • Traditional agricultural producers are generally excluded.
  • Online systems can disadvantage applicants with weak broadband or digital access.
  • Rapid pandemic processing created extraordinary fraud and improper-payment risk.
  • Large COVID-19 servicing and collection workloads continue years after the emergency.

Major Controversies

Loan versus Grant

Borrowers sometimes misunderstood COVID-19 EIDL loans as forgivable assistance because advances and PPP operated alongside the loan program.

Speed versus Fraud Control

Rapid emergency disbursement helped businesses but weakened verification and enabled extensive fraud.

Ability to Repay

A business most harmed by disaster may also be least able to qualify for additional debt.

Credit Elsewhere

The test preserves private lending but can delay federal assistance and produce difficult judgements concerning reasonable terms.

Collateral and Personal Exposure

Federal liens and guaranties can burden owners long after the disaster.

Program Funding

Supplemental appropriations and disaster subsidy calculations can affect whether SBA continues accepting applications.

COVID-19 Collection

Policymakers have debated hardship relief, compromise, charge-off, Treasury referrals, and treatment of very small pandemic loans.

Documented Outcomes

Traditional EIDL has provided working capital after hurricanes, floods, droughts, fires, earthquakes, terrorist attacks, industrial accidents, fishing closures, and other disasters.

The program can prevent otherwise viable businesses from closing while a community recovers.

After the September 11 attacks, SBA provided more than $1 billion in physical and economic-injury disaster loans to affected businesses.

COVID-19 EIDL disbursed approximately $385 billion, exceeding the total scale of prior SBA disasters and creating a very large long-term federal loan portfolio.

Oversight bodies documented both successful rapid assistance and severe fraud-control, communication, data-quality, and servicing challenges.

Loan approvals alone do not establish that a firm survived, retained workers, or recovered profitably.

Historical Significance

EIDL made federal disaster policy responsive to business cash-flow injury rather than property destruction alone.

The program recognised that a disaster can destroy an enterprise through interrupted commerce even when its building remains intact.

It linked disaster recovery with federal small-business credit and local employment preservation.

MREIDL extended the concept of economic disaster to the absence of an essential employee serving in the military.

COVID-19 transformed a traditionally event-specific program into one of the largest emergency business-credit programs in American history.

The pandemic experience illustrates the recurring tension among speed, access, documentation, repayment, fraud prevention, and long-term federal debt collection.

Research Cautions

  • EIDL is usually a direct SBA loan. Do not describe it as an ordinary lender-guaranteed 7(a) loan.
  • Economic injury does not require property damage. Physical damage uses a separate loan category.
  • Lower sales are not enough. The applicant must be unable to meet ordinary obligations and expenses.
  • The maximum is not automatic. SBA lends only the verified eligible need.
  • Rates are declaration specific. Four percent is the statutory maximum for traditional EIDL, not necessarily the exact rate in every declaration.
  • The initial no-interest policy is current. Historical loans may have accrued interest during deferment.
  • Traditional EIDL and COVID-19 EIDL differ. Eligibility, rates, uses, advances, deferments, and servicing rules changed during the pandemic.
  • EIDL Advances were not loans. The repayable COVID-19 EIDL principal is separate.
  • Most farmers are excluded. Cooperatives, aquaculture enterprises, and certain nurseries are exceptions.
  • Private nonprofits can qualify. Activity and organisational restrictions still apply.
  • Collateral and guaranty requirements differ. The actual loan documents control.
  • Disbursed volume is not borrower benefit. Loans create repayment obligations and can end in default.

Summary

The Economic Injury Disaster Loan Program is SBA’s direct federal working-capital program for eligible organisations harmed financially by a declared disaster.

Physical property damage is not required. The applicant must show direct disaster causation and substantial economic injury: inability to meet obligations or pay ordinary and necessary operating expenses.

Eligible applicants include qualifying small businesses, small agricultural and producer cooperatives, aquaculture enterprises, specified nurseries, and most private nonprofit organisations.

Traditional agricultural producers, farms, and ranches are generally excluded.

The applicant ordinarily must be unable to obtain reasonable credit elsewhere, use reasonably available business and principal-owner resources, satisfy size and character requirements, and demonstrate ability to repay.

Proceeds can pay working capital, payroll, benefits, rent, utilities, accounts payable, and other approved normal expenses. They generally cannot finance expansion, fixed-asset acquisition, physical repairs, owner distributions, speculative activity, or most pre-disaster debt refinancing.

The ordinary combined maximum for physical and economic-injury business loans is $2 million, with a possible waiver for a major source of employment.

Traditional EIDL rates are fixed at 4 percent or less. Terms can extend to thirty years. Current policy provides twelve months with no payment and no interest accrual after initial disbursement.

Collateral is generally not required at $50,000 or less. Above that amount, available collateral must be pledged, but insufficient collateral alone does not necessarily cause decline.

A declined applicant generally has six months to seek reconsideration and thirty days to appeal a second decline.

Borrowers must retain records, use proceeds exactly as authorised, obtain approval for significant servicing actions, and repay the federal debt. Wrongful use can produce one-and-one-half-times liability and civil or criminal enforcement.

MREIDL provides similar working capital when an essential employee is called to active military service.

COVID-19 EIDL temporarily expanded the program nationwide, created separate advances, and disbursed approximately $385 billion. It is closed to new applications but remains a major federal servicing, hardship, collection, and fraud-control portfolio.

Traditional EIDL remains active in 2026 as a central federal tool for preserving small businesses and private nonprofits during disaster recovery.
