Federal-Aid Highway Program

The continuing federal–state system through which Congress finances eligible highways, bridges, safety improvements, planning, freight facilities, resilience measures, and related surface-transportation projects.

Overview

The Federal-Aid Highway Program is the principal national framework for financing and overseeing highway and bridge improvements undertaken by state transportation departments and other eligible public agencies. Congress created the enduring federal-aid model through the Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 and refined it through the Federal Highway Act of 1921. The program expanded substantially with the Federal-Aid Highway Act and Highway Revenue Act of 1956, which accelerated construction of the Interstate Highway System and established the Highway Trust Fund.

The program is not ordinarily a direct federal road-construction service. State governments select and develop most projects, subject to federal eligibility, planning, environmental, civil-rights, procurement, design, financial-management, and maintenance requirements. The Federal Highway Administration apportions or allocates funds, authorizes eligible projects, reimburses the federal share of approved costs, and exercises stewardship and oversight.

Federal-aid highways support passenger travel, freight movement, emergency response, military mobility, interstate commerce, regional development, and access to employment and services. The same system also enabled large-scale roadbuilding that displaced residents, divided neighborhoods, intensified automobile dependence, exposed communities to pollution and noise, and reinforced existing racial and economic inequalities. These consequences are especially visible in African American neighborhoods affected by postwar urban expressway construction.

The program remains active. Its current statutory framework is contained principally in Title 23 of the United States Code. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 authorized major federal highway programs for fiscal years 2022 through 2026 and added or expanded programs involving bridges, carbon reduction, resilience, electric-vehicle infrastructure, and community reconnection.

Definitions

Federal-Aid Highway Program
The collection of federal statutory programs through which the Federal Highway Administration provides financial assistance, technical standards, approvals, and oversight for eligible surface-transportation projects undertaken by states and other public entities.
Federal-aid highway
A public highway eligible for assistance under Chapter 1 of Title 23, generally excluding a highway functionally classified as a local road or rural minor collector unless a specific statutory eligibility applies.
Federal-aid project
An approved transportation project financed in whole or in part with federal highway funds and subject to applicable federal requirements.
Authorization act
A law establishing or continuing transportation programs, setting policy, defining eligibility, and authorizing funding levels for a specified period.
Apportionment
The statutory distribution of federal funds among states according to formulas or shares established by law.
Allocation
The distribution of federal funds by administrative action, statutory direction, project selection, or another method other than ordinary formula apportionment.
Contract authority
Budget authority made available by authorization law that permits states to incur obligations in advance of annual liquidation appropriations, subject to applicable limitations.
Obligation limitation
An annual ceiling established through the federal appropriations process that controls how much contract authority may be obligated during a fiscal year.
Obligation
A federal commitment to reimburse the eligible federal share of an approved project after the project agreement or authorization is executed.
Reimbursement
Payment to a state or other recipient for the federal share of eligible project costs already incurred. The program generally provides authority and reimbursement rather than an immediate cash transfer of the full project amount.
Federal share
The percentage of eligible project costs paid with federal funds. The standard share is generally 90 percent for qualifying Interstate projects and 80 percent for most other projects, subject to exceptions and adjustments.
Nonfederal match
The state, local, private, toll, or other eligible contribution required to cover project costs not paid by the federal share.
Highway Trust Fund
The federal trust fund established in 1956 and financed principally through federal taxes associated with motor fuels and highway users. Its Highway Account finances most federal highway obligations.
State department of transportation
The state-level public agency principally responsible for statewide transportation planning, project selection, design, construction, maintenance, and administration of federal-aid funds.
Metropolitan planning organization
A regional transportation-planning body designated for an urbanized area and responsible for developing long-range plans and transportation improvement programs through a continuing, cooperative, and comprehensive process.
Statewide Transportation Improvement Program
A fiscally constrained statewide schedule of transportation projects expected to receive federal approval or funding during a specified multiyear period.
Transportation Improvement Program
A metropolitan area’s fiscally constrained list of near-term transportation projects, developed by the metropolitan planning organization and incorporated into the statewide program.
National Highway System
A network of highways important to national mobility, economic activity, defence, and intermodal connectivity, including the Interstate System and other principal routes and connectors.
Interstate Highway System
The nationally integrated system of controlled-access highways formally known as the Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways.
Functional classification
The grouping of roads according to the character of service they provide, such as arterial, collector, or local access.
Stewardship and oversight
The division of responsibility between FHWA and recipients for ensuring that federal-aid projects comply with law, safeguard federal funds, meet standards, and achieve program objectives.
Project agreement
The formal federal-state agreement or electronic authorization establishing project scope, eligible costs, funding participation, and the federal obligation.

Historical Development

Good Roads Movement and Federal Entry

Before the twentieth century, road construction and maintenance were principally state and local responsibilities. The Good Roads Movement, rural free delivery, automobile ownership, agricultural commerce, and demands for more reliable interstate travel increased pressure for a national role. Early federal road offices collected information and demonstrated construction methods, but Congress remained divided over direct national roadbuilding.

The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 resolved the immediate institutional question through a partnership model. Federal funds would assist projects selected and administered through state highway departments rather than create a general federal road service. States were required to possess or establish capable highway agencies, helping professionalize road administration across the country.

Creation of a Connected Federal-Aid System

The Federal Highway Act of 1921 limited federal assistance to designated systems representing a portion of each state’s road mileage. This concentrated resources on routes considered nationally or regionally significant and strengthened federal review of engineering, planning, and financial administration.

During the 1920s and 1930s, federal-aid roads supported rural market access, intercity travel, employment, and state highway-system development. Depression-era relief and public-works programs supplied additional road funding and labour, while federal law gradually permitted greater investment in urban extensions and grade separations.

Interstate Planning and Construction

Congress authorized an Interstate system in 1944, and initial routes were designated in 1947. The early authorization did not provide sufficient dedicated funding for rapid construction. President Dwight D. Eisenhower later made a modern national highway network a major administration objective, emphasizing mobility, safety, commerce, defence, and evacuation.

The 1956 legislation provided the decisive financial structure. The federal government would pay most eligible Interstate construction costs, while dedicated highway-user taxes flowed into the Highway Trust Fund. The resulting program constructed a national controlled-access network and financed connecting urban expressways, bridges, interchanges, and approaches.

Community Conflict and Regulatory Reform

Early Interstate and expressway planning often treated dense urban neighborhoods as obstacles to through movement. State and local agencies acquired property and selected routes before the modern environmental-review, civil-rights, preservation, relocation, and public-participation framework had fully developed.

Opposition to highway projects contributed to new legal requirements. Congress required metropolitan planning, strengthened public hearings, protected parks and historic sites, expanded relocation assistance, and permitted limited transit substitution. NEPA required federal agencies to study environmental consequences and alternatives. Courts enforced procedural duties and reviewed agency decisions.

Post-Interstate Program

As the original Interstate construction program approached completion, federal policy shifted toward system preservation, bridge repair, highway safety, congestion management, metropolitan planning, air quality, freight, and multimodal flexibility. ISTEA in 1991 marked a major transition from categorical road-system construction toward integrated surface-transportation planning and performance.

Later authorization laws continued this evolution. MAP-21 emphasized national performance goals and consolidated formula programs. The FAST Act added a national freight formula program. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act expanded funding and established new priorities involving resilience, emissions reduction, bridges, charging infrastructure, and repair of community divisions caused by earlier transportation facilities.

Program Structure and Administration

Congress

Congress establishes program authority, eligible uses, formula structures, federal shares, policy objectives, funding levels, and compliance requirements. Multiyear surface-transportation authorization laws provide the main program framework, while annual appropriations legislation controls obligation limitations, liquidating cash, administrative funding, and specified supplemental programs.

Federal Highway Administration

FHWA administers federal highway laws through headquarters offices, a division office in each state, and specialized program units. Its responsibilities include:

  • Distributing formula and allocated funds
  • Interpreting and implementing federal statutes and regulations
  • Reviewing program and project eligibility
  • Authorizing federal obligations
  • Reimbursing eligible costs
  • Overseeing financial management and internal controls
  • Monitoring civil-rights, environmental, labour, procurement, and safety requirements
  • Approving specified Interstate and major-project actions
  • Providing engineering, planning, research, and technical assistance
  • Conducting risk-based stewardship and oversight

State Departments of Transportation

State transportation departments control most day-to-day project development. They plan statewide systems, select projects, coordinate with regional and local governments, conduct environmental studies, acquire rights-of-way, prepare designs, award contracts, inspect construction, maintain records, request reimbursement, and preserve completed facilities.

Metropolitan Planning Organizations

In metropolitan areas, projects must emerge from a regional planning process. Metropolitan planning organizations prepare long-range transportation plans and near-term transportation improvement programs, evaluate fiscal constraint, coordinate with transit operators and state agencies, and provide a forum for local and public participation.

Local Public Agencies

Counties, cities, towns, public authorities, and other local entities may sponsor eligible projects. When a local agency receives federal-aid funds through a state, the state remains responsible for ensuring that the project satisfies federal requirements.

Federal Lands and Tribal Programs

Separate but related Title 23 programs support transportation facilities serving national parks, forests, wildlife refuges, tribal lands, federal lands access routes, and other public lands. Administration may involve FHWA, federal land-management agencies, tribal governments, states, and local governments.

How the Program Works

1. Federal Authorization

Congress enacts a surface-transportation law establishing programs and funding authority. Statutory formulas determine each state’s share of most apportioned funds, while other money is distributed through competitive grants, statutory designations, administrative allocations, or special-purpose formulas.

2. Apportionment and Obligation Authority

FHWA notifies states of available contract authority and program balances. Annual appropriations legislation generally limits how much of that authority may be obligated during the fiscal year. A state therefore may possess authorized balances that cannot all be committed immediately.

3. Statewide and Metropolitan Planning

Proposed projects are evaluated through statewide and, where applicable, metropolitan planning. Federally significant projects ordinarily must appear in an approved long-range plan, metropolitan transportation improvement program, and statewide transportation improvement program before federal authorization.

4. Project Development

The project sponsor defines purpose and need, develops alternatives, completes preliminary engineering, consults affected agencies, conducts environmental review, considers civil-rights and community effects, secures permits, evaluates historic resources, and develops cost estimates and financing.

5. Right-of-Way and Relocation

When property is required, the responsible agency follows federal and state acquisition procedures. Federally assisted acquisitions and displacement may trigger appraisal, negotiation, just-compensation, advisory-service, moving-expense, replacement-housing, and other relocation requirements.

6. Federal Authorization and Obligation

Before federally reimbursable work begins, FHWA or a state exercising delegated authority approves the project and records a federal obligation. Costs incurred before required authorization may be ineligible unless a statutory or regulatory exception applies.

7. Procurement and Construction

States or local sponsors advertise, award, and administer construction contracts under applicable competitive-bidding, disadvantaged-business, labour, domestic-content, safety, and quality-control requirements. Some work may be performed through alternative contracting or other authorized delivery methods.

8. Reimbursement

The recipient pays eligible project costs and submits reimbursement requests. FHWA then pays the federal share from the applicable account. Reimbursement may occur throughout construction rather than only after final completion.

9. Project Closeout and Continuing Responsibility

After completion, the responsible agency closes the financial records, resolves audit findings, maintains the facility, preserves its public transportation purpose, and complies with restrictions on disposal or change in use. Major federal investments may remain subject to continuing federal interests.

Funding and Financing

Highway Trust Fund

The Highway Trust Fund has served as the central financing mechanism for federal highway assistance since 1956. Federal taxes associated with gasoline, diesel fuel, heavy vehicles, tyres, and truck use have historically supplied its principal revenue. Congress has also transferred general revenues to maintain the fund’s ability to meet authorized obligations.

Contract Authority and Liquidation

Most core highway programs receive contract authority through authorization law. This permits federal obligations without waiting for a separate annual appropriation of budget authority for each project. Congress nevertheless provides annual liquidating cash and usually imposes an obligation limitation controlling the pace of new commitments.

Federal Matching Share

The standard federal share is generally 90 percent for qualifying projects on the Interstate System and 80 percent for most other federal-aid projects. Statutory provisions may increase, reduce, or eliminate the nonfederal match for specified programs, states with substantial federal lands, emergency work, safety activities, tribal or federal-lands projects, and other designated purposes.

State and Local Contributions

Nonfederal contributions may come from state fuel taxes, vehicle fees, toll revenues, sales taxes, general funds, bonds, local revenues, impact fees, private contributions, or other eligible sources. The availability of matching funds can influence which projects advance and when construction begins.

Advance Construction and Debt Finance

States may begin eligible work using nonfederal resources and later convert qualifying costs to federal obligations through advance-construction procedures. Some jurisdictions also issue debt expected to be repaid with future federal-aid reimbursements, although the federal government does not guarantee that debt to investors.

Major Program Components

The Federal-Aid Highway Program is an umbrella rather than a single project category. Its components change when Congress enacts new authorization laws. Major formula and related programs under the fiscal year 2022–2026 framework include:

National Highway Performance Program
Supports the condition and performance of the National Highway System, including construction, reconstruction, preservation, resilience, and progress toward state performance targets.
Surface Transportation Block Grant Program
Provides flexible funding for highways, bridges, tunnels, pedestrian and bicycle facilities, transit capital projects, and other eligible transportation purposes.
Highway Safety Improvement Program
Funds data-driven infrastructure strategies intended to reduce fatalities and serious injuries on public roads.
Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality Improvement Program
Supports transportation projects and programs that contribute to attainment or maintenance of federal air-quality standards in eligible areas.
National Highway Freight Program
Supports freight movement on nationally significant routes and eligible intermodal or highway freight projects.
Metropolitan Planning Program
Funds the cooperative transportation-planning process conducted by states, metropolitan planning organizations, and public transportation operators.
Carbon Reduction Program
Provides formula funding for projects designed to reduce transportation-related carbon-dioxide emissions.
PROTECT Formula Program
Supports planning, resilience improvements, community resilience, evacuation routes, and protection of surface transportation from natural hazards and changing conditions.
Railway–Highway Crossings Program
Funds safety improvements intended to reduce fatalities and injuries at public railway–highway grade crossings.
Transportation Alternatives
Provides a set-aside within the surface-transportation block-grant framework for pedestrian, bicycle, recreational-trail, safe-routes, community-improvement, and related eligible projects.
Bridge Formula Program
Provides substantial formula funding for bridge replacement, rehabilitation, preservation, protection, and construction under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act.
National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Formula Program
Supports a national network of electric-vehicle charging infrastructure along designated corridors and in communities, subject to applicable program rules.

Benefits and Documented Outcomes

National Connectivity

Federal-aid investment created and preserved a connected national network linking states, metropolitan areas, rural communities, ports, airports, border crossings, military installations, industrial centres, and agricultural regions.

Freight and Economic Activity

Highways support supply chains, trucking, construction, tourism, retail distribution, emergency services, and labour-market access. Reliable bridges and corridors can reduce shipping delays and connect businesses to national and international markets.

Safety and Engineering Standards

Federal assistance helped professionalize road engineering and establish national expectations involving geometric design, bridges, traffic control, crash data, work zones, roadside safety, and system performance. Safety programs now direct funding toward locations and countermeasures supported by crash analysis.

System Preservation

Modern federal policy increasingly emphasizes maintaining existing roads and bridges rather than simply adding mileage. Pavement rehabilitation, bridge replacement, operations, asset management, and resilience account for a substantial share of present-day investment.

Emergency and Defence Functions

The network supports evacuation, disaster response, military deployment, delivery of essential goods, and restoration following floods, hurricanes, earthquakes, wildfires, landslides, and other emergencies.

Local and Regional Access

Although local roads are generally outside ordinary federal-aid eligibility, flexible programs, bridge authorities, safety programs, and local suballocations can support projects important to counties, municipalities, tribal communities, pedestrians, cyclists, and transit users.

Racial and Community Impacts

Urban Expressway Routing

Postwar expressway planning occurred within cities already divided by residential segregation, redlining, restrictive covenants, discriminatory lending, unequal municipal services, and racialized definitions of blight. State and local officials frequently selected routes through African American neighborhoods and other communities possessing limited political influence or lower appraised property values.

Federal highway assistance financed many of these projects, although route selection ordinarily involved state and local agencies. Federal approval, generous Interstate matching, and national construction priorities made large urban projects financially possible on a scale that local governments could not have sustained independently.

Displacement

Highway rights-of-way required acquisition and demolition of homes, businesses, churches, schools, and community institutions. Before modern relocation law, displaced households and businesses often received limited assistance beyond property compensation. Renters, informal occupants, and businesses dependent on neighborhood customers could experience losses not fully reflected in acquisition payments.

Neighborhood Division

Limited-access highways divided street networks, removed crossings, changed pedestrian movement, and created physical barriers between residents and employment, education, healthcare, retail, parks, and places of worship. Elevated structures, frontage roads, interchanges, and large rights-of-way could remain long after the original construction period.

Environmental Burden

Communities adjacent to major highways may experience traffic noise, air pollution, visual intrusion, crash risk, heat, stormwater effects, and loss of developable land. These burdens can persist even where the facility produces regional mobility benefits.

Suburbanization and Unequal Access

Highway construction supported suburban residential and commercial growth, but discriminatory housing and lending systems often prevented African American families from accessing the same suburban opportunities. Central-city communities therefore experienced both direct infrastructure disruption and the movement of investment, employment, and tax base toward areas from which they had been excluded.

Community Resistance

Residents, civil-rights organizations, preservationists, environmental groups, businesses, and local officials challenged highway projects in numerous cities. Some campaigns secured route changes, mitigation, transit investment, or cancellation. Others were unsuccessful but helped establish modern public-participation, environmental-review, historic-preservation, and judicial-review practices.

Repair and Reconnection

Contemporary policy includes planning grants, capital projects, highway caps, boulevard conversions, crossings, transit improvements, parks, and other strategies intended to reconnect communities divided by transportation infrastructure. These efforts raise additional questions involving resident control, affordable housing, business preservation, land value, displacement, and whether the benefits of reconnection remain with existing communities.

Environmental, Civil-Rights, and Public-Participation Requirements

National Environmental Policy Act

NEPA requires federal agencies to evaluate the environmental consequences of major federal actions and consider reasonable alternatives. Depending on significance, a federal-aid highway project may proceed through a categorical exclusion, environmental assessment, or environmental impact statement.

Title VI

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin in federally assisted programs. State and local recipients must maintain nondiscrimination programs, collect and review information, investigate complaints, and ensure that subrecipients comply with applicable requirements.

Section 4(f)

Section 4(f), codified principally at 49 U.S.C. § 303 and 23 U.S.C. § 138, protects significant publicly owned parks, recreation areas, wildlife and waterfowl refuges, and historic sites. Approval of a project using protected property generally requires a finding that no feasible and prudent avoidance alternative exists and that planning has minimized harm, subject to statutory and regulatory procedures.

Historic Preservation

Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires federal agencies to consider effects on historic properties and consult appropriate officials, tribes, organizations, and members of the public.

Air Quality

The Clean Air Act links transportation planning and project approval to state air-quality plans in nonattainment and maintenance areas. Metropolitan transportation plans and programs may be required to demonstrate conformity with applicable emissions requirements.

Relocation and Acquisition

The Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act establishes standards for fair treatment of people and businesses displaced by federally funded projects. It governs acquisition procedures, advisory services, moving payments, replacement housing, and related assistance.

Public Hearings and Participation

Federal planning and environmental rules require opportunities for public involvement. The adequacy of participation remains contested when technical complexity, meeting format, language access, timing, project momentum, or unequal institutional power limits a community’s practical influence over decisions.

Major Policy Debates

Expansion or Preservation

States must decide how much funding to devote to new lanes and corridors versus repair of existing pavements, bridges, tunnels, and interchanges. Expansion may address particular bottlenecks or growth areas, while preservation advocates argue that deferred maintenance increases long-term cost and risk.

Highways or Multimodal Investment

Modern federal highway programs permit varying levels of support for transit, walking, cycling, intermodal freight, and operations. Debate concerns whether flexible funds should remain focused on roads or be used to reduce automobile dependence and expand transportation choice.

User-Pay Financing or General Revenue

The traditional funding model links federal motor-fuel and highway-user taxes to transportation spending. Improved fuel economy, electric vehicles, inflation, and unchanged tax rates have weakened the relationship between trust-fund receipts and authorized spending, producing recurring debate over tax increases, user charges, mileage fees, general-fund transfers, and program scale.

State Discretion or Federal Direction

State officials generally seek flexibility to address local priorities. Federal requirements pursue national objectives involving safety, civil rights, environmental protection, labour standards, domestic sourcing, freight, resilience, and fiscal accountability. The balance changes with each authorization law and administration.

Mobility or Community Protection

Regional traffic movement and freight efficiency can conflict with neighborhood preservation, public health, local access, housing stability, and environmental quality. Project evaluation increasingly considers whether a transportation investment creates broad net benefits or shifts costs onto communities with limited political power.

Reconnection or Secondary Displacement

Removing, capping, or redesigning divisive highways may restore land and connectivity. Rising property values and new development can also displace existing residents and businesses unless transportation policy is coordinated with affordable housing, land control, community ownership, and anti-displacement measures.

Key Distinctions

Federal-Aid Highway Program and Interstate Highway System

The Federal-Aid Highway Program is the broad funding and administrative framework. The Interstate System is one highway network eligible for support within that framework. Federal-aid funds also support non-Interstate highways, bridges, safety projects, freight facilities, planning, bicycle and pedestrian infrastructure, and other eligible activities.

Federal-Aid Highway and Federal Lands Highway Programs

The ordinary federal-aid program primarily assists state-administered facilities. Federal Lands Highway programs address transportation facilities owned by, located on, serving, or providing access to federal and tribal lands under distinct statutory arrangements.

Federal Funding and Federal Ownership

Federal financial participation does not ordinarily make a road federal property. State and local governments generally retain ownership, operation, policing, and maintenance responsibility.

Apportionment and Appropriation

Apportionment distributes authorized program funds among states according to law. Appropriation legislation controls annual obligation limits, administrative funding, and cash used to liquidate federal obligations. The two processes are related but not identical.

Obligation and Expenditure

An obligation is the federal commitment to reimburse an approved project. An expenditure or outlay occurs when the federal government actually pays a reimbursement request after eligible costs have been incurred.

Highway Planning and Project Approval

Placement of a project in a transportation plan does not by itself constitute final environmental approval, right-of-way authorization, construction approval, or a guarantee of federal funding.

Urban Renewal and Highway Construction

Urban renewal and federal-aid highways were separate legal programs. They frequently operated in the same neighborhoods, used related blight and redevelopment objectives, shared local planning assumptions, and produced overlapping displacement and land-use change.

Geographic Scope

The Federal-Aid Highway Program operates nationally through state transportation agencies in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. Separate and related authorities support the territories, tribal transportation facilities, and roads serving or located within federal lands.

Ordinary federal-aid eligibility focuses on routes above the local-road and rural-minor-collector classifications, but Congress has created important exceptions for bridges, safety work, transportation alternatives, emergency relief, and other special purposes.

The program serves rural, suburban, metropolitan, interstate, coastal, mountain, border, and island transportation systems. Its effects therefore range from farm-to-market roads and small-town bridges to major urban freeways, international freight corridors, evacuation routes, and national defence connections.

Federal-aid investment is geographically uneven because formula shares, population, road mileage, freight activity, statutory minimums, federal lands, state priorities, project readiness, matching capacity, competitive grants, and congressional policy all influence distribution.

Legal Context

Federal Aid Road Act of 1916

The 1916 law established the durable principle of federal financial aid administered cooperatively through state highway departments. It required states to possess an organized highway agency capable of administering federal projects.

Federal Highway Act of 1921

The 1921 law concentrated federal aid on connected state highway systems, strengthened administrative standards, and established the basic architecture that governed federal highway assistance for decades.

Federal-Aid Highway Act and Highway Revenue Act of 1956

These laws accelerated Interstate construction, established the principal 90-percent Interstate federal share, expanded system mileage, created dedicated revenue, and established the Highway Trust Fund.

Title 23 of the United States Code

Title 23 contains the principal permanent highway statutes governing definitions, apportionment, federal shares, project approval, planning, construction, maintenance, civil rights, safety, bridges, federal lands, research, and other program functions.

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

Title VI prohibits race, color, and national-origin discrimination in federally assisted transportation programs and applies to state and local recipients and subrecipients.

Department of Transportation Act of 1966

The law created the Department of Transportation and included the preservation policy later known as Section 4(f), governing transportation use of certain parks, recreation areas, refuges, and historic sites.

National Environmental Policy Act of 1969

NEPA requires federal environmental review of major federal actions and has become a central component of federal-aid highway project development.

Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970

The Uniform Act establishes national standards for property acquisition and assistance to people, businesses, farms, and organizations displaced by federally assisted projects.

Clean Air Act

The Clean Air Act requires transportation conformity in specified nonattainment and maintenance areas and links federally supported transportation plans and programs to air-quality objectives.

Intermodal Surface Transportation Efficiency Act of 1991

ISTEA reorganized the post-Interstate program, expanded flexibility, strengthened metropolitan planning, supported intermodalism, and established the modern National Highway System framework.

Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021

The law authorized federal surface-transportation programs for fiscal years 2022 through 2026 and created or expanded programs for bridges, carbon reduction, resilience, electric-vehicle infrastructure, and community reconnection.

Selected Legal Cases

  • Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe (1971): Required searching judicial review of the Secretary of Transportation’s approval of a highway affecting protected parkland and clarified the force of Section 4(f).
  • Named Individual Members of the San Antonio Conservation Society v. Texas Highway Department (1971): Applied environmental and preservation requirements to a federally assisted highway project and influenced enforcement of federal project-review duties.
  • Arlington Coalition on Transportation v. Volpe (1972): Applied NEPA and Section 4(f) requirements to an Interstate project and rejected attempts to avoid review merely because planning had begun before the statutes took effect.
  • Department of Transportation v. Public Citizen (2004): Addressed the scope of federal responsibility under NEPA where the agency lacked authority to prevent certain effects attributed to broader governmental action.

Related Events

  • Enactment of the Federal Aid Road Act of 1916

    The creation of the permanent federal–state highway funding partnership.

  • Designation of the Interstate Highway System

    The 1947 designation of the first Interstate routes following congressional authorization in 1944.

  • Enactment of the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956

    The decisive federal commitment to accelerated Interstate construction and dedicated highway financing.

  • Urban Renewal Program

    A separate federal–local redevelopment system that frequently overlapped with urban expressway planning and neighborhood clearance.

  • Urban Freeway Revolts

    Local campaigns during the 1950s through the 1970s that opposed, altered, or stopped proposed expressways in numerous American cities.

  • Completion of the Original Interstate Construction Program

    The transition from building the initially planned Interstate network toward preservation, reconstruction, safety, and broader surface-transportation policy.

  • Enactment of ISTEA

    The 1991 restructuring that established the modern post-Interstate planning and funding framework.

  • Creation of the Reconnecting Communities Pilot Program

    A 2021 federal initiative supporting planning and capital projects intended to address barriers created by transportation infrastructure.

Related Concepts

  • Infrastructure

    The physical systems and facilities supporting transportation, utilities, communications, public services, and economic activity.

  • Induced Demand

    The increase in travel that may occur after roadway capacity expands or the cost and convenience of driving change.

  • Automobile Dependence

    A development pattern in which access to employment, services, and daily needs depends heavily on private motor vehicles.

  • Transportation Equity

    The distribution of transportation benefits, burdens, access, decision-making power, and public investment among populations and places.

  • Environmental Justice

    The principle that no population should bear an unfair share of environmental harms and that affected communities should participate meaningfully in decisions.

  • Eminent Domain

    Governmental authority to acquire private property for public use upon payment of just compensation.

  • Residential Segregation

    The geographic separation of population groups through law, policy, markets, discrimination, violence, or institutional practice.

  • Community Displacement

    The removal or forced movement of residents, businesses, and institutions through acquisition, demolition, rising costs, environmental conditions, or redevelopment.

  • Metropolitan Planning

    Regional coordination of transportation investments, land use, fiscal constraint, public participation, and long-term system development.

  • Public–Private Partnership

    An arrangement combining public authority or funding with private design, construction, finance, operation, or maintenance.

Summary

The Federal-Aid Highway Program is the national system through which Congress finances eligible road, bridge, safety, planning, freight, resilience, and related transportation projects administered primarily by state governments. Created in 1916 and structurally refined in 1921, it established a durable federal–state partnership rather than a general system of direct federal road ownership.

The 1956 highway and revenue acts transformed the program by creating dedicated financing and accelerating construction of the Interstate Highway System. The resulting network strengthened national mobility, commerce, engineering, emergency response, and defence, while urban expressway construction displaced residents, divided neighborhoods, and imposed disproportionate costs on many African American and low-income communities.

Modern federal highway policy extends far beyond new road construction. It includes system preservation, bridge repair, safety, metropolitan planning, civil-rights compliance, environmental review, freight, air quality, carbon reduction, resilience, multimodal projects, and efforts to repair past community divisions. The central policy challenge remains how to combine national mobility and infrastructure investment with fiscal responsibility, public participation, environmental protection, and equitable treatment of affected communities.
