Home Owners’ Loan Corporation

The New Deal federal corporation that refinanced distressed home mortgages, converted short-term and nonamortizing debts into long-term loans, prevented many foreclosures, serviced more than one million borrower accounts, managed acquired properties, and helped standardize residential appraisal and mortgage administration.

Terminology Note

The official name was the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation, commonly abbreviated HOLC. The possessive form in the name places the apostrophe after Owners.

The corporation was created under the Home Owners’ Loan Act of 1933. Historical summaries sometimes call the statute the Home Owners’ Refinancing Act because emergency refinancing was its central purpose.

HOLC’s authority to accept new applications and acquire distressed mortgages was temporary, but the corporation continued for many years to service loans, grant extensions, foreclose where necessary, repair and sell acquired properties, administer related financial investments, assist wartime housing conversion, and complete liquidation.

The term redlining came into widespread use later. HOLC’s color-coded residential security maps are now commonly called redlining maps because they classified neighbourhoods through risk standards that explicitly incorporated race, ethnicity, immigration, housing age, social class, and perceived “infiltration.”

Overview

The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation was created during the national mortgage and foreclosure crisis of the Great Depression.

Falling employment, wages, and property values left homeowners unable to meet mortgage interest, principal, taxes, assessments, and insurance obligations. Banks, building and loan associations, insurance companies, individuals, and other creditors faced mounting delinquency and declining collateral values.

Many pre-Depression mortgages were short-term, interest-only, partially amortizing, or dependent on a large balloon payment. A homeowner could make regular interest payments for years without reducing the principal substantially and then be unable to refinance when the loan matured.

HOLC intervened by acquiring distressed mortgages and replacing them with government-administered loans carrying longer repayment periods, regular amortization, lower interest in many cases, and immediate settlement of delinquent taxes and approved property charges.

The corporation assisted creditors as well as homeowners. Lenders exchanged frozen or delinquent mortgages for federal HOLC bonds or cash, improving liquidity and reducing the risk and administrative burden of widespread foreclosure.

HOLC’s mission did not end when new refinancing ceased. It became a national mortgage servicer, collection agency, loss-mitigation institution, property manager, repair administrator, landlord, real-estate seller, bond issuer, and liquidating corporation.

Its legacy is divided. The corporation saved many owner-occupied homes and established important mortgage standards, but nearly one-fifth of borrowers ultimately lost their property. Its appraisal and neighbourhood-survey systems also formalised racial and ethnic assumptions that treated Black residence and demographic change as threats to real-estate value.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because the central issue is HOLC’s operation: eligibility, applications, bond exchanges, loan underwriting, appraisals, amortization, servicing, forbearance, foreclosure, repair, property management, resale, City Survey work, wartime housing conversion, and liquidation.

The Home Owners’ Loan Act of 1933 and later amendments belong primarily under Laws and Legislation.

The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation may also be cross-referenced under Institutions because it was a chartered federal corporation with its own assets, liabilities, employees, offices, bonds, contracts, and continuing legal identity.

Residential security mapping and redlining belong under Concepts and Terminology. Their relationship to HOLC should be explained here without treating every form of mortgage discrimination as the product of one agency.

HOLC must be distinguished from the Federal Housing Administration. HOLC directly acquired and refinanced distressed existing mortgages for a limited emergency period. The FHA insured qualifying private mortgages and influenced future lending and construction on a continuing basis.

Historical Context

Home construction and ownership expanded during the 1920s, but the mortgage market lacked the standardized long-term financing familiar in later decades.

Mortgages frequently matured within three to five years. Many required interest payments with little principal reduction and depended on repeated refinancing.

Local building and loan associations offered amortizing loans in many communities, but commercial banks, insurance companies, mortgage companies, and private individuals also supplied credit under varying terms.

The Great Depression disrupted this system. Unemployment and wage cuts weakened household repayment. Bank failures and depositor withdrawals reduced available credit. Property values fell, making refinancing difficult even where a borrower had paid reliably before the crisis.

State foreclosure moratoriums and local relief measures postponed some sales but did not restore a functioning national refinancing market.

Congress created the Federal Home Loan Bank System in 1932 to supply liquidity to member home-financing institutions. Its initial direct-loan provision proved administratively inadequate for the scale of homeowner distress.

The Roosevelt administration therefore proposed a separate government corporation capable of acquiring distressed debts directly and restructuring them on a national scale.

Program Purpose

Prevent Avoidable Foreclosure

HOLC sought to preserve owner occupancy where the borrower had a reasonable prospect of meeting modified obligations.

Replace Unmanageable Mortgage Terms

Short maturities, balloon payments, unpaid taxes, accumulated interest, and multiple liens were consolidated into one longer-term first mortgage.

Restore Mortgage-Market Liquidity

Creditors exchanged distressed claims for federally backed bonds or approved cash payments.

Stabilise Property Values

Avoiding mass foreclosure and fire-sale liquidation was intended to prevent additional neighbourhood price collapse.

Standardise Mortgage Administration

The corporation developed national procedures for appraisal, title examination, loan closing, amortization, servicing, delinquency management, repair, and property disposition.

Protect the Federal Investment

Where a borrower could not recover, HOLC foreclosed, acquired the property, repaired it when appropriate, rented it, and sold it in an orderly manner.

Support the Broader Housing-Finance System

HOLC capitalised or invested in federal savings-and-loan and mortgage-insurance institutions and supplied technical and appraisal knowledge to other agencies.

Home Owners’ Loan Act of 1933

President Roosevelt signed the Home Owners’ Loan Act on June 13, 1933.

The act:

  • directed the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to charter HOLC;
  • declared the corporation an instrumentality of the United States;
  • authorised $200 million in Treasury-owned capital stock;
  • authorised issuance of federal-backed HOLC bonds;
  • allowed acquisition of qualifying distressed mortgages and liens;
  • authorised bond exchange and specified cash advances;
  • limited eligible dwellings to no more than four families;
  • required owner occupancy or homestead status;
  • limited property value and loan amount;
  • required first-lien security;
  • established amortization and interest rules;
  • authorised federal savings and loan associations;
  • allowed repair, rehabilitation, tax, and assessment advances; and
  • provided a temporary period for emergency refinancing.

Congress amended the law repeatedly to guarantee bond principal, expand bond authority, adjust application deadlines, permit additional repair advances, support related housing institutions, and provide later loan extensions.

Federal Corporation and Capital Structure

HOLC was capitalised through stock subscribed by the United States Treasury and through bonds issued to acquire private mortgage claims.

The original statute authorised $200 million in capital stock and $2 billion in bonds. Amendments expanded aggregate bond authority as the scale of applications became clear.

Bonds initially carried a federal guarantee of interest. The 1934 amendment strengthened their acceptance by guaranteeing principal as well.

The corporation used bond exchange because the federal government could not refinance more than one million mortgages through cash appropriations alone.

Creditors evaluated whether to accept HOLC bonds in place of their existing liens. Negotiation could require reduction of principal, accrued interest, penalties, or other claims so that the new debt fit statutory appraisal limits.

HOLC ultimately redeemed its bond issues, repurchased and retired its Treasury capital stock, and returned accumulated surplus to the Treasury.

Governance and Administrative Development

The Federal Home Loan Bank Board served as HOLC’s board of directors from 1933 until the wartime reorganisation of 1942.

The corporation operated through a general manager, general counsel, comptroller, treasurer, auditor, assistant managers, regional managers, state managers, attorneys, appraisers, loan closers, accountants, property managers, repair personnel, and clerical staff.

Initial state and branch offices processed applications, titles, appraisals, creditor negotiations, and closings. The nationwide operation expanded rapidly into thousands of counties.

Eleven regional offices were established during 1934 to coordinate state operations.

The central office moved from Washington, D.C., to New York in September 1941 so that Washington office space could be used by expanding defense agencies.

As loan origination ended and accounts declined, the organisation reduced staff and consolidated offices.

Homeowner and Property Eligibility

HOLC was intended for borrowers unable to obtain satisfactory refinancing through ordinary channels.

Eligibility generally required:

  • an existing mortgage, tax lien, assessment, or related qualifying obligation dating from the statutory period;
  • a nonfarm residential property;
  • no more than four family dwelling units;
  • use by the applicant as a home or homestead;
  • a property value not exceeding $20,000;
  • sufficient appraised value to secure the proposed loan;
  • a valid title or long-term leasehold interest;
  • creditor agreement where bond exchange or settlement was necessary;
  • evidence that the distress resulted from circumstances recognised by the program;
  • a reasonable prospect that modified terms could be paid; and
  • HOLC’s ability to obtain a first lien.

Eligibility did not create an entitlement to refinancing. A property could be rejected because of title problems, inadequate value, excessive debt, ineligible occupancy, creditor refusal, inability to pay even modified terms, or another statutory restriction.

Exclusions and Program Boundaries

HOLC was not a universal mortgage-assistance program.

Farms and agricultural properties generally fell outside its principal authority and were addressed through farm-credit and emergency agricultural mortgage programs.

Pure rental or investment properties lacking owner occupancy were ordinarily ineligible.

Homes exceeding the statutory value limit or containing more than four family units were excluded.

Renters received no direct ownership benefit from HOLC refinancing unless their landlord’s participation indirectly preserved the property.

Households without recorded ownership, legally recognised homestead rights, clear title, or creditor cooperation could be unable to participate.

Racial discrimination in property markets, title systems, appraisal, local administration, and creditor negotiation shaped access even though the statute did not expressly exclude borrowers by race.

Application and Refinancing Process

  1. Submit an application. The homeowner reported the property, liens, creditors, income, household circumstances, taxes, insurance, and reason for distress.
  2. Conduct preliminary eligibility review. Staff determined whether the property and obligations fell within the statute.
  3. Investigate title. Attorneys, abstractors, and title personnel identified ownership, mortgages, tax claims, judgments, assessments, and other encumbrances.
  4. Appraise the property. A trained or fee appraiser estimated the present value and evaluated the dwelling and neighbourhood.
  5. Evaluate ability to pay. Staff considered income, employment, taxes, insurance, family obligations, and the proposed payment.
  6. Negotiate with creditors. Existing lienholders were asked to accept HOLC bonds, cash within statutory limits, or a reduced settlement.
  7. Determine repairs and advances. HOLC could include funds for taxes, assessments, maintenance, rehabilitation, and transaction expenses.
  8. Close the loan. Existing debts were satisfied or assigned, and HOLC received a recorded first mortgage.
  9. Establish amortization. The borrower entered a scheduled repayment plan with interest, principal, taxes, insurance, and property obligations.
  10. Service the account. HOLC collected payments, monitored taxes and insurance, handled delinquency, and modified or foreclosed where necessary.

Mortgage Acquisition through Bond Exchange

The largest loan category involved exchange of HOLC bonds for an existing mortgage or lien.

The creditor surrendered the original claim and received federal corporation bonds with guaranteed interest and, after the 1934 amendment, guaranteed principal.

The homeowner did not receive the bond proceeds as unrestricted cash. HOLC used the transaction to acquire and settle existing debt.

The new HOLC mortgage could include accrued interest, delinquent taxes, assessments, approved repairs, and limited closing expenses, subject to statutory loan-to-value and dollar ceilings.

Where the debt exceeded allowable value, creditors could accept less than the full amount. A creditor’s refusal could prevent a bond-exchange refinancing unless another statutory cash method was available.

Bond exchange transferred mortgage-market risk from distressed private creditors to a federal portfolio administered over many years.

Cash, Tax-Lien, and Recovery Loans

Cash authority was narrower than the principal bond-exchange authority.

Where a creditor refused HOLC bonds and private refinancing was unavailable, the corporation could make a cash loan within a lower loan-to-value limit.

A homeowner whose otherwise unencumbered property faced tax or assessment sale could obtain a limited cash loan secured by a new mortgage.

HOLC could also recover qualifying homes lost through foreclosure, trustee sale, or voluntary surrender after January 1, 1930, where statutory conditions were satisfied.

Repair, rehabilitation, rebuilding, and modernisation advances were added or expanded through amendments.

Loan Terms and Amortization

HOLC helped establish the long-term, fully amortizing residential mortgage as a national standard.

The original maximum term was fifteen years. Monthly payments were designed to retire both interest and principal over the life of the loan.

Quarterly, semiannual, or annual schedules could be approved where a borrower’s income pattern justified them.

Early law permitted a three-year principal moratorium beginning in 1933, provided the borrower kept current on interest and property charges.

Interest was generally capped at 5 percent for bond-exchange and tax-related categories and 6 percent for specified cash loans.

The Mead–Barry Act of 1939 permitted delinquent loans to be extended or recast for repayment within a maximum of twenty-five years.

HOLC terms were more sustainable than many short-term balloon mortgages, but payment remained difficult for households experiencing prolonged unemployment, illness, death, divorce, tax burdens, or inadequate income.

Taxes, Insurance, and Property Maintenance

Refinancing a mortgage did not remove the continuing cost of ownership.

Borrowers had to maintain taxes, assessments, hazard insurance, repairs, and other property obligations.

HOLC could advance funds to protect its lien where taxes, insurance, emergency repairs, or structural problems threatened the property.

Approved advances were generally added to the borrower’s account and increased the amount requiring repayment.

Failure to maintain taxes or insurance could cause delinquency or foreclosure even where scheduled principal and interest payments were partly maintained.

Residential Appraisal System

Statutory loan limits required HOLC to determine the value of more than one million properties under national procedures.

Appraisers examined the building, site, construction, condition, age, rooms, utilities, repairs, rental potential, comparable sales, taxes, liens, neighbourhood, land use, transportation, schools, services, and local market.

HOLC trained and examined appraisers. The final report states that approximately 6,000 were classified, including about 1,300 salaried appraisers and 2,700 qualified fee appraisers.

Approximately 4.65 million original and review appraisals were completed during the loan-closing period.

The volume exceeded the number of closed loans because properties could be reviewed multiple times and many applications did not close.

HOLC appraisal forms and procedures helped create a national professional standard used by real-estate boards, lenders, and government agencies.

Professionalisation did not make appraisal neutral. Appraisal methods treated neighbourhood racial composition, ethnicity, immigration, class, housing age, and land-use change as indicators of future value.

Repair, Rehabilitation, and Reconditioning

Distressed owners often had postponed maintenance, leaving roofs, foundations, heating, plumbing, wiring, paint, and structural elements in poor condition.

HOLC could include approved repair costs in a refinancing or advance additional funds later.

Architects, engineers, inspectors, and repair personnel prepared specifications, estimated costs, selected contractors, and supervised work.

The final report records 417,396 repair contracts included in original closed loans.

More than 450,000 additional repair or reconditioning contracts were completed by June 1944, most involving properties the corporation had acquired.

Repairs protected collateral, improved habitability, created construction employment, and supported resale. They also increased the borrower or property account where costs were capitalised.

Loan Servicing and Collection

Once the emergency loan-closing period ended, servicing became HOLC’s principal activity.

Servicing included:

  • collecting principal and interest;
  • monitoring taxes, assessments, and insurance;
  • maintaining borrower accounts;
  • processing partial payments;
  • investigating delinquency;
  • granting extensions or temporary indulgence;
  • recasting eligible delinquent loans;
  • advancing protective expenses;
  • reviewing property condition;
  • negotiating voluntary sale or assignment;
  • authorising foreclosure where recovery failed; and
  • closing accounts through payment, sale, assignment, or property acquisition.

The national portfolio required standard accounting and collection methods while allowing field offices to consider individual hardship.

Forbearance and Loss Mitigation

HOLC policy emphasised preserving ownership where a borrower could reasonably recover.

Field staff investigated income, employment, health, family changes, taxes, insurance, and property condition before recommending foreclosure.

The corporation accepted partial payments, granted extensions, postponed enforcement, modified schedules, and later used twenty-five-year recasting authority.

Forbearance was not unlimited. Continuing delinquency increased interest and protective advances, and the total balance at foreclosure could exceed the original principal.

Borrowers who could not meet even reduced obligations eventually faced voluntary surrender, third-party sale, or foreclosure.

HOLC illustrates that government ownership of distressed mortgages can allow more flexible servicing than immediate private liquidation, but it cannot eliminate income loss or make every debt affordable.

Foreclosure and Property Acquisition

HOLC foreclosed when continued delay threatened the federal investment and staff concluded that the borrower lacked a workable repayment path.

Approximately 194,134 original mortgage accounts were foreclosed and transferred into property accounts.

The corporation recorded approximately 198,215 acquired properties after including other acquisition and retransfer pathways.

Foreclosure timing varied according to state law, judicial procedures, statutory redemption periods, title conditions, and local property markets.

HOLC ordinarily exercised substantial patience. The final report noted that borrowers in a sample of 1939–1940 foreclosures had often accumulated balances greater than their original loans before foreclosure occurred.

The resulting foreclosure share—approximately one in five original borrower accounts—was large, but substantially below the level that might have occurred without refinancing and extended servicing.

Management of Acquired Homes

After acquisition, HOLC became responsible for taxes, insurance, repairs, security, rent, utilities, legal issues, brokers, and sale.

Regional and state property-management divisions supervised local real-estate brokers.

Brokers found tenants, collected rent, arranged leases, inspected properties, supervised authorised repairs, and marketed homes for sale.

HOLC avoided placing every property on the market immediately. Large simultaneous sales could depress neighbourhood prices and increase federal losses.

The final report records approximately $138.6 million in gross rent collections and approximately $13.4 million in rental commissions.

The corporation’s acquired-home portfolio made it one of the nation’s largest residential property owners and managers during the late 1930s and early 1940s.

Property Repair and Resale

Acquired properties were evaluated for repair, rental, cash sale, or instalment sale.

Reconditioning could improve safety, appearance, rental income, marketability, and collateral recovery.

Cash sales disposed of properties immediately, while instalment or vendee sales created a new account under which the purchaser paid over time.

Properties sold through brokers remained subject to local real-estate customs and discriminatory housing markets.

Vendee accounts could themselves become delinquent, be paid, assigned, redeemed, sold through third-party foreclosure, or return to HOLC property ownership.

By liquidation, the corporation had sold or otherwise disposed of its acquired property portfolio.

City Survey Program

After the emergency refinancing portfolio had been assembled, the Federal Home Loan Bank Board and HOLC developed a City Survey Program to evaluate urban residential markets and the security of federal mortgage holdings.

Local real-estate professionals, lenders, appraisers, brokers, city officials, and HOLC personnel supplied neighbourhood information.

Survey files described housing type, age, condition, sales, rents, occupancy, homeownership, income, transportation, land use, topography, hazards, racial and ethnic composition, population change, building restrictions, and anticipated development.

The program produced maps and area descriptions for approximately 239 cities between 1935 and 1940.

The surveys were designed to assess neighbourhood-level mortgage risk and support appraisal, portfolio review, and housing-finance analysis.

Residential Security Maps

HOLC maps commonly assigned one of four grades:

A — “Best”
Green areas described as new, homogeneous, well-maintained, stable, and highly desirable for mortgage investment.
B — “Still Desirable”
Blue areas viewed as sound but somewhat older or less protected than grade A districts.
C — “Definitely Declining”
Yellow areas associated with ageing property, mixed use, lower-income residents, demographic change, maintenance problems, or perceived instability.
D — “Hazardous”
Red areas considered the greatest mortgage risk under the survey’s discriminatory standards.

Neighbourhood descriptions could identify Black residents, immigrants, Jewish residents, Mexican Americans, working-class populations, and racial or ethnic “infiltration” as negative influences.

Restrictive covenants and racial homogeneity were frequently treated as protections of property value.

These assumptions converted segregation and exclusion into technical categories appearing to measure financial risk.

Redlining and Racialized Risk Classification

Redlining is the denial, restriction, pricing, or avoidance of mortgages, insurance, investment, or services based on the racial or ethnic composition of a neighbourhood rather than an individual applicant’s qualifications alone.

HOLC did not invent all racial discrimination in housing finance. Banks, insurers, appraisers, developers, real-estate boards, restrictive covenants, zoning systems, and government institutions had already reinforced segregation.

HOLC’s national survey system nevertheless gave racialized neighbourhood appraisal a federal, systematic, visual, and apparently professional form.

Grade D boundaries often corresponded closely with Black neighbourhoods and areas occupied by other groups treated as undesirable under contemporary real-estate ideology.

Low ratings could become self-reinforcing. Neighbourhoods classified as risky were less likely to receive ordinary credit and investment; reduced investment worsened housing condition; deteriorating conditions were then cited as confirmation of the original risk judgment.

The maps became enduring symbols of federal participation in residential segregation and unequal urban investment.

Relationship between the Maps and HOLC Lending

Researchers distinguish HOLC’s emergency refinancing loans from the later City Survey maps.

Most original HOLC loans were applied for and substantially processed before many residential security maps were completed.

HOLC refinanced borrowers in neighbourhoods later graded C and D, including some Black homeowners and residents of older urban areas.

For this reason, historians debate whether the maps directly determined HOLC’s original refinancing distribution in the same manner that later redlining restricted private and FHA-supported lending.

The absence of a simple one-to-one relationship does not make the survey system racially neutral. HOLC area descriptions explicitly used race and ethnicity as risk variables and helped standardise discriminatory appraisal ideas across federal and private housing institutions.

A careful account should therefore avoid two errors: claiming that every redlining practice originated with HOLC, and claiming that HOLC’s maps were harmless because the emergency loan program sometimes operated in low-rated areas.

African American Homeowners

Black homeowners entered the Depression with lower average income and wealth, limited access to banks and building associations, racially restricted housing markets, discriminatory property valuation, employment inequality, and greater exposure to exploitative contract sales and private financing.

African Americans who obtained HOLC refinancing could benefit from lower interest, long amortization, payment flexibility, tax settlement, and foreclosure delay.

The statute did not contain an express racial eligibility exclusion, and evidence from several cities shows that HOLC made loans in Black and racially mixed neighbourhoods.

Access remained unequal because local appraisers, title systems, creditors, neighbourhood valuations, branch offices, brokers, and segregated property markets shaped whether a loan could be approved and how much value was recognised.

A Black-owned home could receive a lower appraisal because the surrounding neighbourhood contained Black residents—making federal assistance harder to secure or more limited.

Residential security mapping further classified Black presence and racial change as threats to mortgage security, contributing to long-term disinvestment and racial wealth inequality.

Other Racial and Ethnic Communities

HOLC survey descriptions also used discriminatory assumptions about Mexican Americans, Asian Americans, immigrants, Jewish residents, southern and eastern Europeans, and working-class ethnic neighbourhoods.

The terminology and severity varied by city.

Some neighbourhoods received lower grades because surveyors described them as racially or ethnically mixed, vulnerable to “infiltration,” or lacking restrictive covenants.

Such classifications treated demographic diversity as a threat rather than evaluating residents and borrowers on individual financial circumstances.

The resulting system contributed to unequal access to conventional credit, suburban development, home appreciation, and intergenerational wealth.

Women Homeowners and Households

Widows, divorced or separated women, unmarried women, and married women holding title could apply if they met program requirements.

Women’s access depended on state property law, employment, household income, title, creditor cooperation, appraisal, and prevailing assumptions about male wage earners.

A husband’s death, unemployment, abandonment, illness, or disability could transform an otherwise manageable mortgage into a distressed debt.

HOLC’s extended terms could preserve housing stability for women and children, but low wages and restricted employment often made repayment difficult.

Historical forms frequently centred a male “head of household,” potentially obscuring women’s income, ownership, and repayment contributions.

Effects on Lenders and the Mortgage Industry

HOLC acquired delinquent claims from banks, building and loan associations, insurance companies, mortgage firms, trusts, estates, and individuals.

Bond exchange converted illiquid local mortgages into federally supported securities.

This relieved lenders of collection and foreclosure costs and supplied assets that could be sold, pledged, or held.

The program therefore rescued parts of the creditor system as well as homeowners.

HOLC’s standardized servicing, amortization, appraisal, repair, and property-management methods influenced the long-term development of institutional mortgage lending.

Its investments in federal savings-and-loan associations and the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation helped build the thrift and mortgage-insurance structure created by New Deal housing policy.

Relationship to the Federal Home Loan Bank System

Congress created the Federal Home Loan Bank System in 1932 to provide a reserve-credit structure for member institutions specialising in home finance.

The Federal Home Loan Bank Board supervised both the regional banks and HOLC.

The bank system supplied liquidity to institutions, while HOLC directly acquired and refinanced qualifying distressed homeowner debts.

The two mechanisms addressed different problems: institutional funding and individual mortgage distress.

The shared board promoted common standards in appraisal, examination, thrift development, mortgage finance, and regulation.

Relationship to the Federal Housing Administration

The National Housing Act of 1934 created the Federal Housing Administration.

HOLC directly held distressed loans already in existence. The FHA generally insured qualifying loans made by private lenders for purchase, construction, refinancing, or improvement.

HOLC was an emergency portfolio operation with a limited origination period. FHA mortgage insurance became a continuing feature of the housing market.

Both institutions promoted longer-term amortization and professional underwriting.

Both operated within a federal housing system that assigned lower value and greater risk to racially integrated and minority neighbourhoods.

FHA underwriting manuals, private lending practices, subdivision covenants, and HOLC survey methods developed through related but distinct institutional channels.

Federal Savings and Loan Development

The Home Owners’ Loan Act authorised the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to charter federal savings and loan associations in communities lacking adequate local home-financing institutions.

HOLC later acquired shares in federal and state-chartered savings and loan associations under statutory authority.

It also subscribed $100 million in capital stock of the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation through HOLC bonds.

These activities extended the New Deal response beyond emergency refinancing toward a permanent national system of thrift institutions, federal charters, liquidity, mortgage insurance, and household savings.

Wartime Homes Use Conversion Program

During World War II, rapid population growth in defense centres created severe housing shortages.

The National Housing Agency assigned HOLC to inspect, lease, and convert eligible existing properties into additional dwelling units.

HOLC used its appraisal, title, contracting, repair, and property-management experience to administer conversions.

The final report records 8,866 completed leased-property conversion cases and 47,811 dwelling units made available.

Responsibility for operating completed units transferred to the Federal Public Housing Authority.

This wartime assignment was separate from the corporation’s original homeowner-refinancing mission.

Federal Instrumentality and Legal Status

The statute declared HOLC an instrumentality of the United States with authority to sue and be sued.

Courts addressed taxation, mortgage recording, contracts, foreclosure, title, employment, and the corporation’s federal privileges.

In Pittman v. Home Owners’ Loan Corporation, the Supreme Court held that Maryland could not impose its mortgage-recording tax on HOLC because Congress had exempted the federal instrumentality from state taxation.

Such immunity reduced transaction costs and protected uniform national operations but generated conflict with states relying on recording and property-related revenue.

Administrative Transfers

Federal Home Loan Bank Board, 1933–1939

HOLC was created and initially directed by the Federal Home Loan Bank Board.

Federal Loan Agency, 1939–1942

Reorganization Plan No. I of 1939 placed the board and corporation within the Federal Loan Agency.

National Housing Agency, 1942–1947

Executive Order 9070 abolished the original board structure during wartime and placed HOLC under the Federal Home Loan Bank Administration within the National Housing Agency.

Housing and Home Finance Agency, 1947–1954

Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1947 transferred remaining direction and liquidation functions to the Home Loan Bank Board within the new Housing and Home Finance Agency.

These transfers did not reopen emergency refinancing. They changed supervision of a shrinking service and liquidation portfolio.

Portfolio Liquidation

Liquidation began as loans were paid, sold, assigned, foreclosed, or converted into property and resale accounts.

HOLC reduced field offices and staff as the portfolio declined.

Remaining loans and vendee accounts were eventually paid or sold to private institutions.

Acquired properties were repaired, rented, and sold. Bond issues were called or matured and redeemed.

HOLC repurchased its $200 million in Treasury-owned capital stock at par and cancelled the stock receipts.

By June 30, 1951, all material assets had been realised and the main operational liquidation was complete.

Residual accounting, unclaimed bond funds, records, and statutory termination continued after the operational closure.

Financial Outcome

HOLC incurred substantial mortgage, interest, foreclosure, repair, and property-sale losses.

The cumulative deficit reached approximately $134.1 million in 1944.

Later interest income, loan repayments, rents, property sales, investment income, declining administrative expense, and reserve adjustments eliminated the deficit.

The final report recorded:

  • approximately $1.417 billion in operating and other income;
  • approximately $1.065 billion in operating and other expense;
  • approximately $352.1 million in net operating income;
  • approximately $338.0 million in mortgage, property, casualty, hazard, and miscellaneous losses; and
  • a cumulative surplus of approximately $14.07 million by the end of liquidation accounting.

The reported surplus does not mean that the intervention imposed no economic cost. Federal backing, tax treatment, administrative resources, opportunity costs, and the broader distribution of gains and losses require separate evaluation.

Program Scale and Selected Measures

  • more than 1.886 million applications filed;
  • more than $6.173 billion requested;
  • 1,017,821 original borrowers refinanced;
  • $3,093,451,321 in original loans acquired;
  • 1,026,411 original and supplemental loan transactions;
  • 20,811 peak employees in November 1934;
  • approximately 4.65 million original and review appraisals during the loan-closing period;
  • 417,396 repair contracts included in original closed loans;
  • more than 450,000 later repair and reconditioning contracts completed by mid-1944;
  • approximately 194,134 original mortgage accounts transferred to property through foreclosure;
  • approximately 198,215 total acquired property accounts;
  • approximately $138.6 million in gross rent collections;
  • residential security surveys and maps for approximately 239 cities;
  • 47,811 dwelling units made available through wartime conversion work; and
  • approximately $14.07 million cumulative reported surplus at final liquidation accounting.

These figures measure different units. Applications, borrowers, loans, mortgage accounts, properties, repair contracts, appraisals, vendee accounts, and dwelling units are not interchangeable.

Program Achievements

  • It refinanced more than one million distressed homeowners.
  • It prevented or delayed foreclosure for hundreds of thousands of households.
  • It replaced short-term and balloon mortgages with longer-term amortized loans.
  • It settled delinquent taxes, assessments, interest, and approved property charges.
  • It improved liquidity for banks, insurers, building associations, and private mortgage holders.
  • It introduced a national system of mortgage servicing and loss mitigation.
  • It exercised greater forbearance than immediate foreclosure would have provided.
  • It financed or supervised extensive home repair and rehabilitation.
  • It managed and disposed of a vast acquired-property portfolio without immediate mass liquidation.
  • It developed national appraisal training and documentation standards.
  • It supported federal savings-and-loan and mortgage-insurance institutions.
  • It used existing housing for additional wartime dwelling units.
  • It completed operational liquidation, redeemed its bonds, retired federal capital, and returned a reported surplus.
  • It established precedents for later federal mortgage-modification and foreclosure-relief policy.

Limitations and Failures

  • Approximately 46 percent of applications did not produce eligible closed loans.
  • Renters, farm owners, large multifamily properties, and many non-owner-occupied properties were outside the principal program.
  • Creditor refusal or excessive debt could prevent refinancing.
  • Borrowers still had to pay taxes, insurance, maintenance, interest, and principal.
  • Long-term unemployment and poverty made some modified loans unaffordable.
  • Approximately one-fifth of original borrower accounts ultimately resulted in foreclosure or comparable loss of the property.
  • Interest and advances could increase the balance during prolonged delinquency.
  • Appraisal systems incorporated discriminatory assumptions about race, ethnicity, class, and neighbourhood change.
  • Black-owned and minority-neighbourhood property could receive systematically lower valuation.
  • Local creditors, appraisers, brokers, attorneys, and offices operated within segregated housing markets.
  • Residential security maps formalised neighbourhood risk categories closely tied to racial segregation.
  • HOLC stabilised ownership for existing eligible owners but did not create equal access to future mortgage credit.
  • Property acquisition made the federal government a large landlord and seller in racially discriminatory markets.
  • Financial success cannot by itself measure social equity or long-term neighbourhood consequences.

Major Controversies

Homeowner Relief versus Creditor Rescue

The program helped families retain homes, but it also converted distressed private claims into federal bonds and protected financial institutions from losses and foreclosure costs.

Federal Risk

Critics argued that the government acquired mortgages private lenders considered unsafe. Supporters argued that national crisis and better loan terms made many borrowers recoverable.

Eligibility and Rejection

Distressed owners could experience long processing times and rejection where value, title, income, debt, occupancy, or creditor agreement failed program standards.

Foreclosure by a Relief Agency

HOLC was created to prevent foreclosure but ultimately foreclosed on a substantial minority of its borrowers to protect federal funds.

Racialized Appraisal

The corporation’s neighbourhood surveys treated race and ethnicity as evidence of mortgage risk and gave discriminatory real-estate ideology federal administrative form.

Historical Responsibility for Redlining

Scholars debate whether HOLC maps directly controlled the original loan portfolio, how widely they circulated, and how much later disinvestment was caused by HOLC versus FHA, private lenders, covenants, zoning, and other institutions.

Government Competition

Private lenders and real-estate interests questioned federal mortgage ownership, low-interest refinancing, property sales, and institutional expansion.

Reported Profit

The final surplus is often cited as proof of success, while critics note that conventional accounting does not capture every subsidy, guarantee, administrative benefit, distributional effect, or opportunity cost.

Documented Outcomes

HOLC converted more than one million distressed mortgage situations into standardized federal loans.

More than 800,000 original mortgage accounts ended through payment, sale, assignment, redemption, or other disposition without transfer into the main acquired-property portfolio.

Mortgage lenders received liquid federal securities or approved cash settlement during a period of severe institutional stress.

Long-term amortization became more widely accepted as a safer form of owner-occupied mortgage finance.

Federal appraisal, servicing, accounting, repair, and property-management practices became influential national standards.

The City Survey Program produced an enduring archive of neighbourhood conditions while embedding discriminatory judgments in its risk classifications.

Empirical studies generally find that HOLC refinancing reduced foreclosure pressure and supported property values, although results varied across cities and borrowers.

The maps’ classifications remain associated with later patterns of disinvestment, segregation, lower property values, environmental inequality, and health disparities, but the causal pathway involved multiple public and private institutions.

Historical Significance

HOLC demonstrated that the federal government could intervene directly in a national mortgage crisis by purchasing distressed debt and modifying repayment terms.

It helped shift American housing finance away from short-term, renewable balloon loans toward regular long-term amortization.

The corporation linked mortgage relief with appraisal, property repair, servicing, foreclosure management, resale, thrift regulation, and mortgage insurance.

Its experience became a reference point during later foreclosure crises, including debates surrounding federal purchase or modification of distressed loans.

Its racial legacy shows that apparently technical standards of value and risk can institutionalise social hierarchy.

The corporation therefore occupies two central places in American history: an effective emergency refinancing institution and an important contributor to the federal architecture of racially unequal housing finance.

Records and Research Value

Principal records of the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation are preserved by the National Archives in Record Group 195, Records of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board.

Records include:

  • board minutes, bylaws, regulations, and administrative correspondence;
  • general-manager and executive files;
  • regional, state, district, and branch-office records;
  • loan applications and eligibility materials;
  • mortgage, title, lien, tax, and closing documents;
  • borrower financial and household information;
  • appraisal forms, manuals, examinations, and review files;
  • property descriptions, photographs, plans, and repair estimates;
  • payment, delinquency, extension, and modification records;
  • foreclosure and redemption files;
  • acquired-property management, rental, broker, and sale records;
  • vendee and instalment-sale accounts;
  • bond, Treasury stock, investment, accounting, and audit records;
  • Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation and association-investment files;
  • City Survey Program area descriptions and residential security maps;
  • wartime housing-conversion records;
  • personnel and organisational records;
  • annual reports and the final liquidation report;
  • statistical tables by state and territory; and
  • records transferred to successor housing and home-finance agencies.

Some individual case files may have retention, privacy, sampling, or regional-location limitations. Researchers should consult National Archives finding aids and successor-agency records.

Genealogical and Property-History Value

HOLC files may identify owners, spouses, dependents, addresses, occupations, employers, income, creditors, property descriptions, liens, taxes, repairs, appraised value, payment history, foreclosure, tenants, and later purchasers.

Recorded HOLC mortgages, releases, assignments, foreclosure deeds, and resale instruments may appear in county land records.

Residential security area descriptions can document neighbourhood institutions, demographic composition, housing types, property condition, land use, and local real-estate opinion.

City directories, census records, deeds, tax assessments, building permits, newspapers, court files, fire-insurance maps, and FHA records can supplement the federal material.

A map grade should not be treated as a neutral description of the residents or neighbourhood. It reflects the discriminatory assumptions and market judgments of the survey process.

Research Cautions

  • HOLC and FHA were different institutions. HOLC refinanced distressed existing mortgages; FHA insured qualifying private loans.
  • Loan operations and corporate existence ended at different times. New lending authority ended in 1936, main liquidation concluded in 1951, and formal termination occurred in 1954.
  • Applications and borrowers are not the same count. More than 1.886 million applications produced 1,017,821 original borrower accounts.
  • Loans and borrowers are not the same count. Supplemental advances increased total loan transactions beyond the number of original borrowers.
  • Mortgage foreclosures and property accounts differ. Transfers, reacquisitions, and other pathways explain variation among foreclosure and acquired-property totals.
  • The City Survey maps postdated much of the emergency lending. They should not automatically be treated as the sole underwriting instrument for every original HOLC loan.
  • The maps were nevertheless discriminatory. Area descriptions explicitly incorporated race and ethnicity into neighbourhood-risk judgments.
  • Redlining had multiple institutional sources. HOLC, FHA, private lenders, insurers, real-estate boards, restrictive covenants, zoning, and government policy interacted.
  • A reported surplus is an accounting outcome. It does not measure every economic subsidy, social benefit, racial cost, or opportunity cost.
  • Contemporary property values cannot be substituted for historical appraisals. Values, boundaries, structures, neighbourhoods, and market conditions changed.
  • Period racial terminology requires critical treatment. Archival language should be quoted only when necessary and placed in historical context.

Definitions

Amortization
Scheduled repayment that gradually reduces principal while paying interest over the life of a loan.
Appraisal
An estimate of property value based on the structure, land, condition, location, comparable sales, neighbourhood, and market factors.
Balloon mortgage
A loan requiring a large principal payment at maturity because regular instalments do not fully repay the debt.
Bond exchange
HOLC’s acquisition of a mortgage or lien in return for federal corporation bonds delivered to the creditor.
City Survey Program
The HOLC and Federal Home Loan Bank Board program collecting neighbourhood-level housing and mortgage-risk information and producing residential security maps.
First lien
A claim against property that generally has priority over later mortgages and liens.
Forbearance
Temporary delay or modification of collection or foreclosure while the borrower attempts to cure delinquency.
Foreclosure
A legal process through which a creditor enforces a mortgage and obtains or sells the property securing the debt.
Home Owners’ Loan Corporation
The federal corporation created in 1933 to refinance distressed owner-occupied home mortgages and administer the resulting loan portfolio.
Loan-to-value ratio
The amount of a loan expressed as a percentage of the property’s appraised value.
Principal moratorium
A temporary period during which required principal payments are postponed while other obligations may continue.
Reconditioning
Repair, rehabilitation, rebuilding, modernisation, or maintenance intended to preserve or improve a property.
Redlining
Systematic denial or restriction of credit, insurance, investment, or services to neighbourhoods based on race, ethnicity, or location rather than individual merit alone.
Residential security map
A color-coded HOLC City Survey map grading neighbourhoods according to discriminatory mortgage-risk standards.
Vendee account
An instalment-sale account created when HOLC sold an acquired property to a purchaser over time.

Geographic Scope

HOLC operated throughout the forty-eight states, District of Columbia, Hawai‘i Territory, and Puerto Rico.

The program extended into nearly every county, although loan volume varied with population, homeownership, foreclosure pressure, property values, urbanisation, creditor participation, and local administration.

Urban and suburban areas generated the largest volume because the statute principally covered nonfarm owner-occupied housing.

City Survey mapping focused on major urban areas rather than every place where HOLC made loans.

Racial and ethnic classification differed regionally but operated within a national real-estate ideology favouring homogeneity, restrictive covenants, new construction, and suburban development.

Summary

The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation was a federal New Deal corporation created in June 1933 to refinance distressed mortgages on eligible owner-occupied nonfarm homes.

HOLC acquired existing mortgages and liens principally by exchanging federal bonds with creditors. It replaced short-term and balloon debts with first mortgages generally amortized over as long as fifteen years, paid delinquent taxes and approved charges, and financed necessary repairs.

More than 1.886 million applications were filed. The corporation refinanced 1,017,821 original borrowers through approximately $3.093 billion in original loans.

New applications ended in 1935 and loan-closing authority ended in 1936. HOLC then concentrated on servicing, forbearance, extension, repair, foreclosure, property rental, resale, bond redemption, and liquidation.

The corporation exercised substantial patience with distressed borrowers, but approximately 194,134 original mortgage accounts were eventually transferred into acquired property through foreclosure. Total acquired property accounts reached approximately 198,215 through all pathways.

HOLC developed national standards for appraisal, amortization, servicing, repair, and property administration. Its City Survey Program also created color-coded residential security maps that incorporated race and ethnicity into neighbourhood risk ratings.

Historians debate the precise relationship between the maps and HOLC’s original loan decisions because much refinancing preceded the surveys and loans were made in areas later graded C and D. Nevertheless, the maps institutionalised discriminatory concepts of neighbourhood value and became central to the history of redlining.

HOLC supported wartime conversion of existing properties into additional housing, closed its remaining operational portfolio in 1951, reported a cumulative surplus of approximately $14.07 million, and was formally terminated in 1954.
