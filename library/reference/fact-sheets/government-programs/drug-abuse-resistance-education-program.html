Drug Abuse Resistance Education Program

The police-led school prevention programme founded in Los Angeles in 1983, nationally known as D.A.R.E., and later redesigned around the interactive keepin’ it REAL curriculum after long-term evaluations found little lasting drug-use effect from the original model.

What Is D.A.R.E.?

Drug Abuse Resistance Education is a branded school program in which specially trained law-enforcement personnel teach structured prevention and safety lessons.

The original program focused heavily on information about drugs, peer-pressure resistance, self-esteem, and the ability to say no. Modern curricula place greater emphasis on interactive communication, decision-making, social-emotional learning, risk assessment, and the REAL resistance strategies.

D.A.R.E. is simultaneously a curriculum, an officer-training system, a school-police partnership, a nonprofit brand, and a network of local programs.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains D.A.R.E.’s Los Angeles origins, national expansion, nonprofit organization, federal support, officer selection and training, original and current curricula, classroom model, parent and community components, student graduations, research findings, curriculum reform, cultural impact, police-community objectives, funding, civil-rights concerns, criticism, and current status.

It distinguishes the historical seventeen-lesson D.A.R.E. curriculum from keepin’ it REAL and distinguishes D.A.R.E. from general school health education, school resource officers, Drug-Free Communities grants, Red Ribbon Week, and the federal “Just Say No” campaign.

Geographic Scope

D.A.R.E. began in Los Angeles and spread throughout the United States through local police departments, sheriffs’ offices, state police, school systems, military schools, tribal agencies, and regional training centres.

International affiliates later adapted the brand and curriculum for local legal, linguistic, cultural, and educational systems.

Participation has never been uniform. Individual communities can adopt, suspend, modify, replace, or restart D.A.R.E. according to local policy and funding.

Historical Context

D.A.R.E. emerged during the early War on Drugs, when public concern about adolescent drug use, marijuana, cocaine, gangs, school disorder, and urban crime was increasing.

The program reflected a demand-reduction theory: law enforcement would continue suppressing drug supply while schools and police attempted to prevent future demand by reaching children before drug experimentation began.

It also reflected a community-relations objective. Police leaders sought positive contact with children at a time when aggressive enforcement and strained police-community relations damaged public trust.

Los Angeles Origins

LAPD Chief Daryl Gates approached LAUSD Superintendent Harry Handler concerning a joint anti-drug education program.

Police and educators developed a curriculum intended to combine officers’ knowledge of illegal drugs and street activity with teachers’ knowledge of children, classrooms, and instruction.

The 1983 Pilot

Ten LAPD officers began teaching in Los Angeles schools in September 1983.

The pilot assigned uniformed officers to classrooms as instructors rather than bringing them in only for enforcement, assemblies, or emergencies.

Daryl F. Gates

Gates was the LAPD chief who promoted D.A.R.E. as a prevention complement to drug enforcement.

His role connected the program to the LAPD’s broader crime-control and community-relations strategies and later made D.A.R.E. part of debates over aggressive policing and the Los Angeles drug war.

Harry Handler

Handler was the LAUSD superintendent who supported placing trained officers in classrooms.

School-system participation was essential because officers required classroom access, teacher cooperation, schedules, curriculum review, and administrative support.

University and Prevention-Curriculum Influence

Early D.A.R.E. drew on social-influence and resistance-skills approaches being developed in prevention research, including work associated with the University of Southern California.

The curriculum was adapted for delivery by police officers and included police-community goals beyond ordinary health instruction.

Supply Reduction and Demand Reduction

Drug enforcement sought to reduce supply through arrests, seizures, and prosecution.

D.A.R.E. sought to reduce future demand by shaping children’s knowledge, attitudes, skills, and intentions.

Critics argued that a classroom program could not overcome broader social, family, economic, commercial, and peer influences on substance use.

Police-Community Relations Objective

Program planners expected children to see officers as teachers, mentors, and trusted adults rather than only as agents of punishment.

Officers often spent time in cafeterias, playgrounds, assemblies, athletic activities, and school events to create informal contact.

Supporters describe this as community policing; critics describe it as police image management or institutional propaganda.

Early Expansion

School and police enthusiasm caused D.A.R.E. to spread rapidly beyond Los Angeles during the mid-1980s.

Expansion required standardized curriculum, officer training, instructor certification, program monitoring, regional training capacity, and a national organization.

Drug-Free Schools and Communities Act Context

The Drug-Free Schools and Communities Act of 1986 expanded federal support for school drug-prevention activity.

States and school systems could use federal prevention resources for qualifying programs, while the national “drug-free schools” movement helped create demand for D.A.R.E.

D.A.R.E. remained locally implemented rather than becoming one federally administered curriculum.

National D.A.R.E. Day

President Ronald Reagan proclaimed a National D.A.R.E. Day in 1988.

White House recognition reinforced the program’s association with the Reagan administration’s anti-drug campaign and helped transform a local project into a national symbol.

D.A.R.E. America

D.A.R.E. America became the national nonprofit institution responsible for the brand, curriculum development, training standards, program support, partnerships, conferences, and international relationships.

Historical sources describe a national corporation formed during 1987 and formally incorporated as a nonprofit in January 1989.

Nonprofit Rather Than Government Agency

D.A.R.E. America is a private nonprofit organization, not a police department, school district, cabinet department, or federal agency.

Local implementation is usually performed by public employees and schools, which means the program can combine nonprofit intellectual property with taxpayer-funded personnel and facilities.

Brand and Curriculum Control

D.A.R.E. America oversees use of the D.A.R.E. name, logo, training model, and official curricula.

Brand control supports consistency but can make independent local adaptation difficult and can blur the distinction between educational evidence and organizational promotion.

Regional Training Centers

By 1989, five federally supported regional training centres served the United States.

They trained new officers, mentor officers, parent-program instructors, and secondary-school instructors; accredited state training centres; monitored quality; and provided technical assistance.

Bureau of Justice Assistance Support

BJA provided discretionary federal support to the five regional training centres during D.A.R.E.’s national expansion.

Historical annual BJA discretionary support rose from $625,000 in 1989 to approximately $1.75 million in 1995.

These figures covered national training infrastructure rather than the full cost of local officers, schools, materials, or programs.

Byrne Formula-Grant Funding

States could use Edward Byrne Memorial State and Local Law Enforcement Assistance formula funds for demand-reduction education involving law-enforcement officers.

States made subawards to local agencies, so D.A.R.E. funding varied among jurisdictions and years.

Local Funding

Police salaries, overtime, vehicles, travel, uniforms, school facilities, teacher time, materials, and ceremonies were often supported through local government and school budgets.

Programs also used state funds, donations, foundations, businesses, civic groups, forfeiture-related funds where lawful, and nonprofit fundraising.

Not a Federal Entitlement or Formula Program

No community has a permanent federal right to receive D.A.R.E. funding.

Historical federal grants supported training and local prevention, but D.A.R.E. did not become a stand-alone national entitlement comparable to a formula grant established for every jurisdiction.

Rapid Growth During the 1990s

A 1995 BJA fact sheet projected that the core curriculum would reach approximately 5.5 million children that year, while additional components would influence millions more.

It reported approximately 22,000 trained officers from 7,000 communities and implementation in roughly 65 to 70 percent of school districts.

Later popular accounts commonly describe peak implementation in approximately three-quarters of United States school districts.

International Expansion

D.A.R.E. expanded through police and school partnerships outside the United States and through Department of Defense schools serving military families.

International adaptations vary in instructor qualifications, curriculum, legal context, language, culture, and institutional control.

Original Core Curriculum

The best-known original curriculum consisted of seventeen weekly lessons taught in fifth or sixth grade.

Lessons combined drug information, refusal practice, decision-making, self-esteem, alternatives, stress management, media analysis, resistance to peer pressure, and a final commitment or graduation.

Original Teaching Methods

Officers used presentations, question-and-answer sessions, discussions, role playing, worksheets, student journals, skits, and rehearsed refusal responses.

Despite interactive elements, evaluations and later reformers criticized the original model as too didactic and information driven.

Social-Influence Model

The original theory assumed that peers, media, and social expectations pressured young people toward substance use.

Students would be less likely to use substances if they recognized pressure, corrected beliefs about how common use was, practiced refusal, and developed social competence.

Peer-Pressure Resistance

D.A.R.E. became strongly associated with teaching children to say no.

Modern prevention research treats peer influence as more complex than direct pressure and also considers perceived norms, identity, family, trauma, stress, marketing, availability, and opportunity.

Drug Information

Lessons described alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, inhalants, cocaine, and other drugs and discussed legal, health, social, and educational consequences.

Information must be age appropriate and factually accurate. Exaggeration can damage credibility when students later discover exceptions or conflicting evidence.

Self-Esteem

The original curriculum attempted to strengthen self-esteem as a protective factor.

Evaluation found little evidence that D.A.R.E. produced lasting self-esteem differences, and prevention science no longer treats general self-esteem improvement as a sufficient drug-prevention mechanism.

Assertiveness

Students practiced stating choices clearly, resisting pressure, and leaving unsafe situations.

Assertiveness remains part of modern communication and resistance-skills instruction.

Decision-Making

D.A.R.E. taught students to identify choices, consider consequences, make decisions, and evaluate results.

Modern curricula make structured decision-making a central skill rather than presenting one universal response for every situation.

Positive Alternatives

Lessons encouraged sports, clubs, hobbies, friendships, education, family activities, and other alternatives to substance use and gang involvement.

The effectiveness of this approach depends on whether meaningful and accessible alternatives actually exist in the student’s community.

Conflict Resolution and Violence Prevention

D.A.R.E. expanded beyond drug prevention to anger management, conflict resolution, bullying, gangs, and violence.

This broadened the program’s school-safety role but made it harder to define and measure one primary outcome.

Gang Resistance

Some curricula addressed gang recruitment, pressure, symbols, conflict, and alternatives.

Gang content requires care to avoid stereotyping race, clothing, neighborhoods, or friendship groups and should distinguish association from criminal conduct.

Teacher Role

Historical standards required a certified teacher to remain present and participate during classroom lessons.

Teachers provided classroom knowledge, reinforced skills, assisted with management, and connected D.A.R.E. to the broader school curriculum.

Officer Role

The officer served as instructor, police representative, role model, mentor, and liaison between school and law enforcement.

This combination was central to D.A.R.E.’s popularity and to criticism concerning police authority in educational settings.

Officer Selection

Agencies historically selected experienced officers through screening and interviews involving police and school personnel.

Desirable qualities include communication ability, patience, reliability, sound judgement, classroom presence, knowledge of youth development, cultural competence, and a record consistent with school trust.

Eighty-Hour Officer Training

Current certification generally requires eighty hours of intensive instruction over two weeks.

Training covers curriculum content, facilitation, lesson planning, classroom management, learning modalities, youth development, communication, rehearsal, and evaluated practice teaching.

Mentor Officers

Experienced D.A.R.E. officers can receive additional preparation to train, observe, coach, and assess new instructors.

Mentor systems support fidelity but can also reproduce organizational assumptions unless external educators and researchers participate.

In-Service Training

Officers require continuing education concerning new substances, products, laws, research, curriculum revisions, technology, youth culture, teaching methods, and safety.

Initial certification alone cannot ensure current or accurate instruction over an entire career.

Curriculum Fidelity

Fidelity means delivering the intended lesson sequence, activities, content, and teaching methods with sufficient quality.

Unauthorized additions, scare stories, personal opinions, legal inaccuracies, or omitted interactive exercises can change the intervention being evaluated.

Officer Quality and Student Engagement

A 2023 fidelity study found that student engagement and teacher ratings of officer implementation were related to several targeted skills and intentions.

Elementary classrooms generally showed stronger engagement and implementation ratings than middle-school classrooms.

The study lacked a control group and could not establish program impact on substance use.

Informal Officer–Student Contact

Historical standards encouraged officers to interact during lunch, recess, assemblies, sports, and other school activities.

Supporters view informal contact as trust building; critics question whether police presence should extend beyond clearly defined educational duties.

Parent Component

D.A.R.E. developed parent sessions concerning communication, self-esteem, risk and protective factors, adolescent development, substance information, conflict, and community resources.

Parent involvement can reinforce lessons but participation often varies with work schedules, transportation, language, childcare, and trust.

Community Presentations

Police, educators, students, parents, and supporters have used public meetings, graduations, media, civic groups, and fundraising events to promote the program.

Public visibility helped create political support that sometimes exceeded the strength of evaluation evidence.

Early-Grade Lessons

D.A.R.E. developed shorter visits and safety lessons for younger children.

Current offerings can include prekindergarten through fourth-grade material concerning personal safety, medicines, help seeking, and basic social-emotional skills.

Original Junior-High Curriculum

Historical middle-school lessons reinforced refusal, anger management, conflict resolution, and personal choices.

They were created because one elementary intervention could not reasonably protect students throughout adolescence.

Original Senior-High Curriculum

Historical high-school material addressed high-risk, low-gain choices, anger, decision-making, and everyday adolescent situations.

Officer-teacher team teaching and follow-up lessons were intended to reinforce earlier instruction.

Special-Education Adaptations

Regional programmes adapted D.A.R.E. for students with learning disabilities and behavioural needs.

Effective adaptation requires accessible language, pacing, sensory support, disability expertise, individualized accommodation, and avoidance of treating disability as delinquency risk.

D.A.R.E. Plus

D.A.R.E. Plus or P.L.U.S. referred to after-school and reinforcement activity intended to keep students engaged in supervised educational, vocational, and recreational programs.

It demonstrated recognition that classroom lessons require supportive environments and opportunities.

D.A.R.E. Graduation

Classes commonly conclude with student essays, pledges, certificates, shirts, family attendance, speeches, and recognition ceremonies.

Graduation creates visibility and positive reinforcement but is not evidence that long-term behaviour changed.

Branding and Merchandise

Shirts, stickers, pencils, banners, mascots, police vehicles, and the red-and-black logo made D.A.R.E. unusually recognizable.

Branding strengthened identity and fundraising but also made the program part of popular culture, parody, nostalgia, and ironic drug-related fashion.

Daren the Lion

Daren the Lion became the program mascot and appeared in school materials, events, costumes, and youth outreach.

Mascot-based communication was intended to make police-led prevention more approachable for young children.

Relationship to “Just Say No”

D.A.R.E. and the national “Just Say No” campaign shared an abstinence-oriented anti-drug message and rose during the same political era.

They were not the same institution. D.A.R.E. was a structured police-taught curriculum, while “Just Say No” was a broader public campaign associated with First Lady Nancy Reagan.

Relationship to Red Ribbon Week

Schools and police departments often connect D.A.R.E. activities with Red Ribbon Week.

Red Ribbon Week is a separate annual prevention campaign commemorating DEA Special Agent Enrique Camarena.

Relationship to Community Policing

D.A.R.E. became a prominent form of non-enforcement police contact with youth.

Departments used it to create school relationships, make officers familiar, receive community information, and demonstrate a prevention role.

Whether such contact produces trust depends on broader police conduct outside the classroom.

Relationship to School Resource Officers

A D.A.R.E. officer is a curriculum instructor; a school resource officer is generally assigned broader school-safety, relationship, and law-enforcement duties.

One officer can perform both roles, but the functions, training, authority, and evaluation should remain distinct.

Police Presence in Schools

D.A.R.E. normalized regular uniformed police presence in elementary classrooms before school resource officers became widespread.

Supporters view this as constructive prevention and mentoring. Critics argue that it extended policing into education and encouraged children to accept police authority in noncriminal matters.

Student Disclosures

Drug and family discussions can cause students to disclose personal use, parental use, illegal activity, neglect, abuse, or safety concerns.

Schools and officers need clear protocols concerning confidentiality, mandatory reporting, immediate danger, questioning, referrals, and parental notification.

Self-Incrimination and Questioning

Classroom participation should not become investigative interrogation.

Officers should avoid soliciting identifiable admissions about students or family members and should explain limits of confidentiality in age-appropriate language.

Search and Disciplinary Boundaries

D.A.R.E. instruction does not change constitutional rules governing school searches, police searches, seizures, questioning, or discipline.

Information learned through a trusted educational relationship raises heightened fairness and legitimacy concerns if later used for unrelated enforcement.

Why the Curriculum Changed

By the 1990s and early 2000s, multiple evaluations found that the original program produced little or no lasting reduction in drug use.

Evidence-based funding rules, prevention-science developments, criticism from researchers, and declining institutional support created pressure for substantial redesign.

Retirement of the Original Curriculum

D.A.R.E. retired the curriculum most strongly associated with the seventeen-lesson “just say no” model.

CrimeSolutions treats the 1983–2009 version as a historical inactive program and warns that its rating does not describe current D.A.R.E. curricula.

keepin’ it REAL

keepin’ it REAL is a culturally grounded, communication-based prevention curriculum originally developed by university researchers for classroom delivery.

It teaches students to assess situations and use four resistance strategies: Refuse, Explain, Avoid, and Leave.

Refuse

Refuse means directly declining an offer or pressure.

Students practice clear language, confidence, and safe responses appropriate to the relationship and situation.

Explain

Explain means giving a reason for declining.

Reasons can concern health, goals, family expectations, law, sports, school, safety, or personal choice.

Avoid

Avoid means staying away from people, places, communications, or situations likely to create unwanted pressure or risk.

Avoidance should not be presented as possible in every home, school, or social environment.

Leave

Leave means exiting an unsafe or pressuring situation.

Students need realistic plans for transportation, trusted adults, communication, and immediate safety.

Middle-School Adoption in 2009

D.A.R.E. America adopted keepin’ it REAL for middle-school delivery in 2009.

The D.A.R.E. version differed from the original research version principally because certified law-enforcement officers rather than teachers delivered it.

Elementary keepin’ it REAL

D.A.R.E. America and curriculum researchers adapted keepin’ it REAL for fifth- and sixth-grade students.

The elementary version adds social-emotional learning, self-awareness, relationship skills, communication, decision-making, and management of responsibilities and challenges.

Ten Elementary Lessons

The elementary curriculum consists of ten approximately forty-five-minute lessons with family-talk activities.

Lessons use discussion, demonstration, role play, scenarios, student participation, and structured decision-making rather than extended officer lectures.

D.A.R.E. Decision-Making Model

Modern D.A.R.E. instruction uses a structured decision model that guides students to define a problem, assess choices, respond, and evaluate the outcome.

The model can be applied to drugs, bullying, peer conflict, social media, stress, and other situations.

Social-Emotional Learning

The modern curriculum addresses self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationships, communication, responsible decisions, and handling challenges.

Broadening the curriculum can improve relevance but makes drug-use outcomes only one part of program assessment.

Interactive Instruction

Modern lessons emphasize student discussion, practice, scenarios, group activity, role playing, and reflection.

Prevention research generally finds interactive social-skills approaches more promising than information-only lectures.

Family-Talk Activities

Take-home activities invite students to discuss lesson themes with parents or caregivers.

Family reinforcement can strengthen prevention, but assignments should accommodate foster care, absent caregivers, family substance use, language differences, and unsafe home situations.

Current Middle-School Program

The middle-school curriculum builds on REAL strategies and earlier decision-making lessons.

State program materials commonly describe ten to twelve forty-five-minute sessions for sixth- or seventh-grade students.

High-School and Supplemental Programming

D.A.R.E. has historically offered high-school material and currently permits local programmes to use age-appropriate supplemental lessons and safety content.

Current national implementation is less uniform at the high-school level than the elementary keepin’ it REAL core.

Opioid and Medication-Safety Lessons

Enhanced material addresses prescription medicine, over-the-counter drugs, opioids, medicine labels, safe use, storage, addiction, brain development, counterfeit pills, and progression from prescription misuse to illicit substances.

Current age-specific versions range from short early-childhood safety lessons to middle-school instruction.

Vaping Prevention

Middle-school vaping material teaches basic product information, nicotine risks, uncertainty concerning newer products, marketing, and decision-making.

Accurate instruction should distinguish nicotine vaping, cannabis vaping, adult smoking cessation, youth initiation, addiction, and product contamination.

Online and Social-Media Safety

Enhanced lessons address online drug sales, bullying, exploitation, fraud, manipulation, identity protection, mental health, interpersonal violence, and safer platform use.

The topic extends D.A.R.E. beyond drug education into broader digital safety.

Mental Health

Modern supplemental content can address stress, emotions, help seeking, social connection, bullying, and mental well-being.

Officers are not substitutes for licensed mental-health professionals and should use established referral and crisis protocols.

Bullying Prevention

D.A.R.E. lessons can include communication, empathy, bystander choices, reporting, conflict resolution, and online bullying.

School bullying policy should distinguish repeated power-based harm from ordinary disagreement and should avoid automatic criminalization.

Remote and Alternative Delivery

Some state programmes provide online lessons, after-school delivery, summer camps, or other flexible formats.

Changes in format can affect engagement and fidelity and should be evaluated separately from in-person classroom delivery.

Evaluation of the Original Curriculum

The original D.A.R.E. curriculum became one of the most frequently evaluated school-prevention programmes in the United States.

Research examined substance use, attitudes, knowledge, refusal skills, self-esteem, police attitudes, and other outcomes over periods ranging from immediate posttest to ten years.

The 1994 Meta-Analysis

A major meta-analysis found that D.A.R.E.’s short-term effect on drug-use behaviour was small, with a weighted mean effect size of approximately 0.06.

Effects were substantially smaller than those of interactive programmes teaching broader social and personal competencies.

Five-Year Follow-Up

A Kentucky longitudinal evaluation found no significant difference between D.A.R.E. and comparison students in marijuana use at the five-year follow-up.

Some short-term knowledge, attitude, and social-skill changes did not produce lasting behavioural differences.

Six-Year Follow-Up

A multilevel Illinois study following students from sixth through twelfth grade found no significant difference in recent or lifetime illicit-drug use at the six-year follow-up.

Immediate improvements in negative drug attitudes and social resistance skills diminished over time.

Ten-Year Follow-Up

A Colorado follow-up reported no significant differences in illicit-drug use, peer-pressure resistance, or self-esteem ten years after the elementary intervention.

GAO’s 2003 Review

GAO reviewed six long-term evaluations conducted in Kentucky, Colorado, and Illinois.

All six suggested that the original elementary curriculum had no statistically significant long-term effect on illicit-drug use.

Some short-term attitudinal and social-skill benefits faded over time.

The 2004 Updated Meta-Analysis

An updated meta-analysis concluded that the original D.A.R.E. programme did not produce meaningful reductions in alcohol, tobacco, or illicit-drug use.

Small effects on knowledge, attitudes, or social skills did not justify claims of strong behavioural prevention.

The 2009 Multivariate Meta-Analysis

A later review of twenty United States studies found small overall effects and examined variation among outcomes and study designs.

The debate demonstrated that statistical interpretation, study quality, outcome choice, and publication practices affected conclusions, but it did not establish a large durable drug-use effect for the original curriculum.

CrimeSolutions and the Original Program

CrimeSolutions identifies the reviewed D.A.R.E. programme as the version used from 1983 through 2009 and marks it inactive.

The profile reports no significant long-term differences on major drug-use outcomes in the studies reviewed.

It expressly warns that the profile does not describe the current curriculum.

Popularity and Evidence

D.A.R.E. received unusually high support from students, parents, school administrators, police, elected officials, and communities.

Satisfaction, visibility, and positive memories are relevant implementation outcomes but cannot substitute for controlled evidence concerning substance use.

The 1993 Graduate Survey

A Gallup survey of more than 2,000 graduates found that more than 90 percent believed D.A.R.E. helped them avoid alcohol and drugs.

Self-reported belief about programme value is not equivalent to a comparison-group measure of actual behaviour.

Evidence for keepin’ it REAL

The original teacher-delivered keepin’ it REAL curriculum has a prevention-research foundation distinct from the original D.A.R.E. programme.

Evidence concerning the D.A.R.E. officer-delivered adaptations must be evaluated separately because instructor, age group, adaptation, context, and implementation differ.

Middle-School Evidence

CrimeSolutions reviewed three studies of keepin’ it REAL and classified the evidence as inconclusive.

Evidence for a curriculum developed under one delivery model does not automatically establish effectiveness when police officers deliver an adapted version nationwide.

The 2017 Elementary Preliminary Test

A preliminary study reported promising changes in targeted mediators after certified officers delivered elementary keepin’ it REAL.

Preliminary psychosocial findings did not by themselves establish long-term reductions in substance use.

The 2023 Elementary Outcome Study

A 2023 PLOS ONE study evaluated two cohorts of fifth-grade students during the 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 school years.

It reported improvements in a targeted psychosocial measure and delayed onset of past-thirty-day alcohol use, drunkenness, and vaping compared with algorithmically generated virtual controls.

The 2023 Study Sample

A total of 6,122 students completed pretests.

Only 2,049 students—33.5 percent—had analysable immediate posttests, and 1,486 students—24.3 percent—had usable one-year follow-up surveys.

COVID-19 school disruption contributed to substantial attrition.

Virtual Control Group

The 2023 study used algorithmically generated control cases based on treatment students’ pretest psychosocial scores rather than a conventional randomized or contemporaneous untreated control group.

This method can estimate expected outcomes but is more assumption dependent than random assignment.

Study Funding and Independence

D.A.R.E. America funded the 2023 evaluation through a contract initiated in 2018.

The authors reported that the funder had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, publication decision, or manuscript preparation.

Funding source should still be disclosed when interpreting organizational programme research.

The 2023 Fidelity Study

A separate study examined officer implementation, teacher ratings, student engagement, and targeted mediators in elementary and middle-school classrooms.

It found stronger implementation and engagement patterns in elementary grades and more difficulty engaging middle-school students.

The pre-post study had no control group and could not determine causal substance-use effects.

Balanced Evidence Summary

The strongest conclusion concerning the original curriculum is that it did not create meaningful lasting reductions in substance use.

The modern programme is materially different and has more promising elementary evidence, but the officer-delivered version still needs independent randomized or strong quasi-experimental replication, long-term follow-up, implementation analysis, and transparent reporting.

Use of the Term “Evidence Based”

D.A.R.E. organizations frequently describe keepin’ it REAL as evidence based because the curriculum was developed and tested through prevention research.

Researchers distinguish evidence for the original teacher-delivered curriculum, evidence for adapted curricula, evidence for officer delivery, and evidence for specific behavioural outcomes.

The term should therefore be accompanied by the version, population, instructor, outcome, and quality of evidence.

Most Recent Published Scale Estimate

A 2023 peer-reviewed article reported that more than 6,000 law-enforcement agencies delivered D.A.R.E. to more than 1.2 million K–12 students in more than 10,000 United States communities.

It estimated that twenty-nine international programmes reached another three million students annually.

These are published estimates rather than a verified fiscal year 2026 federal census.

Current Funding Status

D.A.R.E. continues through varied local police and school budgets, nonprofit support, state associations, donations, foundations, training fees, community fundraising, and other lawful sources.

There is no current dedicated nationwide federal D.A.R.E. entitlement or one uniform national appropriation paying for every local programme.

Local Adoption Process

A community ordinarily needs agreement between a law-enforcement agency and school system, selection of an instructor, officer certification, scheduling, curriculum materials, funding, and ongoing supervision.

Written agreements should define educational and law-enforcement roles, costs, records, complaints, student disclosures, officer conduct, evaluation, and termination.

School Governance

School boards and administrators remain responsible for curriculum approval, student welfare, instructional time, parent notice, accessibility, teacher involvement, and compliance with education law.

Police departments should not select school curriculum unilaterally.

Parent Notice and Consent

Schools should explain lesson content, instructor role, surveys, take-home activities, limits of confidentiality, and options under applicable state and local policy.

Sensitive questions or research surveys can require additional parental notice or consent.

Student Voice

Students can provide feedback concerning relevance, accuracy, officer conduct, classroom safety, engagement, and whether the lessons reflect actual pressures.

Graduation enthusiasm should not replace anonymous student evaluation.

Scientific and Legal Accuracy

Drug information, legal claims, health effects, prevalence, risk, and terminology should be reviewed regularly by qualified educators, clinicians, researchers, and legal specialists.

Officers should avoid absolute claims unsupported by evidence and should distinguish federal law from state law.

Age Appropriateness

Prevention content should match students’ developmental stage, likely exposure, vocabulary, cognitive ability, and real-world decision-making.

Presenting detailed information about unfamiliar drugs can be educational, but poorly designed instruction can create confusion, curiosity, or fear.

Cultural Adaptation

keepin’ it REAL was originally developed as a culturally grounded curriculum.

Local adaptation should preserve core mechanisms while reflecting language, community norms, family structures, Indigenous sovereignty, immigration context, disability, rural life, and student experience.

Language Access

Students and families with limited English proficiency need translated materials, interpretation, bilingual instruction, and culturally understandable examples.

A uniform national script can exclude families when language access is treated as optional.

Disability Access

Lessons, activities, videos, workbooks, websites, and ceremonies must be accessible to students with disabilities where required.

Officers need support from special educators and related-service professionals rather than relying on enforcement experience alone.

Race and Equity

D.A.R.E. grew during a drug war that produced large racial disparities in surveillance, arrest, sentencing, and incarceration.

Police-led prevention can be experienced differently by children whose families and communities have experienced discriminatory enforcement.

Evaluation should measure trust, belonging, disciplinary consequences, representation, and student safety across racial and socioeconomic groups.

Police Legitimacy

Friendly classroom contact can improve student attitudes toward individual officers.

Durable legitimacy depends on fairness, accountability, respectful treatment, lawful conduct, and consistency between classroom messages and policing outside school.

Police-Propaganda Critique

Historians and critics argue that D.A.R.E. did more than teach drug prevention: it encouraged children to identify police with safety, morality, and authority during an era of punitive drug enforcement.

Supporters respond that positive non-enforcement contact is a legitimate form of community policing and youth mentorship.

Who Should Teach Prevention?

Police bring authority, practical experience, visibility, and potential mentoring.

Teachers and health educators bring pedagogy, developmental knowledge, continuity, and professional expertise in instruction.

Clinicians and prevention specialists bring health expertise. A strong programme should define why an officer is the appropriate instructor and how educational and health expertise are incorporated.

Opportunity Cost

Officer salaries, training, classroom time, materials, and ceremonies have alternative uses.

Communities should compare D.A.R.E. with other evidence-supported prevention, mental-health, family, mentoring, after-school, and public-health interventions.

Limits of a One-Time Elementary Program

Substance-use risk changes substantially from elementary school through high school.

A short fifth-grade intervention is unlikely to create durable effects without reinforcement through families, schools, healthcare, policy, peer environments, and developmentally appropriate later programming.

Complex Causes of Youth Substance Use

Youth use is influenced by availability, family use, trauma, mental health, peers, marketing, social norms, pain, stress, poverty, school connection, identity, genetics, and community opportunity.

Refusal skills address only part of this causal structure.

Abstinence Orientation

D.A.R.E. seeks to prevent initiation and promote drug-free youth development.

Clear prevention goals can protect children, but instruction should avoid shame and should provide accurate help-seeking information for students already exposed to substances.

Harm Reduction and Immediate Safety

Modern youth education can include medication safety, counterfeit-pill awareness, overdose recognition, naloxone, impaired-driving prevention, and help seeking without endorsing substance use.

Prevention and harm reduction address different points on the risk continuum and can coexist.

Students Already Using Substances

A universal classroom can include students who already use nicotine, alcohol, cannabis, prescription drugs, or other substances.

Lessons should avoid public shaming and should provide confidential pathways to qualified health, counselling, treatment, and family support.

Family Substance Use

Students may live with parents or caregivers affected by substance-use disorder, recovery, overdose, incarceration, or prescribed medications.

Simplistic moral language can stigmatize families and prevent disclosure or help seeking.

Changing Drug Laws

Cannabis legalization, prescription rules, tobacco regulation, vaping products, and local ordinances have changed substantially since 1983.

Officers must explain differences among legal adult use, illegal youth use, medical use, misuse, federal law, and state law accurately.

Officer Screening and Misconduct

Because D.A.R.E. officers work closely with children, agencies need background review, supervision, complaint systems, boundaries, mandatory-reporting knowledge, and removal procedures.

Certification does not replace ordinary personnel accountability.

Uniforms and Weapons in the Classroom

Traditional D.A.R.E. delivery uses uniformed officers, who may carry duty equipment according to agency policy.

Schools should assess student comfort, safety, disability and trauma concerns, local law, and whether full tactical equipment is educationally necessary.

Surveys and Student Data

Programme evaluation can collect knowledge, attitudes, intentions, police perceptions, engagement, and substance-use reports.

Research requires clear consent, confidentiality, secure storage, limited access, independent analysis, and protection from disciplinary or law-enforcement use.

Implementation Measures

Appropriate measures include lesson completion, fidelity, teacher participation, officer quality, student engagement, accessibility, family activity, referrals, complaints, and curriculum accuracy.

Outcome Measures

Relevant outcomes include initiation and frequency of alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, and other drug use; refusal and communication skills; decision-making; perceived norms; school connection; help seeking; bullying; safety; and attitudes toward police.

Outcomes should be measured long enough to determine whether immediate changes persist.

Independent Evaluation

Independent researchers should be able to publish unfavourable, null, and favourable findings.

Programme developers should disclose funding and involvement, preregister major outcomes where possible, release methods, and support replication.

Version-Specific Evaluation

Research must name the curriculum version, year, grade, instructor, training, lesson dose, setting, and adaptations.

Conclusions about the 1983–2009 curriculum should not be applied automatically to keepin’ it REAL, and evidence for teacher delivery should not be applied automatically to officer delivery.

Major Achievements

  • Created the most recognizable school drug-prevention brand in United States history.
  • Built sustained partnerships between schools and law-enforcement agencies.
  • Trained tens of thousands of officers in classroom instruction and youth communication.
  • Reached millions of students in the United States and internationally.
  • Made refusal skills, decision-making, and peer influence prominent prevention topics.
  • Created positive non-enforcement contact between many students and officers.
  • Mobilized parents, schools, businesses, civic groups, and police around youth prevention.
  • Generated an unusually large research literature on school prevention.
  • Retired its most criticized curriculum and adopted a more interactive research-based model.
  • Developed contemporary lessons concerning opioids, medicines, vaping, and online safety.

Limitations and Failures

  • The original curriculum did not produce meaningful lasting reductions in drug use.
  • Short-term attitude and social-skill gains commonly faded.
  • Public popularity often outpaced scientific evidence.
  • The programme relied heavily on one-time elementary delivery.
  • Officer quality and local implementation varied.
  • Information could become exaggerated, moralistic, or outdated.
  • Police instruction created trust, privacy, and role-conflict concerns.
  • Branding and graduations could be mistaken for behavioural effectiveness.
  • Federal and local resources supported a programme with weak original evidence.
  • Current officer-delivered curricula still need stronger independent long-term evaluation.
  • Middle-school engagement and implementation appear more difficult than elementary delivery.
  • Universal refusal lessons do not address all structural and clinical causes of substance use.

Current Status as of August 3, 2026

D.A.R.E. remains active as a nonprofit-supported network of locally operated police-school programmes.

The historical seventeen-lesson programme is inactive. Current delivery centres on elementary and middle-school keepin’ it REAL, early-grade safety lessons, and enhanced instruction concerning medications, opioids, vaping, online safety, and related topics.

There is no single federal D.A.R.E. agency, nationwide entitlement, mandatory school requirement, or current uniform federal grant paying for all local programmes.

Current reach varies by state and community, and the most recent published nationwide estimates should not be treated as a verified 2026 census.

How D.A.R.E. Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the curriculum version, grade, year, instructor, officer training, lesson dose, school context, teacher role, student population, comparison condition, follow-up period, attrition, funding source, and outcomes.

Important distinctions include:

  • D.A.R.E. America versus a local D.A.R.E. programme;
  • the nonprofit organization versus a government agency;
  • historical federal support versus a permanent federal program;
  • the 1983–2009 curriculum versus keepin’ it REAL;
  • teacher-delivered keepin’ it REAL versus officer-delivered adaptations;
  • elementary versus middle-school implementation;
  • knowledge and attitudes versus actual substance use;
  • immediate posttest effects versus long-term effects;
  • student satisfaction versus behavioural effectiveness;
  • programme fidelity versus instructor charisma;
  • police trust outcomes versus drug-prevention outcomes;
  • randomized controls versus virtual or algorithmic controls;
  • students retained in follow-up versus the full baseline sample; and
  • national programme claims versus results from one state or community.

Why D.A.R.E. Matters

D.A.R.E. shaped how generations of Americans understood drug prevention, police officers, peer pressure, and the War on Drugs.

Its scale demonstrated the power of a simple message, recognizable brand, enthusiastic local institutions, federal support, and standardized training.

Its evaluation record demonstrated that popularity and good intentions do not establish behavioural effectiveness.

The programme’s transition to keepin’ it REAL is an important example of an established institution responding to scientific criticism by replacing its core curriculum rather than merely defending the original model.

D.A.R.E. remains central to debates over police in schools, prevention science, student confidentiality, community trust, public funding, and the standards required before a programme is called evidence based.

Summary

Drug Abuse Resistance Education is a police-led school prevention programme founded by the Los Angeles Police Department and Los Angeles Unified School District in 1983.

Ten LAPD officers began the pilot in September 1983. Daryl Gates and Harry Handler were the principal police and school founders.

The original programme used uniformed officers to teach seventeen weekly lessons in fifth or sixth grade concerning drugs, peer pressure, refusal, self-esteem, decision-making, conflict, and gangs.

D.A.R.E. expanded rapidly through national publicity, regional training centres, BJA assistance, Byrne formula-grant subawards, school-prevention resources, local budgets, and nonprofit support.

D.A.R.E. America became the national nonprofit responsible for the official brand, curriculum, training standards, and programme network.

During the 1990s, D.A.R.E. operated in roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of United States school districts and became the nation’s most visible drug-education programme.

Long-term evaluations and meta-analyses found that the original curriculum produced little or no lasting reduction in alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, or illicit-drug use. Some immediate attitude and social-skill benefits diminished over time.

D.A.R.E. retired the original model and adopted keepin’ it REAL for middle-school students in 2009. An elementary adaptation followed during the early 2010s.

Modern lessons use the REAL strategies—Refuse, Explain, Avoid, and Leave—and place greater emphasis on interactive decision-making and social-emotional learning.

A 2023 elementary study reported delayed onset of alcohol use, drunkenness, and vaping, but COVID-related attrition and use of virtual rather than conventional controls limit causal certainty.

D.A.R.E. remains active as of August 3, 2026 through a nonprofit and local police-school network. It is not a federal agency, entitlement, mandatory curriculum, or unified nationwide federal grant.

Its legacy combines extraordinary public recognition and police-school partnership with one of the clearest historical warnings that popularity, branding, and institutional support cannot substitute for rigorous long-term evidence.
