Federal Prison Construction Grant Program

The Violent Offender Incarceration and Truth-in-Sentencing Incentive Formula Grant Program created by the 1994 crime law to help states expand correctional capacity and encourage longer time served by people convicted of violent offenses.

What Was the Federal Prison Construction Grant Program?

The term “Federal Prison Construction Grant Program” commonly refers to the Violent Offender Incarceration and Truth-in-Sentencing Incentive Formula Grant Program created by the 1994 federal crime law.

The program did not principally construct federal prisons operated by the Federal Bureau of Prisons. It provided federal money to state and territorial governments for state prisons, juvenile correctional facilities, local jails, alternative correctional facilities, and related capacity.

Federal grants were intended to ensure that prison space existed for people convicted of violent crimes and to encourage state laws requiring longer portions of imposed sentences to be served.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s creation, 1996 amendments, statutory structure, two grant streams, eligibility standards, formula, matching requirements, allowable facilities, jail subgrants, drug testing, environmental review, state implementation, funding history, sentencing effects, prison-capacity effects, fiscal consequences, racial implications, controversies, and termination.

It distinguishes VOI/TIS from Federal Bureau of Prisons construction, the State Criminal Alien Assistance Program, tribal jail-construction grants, correctional boot-camp grants, Residential Substance Abuse Treatment grants, and modern correctional assistance.

Geographic Scope

All fifty states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, the United States Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands were eligible to apply.

States could form multistate compacts, but each participating state had to qualify individually and could not receive funds both individually and through the compact.

States could make subgrants to state agencies, counties, municipalities, and other units of local government for eligible correctional projects.

Historical Context

The program emerged from the late-twentieth-century expansion of incarceration, determinate sentencing, mandatory minimums, habitual-offender laws, parole restrictions, the War on Drugs, and political demands for certainty and severity in punishment.

State prison populations had grown rapidly during the 1980s and early 1990s, creating crowding, litigation, emergency releases, fiscal pressure, and demand for new beds.

Federal policymakers sought to encourage states to reserve prison capacity for violent offenders while reducing the difference between sentences announced in court and time actually served.

State Prison Growth Before 1994

State incarceration expanded because of increased admissions, longer sentences, drug enforcement, parole revocation, mandatory penalties, and policy changes limiting release.

Capacity frequently lagged behind population, producing double-celling, temporary housing, prison camps, contracted beds, and court-ordered reforms.

Truth-in-Sentencing Movement

Truth-in-sentencing policies sought to make the prison term actually served more closely resemble the judicial sentence announced.

States pursued the concept through determinate sentencing, abolition or restriction of parole, mandatory minimum time served, earned-credit limits, sentencing guidelines, and release rules.

There was no single state model, which required federal law to recognize several paths to the 85-percent standard.

Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994

President Bill Clinton signed the 1994 crime law on September 13, 1994.

The law created a broad prison title intended to provide billions of dollars for correctional capacity, criminal-alien incarceration, tribal facilities, federal-prisoner agreements, and related programs.

The initial truth-in-sentencing construction provisions were revised substantially before major formula awards began.

The 1996 Amendments

Congress rewrote the prison-construction formula and eligibility rules in April 1996.

The revised statute created separate Violent Offender Incarceration and Truth-in-Sentencing streams, established the three-tier VOI formula, clarified the 85-percent standard, authorized local jail subgrants, and created related set-asides.

Most awards discussed as VOI/TIS were made under this amended structure.

Federal Administration

The Office of Justice Programs originally administered VOI/TIS through the Corrections Program Office.

After an OJP reorganization in November 2002, remaining administration and closeout responsibility moved to the Bureau of Justice Assistance.

Federal responsibilities included formula calculation, eligibility review, grant awards, environmental review, reporting guidance, monitoring, technical assistance, and final disposition of funded facilities.

State Administration

Each governor designated a state office to apply for and administer the formula award.

State administering agencies coordinated corrections departments, juvenile agencies, local governments, budget offices, construction authorities, environmental agencies, and sentencing-policy officials.

States selected projects, supplied matching funds, monitored subgrants, reported progress, and maintained federal interest in funded property.

Two Grant Streams

From fiscal years 1996 through 2001, one-half of formula funds was available for Violent Offender Incarceration grants and one-half for Truth-in-Sentencing Incentive grants.

States could qualify for VOI without satisfying the full federal 85-percent truth-in-sentencing standard.

TIS funding created the stronger incentive for adopting or maintaining laws requiring violent offenders to serve most of the imposed term.

Violent Offender Incarceration Grants

VOI grants supported states that implemented or promised correctional policies ensuring that violent offenders served a substantial portion of imposed sentences.

The state also had to assure that punishment was sufficiently severe and that time served was related to the determination that the person was violent and to protection of the public.

VOI Tier One

Tier One established the minimum VOI eligibility requirement.

A state supplied assurances that it had implemented or would implement policies, including truth-in-sentencing laws, designed to produce substantial time served and sufficiently severe punishment for violent adult and juvenile offenders.

VOI Tier Two

A state receiving Tier One funds could receive additional Tier Two money by demonstrating that, since 1993, it had:

  • increased the percentage of people arrested for Part 1 violent crimes who were sentenced to prison; or
  • increased the average prison time actually served or the average percentage of sentence served by people convicted of Part 1 violent crimes.

VOI Tier Three

Tier Three rewarded states demonstrating stronger combined changes in incarceration and time served.

Fifteen percent of annual VOI funding was reserved for states satisfying the Tier Three conditions.

The remaining 85 percent supported Tiers One and Two, subject to a statutory cap preventing one state from receiving more than 9 percent of those funds.

Truth-in-Sentencing Incentive Grants

TIS grants supported states whose sentencing and release laws caused people convicted of Part 1 violent crimes to serve at least 85 percent of the sentence or an approved equivalent.

The federal standard focused on time actually served and excluded credits or incentives representing time not spent in confinement.

The 85-Percent Rule

A qualifying state could require each covered violent offender to serve at least 85 percent of the imposed sentence.

Alternatively, the state could demonstrate that covered offenders served an average of at least 85 percent.

A state with an enacted but not yet implemented law could qualify if full implementation occurred within three years.

Indeterminate-Sentencing Alternatives

Congress recognized that some states used parole boards, sentence ranges, or sentencing-and-release guidelines.

A state practising indeterminate sentencing on April 26, 1996 could qualify by demonstrating that covered offenders served an average of at least 85 percent of the prison term established by state guidelines or of the maximum term allowed by the court’s sentence.

Part 1 Violent Crime

Program regulations defined Part 1 violent crimes as murder and nonnegligent manslaughter, forcible rape, robbery, and aggravated assault as reported through the FBI Uniform Crime Reports.

The category served both as an eligibility benchmark and as a statistical basis for allocation.

Violent Juvenile Offenders

The program included people adjudicated delinquent for acts that would constitute Part 1 violent crimes if committed by adults.

States could construct or expand juvenile correctional capacity and facilities separating juveniles under adult-court jurisdiction from adult prisoners.

General Eligibility Requirements

Beyond the VOI or TIS sentencing standard, states had to:

  • submit an approved application;
  • demonstrate the ability to operate funded facilities after construction;
  • provide the required nonfederal match;
  • recognize the rights and needs of crime victims;
  • meet drug-testing and intervention requirements after their effective date;
  • report deaths and other required correctional data;
  • comply with civil-rights and environmental laws; and
  • use property for authorized correctional purposes.

Ability to Operate New Capacity

A state had to demonstrate that it could pay the continuing cost of staffing, food, health care, utilities, maintenance, transportation, programming, and security for new or expanded facilities.

Federal grants paid capital and limited related costs but did not create a permanent operating subsidy.

Matching Requirement

Federal funds ordinarily could cover no more than 90 percent of an eligible project.

The state or subrecipient supplied at least 10 percent from nonfederal sources.

Match requirements increased local commitment but also favoured states able to finance construction and future operations.

Allocation Formula

The statute divided funding between VOI and TIS streams and then used formulas based on minimum allocations, violent-crime measures, prison admissions, time served, and demonstrated policy changes.

Formula calculations were not competitive rankings of construction proposals.

Project selection occurred after a state received its calculated award.

Minimum Allocations

The formula protected states and territories with small populations or low reported violent-crime totals through statutory minimums.

Minimums broadened national participation but reduced the share distributed strictly according to crime and correctional data.

Regional Compacts

States could combine awards through a regional compact to develop shared correctional capacity.

Each state had to qualify individually, and no state could receive both an individual and compact award for the same period.

Regional facilities raised questions of distance from families, interstate authority, cost allocation, classification, and legal responsibility.

Primary Allowable Purpose

The principal authorized use was construction or expansion of correctional facilities to increase bed capacity for people convicted of Part 1 violent crimes.

The program treated additional physical capacity as a necessary complement to sentencing policies that increased time served.

Temporary and Permanent Facilities

Funds could build or expand temporary or permanent correctional facilities.

The statute expressly identified facilities on military bases, prison barges, and boot camps as possible capacity options.

Temporary capacity could free conventional prison space for violent offenders even when the temporary facility housed other people.

Facilities for Nonviolent Offenders

VOI/TIS did not require every funded bed to hold a violent offender directly.

States could construct capacity for nonviolent offenders or criminal aliens when moving those populations freed existing secure prison space for Part 1 violent offenders.

Local Jails

The amended statute expressly allowed states to build or expand jails.

Local jail projects could address pretrial detention, short sentences, state-prison backlogs, violent offenders, juveniles under adult jurisdiction, and crowding.

Local-Government Subgrants

A state could reserve up to 15 percent of its annual award for counties and units of local government.

Subgrants could construct, develop, expand, modify, or improve jails and other correctional facilities.

The state remained responsible for federal compliance and monitoring.

Juvenile Correctional Facilities

Funds could support facilities for juveniles adjudicated for violent conduct and facilities separating juveniles under adult criminal-court jurisdiction from adult prisoners.

Several states dedicated substantial or complete annual allocations to juvenile facilities.

Separation of Juveniles from Adults

Later statutory language expressly allowed alteration of adult prisons or jails to create separate facilities for juveniles under adult-court jurisdiction.

Separation addressed safety, victimization, supervision, education, and compliance with juvenile-justice standards.

Correctional Boot Camps

Boot camps were short-term correctional programs modelled on military discipline, physical training, strict schedules, education, and treatment.

The 1994 law created a separate fiscal year 1995 boot-camp initiative, while the VOI/TIS authority also recognized boot camps as alternative capacity.

Evaluations generally found that boot camps did not consistently reduce recidivism without strong treatment and aftercare.

Facilities on Military Bases

Congress permitted use of closed or underused military installations for correctional facilities.

Conversion could reduce acquisition costs but required environmental remediation, security redesign, staffing, transportation, and community approval.

Prison Barges

The statute expressly recognized prison barges as temporary or permanent correctional capacity.

Barge facilities raised concerns concerning evacuation, health care, isolation, fire safety, access to courts and counsel, and environmental conditions.

Private Correctional Facilities

States could use VOI/TIS funds for privatized facilities when federal and state requirements were satisfied.

Public funding of private construction or operation required contracts addressing ownership, federal interest, accountability, standards, continued use, and disposition.

Renovation and Security Hardening

Eligible projects included renovation, expansion, modification, security upgrades, perimeter improvement, control rooms, housing-unit conversion, and other work increasing usable secure capacity.

Not every award constructed an entirely new institution.

Drug-Testing and Intervention Requirement

Beginning in fiscal year 1999, a state could not receive VOI/TIS funds unless it had implemented a program of controlled-substance testing and intervention for appropriate categories of convicted offenders.

The program applied during incarceration and post-incarceration supervision and required sanctions for positive tests, including possible denial or revocation of release.

Ten-Percent Drug Program Option

States could use up to 10 percent of VOI/TIS awards for:

  • offender drug testing;
  • intervention and treatment during incarceration;
  • testing and treatment during post-release supervision;
  • sanctions and supervision connected to positive tests; and
  • required reporting concerning prison drug use.

Treatment and Testing Versus Construction

The drug-program amendment added a limited treatment and supervision component to an overwhelmingly capital-focused program.

The 10-percent ceiling ensured that most VOI/TIS funding remained directed toward correctional capacity and facility-related purposes.

Crime-Victim Rights and Needs

State applications had to provide assurances that the state had implemented or would implement policies recognizing the rights and needs of crime victims.

The requirement reflected the broader victim-rights provisions of the 1994 crime law.

Deaths in Custody Reporting

TIS eligibility included quarterly reporting concerning deaths of people in custody under federal guidelines.

VOI/TIS reporting helped create an early national correctional mortality data system later replaced and expanded by subsequent Death in Custody Reporting Acts.

Environmental Review

Prison construction and expansion required compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act and related federal environmental laws.

OJP classified projects according to whether they were categorically excluded or required an environmental assessment or environmental impact statement.

Environmental Assessment

Projects with potential but uncertain environmental effects could require an environmental assessment.

Review examined land use, water, waste, traffic, air quality, noise, wetlands, historic resources, environmental justice, utilities, and community effects.

Environmental Impact Statement

A major project expected to have significant environmental effects could require a full environmental impact statement.

Construction could not proceed with federal participation until the required process was completed.

Public Participation in Environmental Review

Environmental procedures required public notice and participation where applicable.

Local debates commonly involved siting, land use, employment, traffic, infrastructure, environmental burden, and the social effects of locating a prison in a rural or economically distressed community.

Federal Interest in Funded Facilities

Federal grant funds created a continuing federal interest in property constructed or improved with VOI/TIS money.

States could not freely sell, close, convert, or dispose of funded facilities without addressing federal grant conditions.

Use and Disposition Requirements

A funded facility was expected to remain in authorized correctional use for its useful life or for the period required by grant rules.

Closure or conversion could require federal approval, repayment, transfer, continued public use, or another disposition remedy.

Construction Management

States and subrecipients were responsible for design, procurement, contractor selection, construction oversight, change orders, prevailing requirements where applicable, inspection, and completion.

Cost overruns and delays were borne primarily by state and local governments.

Operating Costs

Construction represented only a portion of long-term prison cost.

Staffing, pensions, health care, food, utilities, maintenance, transport, programming, litigation, and capital repair continued for decades after the federal grant ended.

The program therefore encouraged permanent state obligations with temporary federal assistance.

Funding History

Congress appropriated more than $3 billion for VOI/TIS and related correctional set-asides through fiscal year 2001.

OJP awarded $2,707,534,780 directly to states and territories for correctional construction, expansion, and renovation from fiscal years 1996 through 2001.

No new program appropriation followed fiscal year 2001.

Related Appropriation Set-Asides

Not every dollar associated with the prison title became a VOI/TIS state construction grant.

Funds were also set aside for:

  • State Criminal Alien Assistance Program reimbursements;
  • payments for holding federal prisoners in state and local facilities;
  • correctional facilities on tribal lands;
  • boot camps;
  • training and technical assistance;
  • environmental review; and
  • program administration.

Authorized, Appropriated, and Awarded Amounts

Contemporary accounts frequently cited approximately $9.7 billion or $10.3 billion for prison-related provisions of the 1994 crime law and later amendments.

Authorization established a legal ceiling or policy commitment; appropriations supplied actual budget authority; awards transferred money to grantees.

Only approximately $2.7 billion was ultimately awarded through the VOI/TIS state and territorial formula grants.

Large State Awards

States with large populations, violent-crime totals, prison admissions, and qualifying sentencing systems received the largest cumulative amounts.

California received the largest cumulative award, followed by other large states including Florida, Texas, New York, Illinois, Ohio, and Pennsylvania.

Each state selected different combinations of adult prisons, juvenile facilities, jails, renovations, and related activities.

Examples of State Uses

State projects included:

  • new medium- and maximum-security prisons;
  • housing-unit additions;
  • juvenile correctional centres;
  • county jail construction and expansion;
  • conversion of military or other public property;
  • private-prison capacity;
  • security and perimeter upgrades;
  • female correctional capacity;
  • medical and special-needs housing;
  • drug testing and treatment;
  • environmental and planning costs; and
  • administration and reporting.

California

California received the largest cumulative VOI/TIS allocation.

The state used funds for state prison projects and local correctional facilities while confronting rapid population growth, court litigation, overcrowding, and the effects of sentencing enhancements.

Texas

Texas expanded prison capacity dramatically during the 1990s through state financing and federal assistance.

VOI/TIS funding supplemented a much larger state construction program associated with sentencing, parole, and correctional restructuring.

New York

New York used federal funds within an established state prison system shaped by violent-offender and drug sentencing policy.

The state’s experience also illustrated that major prison growth and sentencing reform predated the 1994 federal incentive.

Juvenile Facility Examples

Idaho, Kansas, Maine, and South Dakota reported plans to use their entire fiscal year 1997 VOI/TIS allocations for juvenile facilities.

Other jurisdictions combined adult and juvenile projects or altered adult facilities to provide legally separate juvenile housing.

Territorial Projects

Territories used grants to address severe crowding, deteriorated infrastructure, limited local tax bases, and geographically isolated correctional systems.

Projects included new facilities, female and juvenile capacity, perimeter security, housing expansion, and renovation of existing institutions.

End of New Appropriations

Congress made no new VOI/TIS appropriation after fiscal year 2001.

The end reflected changing federal priorities, lower crime rates, concern about incarceration costs, competing homeland-security spending, and the incomplete implementation of the much larger sums originally authorized.

Continued Spending Through 2009

Correctional construction projects often required years for planning, environmental review, procurement, construction, litigation, and final inspection.

Some states continued drawing and spending prior-year balances through September 2009.

The final federal implementation report was transmitted to Congress in February 2012.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

The VOI/TIS formula grant program is inactive and receives no current appropriation.

BJA maintains an archived program page, publications, final reports, and property-disposition guidance.

The statutory provisions remain codified at 34 U.S.C. 12101–12108, and 28 C.F.R. Part 91 remains published, but neither fact indicates that new prison-construction grants are currently available.

Adoption of Truth-in-Sentencing Laws

By the end of the 1990s, twenty-eight states and the District of Columbia met the federal TIS criteria and received TIS grants.

Thirteen additional states used some form of truth in sentencing without meeting or using the federal standard.

Several qualifying states had adopted relevant policies before the 1994 grant program.

Limited Federal Influence on State Sentencing Reform

NIJ-sponsored research concluded that the federal incentive modestly influenced state truth-in-sentencing reform.

Many major sentencing changes preceded the federal law, and some post-1994 changes would likely have occurred without construction grants.

Federal funding was more often one factor in incremental policy adjustment than the sole cause of reform.

Time Served

Truth-in-sentencing policies generally increased the expected percentage of a sentence served.

Time served by released violent offenders increased in many TIS states after 1993, although sentence lengths, admissions, release practices, and offense mix also changed.

By the end of the 1990s, differences between TIS and non-TIS states were smaller than the policy label suggested.

Sentence Lengths

Requiring a larger percentage of a sentence to be served does not necessarily increase the sentence imposed.

Judges, prosecutors, and legislatures can adjust nominal sentence lengths when parole and credits are restricted.

Evaluation found that mean violent-offense sentence lengths declined in TIS states from 1994 to 2001 while time served increased.

Effect on Prison Capacity

States used federal grants to create or improve prison and jail beds, but the national increase attributable to the grants was modest relative to total state capacity.

By 1999, most states had increased capacity by less than 4 percent through the program, although many projects remained incomplete.

Effect on Prison Populations

Truth-in-sentencing reforms could increase prison populations by reducing release and increasing time served.

Research found that crime rates, arrests, admissions, sentence lengths, revocations, and other policies were often more important drivers of population growth than the federal grant alone.

Prison Crowding

New capacity relieved crowding in some facilities and allowed closure or renovation of obsolete units.

Capacity expansion could also reduce pressure to reform admissions and sentencing, allowing populations to grow until new beds became crowded.

Incapacitation

Supporters argued that longer confinement of serious violent offenders prevented crimes that would have occurred during earlier release.

Incapacitation benefits depend on the individual’s future offending risk, age, offense pattern, sentence length, and the extent to which another person replaces criminal activity.

Deterrence

Truth-in-sentencing advocates argued that predictable punishment could deter violent crime and improve public understanding of sentencing.

Research generally finds that certainty of punishment has a stronger deterrent relationship than marginal increases in sentence severity.

Public Confidence and Sentence Transparency

The program responded to public concern that court-imposed sentences overstated actual confinement.

Clear release rules can improve transparency, but an 85-percent requirement does not eliminate complexity involving jail credit, consecutive sentences, parole supervision, compassionate release, or sentence calculation.

Institutional Incentives and Rehabilitation

Restricting good-time and parole discretion can reduce incentives for programme participation and institutional behaviour.

States retained different earned-credit, disciplinary, and release systems, and the federal standard excluded time not actually served when calculating 85 percent.

Ageing Prison Populations

Longer prison terms contributed to growth in older incarcerated populations and increased medical, disability, accessibility, and end-of-life costs.

Many violent offenders age beyond their highest-risk years before completing long sentences.

Women and Prison Construction

Women represented a smaller share of the prison population, and many states historically lacked appropriate female capacity.

Some grants expanded or renovated women’s facilities, but sentencing and construction policy was often designed around male populations.

Juveniles and Adult-Court Transfer

The 1990s also saw laws making it easier to prosecute young people in adult court.

VOI/TIS funding could build separate juvenile space within adult systems, but the grant structure did not resolve broader concerns about developmental differences, safety, education, family access, and racial disparity.

Race and Mass Incarceration

The program operated within a criminal-justice system marked by substantial racial disparities in arrest, charging, sentencing, and imprisonment.

Longer time served and increased capacity therefore had unequal effects on Black, Latino, Native, and other communities disproportionately represented in state prisons.

The statute did not require a racial-impact analysis before awarding construction funds.

Rural Prison Development

Many states located new prisons in rural communities seeking employment and public investment.

Construction could produce jobs and infrastructure but also create dependence on incarceration, strain services, alter land use, and place incarcerated people far from families, lawyers, and reentry communities.

Private-Prison Growth

The 1990s prison boom increased demand for private prison construction and operation.

VOI/TIS permitted privatized facilities, connecting federal construction policy to debates over profit incentives, contract oversight, safety, transparency, and government accountability.

Fiscal Federalism

Federal grants lowered the initial state cost of prison expansion while states retained long-term operating responsibility.

This structure encouraged capital decisions whose future costs were borne by state taxpayers after federal funding ended.

Opportunity Costs

Money used to build and operate prison beds could not be used simultaneously for education, health care, housing, treatment, victim services, community supervision, prevention, or infrastructure.

Construction created recurring costs that could crowd out future alternatives even when crime and prison populations declined.

Effects on Local Jails

Local subgrants helped counties replace deteriorated facilities and address crowding.

Jail expansion could also increase local detention capacity, pretrial incarceration, boarding of state or federal prisoners, and operating costs.

Distinction from Federal Bureau of Prisons Construction

The Federal Bureau of Prisons builds and operates institutions for people sentenced under federal law.

VOI/TIS awarded money to states and territories for nonfederal correctional systems.

A state prison constructed with federal grant money did not become a federal prison.

Distinction from the State Criminal Alien Assistance Program

SCAAP reimburses states and localities for a portion of the cost of incarcerating qualifying noncitizens.

It was funded through related prison-title appropriations but did not principally finance construction under the VOI/TIS formula.

Distinction from Tribal Correctional Facility Grants

The 1994 crime law authorized separate discretionary grants for correctional facilities on tribal lands.

Tribal construction awards followed different eligibility, distribution, ownership, and federal trust considerations.

Distinction from Residential Substance Abuse Treatment Grants

The Residential Substance Abuse Treatment for State Prisoners Program funds treatment in prisons and jails and continues as a separate BJA formula program.

VOI/TIS permitted a limited 10-percent drug-testing and intervention use but remained principally a correctional-capacity program.

Distinction from the Fiscal Year 1995 Boot Camp Initiative

The 1994 law created a separate correctional boot-camp initiative for fiscal year 1995.

VOI/TIS later permitted boot-camp facilities as one form of alternative capacity, but the programs were not identical.

Truth in Sentencing and Mandatory Minimums

Truth in sentencing governs the percentage of an imposed sentence served.

A mandatory minimum governs the minimum sentence a court must impose.

A state can use one policy without the other, although both can increase confinement.

Truth in Sentencing and Three-Strikes Laws

Three-strikes laws increase sentences for people with specified prior convictions.

Truth-in-sentencing rules determine how much of the resulting sentence must be served.

The policies often operated together during the 1990s but arose from separate legal provisions.

Claimed Achievements

  • Provided federal assistance for overcrowded and obsolete state correctional systems.
  • Created or improved prison, jail, and juvenile facility capacity.
  • Helped states separate juveniles from adult prisoners.
  • Supported female, medical, security, and specialized correctional capacity.
  • Promoted clearer relationships between imposed sentences and time served.
  • Required states to demonstrate the ability to operate funded facilities.
  • Added drug-testing, intervention, and treatment requirements.
  • Required environmental review and long-term property accountability.
  • Produced national data on sentencing, time served, construction, and deaths in custody.
  • Distributed funds to every state and participating territory.

Limitations and Failures

  • Only approximately $2.7 billion of the much larger authorized prison agenda reached states as VOI/TIS grants.
  • New appropriations ended after fiscal year 2001.
  • The grants increased capital capacity only modestly relative to total state systems.
  • Many states had already enacted truth-in-sentencing policies before the incentive.
  • Federal influence on major sentencing reform was limited.
  • Temporary construction assistance created permanent state operating costs.
  • Capacity expansion could delay sentencing and population reform.
  • The 85-percent benchmark simplified diverse state sentencing systems.
  • Longer confinement increased ageing, medical, and family costs.
  • The statute did not require racial-impact analysis.
  • Private and rural prison development created additional accountability concerns.
  • Construction did not guarantee safe, constitutional, rehabilitative, or humane conditions.

Major Controversies

Federal coercion of sentencing policy
Critics argued that temporary grants pressured states to adopt long-term punitive laws and costs.
85-percent rule
Supporters viewed it as sentence honesty; critics viewed it as an inflexible restriction on parole, good time, and individualized release.
Prison expansion
New beds reduced crowding but could facilitate further incarceration growth.
Crime reduction
Supporters credited incapacitation and deterrence; evaluations found that many other factors shaped crime and prison populations.
Race
Longer sentences and expanded prisons operated within a system containing major racial disparities.
Juvenile confinement
Funding separate space improved safety while supporting an era of increased adult prosecution of youth.
Private prisons
Federal capital support intersected with profit, oversight, and transparency concerns.
Rural economic development
Prison siting promised jobs but could create economic dependence and family separation.
Long-term fiscal cost
States paid decades of operating expense after the federal grant expired.

National Program Evaluation

NIJ sponsored a national evaluation examining legislation, state sentencing change, prison populations, time served, construction, crime, management, and state perceptions.

The evaluation concluded that all states received some funding, forty-one states met standards associated with truth-in-sentencing grants, and states varied widely in implementation.

The study found possible effects on time served and capacity but limited evidence that the federal grant was the principal cause of state sentencing reform.

Evaluation Cautions

The program operated during a period of declining crime, rapid prison growth, state sentencing reform, parole change, drug-policy change, and economic expansion.

These overlapping developments make it difficult to attribute national crime or incarceration trends to VOI/TIS alone.

How the Program Should Be Evaluated

Research should identify the official VOI/TIS program rather than assuming that every federal prison-related appropriation was part of the formula grants.

Important distinctions include:

  • federal prison construction versus federal grants for state prisons;
  • the 1994 original law versus the 1996 amended formula;
  • VOI grants versus TIS grants;
  • authorized amounts versus appropriated amounts versus awarded amounts;
  • construction grants versus SCAAP reimbursements;
  • state prisons versus local jails versus juvenile facilities;
  • new beds versus renovated or hardened beds;
  • sentence imposed versus percentage served;
  • truth in sentencing versus mandatory minimums;
  • federal incentive versus pre-existing state reform;
  • temporary federal capital aid versus long-term state operating cost; and
  • statutory text remaining in the code versus an active funded program.

Why the Federal Prison Construction Grant Program Matters

VOI/TIS represents one of the clearest examples of federal spending used to influence state sentencing and correctional policy.

The federal government offered temporary capital assistance on the condition that states reserve more prison space for violent offenders and restrict release.

The program helped expand correctional infrastructure, but its influence on sentencing reform was more limited than its political prominence suggested because many states were already moving toward truth in sentencing.

Its long-term significance lies in the connection between sentencing severity and physical capacity: longer confinement required new institutions, and new institutions made longer confinement administratively possible.

The program also illustrates a recurring fiscal problem in cooperative federalism—temporary federal grants can create permanent state operating costs and institutional commitments.

Summary

The Federal Prison Construction Grant Program was officially the Violent Offender Incarceration and Truth-in-Sentencing Incentive Formula Grant Program.

Congress created the program through the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 and substantially restructured it in 1996.

VOI grants supported states assuring that violent offenders served substantial and increasingly severe prison terms. TIS grants rewarded laws or practices requiring people convicted of Part 1 violent crimes to serve at least 85 percent of imposed sentences or an approved equivalent.

Funds could construct or expand state prisons, juvenile institutions, local jails, temporary facilities, facilities on military bases, prison barges, boot camps, and capacity for nonviolent offenders when that capacity freed prison space for violent offenders.

States could distribute up to 15 percent to local governments for jails and use up to 10 percent for offender drug testing, sanctions, treatment, supervision, and required reporting.

The federal share ordinarily covered no more than 90 percent of project cost, while states assumed long-term operating responsibility.

From fiscal years 1996 through 2001, OJP awarded $2,707,534,780 to states and territories. No new appropriations followed fiscal year 2001, although some states spent remaining funds through September 2009.

Evaluations found that the grants modestly increased correctional capacity and may have influenced some incremental sentencing changes, but many state truth-in-sentencing reforms predated the federal incentive.

The program remains historically important because it linked federal construction money to longer state prison terms and helped sustain the physical infrastructure of mass incarceration.

As of August 2, 2026, VOI/TIS was inactive and unfunded. Its statutes, regulations, reports, and property obligations remain part of the historical and legal record.
