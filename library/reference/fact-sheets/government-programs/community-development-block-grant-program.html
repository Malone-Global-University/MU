Community Development Block Grant Program

The flexible federal formula-grant program established in 1974 to help states and local governments provide decent housing, suitable living environments, public facilities, and expanded economic opportunities, principally for people with low and moderate incomes.

What Is the Community Development Block Grant Program?

The Community Development Block Grant Program is a federal grant system through which HUD provides annual funding to eligible state and local governments. Unlike a narrowly defined categorical grant, CDBG permits grantees to choose from a broad menu of housing, infrastructure, public-facility, service, neighbourhood-revitalisation, and economic-development activities.

CDBG is a block grant because Congress combines federal resources within a general field of public policy and gives recipients discretion to determine how those resources will be used within statutory boundaries. Local discretion is substantial, but it is not unlimited. Every funded activity must be eligible, meet a national objective, comply with environmental and civil-rights requirements, follow financial-management rules, and fit within the grantee’s approved planning framework.

HUD ordinarily does not award regular CDBG assistance directly to households, businesses, or nonprofit organisations. HUD awards funds to government grantees. Those governments may then operate programmes, procure contractors, make loans or grants, acquire property, improve facilities, or fund qualified subrecipients under locally adopted rules.

The programme’s flexibility means that CDBG can look different from one jurisdiction to another. One city may concentrate funds on housing rehabilitation and lead-hazard control. Another may replace water lines, improve streets, support a homeless shelter, finance a grocery store in an underserved area, or provide loans to small businesses creating jobs for low-income residents.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the regular Community Development Block Grant Program created under Title I of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974. It covers the programme’s origins, federalism structure, allocation formulas, national objectives, eligible and ineligible activities, planning process, citizen participation, financial rules, civil-rights obligations, housing and economic-development uses, administration, historical effects, and policy debates through August 2, 2026.

It also distinguishes regular CDBG from related programmes such as CDBG Disaster Recovery, CDBG Mitigation, CDBG Coronavirus, Indian Community Development Block Grants, and the Section 108 Loan Guarantee Program.

It is not a determination that a specific project is eligible. Eligibility depends on the grantee type, regulatory provision, national objective, beneficiary documentation, environmental review, procurement structure, underwriting, funding source, appropriation conditions, and local programme design.

Historical Context

Federal community-development policy before 1974 operated through a collection of categorical programmes. Cities and local agencies applied separately for urban renewal, Model Cities, rehabilitation loans, open-space acquisition, neighbourhood facilities, water and sewer projects, historic preservation, and other specialised assistance.

Categorical grants allowed federal officials to target particular national priorities, but they also created overlapping rules, separate applications, uncertain annual competitions, fragmented planning, and dependence on federal project approval.

Urban renewal demonstrated both the reach and danger of centralised redevelopment. Federal subsidies helped cities clear deteriorated property and assemble land, but many projects displaced Black, Latino, immigrant, and low-income communities, destroyed commercial districts, and replaced occupied neighbourhoods with highways, civic complexes, universities, hospitals, and private redevelopment.

The Model Cities Program, created in 1966, sought a more comprehensive and participatory approach. It funded coordinated physical and social programmes in selected neighbourhoods but remained administratively complex, competitive, and dependent on federal approval.

During the Nixon administration, “New Federalism” promoted the consolidation of categorical grants and transfer of decision-making authority to state and local governments. Local officials, community-development professionals, mayors, county organisations, and federal policymakers debated how to preserve national commitments to low-income communities while reducing federal micromanagement.

Creation of CDBG

Congress enacted the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 after years of negotiation over housing subsidies, community-development finance, local control, and the future of urban renewal.

President Gerald R. Ford signed the act on August 22, 1974. Title I created CDBG by consolidating eight categorical programmes: Model Cities, urban renewal, rehabilitation loans, historic preservation, open-space programmes, neighbourhood facilities, water and sewer facilities, and public-facility loans.

The new programme substituted annual formula allocations and broad local discretion for many competitive federal project grants. Entitlement communities could anticipate recurring funds, undertake multiyear planning, and combine different activities under one statutory framework.

The compromise preserved federal conditions. Congress declared that the primary objective was development of viable urban communities, principally for low- and moderate-income persons. Grantees had to certify compliance, involve residents, maintain records, and use funds for eligible activities serving national objectives.

The first programme year began in 1975. CDBG became one of HUD’s longest-running and most widely distributed community-development programmes.

Consolidation did not merely rename these programmes. It changed the relationship between HUD and local government by making community-development funding more predictable, flexible, and locally directed.

Federalism and Programme Structure

CDBG operates through a federal-state-local structure. Congress authorises the programme and appropriates funds. HUD calculates formula allocations, issues regulations, reviews plans, monitors grantees, provides technical assistance, and enforces compliance. State and local grantees identify needs, select activities, administer funds, and report performance.

Entitlement communities receive funds directly from HUD because they satisfy statutory population or metropolitan criteria. Smaller jurisdictions generally seek CDBG assistance from their state government rather than directly from HUD.

States have broad discretion in designing methods of distribution for non-entitlement communities. A state may use competitions, formulas, open application periods, set-asides, or combinations of these methods. State priorities commonly include water and sewer systems, public facilities, housing rehabilitation, downtown revitalisation, and economic development.

This decentralised design permits local adaptation, but it produces variation in programme access, application quality, administrative capacity, project selection, beneficiary documentation, and long-term outcomes.

Eligibility classifications and urban-county participation are updated using Census Bureau population data and Office of Management and Budget metropolitan delineations. Urban counties generally requalify on a three-year cycle.

Formula Allocation

Congress appropriates CDBG funding annually. After applicable set-asides and adjustments, HUD distributes the principal share between entitlement communities and states. The traditional structure directs approximately 70 percent to metropolitan cities and urban counties and approximately 30 percent to states for non-entitlement communities.

Entitlement Formula A

One statutory formula weighs population, poverty, and housing overcrowding. Poverty receives the greatest weight.

Entitlement Formula B

The second formula weighs poverty, pre-1940 housing, and population-growth lag. Age of housing receives the greatest weight.

Each entitlement community generally receives the higher allocation produced by the two formulas, subject to proration and statutory rules.

State Formula

State allocations use measures including population, poverty, housing overcrowding, and age of housing. States then establish their own methods for distributing funds among eligible local governments.

The formula uses objective proxies for community need, but it has been criticised because population thresholds and decades-old variables can direct funds to relatively prosperous jurisdictions while failing to fully reflect fiscal capacity, current housing costs, homelessness, infrastructure failure, or concentrated distress.

The Three National Objectives

Every CDBG-funded activity must satisfy at least one national objective. An activity can be locally desirable and statutorily eligible yet still be ineligible for CDBG if the grantee cannot document a national objective.

Benefit to Low- and Moderate-Income Persons

This is the programme’s primary national objective. Activities can qualify through an area benefit, limited clientele, housing benefit, or job creation and retention test.

Area benefit
An activity serves a primarily residential area in which the required proportion of residents have low or moderate incomes, using HUD data or an acceptable local income survey.
Limited clientele
An activity serves a group presumed or documented to consist principally of low- and moderate-income persons, such as qualifying elderly people, severely disabled adults, abused children, homeless persons, or other eligible clientele.
Housing benefit
Housing assistance benefits qualifying low- and moderate-income households under applicable occupancy and unit rules.
Jobs benefit
Economic-development assistance creates or retains permanent jobs, a required proportion of which are held by or made available to low- and moderate-income persons.

Prevention or Elimination of Slums or Blight

Activities may qualify on an area basis, a spot basis, or in connection with an approved urban-renewal area. The grantee must document qualifying physical deterioration, public improvements, property conditions, or other regulatory criteria. The objective is not a general declaration that an area is undesirable.

Urgent Need

An activity may qualify when existing conditions pose a serious and immediate threat to community health or welfare, the conditions are recent or recently became urgent, the grantee cannot finance the response, and other funding is unavailable. This objective is used sparingly and often arises after disasters, infrastructure failures, or severe public-health threats.

Overall Benefit Requirement

In addition to assigning a national objective to each activity, a grantee must ensure that at least 70 percent of its aggregate CDBG expenditures over a selected one-, two-, or three-year certification period benefit low- and moderate-income persons.

The 70-percent test is a programme-wide expenditure requirement rather than a rule that every project must serve low-income residents. A grantee can fund qualifying slum-and-blight or urgent-need activities, but those activities cannot consume so much funding that the overall low- and moderate-income percentage falls below the statutory threshold.

The distinction protects local flexibility while preserving the programme’s central income-targeting purpose.

Eligible Activities

CDBG authorises a broad range of activities, provided each activity meets an applicable national objective and programme requirement.

  • acquisition of real property for an eligible public purpose;
  • disposition of property acquired with CDBG funds;
  • clearance, demolition, and removal of buildings and improvements;
  • relocation payments and assistance;
  • rehabilitation of owner-occupied housing, rental housing, commercial buildings, nonprofit facilities, and public structures;
  • code enforcement in qualifying deteriorated areas;
  • installation or improvement of water, sewer, drainage, streets, sidewalks, lighting, and other infrastructure;
  • construction, reconstruction, or improvement of parks, community centres, shelters, health facilities, senior centres, fire stations, and other eligible public facilities;
  • accessibility modifications for people with disabilities;
  • lead-hazard reduction and healthy-homes activities;
  • energy conservation, resilience, and renewable-energy improvements;
  • public services within applicable limits;
  • microenterprise assistance;
  • commercial rehabilitation and façade improvements;
  • loans, grants, guarantees, and technical assistance for qualifying businesses;
  • job creation and retention activities;
  • homeownership assistance under authorised structures;
  • planning, capacity building, fair-housing activities, and programme administration;
  • neighbourhood revitalisation and community economic development through qualified organisations; and
  • payment of certain non-federal shares for other grant programmes when the underlying activity is eligible.

Housing Activities

Housing rehabilitation has historically been one of the largest CDBG uses. Grantees can operate grants, deferred-payment loans, amortising loans, emergency repair programmes, accessibility programmes, weatherisation activities, rental rehabilitation, and lead-hazard reduction.

CDBG may finance acquisition, site preparation, clearance, infrastructure, rehabilitation, conversion, and certain development costs connected to affordable housing. New housing construction is generally ineligible when undertaken directly by a local government, but statutory exceptions and eligible development structures permit CDBG to support new housing indirectly or through qualified organisations.

Homeownership assistance can include down-payment support, closing costs, interest subsidies, principal reduction, acquisition financing, and rehabilitation, subject to statutory authority and local underwriting.

Housing activities must address affordability, occupancy, property standards, income eligibility, duplication of benefits, environmental review, relocation, lead-based-paint requirements, and long-term compliance where applicable.

Public Facilities and Infrastructure

CDBG can finance facilities and infrastructure serving low- and moderate-income neighbourhoods or meeting another national objective. Common projects include water and sewer systems, streets, sidewalks, drainage, parks, playgrounds, fire stations, libraries, community centres, senior centres, homeless facilities, health clinics, and accessibility improvements.

Infrastructure investments can remove immediate health hazards, support housing development, improve mobility, reduce flooding, and create conditions for private investment.

A public-facility project must be properly owned, controlled, or operated and must serve an eligible purpose. A facility primarily serving the general conduct of government is ordinarily ineligible.

Capital projects often require multiple funding sources because local need exceeds annual CDBG allocations. Grantees combine CDBG with local bonds, state grants, environmental programmes, transportation funds, philanthropic resources, tax credits, and private financing.

Public Services

CDBG can support public services such as childcare, youth programmes, senior services, health care, substance-use treatment, employment services, education, legal assistance, fair-housing counselling, transportation, homelessness services, food programmes, and crime-prevention activities.

Public services are generally subject to a 15-percent expenditure or obligation cap calculated under programme rules. Certain jurisdictions have grandfathered higher caps, and Congress has temporarily altered limits for specialised appropriations.

A service must ordinarily be new or represent a quantifiable increase in the level of an existing service when CDBG begins paying for it. The restriction is intended to prevent local governments from simply replacing ordinary local service funding with federal block-grant dollars.

Because service organisations often seek more funding than the cap permits, local governments commonly operate competitive subrecipient processes.

Economic Development

CDBG can support business creation, expansion, retention, commercial rehabilitation, microenterprise, job training, business incubators, industrial infrastructure, and redevelopment.

Assistance may take the form of loans, grants, loan guarantees, interest subsidies, technical assistance, land assembly, infrastructure, or financing through nonprofit development organisations.

Economic-development activities must meet public-benefit, underwriting, national-objective, and documentation requirements. Grantees must evaluate whether public assistance is necessary, costs are reasonable, private financing is maximised, equity is appropriate, and the project is financially feasible.

Job-based projects generally document the number and type of permanent jobs created or retained and the extent to which those jobs are held by or available to people with low and moderate incomes.

Economic-development flexibility can stimulate local investment, but poorly structured transactions can subsidise projects that would have occurred without public assistance, expose public funds to loss, or produce fewer jobs than promised.

Generally Ineligible Activities

  • construction, acquisition, or rehabilitation of buildings used for the general conduct of government;
  • political activities and partisan campaign functions;
  • ordinary government operating expenses unrelated to eligible CDBG administration;
  • general-purpose equipment, furnishings, or personal property except under authorised circumstances;
  • maintenance and repair that do not constitute eligible rehabilitation or improvement;
  • income payments made directly to individuals, except limited emergency grants under applicable rules;
  • direct new housing construction by a unit of general local government, except where statutory or regulatory authority permits;
  • activities lacking a documented national objective;
  • costs incurred before required environmental review or authorisation when the commitment is choice-limiting;
  • assistance that duplicates another benefit or exceeds reasonable project need; and
  • activities prohibited by the governing appropriation, state plan, local policy, or other federal law.

An activity listed as generally ineligible may have a narrow exception. Grantees must analyse the specific statutory and regulatory authority rather than rely solely on the project’s public value.

Consolidated Planning

Entitlement grantees and states integrate CDBG into HUD’s Consolidated Plan process. The Consolidated Plan generally covers a multiyear period and assesses housing, homelessness, community-development, fair-housing, and market conditions.

Consolidated Plan
Identifies needs, priority populations, geographic priorities, resources, goals, and strategies for HUD Community Planning and Development programmes.
Annual Action Plan
Identifies projects and activities planned for a specific programme year and serves as the annual funding application.
Substantial Amendment
Revises an approved plan when local thresholds for major funding, purpose, method, or beneficiary changes are met.
Consolidated Annual Performance and Evaluation Report
Reports accomplishments, expenditures, progress, and performance after the programme year.
Integrated Disbursement and Information System
HUD’s grants-management system through which grantees establish activities, draw funds, and report results.

Planning requirements are intended to connect community need, resource allocation, public participation, and measurable results. Weak plans can become compliance documents rather than genuine investment strategies.

Citizen Participation

CDBG requires grantees to adopt and follow a citizen-participation plan. The process must emphasise participation by people with low and moderate incomes, especially residents of affected neighbourhoods, slum or blighted areas, and locations where funds will be used.

Grantees must provide reasonable and timely access to meetings, records, proposed uses of funds, and performance information. Public hearings occur during needs assessment, project development, and performance review.

Plans must address complaints, language access, disability accommodations, displacement concerns, anti-displacement policies, and opportunities to comment on substantial amendments.

Citizen participation can improve project design and accountability, but the quality varies. Technical documents, short comment periods, inaccessible meetings, limited translation, digital barriers, and decisions made before hearings can reduce meaningful influence.

Administration

A grantee may implement CDBG directly or through public agencies, nonprofit subrecipients, community-based development organisations, contractors, developers, lenders, and partner governments.

HUD Office of Community Planning and Development
Administers federal policy, formula allocations, technical assistance, data systems, regulation, and national oversight.
HUD field offices
Review plans, monitor grantees, provide guidance, resolve findings, and assess programme performance.
Entitlement grantees
Select activities, enter agreements, manage funds, monitor subrecipients, document beneficiaries, and report accomplishments.
State grantees
Design distribution methods, award funds to eligible local governments, monitor projects, and report to HUD.
Subrecipients
Public or nonprofit entities carrying out eligible activities under written agreements and federal grant requirements.
Contractors
Provide construction, professional, supply, or other procured services but do not exercise the programme discretion ordinarily assigned to a subrecipient.
Community-based development organisations
Qualified organisations that may undertake neighbourhood revitalisation, community economic development, or energy-conservation projects under specialised authority.

Financial Management

CDBG grantees must maintain systems that identify the source and use of funds, support costs, prevent unauthorised obligations, protect assets, and permit audit.

Programme Income

Revenue generated from CDBG-assisted activities—such as loan repayments, property proceeds, and interest—may constitute programme income and usually remains subject to CDBG requirements.

Revolving Loan Funds

Grantees may establish revolving funds in which repayments finance additional housing or economic-development activities.

Timeliness

Entitlement grantees must avoid accumulating excessive undisbursed balances. HUD applies a regulatory timeliness standard before the end of the programme year, subject to corrective actions and exceptions.

Administrative Cap

Planning and programme-administration expenditures are generally limited to 20 percent under the applicable calculation.

Audits and Monitoring

Grantees and subrecipients may be subject to Single Audit requirements, procurement review, cost-principle testing, duplication-of-benefits review, and HUD monitoring.

Environmental Review

CDBG-assisted projects are subject to environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act and related federal laws and authorities. HUD may allow a state or local responsible entity to perform environmental responsibilities.

Review can address floodplains, wetlands, historic properties, contamination, noise, air quality, endangered species, environmental justice, coastal barriers, explosive hazards, and other conditions.

Before environmental clearance, a grantee and project participants must avoid choice-limiting actions such as property acquisition, demolition, construction, or binding commitments that would prejudice alternatives.

Environmental review is not merely a final construction checklist. It must occur early enough to influence project design and site selection.

Relocation, Displacement, and Acquisition

CDBG property acquisition, demolition, rehabilitation, and redevelopment can displace residents, businesses, farms, and nonprofit organisations.

The Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act establishes notices, advisory services, replacement-housing payments, moving expenses, acquisition standards, and other protections for qualifying displaced persons.

Section 104(d) of the Housing and Community Development Act adds specialised protections when CDBG- or HOME-assisted activity demolishes or converts low- and moderate-income dwelling units. Grantees must adopt residential anti-displacement and relocation-assistance plans.

The history of urban renewal makes displacement analysis especially important. Physical improvement does not necessarily benefit existing residents when rents rise, affordable units disappear, or households and businesses are forced from the area.

Civil-Rights and Fair-Housing Requirements

CDBG funds carry federal civil-rights obligations. Grantees must administer programmes without unlawful discrimination and must certify that they will affirmatively further fair housing.

  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
  • Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, commonly called the Fair Housing Act;
  • section 109 of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974;
  • section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973;
  • the Americans with Disabilities Act;
  • the Age Discrimination Act of 1975;
  • Executive Order 11063 concerning equal opportunity in federally assisted housing;
  • Executive Order 13166 concerning language access;
  • section 3 of the Housing and Urban Development Act of 1968; and
  • other applicable federal, state, and local civil-rights laws.

Compliance can require fair-housing analysis, nondiscriminatory beneficiary selection, accessible facilities, language assistance, reasonable accommodation, equal contracting opportunity, geographic equity, and outreach to affected residents.

A project can serve a low-income area and still violate civil-rights law if its location, design, selection criteria, displacement effects, or administration unlawfully discriminate.

Labour Standards, Procurement, and Contracting

Construction projects may be subject to Davis-Bacon and related federal labour standards requiring payment of prevailing wages, subject to statutory thresholds and exceptions.

Procurement must provide full and open competition, avoid conflicts of interest, document cost or price reasonableness, and follow federal, state, and local rules.

Section 3 requires covered recipients to direct employment, training, and contracting opportunities generated by HUD assistance toward low- and very-low-income persons and qualifying businesses to the greatest extent feasible.

Build America, Buy America requirements can apply to covered infrastructure projects using federal financial assistance, subject to product categories, waivers, and implementation guidance.

Contracting requirements can advance worker and community benefits, but they can also challenge small grantees and subrecipients lacking specialised compliance staff.

Section 108 Loan Guarantee Program

Section 108 allows eligible CDBG grantees to borrow private capital for large community and economic-development projects using a federal loan guarantee.

A grantee pledges current and future CDBG allocations and may provide additional collateral or revenue. The borrowing capacity can substantially exceed one annual formula grant, allowing financing of industrial projects, mixed-use redevelopment, housing, public facilities, and infrastructure.

Section 108 converts future grant revenue into current capital, but it creates repayment risk. If a financed project fails to generate expected revenue, the grantee may need to use future CDBG grants that otherwise would have supported neighbourhood programmes.

Section 108 is legally connected to CDBG but operates through a separate federal loan-guarantee authority rather than an ordinary grant allocation.

Related CDBG Programmes and Appropriations

CDBG Disaster Recovery

Congress may appropriate CDBG-DR funds after major disasters to address unmet housing, infrastructure, and economic-recovery needs. Each appropriation is governed by the underlying CDBG statute, the appropriations act, Federal Register notices, waivers, and alternative requirements.

CDBG Mitigation

CDBG-MIT funds support activities that reduce long-term disaster risk and increase resilience in areas receiving designated disaster-recovery appropriations.

CDBG Coronavirus

The CARES Act of 2020 provided special CDBG-CV funds and regulatory flexibility for activities preventing, preparing for, and responding to COVID-19.

Indian Community Development Block Grants

ICDBG provides competitive and imminent-threat assistance to eligible tribes and tribal organisations under separate regulations at 24 C.F.R. Part 1003. It is related to but distinct from the entitlement and State CDBG programmes.

Neighbourhood Stabilisation Program

Congress used CDBG-related authorities for special grants addressing foreclosed, abandoned, and vacant property during the housing crisis.

These programmes should not be treated as interchangeable. Their allocations, beneficiaries, waivers, planning requirements, expenditure deadlines, and eligible activities can differ substantially.

Urban and Rural Use

The programme’s statutory language emphasises viable urban communities, but the State CDBG Program is a major source of rural community-development finance.

Large cities commonly use CDBG for housing rehabilitation, homelessness services, commercial corridors, public services, code enforcement, facilities, and neighbourhood infrastructure.

Rural jurisdictions frequently use State CDBG awards for water and wastewater systems, streets, drainage, community centres, fire protection, health facilities, downtown revitalisation, and industrial infrastructure.

Rural communities may face stronger need but weaker administrative capacity. Engineering expenses, environmental review, grant writing, procurement, and long-term asset management can consume a substantial share of small awards.

Significance for African American Communities

CDBG emerged from the transformation of urban policy during and after the Civil Rights Movement. Many Black neighbourhoods faced deteriorated housing, discriminatory mortgage markets, segregated public services, infrastructure neglect, urban-renewal displacement, highway construction, job loss, and declining municipal tax bases.

The programme created a recurring funding stream that cities could use for housing repair, neighbourhood facilities, public services, minority business assistance, commercial corridors, code enforcement, and infrastructure in low-income areas.

CDBG also institutionalised citizen participation and low-income targeting, giving residents and community organisations formal grounds to question local allocations and demand neighbourhood investment.

Its effects have not been uniformly beneficial. Local control can reproduce local political inequality. Funds may be distributed thinly, directed toward downtown redevelopment, used to subsidise projects with displacement pressure, or awarded through processes that favour established institutions over grassroots organisations.

Because Black communities were disproportionately harmed by urban renewal and discriminatory housing systems, CDBG administration must be evaluated not only by the amount spent in low-income census tracts but also by who controls decisions, who receives contracts, who remains in the neighbourhood, and who gains property, employment, and institutional capacity.

Community Development Corporations and Nonprofit Partners

Community development corporations, housing nonprofits, social-service agencies, small-business lenders, faith-based organisations, and neighbourhood groups frequently carry out CDBG-funded activities.

A nonprofit can serve as a subrecipient operating a public programme, a developer undertaking a project, a contractor providing services, or a community-based development organisation exercising specialised authority.

Nonprofit partnerships can bring neighbourhood knowledge, trust, specialised expertise, and flexible delivery. They also require strong written agreements, financial controls, procurement systems, beneficiary records, conflict-of-interest policies, and grantee monitoring.

Dependence on annual CDBG awards can make nonprofit programmes vulnerable to political changes, federal appropriation declines, and shifting local priorities.

Performance Measurement

Grantees report outputs such as housing units rehabilitated, people served, businesses assisted, jobs created, facilities improved, and infrastructure completed.

HUD uses the Integrated Disbursement and Information System, Consolidated Plan reports, monitoring, financial data, and programme evaluations to assess performance.

Measuring CDBG’s national effect is difficult because thousands of grantees fund different activities in different markets. A repaired sewer line, small-business loan, childcare programme, and demolished unsafe building cannot be reduced easily to one common outcome.

Output measures can show what was purchased but not necessarily whether neighbourhood poverty fell, residents accumulated wealth, displacement was prevented, infrastructure lasted, or private investment would have occurred without federal assistance.

Effective evaluation therefore requires both national accountability and locally appropriate outcome measures.

Major Achievements

  • Consolidated multiple categorical grants into a stable and flexible community-development system.
  • Provided direct annual federal funding to cities and counties without requiring project-by-project national competitions.
  • Financed housing rehabilitation and preservation in low-income neighbourhoods.
  • Supported water, sewer, street, drainage, park, accessibility, and public-facility improvements.
  • Created local funding streams for childcare, senior services, homelessness programmes, health care, employment, and other public services.
  • Supported small businesses, microenterprises, commercial corridors, and job creation.
  • Established statutory low- and moderate-income targeting.
  • Strengthened citizen participation in local community-development planning.
  • Supported nonprofit and community-based delivery organisations.
  • Provided an administrative platform Congress could adapt for disaster recovery, pandemic response, and neighbourhood stabilisation.
  • Enabled grantees to leverage other public, private, and philanthropic funds.
  • Extended federal community-development assistance to both urban and rural jurisdictions.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • The allocation formula does not perfectly target communities with the greatest current need or weakest fiscal capacity.
  • Inflation and long-term appropriation decline have reduced the purchasing power of annual grants.
  • Broad eligibility can disperse funds across many small activities with limited cumulative impact.
  • Local political priorities can redirect funds from the most distressed neighbourhoods.
  • Citizen participation can become procedural rather than influential.
  • Low-income area benefit does not guarantee that existing residents receive lasting benefits.
  • Redevelopment can contribute to displacement, rising land values, or loss of affordable property.
  • Small jurisdictions and nonprofits may lack capacity to meet complex federal requirements.
  • Public-service and administrative caps constrain local responses to social needs and compliance burdens.
  • Economic-development assistance can expose public funds to project failure or weak job outcomes.
  • National performance measurement is difficult because local uses vary widely.
  • Programme income, revolving loans, old activities, and slow projects can create financial-management problems.
  • Annual appropriations and presidential budget proposals create funding uncertainty.
  • Federal flexibility and local autonomy can conflict with civil-rights enforcement and national equity goals.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Fiscal Year 2026 Funding

Congress enacted full-year fiscal year 2026 HUD formula funding, and HUD published community-level CDBG allocations in April 2026. Grantees were incorporating those allocations into Annual Action Plans, amendments, awards, and project schedules.

Fiscal Year 2027 Elimination Proposal

The President’s fiscal year 2027 budget proposed eliminating the Community Development Fund and ending regular CDBG funding. The administration argued that programme purposes had become diffuse and results inconsistent. Congress retains constitutional authority over appropriations, so the proposal did not itself terminate CDBG.

Formula Targeting

Policymakers continue to debate whether poverty, population, overcrowding, pre-1940 housing, and growth lag adequately measure contemporary need. Proposed alternatives include fiscal capacity, housing cost burden, vacancy, infrastructure condition, homelessness, population loss, and concentrated poverty.

Housing Supply

Communities face pressure to use CDBG more directly for housing production and preservation. The programme can support housing, but statutory restrictions, small allocations, environmental review, acquisition costs, and local capacity limit the scale of construction.

Infrastructure and Resilience

Ageing water systems, flooding, heat, wildfire, storms, and insurance-market instability increase demand for resilient infrastructure. CDBG flexibility can help, but annual formula grants are small relative to major capital needs.

Opportunity Zones

HUD issued updated 2026 guidance encouraging coordination of CDBG and other Community Planning and Development resources with designated Opportunity Zone investment, while grantees remain responsible for eligibility, national objectives, underwriting, and public-benefit requirements.

Regulatory Modernisation

HUD’s 2024 proposed rule sought to update CDBG and Indian CDBG requirements. As of August 2, 2026, HUD’s programme page continued to identify the proposal and closed comment period rather than a completed final rule.

Administrative Capacity

Environmental review, procurement, labour standards, civil rights, relocation, financial reporting, and performance measurement require experienced staff. Capacity gaps can delay spending and deter innovative projects.

Equity and Displacement

Grantees continue to face the strategic question of whether neighbourhood investment builds resident wealth and stability or accelerates displacement and transfers value to outside owners.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Block grant
Federal funding provided for a broad policy field with recipient discretion within statutory conditions.
Categorical grant
Federal funding restricted to a narrower activity, service, population, or project type.
Entitlement community
A qualifying metropolitan city or urban county receiving an annual CDBG formula allocation directly from HUD.
Non-entitlement community
A smaller local government generally seeking CDBG assistance through its state programme.
Low- and moderate-income
Income categories based on HUD income limits, generally extending through 80 percent of area median income, with further distinctions for low and very-low income.
National objective
One of three statutory purposes that every CDBG-funded activity must meet.
Area benefit
An activity available to all residents of a defined primarily residential area meeting required low- and moderate-income criteria.
Limited clientele
An activity serving a particular group documented or presumed to consist principally of people with low and moderate incomes.
Slum or blight
A documented physical condition meeting regulatory requirements on an area, spot, or urban-renewal basis.
Urgent need
A serious and immediate community threat that the grantee cannot finance and for which other funding is unavailable.
Programme income
Revenue generated from the use of CDBG funds and subject to continuing programme requirements.
Subrecipient
A public or nonprofit entity carrying out eligible CDBG activities under a written agreement with a grantee.
Contractor
An entity procured to provide specified goods or services rather than administer a programme with discretionary authority.
Consolidated Plan
A multiyear HUD planning document assessing needs, priorities, resources, and strategies.
Annual Action Plan
The annual statement of planned activities and funding.
CAPER
The Consolidated Annual Performance and Evaluation Report describing programme-year results.
Section 108
A federal loan-guarantee authority allowing CDBG grantees to borrow against current and future grants.

Summary

The Community Development Block Grant Program is a flexible federal formula-grant programme created by the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974. It consolidated eight categorical programmes and shifted substantial project-selection authority from HUD to state and local governments.

CDBG supports decent housing, suitable living environments, infrastructure, public facilities, services, and economic opportunity, principally for people with low and moderate incomes. Every activity must be eligible and meet a national objective, and at least 70 percent of aggregate programme expenditures must benefit low- and moderate-income persons.

The programme’s flexibility allows communities to address different local needs and combine resources, but that same flexibility complicates national accountability and can reproduce local political and racial inequality.

CDBG remains an important source of capital for housing rehabilitation, water and sewer systems, neighbourhood facilities, nonprofit services, and economic development. Its long-term challenges include declining purchasing power, outdated allocation formulas, administrative complexity, displacement risk, and debate over whether broad local discretion produces measurable national results.

As of August 2, 2026, fiscal year 2026 allocations had been enacted and published. The President’s fiscal year 2027 budget proposed ending CDBG funding, leaving Congress to determine the programme’s next appropriation.
