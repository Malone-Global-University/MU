Historically Underutilized Business Zone Program

The Small Business Administration certification and federal-contracting program designed to direct business activity, investment, and employment toward economically distressed urban and rural communities, Indian reservations, closed military bases, disaster-affected areas, and other designated locations.

Terminology Note

The official program name is the Historically Underutilized Business Zone Program, commonly shortened to the HUBZone Program. The acronym refers to geographic zones rather than a personal characteristic of the business owner.

A HUBZone is an area appearing on the official SBA HUBZone map under one or more statutory designations. A business located near a distressed area does not qualify unless the relevant office or employee residence is within a mapped eligible area or a specific regulatory exception applies.

A certified HUBZone small business concern is a firm that SBA has formally approved and identified in the federal small-business database. Location alone does not create certification.

The program is not a grant, loan, tax credit, or permanent entitlement. Its principal benefits are federal prime-contract and subcontracting opportunities, including restricted competitions, limited sole-source authority, a price-evaluation preference in full and open competition, and recognition in federal subcontracting plans.

Current eligibility rules changed significantly under a final regulation published December 17, 2024 and effective January 16, 2025, followed by technical corrections in February and June 2025. This fact sheet states the rules in effect on August 2, 2026.

Overview

The HUBZone Program uses federal procurement to encourage businesses to locate operations and hire workers in areas experiencing poverty, unemployment, military-base closure, disaster, geographic isolation, or persistent underinvestment.

Unlike programs based primarily on the identity of the owner, HUBZone eligibility is principally place based. The firm must combine qualifying ownership and small-business status with a qualifying principal office and a workforce that includes a substantial percentage of HUBZone residents.

SBA determines which areas qualify, maintains an online map, certifies businesses, conducts program examinations, decides status protests, and decertifies firms that no longer meet requirements.

Federal contracting agencies conduct market research and may reserve contracts or orders for certified firms, negotiate qualifying sole-source awards, or apply a price-evaluation preference during unrestricted competition.

The program is intended to create a geographic link between federal spending and distressed communities. A certified firm can perform a contract anywhere, but its organisational location and employee residences must satisfy the program’s place-based rules.

Certification does not guarantee a contract. The firm must market its capabilities, identify solicitations, submit responsive offers, demonstrate responsibility, meet size requirements for the assigned industry, offer fair pricing, and perform the contract.

The program has repeatedly faced administrative difficulty because business locations, employee residences, census data, disaster designations, map boundaries, remote work, affiliates, and contracting status can change quickly.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because its central features are certification, geographic designation, federal contracting assistance, employee-residency requirements, recertification, examinations, protests, and compliance.

The HUBZone Act of 1997 belongs primarily under Laws and Legislation. The Small Business Administration belongs under Institutions.

Qualified census tracts, nonmetropolitan counties, Indian reservations, base-closure areas, disaster areas, redesignated areas, and governor-designated covered areas may receive separate concept or geography entries where additional detail is required.

The program should be distinguished from Empowerment Zones, Opportunity Zones, New Markets Tax Credits, and other place-based tax or grant programs. HUBZone’s principal mechanism is federal procurement.

It should also be distinguished from the 8(a), WOSB, VetCert, and Small Disadvantaged Business programs. A firm may hold more than one certification if it independently satisfies each program’s requirements.

Historical Context

Federal and state governments have long used tax incentives, public works, loans, grants, procurement, and enterprise-zone designations to address concentrated poverty and economic disinvestment.

By the 1990s, many inner-city neighbourhoods, rural counties, Indian reservations, and communities surrounding closed military installations experienced persistent unemployment, low household income, population loss, weak business formation, and limited private investment.

Small businesses in these areas often lacked access to established government-contracting networks, working capital, bonding, technical systems, and past performance.

Senator Kit Bond proposed using federal procurement as a job-development tool. The structure would reward small firms that maintained a principal office in a distressed area and hired residents of eligible communities.

Congress included the HUBZone Act in the Small Business Reauthorization Act of 1997.

The initial program combined a geographic certification, a 35-percent employee-residency requirement, federal set-asides, sole-source authority, a price-evaluation preference, and a gradually increasing government-wide contracting goal.

Legislative Purpose

The program was designed as both a small-business contracting initiative and a jobs program.

Its policy objectives include:

  • directing federal contract revenue toward economically distressed areas;
  • encouraging small businesses to establish and retain offices in those areas;
  • increasing employment of residents of distressed communities;
  • building federal contracting capacity among local firms;
  • supporting investment in urban and rural property;
  • assisting Indian reservations and Native-owned businesses;
  • supporting redevelopment after military-base closure;
  • providing temporary support after disasters or loss of area eligibility;
  • expanding the federal supplier base; and
  • improving competition for government goods and services.

HUBZone Act of 1997

Title VI of Public Law 105-135 created the HUBZone Empowerment Contracting Program.

The original law:

  • defined eligible geographic areas;
  • created qualified HUBZone small-business status;
  • required small-business size;
  • required qualifying ownership and control;
  • required a principal office in a HUBZone;
  • required at least 35 percent of employees to reside in a HUBZone;
  • authorised competitive set-asides;
  • authorised limited sole-source awards;
  • created a 10-percent price-evaluation preference;
  • included certified firms in subcontracting plans;
  • established government-wide prime and subcontracting goals; and
  • directed SBA to issue implementing regulations.

The first SBA final rule was published on June 11, 1998, and the program began operating under those regulations later that year.

Federal Contracting Goal

The government-wide goal is to award at least 3 percent of the total value of eligible prime-contract and subcontract awards to certified HUBZone small business concerns each fiscal year.

Each agency negotiates small-business goals with SBA and receives an annual scorecard.

A HUBZone firm may receive credit under more than one category when it also qualifies as an 8(a), small disadvantaged, women-owned, service-disabled veteran-owned, or other recognised small business.

Goal credit is an administrative measure of award dollars. It does not by itself establish that jobs or investment reached HUBZone residents.

Federal agencies have frequently failed to meet the HUBZone goal even when overall small-business contracting goals were achieved.

HUBZone Geographic Categories

A location may qualify under one or more of the following categories:

  • qualified census tract;
  • qualified nonmetropolitan county;
  • land within the external boundaries of an Indian reservation;
  • redesignated area;
  • qualified base-closure area;
  • qualified disaster area; or
  • governor-designated covered area.

The official SBA map is the controlling practical tool for determining whether an address lies within a currently designated HUBZone.

A mailing address, city name, ZIP code, or general neighbourhood description may not correspond precisely with a census-tract, reservation, or county boundary.

Qualified Census Tracts

Qualified census tracts are designated by the Department of Housing and Urban Development using income and poverty criteria associated with the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit.

A qualifying tract generally has either:

  • at least 50 percent of households with income below 60 percent of area median gross income; or
  • a poverty rate of at least 25 percent.

Federal law limits the total population of qualified census tracts within most metropolitan statistical areas.

The temporary Puerto Rico exception to the metropolitan population limitation continues until the statutory date or termination of the federal financial-oversight board, as applicable.

Qualified Nonmetropolitan Counties

A nonmetropolitan county may qualify when it meets at least one statutory distress test.

Current tests include:

  • median household income below 80 percent of the state median based on the applicable five-year data;
  • unemployment of at least 140 percent of the national or state average, whichever produces the lower threshold, using the applicable five-year data; or
  • location of a HUD Difficult Development Area within Alaska, Hawai‘i, or a United States territory or possession.

County eligibility may change as economic data, metropolitan definitions, and five-year map updates change.

Indian Reservations and Tribal Areas

Lands within the external boundaries of eligible Indian reservations are HUBZones.

The statutory and regulatory definition includes specified reservation, pueblo, dependent Indian community, former reservation, and Alaska Native village or regional-corporation land categories.

A Tribal Government-owned concern has alternative rules. It may meet the ordinary principal-office and 35-percent residency tests, or it may certify that at least 35 percent of employees performing a HUBZone contract will reside on a reservation governed by an owner Tribe or in an adjoining HUBZone.

The Tribal alternative links employment to contract performance rather than requiring the firm’s general principal office to be in a HUBZone.

Qualified Base-Closure Areas

Military installation closure can remove a major employer and leave land, buildings, infrastructure, and surrounding communities in economic transition.

HUBZone law includes the closed installation and specified intersecting, containing, and contiguous geographic areas.

A qualified base-closure area generally remains on the HUBZone map for at least eight years under current law and regulation.

The designation is intended to help replacement businesses obtain federal work and support redevelopment of former military property.

Redesignated Areas

A census tract or nonmetropolitan county does not necessarily lose HUBZone status immediately when new data show that it no longer qualifies.

A redesignated area generally remains a HUBZone for three years after loss of qualified census-tract or qualified nonmetropolitan-county status.

This transition period protects businesses and employees from immediate disruption.

Firms should not assume that redesignated status is permanent. The official map includes an expiration date.

Offices in redesignated areas do not qualify for the ten-year long-term-investment protection when the firm first seeks certification.

Qualified Disaster Areas

A census tract or nonmetropolitan county may receive temporary HUBZone status following a presidential major-disaster declaration or qualifying catastrophic incident when the area lost specified HUBZone status during the preceding five-year period.

Disaster areas are generally added to the map monthly from Federal Emergency Management Agency data.

They generally remain eligible until the next five-year HUBZone map update.

The designation is intended to help affected firms compete for federal work and support local recovery.

A qualified disaster area is not the same as the entire geographic area covered by every disaster declaration.

Governor-Designated Covered Areas

A governor may petition SBA once per calendar year to add qualifying rural covered areas.

A covered area generally must be outside an urban area, contain no more than 50,000 people, and have unemployment at least 120 percent of the national or state rate, whichever produces the lower threshold.

A petition may include no more than 10 percent of the eligible covered areas in the state.

SBA may consider:

  • potential job creation and investment;
  • demonstrated small-business interest;
  • integration into state and local development strategy; and
  • the effect of losing a prior HUBZone designation.

An approved designation lasts until the next five-year map update or one year after approval, whichever is later.

Official HUBZone Map

SBA’s interactive map allows users to search an address, examine the surrounding boundary, identify the designation type, and review expiration information.

The map was last comprehensively updated in July 2023.

As of August 2, 2026, SBA states that:

  • expiring redesignated areas may produce map changes during 2026;
  • governor-designated and disaster areas may be added or removed throughout a year; and
  • the next comprehensive update to qualified census tracts and qualified nonmetropolitan counties is scheduled for July 2028.

A business should preserve map documentation for the principal office and each claimed employee residence at the relevant eligibility date.

Map-Update Cycle

Qualified census tracts and qualified nonmetropolitan counties are updated every five years.

Redesignated areas enter the map when an area loses qualifying status and leave after the transition period.

Base-closure areas are added when SBA receives the necessary Department of Defense information and generally leave after eight years.

Disaster areas are generally added monthly and remain until the applicable comprehensive update.

Governor-designated covered areas enter after petition approval and expire according to their statutory cycle.

The periodic cycle improves stability compared with continuous census-data changes but can temporarily retain areas whose conditions have improved or delay recognition of newly distressed areas.

Basic Firm Eligibility

A standard applicant generally must:

  • qualify as a small business under an applicable SBA size standard;
  • be at least 51 percent owned and controlled by eligible United States citizens or a qualifying eligible entity;
  • maintain its principal office in a mapped HUBZone unless the Tribal alternative applies;
  • have at least 35 percent of employees residing in HUBZones;
  • provide complete and consistent ownership, payroll, office, and residency records;
  • maintain active and accurate federal registrations;
  • not be actively excluded from federal contracting;
  • comply with size and affiliation rules;
  • permit SBA to verify eligibility; and
  • remain eligible when SBA issues the certification decision.

Ownership and Control

A concern may qualify through one of the following structures:

  • at least 51 percent ownership and control by one or more United States citizens;
  • an Alaska Native Corporation, an ANC-owned concern, or a wholly owned ANC business entity;
  • at least 51 percent ownership by one or more Indian Tribal Governments or a corporation wholly owned by Tribal Governments;
  • at least 51 percent ownership by one or more Community Development Corporations;
  • a qualifying small agricultural cooperative or a firm at least 51 percent owned by qualifying small agricultural cooperatives; or
  • at least 51 percent ownership by one or more Native Hawaiian Organizations or a corporation wholly owned by one or more NHOs.

Control includes both long-term decision-making authority and day-to-day management.

Ownership documents, voting rights, board arrangements, management agreements, options, leases, franchise relationships, financing, and other contracts must not transfer actual control to an ineligible person or entity.

United States Citizenship

For an individually owned firm, the qualifying owners must be United States citizens.

The regulation defines a citizen as a person born or naturalized in the United States.

Permanent residents and visa holders are not treated as citizens for the citizen-owned eligibility route.

Eligible entity-owned structures follow their own statutory ownership requirements.

Size and Affiliation

At certification and recertification, the concern and its affiliates must be small under at least one NAICS code listed in the concern’s federal profile.

For a specific HUBZone contract, the firm must be small under the size standard assigned to that procurement.

Affiliation can arise through common ownership, management, identity of interest, contractual control, economic dependence, options, joint ventures, or other relationships.

Employees of an affiliate are not automatically counted as the applicant’s HUBZone employees.

SBA may count them when the businesses lack a clear line of fracture, including circumstances involving shared employees, facilities, equipment, customers, contracts, or financial dependence.

Common administrative services alone do not necessarily combine employee counts.

Principal-Office Requirement

The principal office is the location where the greatest number of the concern’s employees at any one location perform their work.

The rule does not necessarily treat the registered agent, mailing address, headquarters named in corporate documents, or owner’s preferred office as the principal office.

The applicant must demonstrate a real business operation at the claimed location.

Evidence may include:

  • a deed or qualifying active lease;
  • utility and communication bills;
  • photographs or a live or virtual walkthrough;
  • dedicated work space, furniture, and equipment;
  • payroll and time records;
  • employee work-location records;
  • business licences;
  • insurance documents;
  • mail and delivery records; and
  • records showing actual business activity.

Current rules generally require the lease to begin at least thirty days before the relevant review date and extend at least sixty days after it.

Shared, Virtual, and Home Offices

A shared workspace may qualify when the firm has dedicated space sufficient for the employees claimed to work there.

A mailbox, occasional conference-room access, registration address, or desk that cannot accommodate the claimed workforce does not establish a principal office.

Remote and hybrid work require careful determination of where each employee actually performs more than half of the employee’s work.

A home office may serve as the ordinary principal office if it is a real business location and satisfies the map and work-location rules.

A personal residence cannot receive the special ten-year long-term-investment protection.

Construction and Service Job-Site Exclusion

For firms whose primary industry is construction or services, employees who spend more than half their work time at temporary or contract-specific job sites are generally excluded when identifying the principal office.

The exclusion prevents a temporary customer site or construction project from becoming the firm’s principal office.

If all employees spend more than half their time at job sites and no employee works primarily from a qualifying office, the firm does not satisfy the principal-office requirement.

The job-site exclusion applies to principal-office calculation, not automatically to the 35-percent employee-residency calculation.

Ten-Year Principal-Office Investment Protection

A certified firm that purchases its principal-office building or enters a lease of at least ten years in an eligible HUBZone may retain principal-office treatment for as long as ten years even if the mapped area later loses status.

The protection is intended to encourage durable investment rather than short-term relocation for certification.

The firm must continue to own or maintain the long-term lease and the property must remain its actual principal office.

The protection does not apply to:

  • an office in a redesignated area or qualified disaster area at initial certification;
  • a shared office;
  • a personal residence; or
  • an investment made within 180 days before the area’s designation expires.

The ten-year clock begins on certification or investment, depending on whether the qualifying investment preceded certification.

Employee Definition

HUBZone employee counting differs from ordinary headcount labels such as full-time equivalent or payroll position.

An employee is an individual who generally works at least ten hours per week during the four weeks immediately before the relevant review date.

Full-time, part-time, temporary, leased, and other worker relationships may be counted depending on the actual relationship.

SBA examines the totality of circumstances and may consider federal tax criteria, payment method, supervision, permanency, shared workers, and control of the work.

Owners who work for the business can count as employees.

A person called an independent contractor may be treated as an employee where the actual relationship supports employee status.

Thirty-Five-Percent Employee-Residency Requirement

At least 35 percent of the firm’s employees must qualify as HUBZone resident employees at certification, required recertification dates, and ordinary offer dates for HUBZone contracts.

The percentage is based on the firm’s employee count under the program definition.

Fractional results are rounded to the nearest whole employee. A one-person firm must have that employee residing in a HUBZone.

The resident employees do not have to live in the same HUBZone as the principal office.

A resident may live in any current HUBZone unless a special Tribal performance alternative applies.

SBA may require identification, leases, deeds, utility bills, property-tax records, payroll, work schedules, and explanations of inconsistent addresses.

Residence and the Ninety-Day Rule

A person generally must live full-time at the claimed address for at least ninety calendar days immediately before the relevant review date.

SBA first looks to a driver’s licence or other government-issued identification.

If the identification is unavailable or inconsistent, the firm must provide specific supporting evidence and an explanation.

A temporary address used solely to create eligibility does not establish residence.

Military, student, family, seasonal, and multi-residence situations require evidence of the person’s actual full-time home.

Legacy HUBZone Employees

The legacy-employee rule allows a firm to continue counting certain long-term workers after the worker moves outside a HUBZone or the residence loses mapped status.

An eligible legacy employee must:

  • have qualified as a HUBZone resident employee at a certification or anniversary date after December 26, 2019;
  • continue living in a HUBZone for at least 180 days after that date;
  • remain continuously employed without interruption;
  • work at least thirty hours per week; and
  • not have qualified originally through residence in a redesignated or qualified disaster area.

A firm may count no more than four legacy employees at one time and must maintain at least one other current HUBZone resident employee.

The firm must retain original residence and continuous-employment documentation for the duration of its participation.

Certification Application Process

  1. Register the business. The concern maintains an active federal entity registration and accurate business profile.
  2. Confirm small-business status. The applicant identifies its NAICS codes and reviews size and affiliation.
  3. Confirm ownership and control. Organisational documents must establish the qualifying ownership route.
  4. Verify the principal office. The applicant maps the address and gathers lease, deed, work-location, and business-operation evidence.
  5. Calculate employees. The firm identifies every worker meeting the ten-hour employee definition.
  6. Verify residence. At least 35 percent must have qualifying addresses and evidence.
  7. Submit through SBA Certifications. The concern uploads the application and supporting records.
  8. Respond to SBA requests. Incomplete, inconsistent, or untimely responses can result in adverse inference and denial.
  9. Remain eligible through the decision date. Changes during application processing can affect approval.
  10. Confirm public database status. The firm must appear as certified in the federal small-business database before competing as a HUBZone firm.

Supporting Documentation

Documents may include:

  • articles of incorporation or organisation;
  • bylaws, operating agreements, partnership agreements, and stock ledgers;
  • citizenship documentation;
  • Tribal, ANC, NHO, CDC, or cooperative ownership records;
  • federal registrations and tax records;
  • payroll registers and payroll-tax filings;
  • employee lists, work schedules, and time records;
  • driver’s licences and other residency evidence;
  • leases, deeds, utility bills, and office photographs;
  • affiliate and shared-resource records;
  • contracts and job-site information;
  • organisational charts and management records;
  • legacy-employee records; and
  • other evidence requested by SBA.

Certification Decision

The applicant bears the burden of proving eligibility.

SBA bases its decision on the application, responses to requests, independent research, program databases, map information, and changed circumstances.

Inconsistent or incomplete evidence may cause SBA to decline the application.

Knowingly false information requires denial even if other accurate documents appear in the file.

SBA’s approval or denial of the application is the final agency certification decision.

A declined concern generally may reapply after ninety calendar days if it has corrected every reason for decline.

Participation Duration

The HUBZone Program does not have the nine-year limit used by the 8(a) Business Development Program.

A concern may remain certified indefinitely if it continues to meet all requirements, completes triennial recertification, survives program examinations and protests, and avoids debarment or decertification.

Geographic designations and workforce composition may change even where ownership and business operations remain stable.

A firm can voluntarily withdraw and later reapply when eligible.

Triennial Program Recertification

Current rules require program recertification every three years rather than annual recertification.

The submission must occur during the ninety calendar days before the certification anniversary.

A firm that received no HUBZone contract during the preceding twelve months must show actual 35-percent employee residency and a qualifying principal office at recertification.

A firm that received a HUBZone contract during the preceding twelve months may recertify while attempting to maintain 35 percent, provided it satisfies the performance and minimum-residency rules.

A firm that misses recertification is decertified at the end of its eligibility period, subject to a thirty-day reinstatement window.

SBA may align recertification dates for firms holding multiple SBA certifications.

Eligibility at the Time of Offer

Under the rules effective in 2025, certification alone is not always enough to submit a HUBZone offer.

The firm generally must:

  • appear as certified in the federal small-business database;
  • remain small for the procurement’s NAICS code;
  • maintain a qualifying principal office;
  • have at least 35 percent HUBZone resident employees;
  • maintain qualifying ownership and control;
  • certify that it will attempt to maintain residency during performance; and
  • meet joint-venture and performance requirements where applicable.

Eligibility is generally measured when the initial offer including price is submitted.

Two-step procurements may measure eligibility at the first-phase proposal even when that phase does not contain price.

Recent HUBZone Award Exception

A firm awarded a HUBZone contract within the twelve months before a new offer may submit the new offer when at least 20 percent of its total employees reside in HUBZones and the firm is making substantive, documented efforts to return to 35 percent.

The exception recognises that contract performance can require rapid hiring outside the available HUBZone labour pool.

It is not a permanent reduction of the 35-percent requirement.

A protest may challenge whether the firm has at least 20 percent HUBZone residents or whether its recruitment efforts are substantive and documented.

Attempt to Maintain

During performance of a HUBZone contract, a firm must attempt to maintain 35-percent HUBZone employee residency.

Documented efforts may include:

  • written offers to HUBZone residents;
  • advertisements targeted to eligible communities;
  • attendance at job fairs;
  • partnerships with local workforce organisations;
  • recruitment through Tribal, municipal, educational, or nonprofit institutions;
  • training and apprenticeship efforts;
  • records of applicants and hiring decisions; and
  • retention programs for HUBZone employees.

A firm with less than 20 percent HUBZone resident employees during contract performance is deemed to have failed to attempt to maintain and faces proposed decertification.

Mergers, Acquisitions, and Ownership Changes

A certified concern that acquires another business, is acquired, or merges must provide evidence to SBA within thirty calendar days that it remains eligible.

The transaction may change ownership, control, size, affiliation, employees, principal office, and residency percentages.

A firm that no longer qualifies may withdraw voluntarily or face decertification.

Contract-specific status recertification may also be required after merger, acquisition, or sale under government-wide small-business rules.

Program Examinations

A program examination is an SBA investigation into the accuracy of the firm’s certification and continued eligibility.

SBA must examine each certified firm at least once every three years and may use risk analysis to select firms for more frequent review.

An examination may occur during application processing, after certification, at recertification, in connection with a contract, or after receipt of credible information.

SBA may review size, affiliation, ownership, control, principal office, employees, residency, legacy employees, attempts to maintain, contract performance, and federal registrations.

SBA representatives may conduct announced or unannounced site visits and virtual walkthroughs.

Failure to cooperate can support adverse inference and decertification.

HUBZone Contracting Methods

A HUBZone contract may result from:

  • a competition set aside entirely for certified HUBZone small businesses;
  • a partial HUBZone set-aside;
  • a qualifying sole-source award;
  • a reserved award under an unrestricted multiple-award solicitation;
  • a HUBZone order under a HUBZone multiple-award contract;
  • a HUBZone order set aside under an unrestricted or general small-business multiple-award contract; or
  • full and open competition using the HUBZone price-evaluation preference.

A contract can be performed outside the geographic HUBZone that supported certification.

The location and employment requirements apply to the firm, not necessarily the federal work site.

Parity among Federal Contracting Programs

Early legal disputes addressed whether the HUBZone statute gave the program priority over 8(a) contracting.

The Government Accountability Office sustained protests requiring HUBZone consideration under the then-current statutory language.

Section 1347 of the Small Business Jobs Act of 2010 clarified that there is no order of precedence among 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran, and women-owned contracting programs.

Contracting officers may choose among the programs based on market research, agency goals, acquisition history, price, capability, and programmatic need.

Above the simplified acquisition threshold, contracting officers must consider the socioeconomic programs before using a general small-business set-aside, but no one socioeconomic program automatically controls.

HUBZone Set-Asides

A contracting officer may restrict competition to certified HUBZone small businesses when:

  • the requirement is eligible for HUBZone contracting;
  • market research indicates at least two responsible certified firms are likely to submit offers; and
  • award can be made at a fair market price.

The government evaluates proposals according to the solicitation’s price, technical, past-performance, and other factors.

The apparent successful offeror may face a HUBZone status protest or size protest before award.

A HUBZone competition cannot be restricted further to firms holding an additional SBA socioeconomic certification.

HUBZone Sole-Source Awards

A contracting officer may award a requirement directly to one certified HUBZone firm when:

  • the procurement is not excluded or required to be competed;
  • the anticipated value including options does not exceed $7 million for manufacturing or $4.5 million for other contracts;
  • two or more certified HUBZone firms are not likely to submit offers;
  • the intended firm is responsible and capable;
  • the price is fair and reasonable; and
  • the intended awardee is certified and eligible at offer and award.

Sole-source authority is discretionary. Certification does not entitle a firm to a direct award.

SBA may appeal a contracting officer’s decision not to use HUBZone sole-source authority under applicable procedures.

Ten-Percent Price-Evaluation Preference

The price-evaluation preference operates in qualifying full-and-open competitions rather than in HUBZone set-asides.

When a large business is the otherwise successful offeror, the contracting officer generally adds 10 percent to the large-business offer for evaluation against the HUBZone offer.

The government does not add 10 percent to the amount actually paid under the large firm’s contract.

The preference does not displace an otherwise successful non-HUBZone small business.

It does not apply when price is not an evaluation factor, when all fair and reasonable offers are accepted, or to the reserved portion of a multiple-award contract.

A certified firm may waive the preference in accordance with solicitation rules.

Price-Preference Example

Assume a certified HUBZone firm offers $98, a non-HUBZone small business offers $95, and a large business offers $93.

The large firm is initially lowest. The contracting officer adds 10 percent for evaluation, producing an evaluated large-business price of $102.30.

The HUBZone offer of $98 becomes lower than the evaluated large-business offer.

The non-HUBZone small business remains at $95 and is not penalised. Actual award depends on the solicitation, responsibility, technical evaluation, and best-value determination.

Multiple-Award Contracts and Orders

Agencies may establish multiple-award contracts set aside for HUBZone firms, partially set aside portions, unrestricted vehicles with HUBZone reserves, or later orders restricted to certified firms.

Status may be measured at the initial contract offer, a later order, recertification event, option, merger, or other specified date.

Where the initial multiple-award offer does not include price, current rules measure HUBZone eligibility when the initial offer is submitted.

Limitations on subcontracting may apply to the contract as a whole, an option period, or an individual order depending on the acquisition structure.

HUBZone Joint Ventures

A certified HUBZone small business may form a joint venture with another small business or an SBA-approved mentor for a HUBZone procurement.

The joint-venture agreement must exist and satisfy the rule by the required offer date.

The agreement must address:

  • purpose and contract scope;
  • designation of the certified firm as managing venturer;
  • a responsible manager employed by or committed to the certified managing venturer;
  • at least 51-percent ownership by the HUBZone firm when the venture is a separate legal entity;
  • profit distribution;
  • a special bank account;
  • equipment, facilities, personnel, and other resources;
  • negotiation and performance responsibilities;
  • recordkeeping and accounting;
  • limitations on subcontracting;
  • inspection of records; and
  • completion obligations if one member withdraws.

Joint-Venture Performance Requirements

The joint venture must comply with the contract’s limitation on subcontracting.

The certified HUBZone partner must perform at least 40 percent of the work performed by the joint-venture partners.

The HUBZone partner’s work must be substantive and provide meaningful experience rather than being limited to administrative or ministerial tasks.

Work performed by a mentor and its affiliates at any subcontracting tier may be counted when determining the mentor’s share.

The parties must submit required pre-performance and completion certifications.

Mentor–Protégé Participation

A certified HUBZone firm may become an SBA-approved protégé.

A mentor may provide management, technical, financial, bonding, equipment, facilities, personnel, proposal, international-trade, and contract-performance assistance.

SBA does not find affiliation solely because of an approved mentor–protégé agreement or authorised assistance.

The mentor’s employees are not counted when determining the protégé’s principal office or 35-percent residency.

A HUBZone prime may subcontract to its mentor, but it must comply with limitations on subcontracting and avoid dependence that makes the mentor an ostensible subcontractor.

Limitations on Subcontracting

HUBZone contracting assistance is intended to create work for certified small businesses rather than allow them to act as pass-throughs.

The prime contractor must satisfy the applicable limitation on subcontracting in 13 C.F.R. § 125.6 and the Federal Acquisition Regulation.

The required performance percentage varies among services, supplies, general construction, and specialty-trade construction.

Work performed by similarly situated certified HUBZone subcontractors may count toward compliance under current rules.

Supply contracts may also be subject to the nonmanufacturer rule.

Prime-Contractor Subcontracting Plans

The HUBZone Act added certified firms to the categories recognised in large-prime subcontracting plans.

Large prime contractors may establish HUBZone subcontracting goals, identify potential vendors, report awards, and receive credit for qualifying subcontracts.

Subcontracting can provide market entry, past performance, technical experience, and relationships without requiring the HUBZone firm to manage an entire prime contract.

A subcontract does not receive the same set-aside or price-preference protection as a HUBZone prime award.

Prime contractors and agencies have faced continuing criticism for failing to meet subcontracting commitments or accurately report performance.

Contract Performance

Certification addresses status, not contractual responsibility.

A HUBZone firm must still demonstrate financial capacity, technical ability, past performance, equipment, personnel, licences, security clearances, quality controls, bonding, and other solicitation requirements.

Contract disputes after award are decided by the procuring agency under the contract disputes system.

Poor performance can produce termination, adverse past performance, damages, suspension, or debarment independent of HUBZone certification.

HUBZone Status Protests

A HUBZone status protest challenges whether an apparent successful offeror meets program eligibility.

On a sole-source procurement, SBA or the contracting officer may protest.

On other HUBZone procurements, SBA, the contracting officer, or an interested offeror may protest.

Protests can challenge:

  • principal-office eligibility;
  • employee-residency percentage;
  • ownership and control;
  • joint-venture compliance;
  • size through a concurrent size protest;
  • failure to attempt to maintain;
  • residency below the 20-percent floor; or
  • undue reliance on non-HUBZone subcontractors.

General conclusions are insufficient. The protest must state specific facts.

Protest Deadlines and Decisions

An interested party generally must file within five business days after notice of the apparent successful offeror in a negotiated procurement.

Sealed-bid deadlines generally run from bid opening or later notice of the eligible low bidder.

The protest is submitted to the contracting officer, who forwards it to SBA.

SBA and contracting-officer protests are not subject to the same interested-party timeliness rule.

SBA’s decision may affect the pending award and can result in immediate decertification.

Appeals proceed to SBA’s Office of Hearings and Appeals under applicable procedures.

Decertification

SBA may propose decertification when:

  • a program examination finds the firm ineligible;
  • the firm fails to respond to an examination;
  • the firm fails to complete recertification;
  • a merger or acquisition destroys eligibility;
  • the firm falls below the attempt-to-maintain floor;
  • ownership, control, size, office, or residency requirements are no longer met;
  • the concern provides false or misleading information; or
  • another program event establishes ineligibility.

SBA generally provides notice and an opportunity to respond before ordinary decertification.

A sustained status protest or government-wide debarment can produce immediate decertification.

A decertified firm may not submit new HUBZone offers and must inform contracting officers of affected pending procurements.

False Information and Cross-Program Consequences

SBA refers knowingly false or misleading HUBZone information to its Office of Inspector General and may recommend suspension or debarment.

A firm decertified for false information may also be removed from the 8(a), WOSB, VetCert, and SBA Mentor–Protégé programs.

False certification can support criminal prosecution, False Claims Act liability, civil penalties, contract remedies, and recovery of federal funds.

SBA may require an administrative agreement before allowing admission or readmission after serious cross-program misconduct.

Fraud and Abuse Risks

Documented risks include:

  • mailbox or virtual offices lacking real employees;
  • employees listed at addresses where they do not live;
  • temporary residence arranged solely for certification;
  • workers omitted from the denominator;
  • independent contractors misclassified to manipulate employee count;
  • undisclosed affiliates and shared workers;
  • ineligible ownership or control;
  • fictitious payroll records;
  • failure to maintain eligibility at offer;
  • large businesses controlling nominal HUBZone primes;
  • pass-through subcontracting;
  • misuse of joint ventures;
  • false map or residency documents; and
  • failure to notify SBA of changes.

GAO undercover investigations in 2008–2010 demonstrated that firms could obtain or retain certification despite obvious office and residency defects under the controls then in place.

Oversight Reforms

SBA responded to GAO and Inspector General findings by increasing site visits, document verification, program examinations, map controls, recertification, fraud referrals, risk analysis, and database review.

The 2019 comprehensive final rule simplified and stabilised map and certification rules while creating legacy-employee and long-term-investment provisions.

The 2024 final rule restored stronger offer-date eligibility and replaced annual recertification with triennial recertification and recurring examinations.

SBA now may remove a firm from the public certified list during a suspension period associated with proposed decertification.

Oversight remains difficult because employee residence, remote work, job sites, acquisitions, map status, and affiliate relationships change between reviews.

Economic-Development Theory

HUBZone attempts to connect a federal contract with local development through two principal mechanisms.

Business Location

The principal-office rule encourages rent, property investment, professional activity, purchases, and business presence in a designated area.

Resident Employment

The 35-percent rule encourages firms to hire people living in eligible communities.

The contract itself may be performed elsewhere. Economic benefit depends on whether payroll, profits, management, purchases, training, and investment remain connected to the designated area.

Certification can create a reason to locate and hire locally, but it does not guarantee broad neighbourhood revitalisation.

Urban Communities

Qualified census tracts can include neighbourhoods experiencing concentrated poverty, unemployment, vacant property, disinvestment, or lower household income.

HUBZone firms may bring office occupancy, wages, federal revenue, training, and procurement relationships into those areas.

Urban firms may face high commercial rents, rapid gentrification, employee displacement, changing tract eligibility, and competition for skilled workers.

A tract can lose eligibility as conditions improve, while low-income residents may move elsewhere because of rising costs.

The program’s address-based rules cannot by themselves prevent displacement or ensure that benefits reach long-term residents.

Rural Communities

Qualified nonmetropolitan counties and governor-designated areas extend the program beyond central-city poverty.

Rural firms may obtain federal opportunities in construction, maintenance, manufacturing, logistics, technology, environmental services, healthcare, food, natural resources, and professional services.

Challenges include distance from federal buyers, limited broadband, small labour pools, transportation cost, bonding, specialised workforce, and few experienced government contractors.

A 35-percent residency requirement may be easier to understand geographically but difficult to maintain when skilled employees live outside county or tract boundaries.

Indian Country and Native Enterprise

Indian reservation designations recognise persistent economic barriers linked to geographic isolation, land status, infrastructure gaps, limited private credit, and historic federal policy.

Tribal ownership can connect contract revenue with governmental and community purposes.

Native-owned HUBZone firms may also qualify for 8(a), Buy Indian, mentor–protégé, and other procurement opportunities under separate rules.

Multiple certifications can expand opportunity but require independent compliance with each program.

Military-Base Redevelopment

Base closure can eliminate military and civilian jobs, local purchasing, housing demand, and municipal revenue.

HUBZone designation can help new firms compete for federal work while communities redevelop former installations.

Existing hangars, warehouses, offices, utilities, transportation, and industrial land may support manufacturing, logistics, maintenance, technology, and commercial reuse.

Contracting preference cannot replace comprehensive environmental remediation, land transfer, infrastructure, and workforce policy.

Disaster Recovery

Qualified disaster designations can provide temporary contracting support to firms in communities affected by hurricanes, floods, fires, earthquakes, and other major events.

Local businesses may be positioned to supply debris removal, construction, logistics, temporary facilities, professional services, and long-term rebuilding.

Disaster contracting also creates risks of fraud, emergency noncompetition, out-of-area contractors, rapid workforce change, and weak documentation.

A disaster declaration does not automatically make every affected address a HUBZone.

Access to Federal Contracting

Certification can help a firm overcome the difficulty of entering unrestricted federal markets dominated by larger incumbents.

Restricted competitions may reduce the bidder pool, while sole-source authority can create an initial contract and past performance.

The price preference can improve competitiveness against large businesses without changing the firm’s actual offered price.

Access remains dependent on procurement demand, NAICS codes, agency market research, capability, proposal quality, bonding, financing, clearances, and performance.

Firms in areas with little federal procurement or no matching agency requirements may receive limited benefit.

Program Achievements

  • It created a national place-based federal-contracting program.
  • It links eligibility to both business location and employee residence.
  • It provides competitive set-asides, sole-source authority, and a price-evaluation preference.
  • It includes urban, rural, Tribal, base-closure, disaster, and governor-designated communities.
  • It provides a federal market-entry route for small firms outside traditional contracting centres.
  • It encourages offices and long-term property investment in designated areas.
  • It encourages firms to hire and retain HUBZone residents.
  • It includes certified firms in large-prime subcontracting plans.
  • It supports mentor–protégé relationships and qualifying joint ventures.
  • It permits continued participation without a fixed maximum term where eligibility continues.
  • It provides transitional protections when map status changes.
  • It supports redevelopment after military closure and qualifying disasters.
  • It has directed billions of dollars in federal obligations to certified firms.

Limitations and Failures

  • Certification does not guarantee a contract.
  • Federal agencies frequently fail to meet the 3-percent goal.
  • The principal-office and residency rules create significant documentation burdens.
  • Map boundaries can divide streets, neighbourhoods, and labour markets arbitrarily.
  • A firm can perform contract work outside the qualifying community.
  • Federal award dollars do not reveal how much payroll or profit remains locally.
  • Qualified employees may move, and areas may lose designation.
  • Remote and hybrid work make office and employee counting more complex.
  • Small rural labour pools can make 35-percent residency difficult for specialised firms.
  • Legacy and attempt-to-maintain rules improve stability but weaken the directness of the 35-percent link.
  • GAO found serious historical vulnerabilities to fraud and abuse.
  • SBA has not consistently measured employment, investment, or community-development outcomes.
  • Certification benefits may concentrate among firms located in procurement-rich metropolitan regions.
  • Set-asides and sole-source awards can create dependency or reduce competition.
  • Place-based preference alone cannot solve inadequate capital, infrastructure, housing, transportation, or education.

Major Controversies

Eligibility Fraud

Firms have used nominal offices, false employee addresses, incomplete payrolls, and undisclosed relationships to claim eligibility.

Employment Impact

Contract awards are measured more consistently than jobs, wages, retention, training, or investment in the designated area.

Map Stability versus Accuracy

Stable five-year maps help businesses plan, but they may retain improving areas or delay newly distressed areas.

Thirty-Five-Percent Requirement

The rule directly connects firms with residents but can be difficult during rapid hiring, specialised recruitment, remote work, or employee relocation.

Long-Term Investment and Legacy Rules

These provisions protect genuine investment and employee retention but allow some continuing eligibility after an office area or employee residence no longer qualifies.

Socioeconomic Program Parity

Agencies choose among several contracting programs, creating competition for the same procurement opportunities and agency goal credit.

Community Benefit

A firm may satisfy the formal tests while producing little broader development beyond the certified business and its qualifying employees.

Documented Outcomes

Certified firms have received billions of dollars in federal prime-contract obligations and additional subcontract awards.

The program has expanded federal opportunities for firms located in distressed urban, rural, Tribal, and redevelopment areas.

Some businesses have used certification to enter federal markets, add employees, purchase facilities, obtain mentors, and build past performance.

GAO has repeatedly concluded that SBA’s performance information does not adequately establish the program’s broader effect on employment, investment, or community economic development.

Award totals alone cannot determine whether a community improved because of HUBZone or whether the contract would have gone to the same firm without the preference.

Outcome assessment requires firm-level and area-level information concerning employment, employee residence, wages, business survival, office investment, subcontracting, profits, and local economic conditions.

Historical Significance

HUBZone made geography a central category of federal small-business procurement policy.

It differs from owner-identity programs by attempting to reward firms for locating and hiring in distressed places.

The program linked urban policy, rural development, Indian-country policy, base-closure redevelopment, disaster recovery, and government contracting.

Its history demonstrates the difficulty of translating contract awards into measurable community development.

The recurring revisions to map, office, residency, recertification, and examination rules illustrate the tension between stable business planning and accurate place-based targeting.

Research Cautions

  • A HUBZone address does not equal certification. SBA must certify the firm, and every other eligibility condition must be met.
  • The principal office is based on employee work location. It is not automatically the registered headquarters or mailing address.
  • Employee and full-time employee are different concepts. A person working ten hours per week may count as an employee, while legacy status generally requires thirty hours.
  • Residence requires actual full-time living. A temporary address or mailing address is insufficient.
  • Certification and contract eligibility are separate. Current rules generally require eligibility at the time of offer.
  • Recertification is triennial, not annual. The rule changed in 2025.
  • The 20-percent provision is limited. It applies only after a recent HUBZone award and requires documented attempts to return to 35 percent.
  • The price preference does not increase the government’s payment by 10 percent. It changes evaluation of a large-business offer.
  • The government-wide 3-percent goal is not a guarantee to any firm. Agencies and procurements vary.
  • There is no current priority over 8(a), WOSB, or VetCert. Contracting programs have parity.
  • Award dollars are not community-development outcomes. Jobs, wages, investment, and local retention require separate evidence.
  • Map status changes. Always preserve the official map result for the relevant date.

Definitions

Attempt to maintain
Substantive and documented efforts during HUBZone contract performance to maintain 35-percent employee residency, with less than 20 percent treated as failure.
Certified HUBZone small business concern
A small business that meets Part 126 requirements and appears as certified in the federal small-business database.
Employee
An individual who generally works at least ten hours per week during the four weeks immediately before the relevant date.
Governor-designated covered area
A qualifying rural area added to the HUBZone map after SBA approves a governor’s petition.
HUBZone
A historically underutilized business zone falling within one or more statutory geographic categories.
HUBZone contract
A federal prime contract or order awarded through a recognised HUBZone contracting method.
HUBZone resident employee
An employee whom SBA determines resides in a current HUBZone or qualifies under an authorised legacy provision.
Legacy HUBZone employee
A qualifying continuously employed individual who can continue to count after moving or after the residence loses status.
Principal office
The location where the greatest number of a concern’s employees at any one location perform their work.
Qualified base-closure area
A former military-installation area and specified surrounding geography treated as a HUBZone for a statutory period.
Qualified census tract
A HUD-designated tract meeting statutory income or poverty criteria.
Qualified disaster area
A qualifying tract or nonmetropolitan county temporarily designated following a presidential disaster or catastrophic incident.
Qualified nonmetropolitan county
A rural county meeting statutory income, unemployment, or specified difficult-development-area criteria.
Redesignated area
A former qualified census tract or nonmetropolitan county retained temporarily as a HUBZone after losing original status.
Reside
Live full-time at a location for at least ninety calendar days immediately before the relevant review date.
Set-aside
A procurement for which competition is restricted to an eligible small-business category.
Sole-source award
A contract negotiated with one eligible firm without competition among multiple offerors.

Summary

The Historically Underutilized Business Zone Program was created by the HUBZone Act of 1997 to use federal contracting as a tool for employment, investment, and small-business development in distressed places.

A standard applicant must qualify as small, meet an eligible ownership and control structure, maintain its principal office in a mapped HUBZone, and have at least 35 percent of its employees residing in HUBZones.

Eligible geographic categories include qualified census tracts, qualified nonmetropolitan counties, Indian reservations, redesignated areas, qualified base-closure areas, qualified disaster areas, and governor-designated covered areas.

SBA maintains the official map, certifies firms, conducts recurring examinations, decides status protests, and decertifies firms that no longer qualify.

Certification can provide access to HUBZone set-asides, limited sole-source awards, restricted orders, subcontracting-plan credit, mentor–protégé and joint-venture opportunities, and a 10-percent price-evaluation preference in qualifying full-and-open competitions.

The government-wide goal is to award at least 3 percent of eligible prime and subcontracting dollars to certified HUBZone firms.

Current rules require program recertification every three years, but firms generally must also meet HUBZone requirements at the time they submit a priced offer for a HUBZone contract.

A firm awarded a HUBZone contract during the preceding twelve months may submit another HUBZone offer with at least 20 percent HUBZone-resident employees when it documents substantive efforts to return to 35 percent.

During contract performance, the firm must attempt to maintain 35-percent residency. Falling below 20 percent establishes failure and can cause decertification.

The program provides meaningful federal opportunities but has faced persistent problems involving false offices, employee-residency evidence, affiliate counting, pass-through contracting, map changes, low agency goal attainment, and weak measurement of community economic outcomes.
