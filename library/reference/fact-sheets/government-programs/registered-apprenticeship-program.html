Registered Apprenticeship Program

The national employment-and-training system combining paid work, experienced mentorship, structured occupational learning, related technical instruction, progressive wages, and a portable completion credential.

Overview

The Registered Apprenticeship Program is the national system through which employers, labor organizations, industry associations, public agencies, educational institutions, and other sponsors establish formally recognized apprenticeship programs meeting federal standards for paid employment, structured workplace learning, related instruction, progressive wages, mentoring, supervision, and occupational credentials.

Registered Apprenticeship is not a single federal cash-benefit or grant program. It is a regulated employment-and-training model authorized under the National Apprenticeship Act of 1937, also called the Fitzgerald Act, and administered by the United States Department of Labor’s Office of Apprenticeship and federally recognized State Apprenticeship Agencies.

A Registered Apprenticeship Program is commonly abbreviated RAP. Registration means that a program’s written standards have been reviewed and accepted by an authorized Registration Agency as satisfying the national labor standards in 29 C.F.R. part 29 and applicable state requirements.

The Department of Labor defines Registered Apprenticeship as an industry-driven career pathway through which individuals obtain a paid job, structured on-the-job learning under experienced mentors, progressive wage increases, supplemental classroom or technical instruction, and a portable nationally recognized credential.

The five commonly identified elements are industry leadership, paid employment, structured on-the-job learning and mentorship, related instruction, and a credential upon successful completion.

Apprentices are workers, not merely students observing a workplace. They perform productive work for participating employers, receive wages, and progress through an occupational training plan. Their employment remains subject to applicable wage, hour, safety, workers’ compensation, collective-bargaining, licensing, discrimination, and other federal, state, and local laws.

Programs can be sponsored by a single employer, multiple employers, an employer association, a joint labor-management committee, a labor organization, an industry intermediary, an educational institution, a community organization, a public agency, or another qualified entity capable of administering the standards.

A sponsor is responsible for program administration even when participating employers, colleges, unions, training centers, workforce boards, or intermediaries provide parts of the training. The sponsor registers apprentices, maintains records, monitors progress, requests completion certificates, and ensures that participating employers follow the approved standards.

An occupation must be apprenticeable. It must be customarily learned through structured supervised work, be clearly identified and recognized within an industry, involve progressive attainment of manual, mechanical, or technical skills and knowledge that would require at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning if taught through a time-based approach, and require related instruction.

Registered Apprenticeship can use a time-based, competency-based, or hybrid completion approach. Time-based programs measure progress through at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning across the full apprenticeship term. Competency-based programs measure demonstrated occupational skills and knowledge while retaining an on-the-job learning component. Hybrid programs combine minimum hours with demonstrated competencies.

The Department’s March 2026 guidance clarified that the 2,000-hour standard defines an apprenticeable occupation and the minimum term of a time-based program; it does not impose a twelve-month minimum on a competency-based apprentice. Sponsors select the training approach, subject to Registration Agency approval.

Program standards include a work-process schedule, related-instruction outline, progressive wage schedule, apprentice-to-journeyworker ratio, minimum qualifications, probationary period, safety provisions, supervision, performance review, recordkeeping, complaint procedures, and a written apprenticeship agreement.

A minimum of 144 hours of organized related instruction for each year of apprenticeship is recommended by federal regulation. Instruction can be delivered by community colleges, technical schools, unions, employers, online providers, secondary schools, universities, or other approved sources. Whether instruction time is paid depends on the program standards and applicable law.

The entry wage must not be below the federal minimum wage where the Fair Labor Standards Act applies, unless a higher wage is required by another federal law, state or local law, a prevailing-wage rule, or a collective-bargaining agreement. The standards must contain progressive wage increases consistent with increasing skill and productivity.

The ordinary minimum entry age is sixteen, although state law, child-labor law, safety requirements, licensing rules, or the occupation can require a higher age. Programs can establish additional lawful minimum qualifications involving education, tests, physical requirements, licenses, driver status, or other job-related criteria.

Every registered apprentice is placed under a written apprenticeship agreement. The agreement identifies the occupation, beginning date, term, training approach, work processes, related instruction, wage schedule, probation, equal-opportunity statement, and procedures for resolving controversies.

A probationary period may not exceed 25 percent of the program term or one year, whichever is shorter. During probation, either party can generally cancel the agreement through written notice. After probation, sponsor-initiated suspension or cancellation generally requires good cause, due notice, a reasonable opportunity for corrective action, and notice to the Registration Agency.

Successful completers receive a Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship issued through the Office of Apprenticeship or a State Apprenticeship Agency after sponsor certification. Programs can also establish approved interim credentials for occupational milestones.

Registration is performed through one of two types of Registration Agencies. The Office of Apprenticeship registers and oversees programs in states and territories without a recognized State Apprenticeship Agency and can register certain national or multistate standards. Recognized State Apprenticeship Agencies register and oversee programs within their jurisdictions under federal recognition.

Registration is voluntary as a matter of federal apprenticeship law, but it can be required to receive particular public funds, tax incentives, prevailing-wage apprentice treatment, veterans’ education benefits, state licenses, or other program advantages.

A person seeking an apprenticeship applies directly to an employer or program sponsor rather than to the Department of Labor for a personal benefit. The Apprenticeship Job Finder and state systems list some openings, but not every registered sponsor posts every opportunity publicly.

Registration does not guarantee that an applicant will be selected. Sponsors establish lawful selection procedures, and apprenticeships are jobs dependent on employer demand, available positions, occupational qualifications, geographic location, and program capacity.

Registered Apprenticeship differs from pre-apprenticeship. Pre-apprenticeship prepares participants for entry and should align with one or more Registered Apprenticeship programs, but it is not itself a Registered Apprenticeship unless separately registered and does not automatically confer apprentice status, wages, or a federal completion credential.

Registered Apprenticeship also differs from an internship, ordinary on-the-job training, informal employer training, cooperative education, or unregistered apprenticeship. Those arrangements can be valuable, but they do not necessarily contain the complete registered standards, oversight, progressive wages, national credential, or written apprentice agreement.

Registered Apprenticeship spans construction, manufacturing, transportation, utilities, public safety, health care, education, technology, cybersecurity, telecommunications, finance, hospitality, agriculture, energy, public service, and other industries.

Construction and skilled trades remain foundational, frequently using joint labor-management training committees, employer associations, union training centers, apprenticeship ratios, journeyworker supervision, and multi-year time-based programs. Recent federal expansion has emphasized advanced manufacturing, shipbuilding, defense, artificial intelligence, semiconductors, nuclear infrastructure, information technology, health care, teaching, and public service.

Registered Apprenticeship can connect with the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act. Registered programs are automatically eligible for inclusion on state Eligible Training Provider Lists while they remain registered, subject to the sponsor’s participation in the state process. WIOA funds can support related instruction, on-the-job training reimbursement, supportive services, recruitment, and other allowable costs for eligible participants.

Veterans and eligible dependents can receive Department of Veterans Affairs education benefits for approved apprenticeship and on-the-job training programs in addition to wages, subject to federal and state approving-agency requirements. Registration by itself does not automatically establish every program’s VA approval.

Apprentices can receive college credit, certificates, or degrees when the sponsor and education provider create an aligned program. A federal apprenticeship completion certificate is a postsecondary occupational credential but is not automatically equivalent to a college degree, occupational license, or academic credit.

Federal and state governments support apprenticeship through formula grants, competitive grants, workforce funds, education funds, tax credits, tuition support, veterans’ benefits, infrastructure and prevailing-wage incentives, technical assistance, and employer or apprentice incentives. Registration does not guarantee that a sponsor or apprentice will receive any particular subsidy.

The Office of Apprenticeship’s official fiscal year 2025 fact sheet reported more than 702,000 active apprentices, more than 26,000 active apprenticeship programs, and more than 3,000 new programs established since January 2025. Data definitions and reporting dates matter, and the national dashboard is updated as states and the United Services Military Apprenticeship Program transmit records.

The administration has established a goal of reaching and surpassing one million active apprentices. In March 2026, the Office of Apprenticeship announced a commitment to issue registration determinations within thirty days after a sponsor’s final submission of signed standards through Standards Builder or the applicable national-standards process in Office of Apprenticeship jurisdictions.

In April 2026, the Department announced approximately $85 million for a fourth round of State Apprenticeship Expansion Formula grants. In July 2026, it awarded $161,999,142 through five pay-for-performance cooperative agreements targeting shipbuilding and the defense industrial base, artificial intelligence, semiconductors, nuclear-energy infrastructure, telecommunications, information technology, and transportation.

The Department’s June 2025 Circular 2025-03 adopted an interim policy of not enforcing 29 C.F.R. part 30 equal-employment-opportunity requirements until further notice. Part 30 remains published in the Code of Federal Regulations, and sponsors remain subject to applicable federal and state laws prohibiting workplace discrimination. The Department stated that discrimination complaints submitted through its apprenticeship form would be referred to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission or another relevant agency.

Registered Apprenticeship has major racial and economic significance. African American workers have historically faced exclusion from unions, segregated craft systems, discriminatory tests and referrals, occupational segregation, unequal schooling, and limited access to employer networks. Registration can create transparent standards and portable credentials, but formal registration alone does not eliminate discrimination, unequal recruitment, hostile work environments, cancellation disparities, or concentration in lower-wage occupations.

Definitions

Registered Apprenticeship Program
A plan containing the terms and conditions governing qualification, recruitment, selection, employment, and training of apprentices and accepted by a Registration Agency as meeting applicable standards.
Registered apprentice
An individual employed and individually registered under a Registered Apprenticeship Program through a written apprenticeship agreement.
Apprenticeship
A structured employment-and-training relationship through which a worker learns an occupation through supervised work and related instruction.
Sponsor
The organization responsible for administering a Registered Apprenticeship Program and whose name appears in the registered standards.
Participating employer
An employer authorized under a sponsor’s standards to employ and train registered apprentices.
Registration Agency
The Office of Apprenticeship or a federally recognized State Apprenticeship Agency responsible for registering and overseeing programs.
Office of Apprenticeship
The Department of Labor office administering the National Apprenticeship Act in federal registration jurisdictions and overseeing the national system.
State Apprenticeship Agency
A state government agency recognized by the Office of Apprenticeship to register and oversee programs within the state.
State Apprenticeship Council
A state advisory or regulatory body whose permissible functions depend on state law and federal recognition requirements.
Apprenticeable occupation
An occupation meeting federal criteria concerning structured supervised learning, industry recognition, progressive skill, at least 2,000 hours of learning if time based, and related instruction.
Journeyworker
A worker recognized as fully skilled in an apprenticeable occupation and qualified to train or supervise apprentices.
On-the-job learning
Structured supervised workplace training in the occupation’s work processes. The abbreviation OJL is commonly used.
Related instruction
Organized technical and theoretical instruction supplementing workplace learning. The terms related technical instruction and RTI are also used.
Work-process schedule
The outline of occupational tasks and processes in which apprentices receive supervised training.
Standards of Apprenticeship
The approved written rules governing the program’s occupation, selection, training, wages, supervision, records, and completion.
Apprenticeship agreement
The written agreement between the apprentice and sponsor or employer incorporating the registered standards.
Time-based approach
A completion method measuring skill acquisition through completion of at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning in the work-process schedule.
Competency-based approach
A completion method based on successful demonstration of occupational skills and knowledge while retaining structured on-the-job learning.
Hybrid approach
A completion method combining a specified minimum amount of on-the-job learning with demonstrated competency.
Progressive wage schedule
A series of wage increases tied to increasing skill, experience, time, competency, or specified program milestones.
Probationary period
The initial program period during which the apprenticeship agreement can generally be cancelled by either party through notice.
Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship
The nationally recognized credential issued through the Registration Agency after the sponsor certifies successful completion.
Interim credential
A recognized occupational milestone approved in the standards before full program completion.
National Program Standards
Standards approved for a sponsor operating a program across multiple locations under Office of Apprenticeship procedures.
National Guideline Standards
Model standards developed by an organization for adoption by local or affiliated sponsors.
RAPIDS
The Registered Apprenticeship Partners Information Database System used for program, apprentice, and performance records.
Pre-apprenticeship
A preparatory education and training program designed to help participants qualify for and enter Registered Apprenticeship.
Youth apprenticeship
An apprenticeship or preparatory model serving secondary-school-age or young adult participants and coordinated with education, child-labor, and employment law.
Direct-entry provision
An approved method allowing members of specified preparatory or priority groups to enter an apprenticeship outside the ordinary ranking process while meeting minimum qualifications.
Apprentice-to-journeyworker ratio
The number of apprentices permitted in relation to qualified journeyworkers to support safety, supervision, training, and continuity of employment.
Completion rate
The percentage of a defined apprentice cohort receiving a completion certificate within the regulatory measurement period.
Cancellation
Termination of a program’s registration or an individual apprenticeship agreement.
Deregistration
Withdrawal of a program’s registered status by the Registration Agency for reasonable cause or at the sponsor’s request.

Core Elements of Registered Apprenticeship

Industry Led

Employers and industry partners identify occupational skills, work processes, wage progression, supervision, and workforce demand. Registration validates the standards but does not replace employer responsibility.

Paid Job

The apprentice is employed and earns wages. The apprenticeship is not an unpaid classroom program or observation experience.

Structured On-the-Job Learning

A trained mentor or journeyworker supervises productive work covering the occupation’s approved processes.

Related Instruction

Technical and theoretical education supplements the practical experience and can be delivered in classrooms, laboratories, online, at colleges, through unions, or by employers.

Progressive Wages

The apprentice receives scheduled increases as skill and productivity increase.

Portable Credential

Successful completion produces a nationally recognized Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship.

Quality Validation

The Registration Agency reviews standards, monitors program quality, records apprentices, and can provide technical assistance or deregister a deficient program.

National Registered Apprenticeship System

Federal-State Structure

The National Apprenticeship System uses shared federal standards and two registration paths: the federal Office of Apprenticeship and recognized State Apprenticeship Agencies.

Office of Apprenticeship States

Federal field staff register and oversee programs in jurisdictions without a recognized state agency and assist sponsors with development, registration, data, and compliance.

State Apprenticeship Agency States

Recognized state agencies perform registration and oversight under state law and federal recognition. Their procedures can differ while remaining consistent with 29 C.F.R. part 29.

National and Multistate Programs

Large sponsors, associations, unions, and intermediaries can develop national standards or national guidelines to support consistent programs across jurisdictions.

RAPIDS

The national database records programs, occupations, apprentices, completions, cancellations, demographics, and other administrative information. Recent data modernization has expanded individual-level reporting from state systems and the military program.

Technical Assistance

Federal and state apprenticeship representatives assist employers and sponsors without charge in identifying occupations, drafting standards, locating instruction, and connecting to partners and funding.

Oversight

Registration Agencies review standards, monitor program operation, calculate completion rates, provide technical assistance, and can suspend or deregister programs for reasonable cause.

Sponsors and Participating Partners

Single Employer

One employer sponsors and operates a program for its own workforce.

Multiple Employers

An association, intermediary, union, community college, or joint committee can sponsor a program used by several participating employers.

Joint Labor-Management Sponsor

A committee representing signatory employers and a union jointly administers standards, selection, training, and trust-funded instruction.

Employer Association

A trade or industry association can administer common standards and place apprentices with participating firms.

Industry Intermediary

An intermediary can reduce administrative burden, recruit employers, coordinate instruction, manage data, and scale programs across regions.

Educational Institution

A college, school district, or training institution can sponsor or partner with employers while ensuring that the apprenticeship remains employment based.

Public Agency

Federal, state, local, and Tribal agencies can sponsor apprenticeships for public-service occupations.

Sponsor Responsibility

Delegating instruction, payroll, recruitment, or recordkeeping does not eliminate the sponsor’s responsibility for the registered standards.

Apprenticeable Occupations

Practical Learning

The occupation must involve skills customarily learned through a structured and systematic program of supervised on-the-job learning.

Industry Recognition

The occupation must be clearly identified and commonly recognized throughout an industry.

Progressive Skill

The occupation must involve progressive attainment of manual, mechanical, or technical skills and knowledge.

2,000-Hour Standard

The occupation must require at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning to attain proficiency if learning is measured through a time-based approach.

Related Instruction

The occupation must require organized technical instruction supplementing workplace learning.

Occupation Approval

Registration Agencies seek industry input and review occupational frameworks, work processes, O*NET classifications, licensing, and existing standards.

Occupation Versus Job Title

Not every job title automatically qualifies. Registration focuses on the actual occupational skills, term, supervision, and instruction rather than a label chosen by the employer.

Standards of Apprenticeship

Occupation and Training Approach

Standards identify the apprenticeable occupation and whether completion is time based, competency based, or hybrid.

Work Processes

The program outlines the major occupational processes and expected workplace learning.

Related Instruction

The standards identify technical subjects, providers, delivery methods, and anticipated instructional hours.

Wage Progression

The standards state the entry wage and progressive increases.

Supervision and Ratio

The apprentice-to-journeyworker ratio must support proper supervision, training, safety, and continuity of employment.

Minimum Qualifications

The sponsor specifies lawful entry qualifications, selection procedures, and an eligible starting age not below sixteen.

Probation

The standards establish a reasonable probationary period not exceeding 25 percent of the term or one year, whichever is shorter.

Safety

Adequate equipment, facilities, supervision, and safety training are required.

Performance Review

Sponsors periodically evaluate on-the-job and related-instruction progress and maintain records.

Completion and Cancellation

Standards explain completion certification, voluntary cancellation, discipline, corrective action, and complaint procedures.

Structured On-the-Job Learning

Productive Employment

Apprentices learn while performing real work for an employer rather than practicing only in a classroom.

Mentorship

Experienced journeyworkers, technicians, professionals, supervisors, or other qualified workers demonstrate tasks, observe performance, and provide feedback.

Work-Process Coverage

The apprentice should receive exposure to all required phases of the occupation rather than remain indefinitely assigned to one narrow task.

Documentation

Sponsors track hours, competencies, evaluations, job assignments, and progress under the approved approach.

Credit for Prior Experience

A sponsor can award advanced standing for verified prior education or experience under uniform procedures and adjust placement and wages accordingly.

Remote and Simulated Learning

Virtual tools and simulation can supplement training when approved, but Registered Apprenticeship retains a substantive workplace-learning component.

Safety

Apprentices must receive safety instruction, adequate supervision, and lawful equipment and facilities.

Related Technical Instruction

Purpose

Related instruction explains the theory, technical knowledge, calculations, codes, science, communication, and problem-solving underlying occupational work.

Recommended Hours

Federal regulations recommend at least 144 hours for each year of apprenticeship. A Registration Agency can approve an appropriate structure based on the occupation and standards.

Providers

  • Community and technical colleges
  • Union training centers
  • Employers and employer associations
  • Secondary schools and career academies
  • Universities
  • Online and hybrid providers
  • Industry certification organizations
  • Public agencies

Instructor Qualifications

Instructors must satisfy state vocational-technical requirements or be recognized subject-matter experts and receive training in teaching techniques and adult-learning styles.

Compensation for Instruction Time

Federal apprenticeship regulations require the standards to state whether related instruction is compensated. Wage-and-hour law, state law, collective bargaining, and the circumstances of attendance determine when payment is legally required.

College Credit

Instruction can carry college credit when an accredited institution approves the curriculum. Credit is not automatic merely because the program is registered.

Coordination

Instruction should be closely correlated with the apprentice’s workplace experience, and sponsors must monitor progress.

Wages, Employment, and Benefits

Employment Status

Registered Apprenticeship is built around paid employment. Apprentices can be employed by the sponsor or by a participating employer.

Entry Wage

The entry wage cannot be below the applicable federal minimum wage where the Fair Labor Standards Act applies. Higher state, local, prevailing-wage, or collectively bargained rates control when applicable.

Progressive Schedule

Standards identify increases tied to hours, competencies, periods, or other milestones.

Journeyworker Rate

Many programs express apprentice wages as percentages of the journeyworker or occupational rate, but the precise structure depends on the standards, law, and collective bargaining.

Benefits

Health insurance, pensions, leave, tools, tuition, and other benefits depend on employment policy, collective bargaining, public contracts, and program design.

Related-Instruction Pay

Time spent in instruction is not automatically unpaid or paid under apprenticeship registration alone; wage-and-hour rules and the program’s facts control.

Prevailing Wage

On covered federal or federally assisted construction, registered apprentice wage treatment depends on Davis-Bacon and related requirements, including registration, ratios, and wage schedules.

Inflation Reduction Act

Certain clean-energy tax incentives use prevailing-wage and apprenticeship requirements. Compliance involves qualified apprentices, labor-hour percentages, ratios, participation, and good-faith requests under tax and labor guidance.

Written Apprenticeship Agreement

Parties

The apprentice and sponsor or employer sign the agreement. A parent or guardian also signs when the apprentice is a minor.

Occupation and Term

The agreement states the occupation, beginning date, duration or competency framework, work processes, and related instruction.

Wages

The agreement identifies the graduated wage schedule and whether related instruction is compensated.

Probation

The agreement states the probationary period and cancellation procedures.

After Probation

The apprentice can request cancellation. Sponsor-initiated suspension or cancellation generally requires good cause, notice, corrective opportunity, and Registration Agency notice.

Incorporation of Standards

The registered standards are incorporated directly or by reference and can be amended under approved procedures.

Equal Opportunity Statement

The agreement includes the regulatory statement concerning equal opportunity, even though the Department currently maintains an interim part 30 non-enforcement policy.

Controversies

The agreement identifies the authority responsible for disputes not resolved locally or through collective bargaining.

Recruitment and Selection

Job Application

Applicants apply to an employer or sponsor. Selection can involve applications, interviews, tests, ranked lists, direct entry, school partnerships, or hiring processes.

Minimum Qualifications

Programs can require education, licenses, physical capacity, tests, driving eligibility, security clearance, drug testing, or other job-related conditions permitted by law.

Selection Procedures

Standards describe how qualified applicants are selected, ranked, referred, or directly entered.

Direct Entry

Approved direct-entry pathways can serve graduates of aligned pre-apprenticeship, veterans, Job Corps participants, youth programs, incumbent workers, or other designated groups.

Employer Demand

An applicant can satisfy minimum qualifications without receiving an offer when no apprentice position is available.

Public Posting

Some opportunities appear in the Apprenticeship Job Finder or state systems; others are recruited through unions, schools, workforce agencies, employers, or community partners.

Fees

Applicants should distinguish lawful instructional, union, licensing, testing, or tool costs from improper recruitment fees or false promises of guaranteed employment.

Equal Opportunity and Current Enforcement Policy

Part 30 Framework

29 C.F.R. part 30 prohibits discrimination and contains equal-opportunity, outreach, harassment-prevention, affirmative-action, utilization-analysis, disability, selection, recordkeeping, complaint, and enforcement provisions.

Protected Bases

The codified regulation identifies race, color, religion, national origin, sex, sexual orientation, age forty or older, genetic information, and disability.

Affirmative-Action Plans

The codified rule generally requires sponsors with five or more apprentices to develop written affirmative-action programs unless an exemption applies.

Seven-Percent Disability Goal

The codified regulation establishes a seven-percent utilization goal for qualified individuals with disabilities within each major occupation group.

Interim Federal Non-Enforcement

Circular 2025-03 states that the Office of Apprenticeship will not conduct enforcement actions concerning part 30 requirements until further notice and that federal program reviews will omit part 30 review.

Status of the Regulation

The circular is an enforcement policy and does not remove part 30 from the Code of Federal Regulations. Sponsors can also be subject to state apprenticeship requirements and separate civil-rights and employment laws.

Other Laws Continue

Title VII, the Americans with Disabilities Act, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, the Equal Pay Act, Section 504, state civil-rights laws, and contract or union obligations can apply independently.

Complaints

The Department stated that apprenticeship discrimination complaints submitted on ETA Form 9039 will be referred to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission or another relevant agency during the interim policy.

Program Registration Process

Explore

A prospective sponsor identifies workforce needs, apprenticeable occupations, participating employers, demand, and potential partners.

Build

The sponsor develops work processes, instruction, wages, supervision, selection, ratios, records, and administration.

Partner

Employers can work with unions, colleges, workforce boards, intermediaries, schools, community organizations, veterans’ agencies, and state offices.

Standards Builder

The federal Standards Builder guides sponsors through preparation of registration documents and connects them with apprenticeship staff.

Registration Agency Review

The Office of Apprenticeship or State Apprenticeship Agency evaluates the occupation and standards, provides technical assistance, and approves or rejects the submission.

Thirty-Day Commitment

The Office of Apprenticeship announced in March 2026 that it would seek to make final determinations within thirty days after receipt of complete signed local standards through Standards Builder and specified national submissions.

Program Registration

Approval registers the program. Individual apprentices must then be registered under written agreements.

Ongoing Amendments

New occupations, employers, locations, wage schedules, instruction, or other material changes can require Registration Agency approval.

Program Operation and Oversight

Apprentice Registration

Sponsors submit apprentice information and agreements to the Registration Agency or enter records in RAPIDS.

Progress Tracking

Sponsors document work processes, hours or competencies, instruction, wages, evaluations, probation, cancellations, and completions.

Participating Employers

Multi-employer sponsors establish employer agreements and verify that each worksite can provide supervision, wages, safety, and occupational training.

Program Reviews

Registration Agencies review whether apprentices receive approved work experience, related instruction, wage progression, supervision, and recordkeeping.

Completion Rates

Registration Agencies compare program completion rates with national averages and provide technical assistance to programs below the benchmark.

Corrective Action

Deficiencies can produce technical assistance, a corrective-action plan, suspension, or deregistration.

Apprentice Transfer

Programs can recognize prior learning or permit transfer when sponsors, employers, and Registration Agencies document credit and continued standards.

Records

Sponsors retain agreements, qualifications, selection, training, wages, progress, cancellations, completion, and other required documentation.

Completion and Credentials

Sponsor Certification

The sponsor determines that the apprentice completed the approved on-the-job learning, competencies, related instruction, and other standards.

Certificate Request

The sponsor submits completion information to the Registration Agency.

National Credential

The Office of Apprenticeship or State Apprenticeship Agency issues the Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship.

Journeyworker Status

Completion commonly establishes journeyworker or fully proficient occupational status within the program, although licensing, union, employer, or state requirements can impose additional conditions.

Interim Credentials

Programs can establish recognized interim credentials for approved milestones.

College Credential

A participant can simultaneously earn college credit, a certificate, or a degree when an education partner awards it. The apprenticeship certificate alone is not automatically a degree.

Occupational License

Registration does not replace a government-issued license where the occupation requires one.

Verification

Apprentices and employers can use federal or state systems to verify registration and completion records.

Career Seeker Pathway

Search

Career seekers identify occupations and openings through employers, unions, schools, American Job Centers, community organizations, state agencies, and the Apprenticeship Job Finder.

Apply

The applicant applies directly to the sponsor or employer and completes the lawful selection process.

Hire and Register

The employer hires the apprentice, and the sponsor registers the apprenticeship agreement.

Learn and Earn

The apprentice performs paid work, receives mentoring, completes related instruction, and earns progressive wage increases.

Demonstrate Progress

The sponsor evaluates hours, competencies, coursework, safety, attendance, and occupational performance.

Complete

The sponsor certifies completion and requests the national apprenticeship credential.

Advance

Completers can become journeyworkers, supervisors, instructors, contractors, professionals, business owners, or advanced apprentices, depending on the occupation.

Funding, Incentives, and Public Support

No Automatic Federal Operating Grant

Registration provides recognition and access to potential resources but does not guarantee reimbursement of wages, instruction, administration, or supportive services.

WIOA

Eligible participants can receive related-instruction funding, on-the-job-training reimbursement, supportive services, or other assistance through state and local workforce programs.

Education Funding

Perkins career and technical education funds, community-college appropriations, state scholarships, employer tuition programs, and federal student aid can support eligible educational components.

Veterans’ Benefits

Approved apprenticeship and on-the-job training programs can qualify veterans and eligible dependents for monthly education benefits in addition to wages.

State Incentives

States can provide tax credits, tuition support, wage reimbursements, equipment grants, employer incentives, and apprentice supports.

Federal Grants

The Department of Labor awards State Apprenticeship Expansion Formula, industry intermediary, Women in Apprenticeship and Nontraditional Occupations, performance-based, and other grants.

2025 Manufacturing Fund

The $35.8 million American Manufacturing Apprenticeship Incentive Fund offers employers incentives for new advanced-manufacturing apprentices through a national cooperative agreement.

The Department announced approximately $85 million for State Apprenticeship Expansion Formula Round 4.

Relationship to WIOA and American Job Centers

Eligible Training Provider Status

Registered Apprenticeship sponsors are automatically eligible for inclusion on state Eligible Training Provider Lists while the corresponding programs remain registered, although sponsors must indicate interest and supply required program information under state procedures.

Related Instruction

WIOA Individual Training Accounts can support approved related instruction for eligible apprentices.

On-the-Job Training

Local workforce boards can reimburse participating employers for a portion of eligible on-the-job training costs under WIOA rules.

Supportive Services

Eligible apprentices can receive transportation, childcare, tools, clothing, testing, technology, and other necessary assistance under local policy.

Career Services

American Job Centers can recruit, screen, assess, refer, and support apprenticeship applicants.

Youth Program

WIOA Youth funds can support pre-apprenticeship, work experience, occupational training, mentoring, and transition into Registered Apprenticeship.

Dislocated Workers

Workers affected by layoffs can use apprenticeship as a retraining and reemployment pathway.

No Automatic Participant Funding

A registered program’s ETPL status does not guarantee that a local board will fund every apprentice or every cost.

Education, College Credit, and Degrees

Community Colleges

Colleges can provide related instruction, credit, certificates, degrees, laboratories, faculty, advising, and financial-aid coordination.

Degree-Connected Apprenticeship

A program can align employment, apprenticeship completion, and an associate or bachelor’s degree so the apprentice progresses toward both occupational and academic credentials.

Secondary Schools

Career and technical education, dual enrollment, work-based learning, and youth apprenticeship can connect high-school students to registered programs.

Credit Articulation

Institutions determine whether apprenticeship instruction and workplace learning qualify for academic credit.

Federal Student Aid

Pell Grants, loans, and work-study can support eligible academic enrollment, but federal student aid generally does not pay the apprentice’s wages and follows separate institutional and program eligibility rules.

Workforce Pell

Short-term Pell eligibility beginning in 2026 can support qualifying workforce programs, including some apprenticeship-related instruction, when statutory and institutional requirements are met.

Instructor and Curriculum Quality

Education partners must coordinate curriculum with occupational standards rather than provide disconnected general coursework.

Veterans, GI Bill Benefits, and USMAP

GI Bill Benefits

Veterans and eligible dependents can receive monthly education benefits while earning apprenticeship wages in programs approved for veterans’ training.

Declining Benefit Schedule

Benefits commonly decline as the apprentice’s wages increase, depending on the applicable GI Bill chapter and approved program.

State Approving Agencies

A State Approving Agency or the Department of Veterans Affairs approves apprenticeship and on-the-job training for veterans’ education benefits.

Registration and VA Approval

Registered status supports quality recognition but does not automatically establish VA approval in every case.

USMAP

The United Services Military Apprenticeship Program registers occupational experience of active-duty service members and is the largest individual Registered Apprenticeship program in the nation.

Civilian Transition

Veterans can use military experience for advanced standing when a sponsor verifies that prior learning matches the standards.

WIOA Priority

Veterans and eligible spouses receive priority of service in covered Department of Labor-funded employment and training programs.

Youth Apprenticeship

Minimum Age

Federal standards permit a starting age of sixteen, but child-labor law and hazardous-occupation restrictions can limit tasks for minors.

Secondary-School Coordination

Youth programs can combine school coursework, dual enrollment, related instruction, paid employment, and progression into adult apprenticeship.

Apprentice Status

A youth apprentice registered under an approved agreement is a registered apprentice. A student participating only in career exploration or pre-apprenticeship is not automatically registered.

Child-Labor Compliance

Employers must comply with federal and state hour, occupation, safety, and school-attendance restrictions.

Intermediaries

School districts, community colleges, workforce boards, nonprofits, and state agencies can help small employers manage youth recruitment and instruction.

Equity

Youth apprenticeship can create early access to skilled careers but can also reproduce tracking if students are steered by race, sex, disability, or income into restricted options.

College Options

High-quality youth programs preserve pathways to college, advanced credentials, and career mobility rather than requiring premature foreclosure of education.

Pre-Apprenticeship

Preparation

Pre-apprenticeship develops foundational academic, technical, safety, employability, and occupational skills.

Alignment

A strong program aligns curriculum and entry requirements with one or more Registered Apprenticeship sponsors.

Hands-On Learning

Participants receive meaningful occupational exposure connected to education and training.

Supportive Services

Programs can provide transportation, tools, childcare, tutoring, case management, and referrals.

Credential

Participants can earn safety, academic, industry, or entry credentials before apprenticeship.

Facilitated Entry

A written partnership can provide interviews, advanced standing, direct entry, or other documented transition into Registered Apprenticeship.

No Guarantee

Completing pre-apprenticeship does not automatically guarantee employment or registration unless the sponsor’s approved arrangement provides it.

WIOA Status

Pre-apprenticeship programs do not receive the same automatic Eligible Training Provider status that registered sponsors receive.

Worker Protections

Minimum Wage and Overtime

The Fair Labor Standards Act and state wage laws apply according to employment duties, age, hours, and exemptions. Registration does not authorize payment below applicable law.

Safety and Health

Occupational Safety and Health Act standards, state plans, workers’ compensation, youth safety rules, and industry regulations continue to apply.

Prevailing Wage

Davis-Bacon and related laws govern wages and ratios for apprentices on covered construction.

Collective Bargaining

Union apprentices can receive rights through collective-bargaining agreements, trust documents, referral rules, grievance procedures, and labor law.

No Worker Displacement

Public workforce funds and government contracts can include protections against replacing existing workers or undermining labor standards.

Complaint and Due Process

Programs must identify procedures for controversies. After probation, sponsor cancellation generally requires good cause and corrective opportunity.

Licensing

State occupational licensing, contractor, health-profession, teacher, transportation, and safety requirements remain independent of registration.

Immigration and Work Authorization

Apprenticeship registration does not create work authorization. Employers must comply with federal employment-verification law.

Misclassification

Calling a worker an apprentice does not lawfully convert an employee into an unpaid trainee or independent contractor.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Historical Exclusion

Black workers were excluded from many craft unions, employer networks, segregated training systems, and skilled occupations through law, custom, referral practices, tests, and discrimination.

Construction Access

Federal-contracting policy, civil-rights litigation, affirmative action, community pressure, and apprenticeship outreach became central to opening construction careers during the 1960s and 1970s.

Occupational Segregation

African American apprentices can remain concentrated in lower-paid occupations or experience restricted access to high-wage trades, advanced manufacturing, technology, and supervisory pathways.

Recruitment Networks

Programs relying primarily on family, employee, union, or employer referrals can reproduce exclusion unless recruitment reaches schools, HBCUs, workforce centers, community organizations, and neighborhoods.

Selection and Testing

Minimum qualifications and tests should be demonstrably job related and monitored for discriminatory impact.

Retention

Equity requires attention to job assignments, mentoring, discipline, harassment, wage progression, layoffs, cancellation, related instruction, and completion—not only entry.

Community Institutions

HBCUs, community colleges, unions, Black contractors, churches, workforce boards, youth programs, and neighborhood organizations can sponsor, teach, recruit, mentor, or support apprentices.

Business Ownership

Completion can support journeyworker advancement, contractor licensing, supervision, instruction, and entrepreneurship when programs connect apprentices to business and capital knowledge.

Current Enforcement Concern

The federal interim non-enforcement of part 30 reduces a specialized apprenticeship enforcement mechanism, making other civil-rights agencies, state law, unions, sponsors, and community monitoring especially important.

Equity Measures

Meaningful analysis examines applications, selection, direct entry, occupation, wages, assignments, cancellations, completions, injuries, promotions, and post-completion earnings by race and sex.

Women and Nontraditional Occupations

Nontraditional Occupations

Women remain underrepresented in many construction, manufacturing, transportation, technology, and utility apprenticeships.

WANTO

The Women in Apprenticeship and Nontraditional Occupations grant program funds technical assistance, pre-apprenticeship, workplace culture, support networks, and retention strategies.

Recruitment

Outreach through schools, workforce agencies, women’s organizations, unions, community groups, and public campaigns can broaden applicant pools.

Retention

Childcare, scheduling, properly fitting equipment, sanitation, pregnancy accommodation, harassment prevention, mentorship, and equitable job assignments affect completion.

Wage Opportunity

Access to high-wage apprenticeship can reduce occupational and gender wage gaps.

Current Policy

The Department announced approximately $10 million for the 2026 WANTO competition, which closed July 6, 2026.

Intersectionality

Women of color can face combined racial, gender, caregiving, and occupational barriers that aggregate statistics conceal.

Disability Access and Inclusion

Nondiscrimination

The Americans with Disabilities Act, Section 504 when applicable, state law, and the codified part 30 framework protect qualified applicants and apprentices with disabilities.

Accommodation

Reasonable accommodation can involve testing, communication, equipment, scheduling, instruction, physical access, or task methods unless it creates undue hardship or conflicts with essential safety requirements.

Seven-Percent Goal

Part 30 contains a seven-percent utilization goal for qualified individuals with disabilities, although the Department currently maintains an interim non-enforcement policy for part 30.

Vocational Rehabilitation

State Vocational Rehabilitation agencies can assist eligible apprentices with counseling, training, assistive technology, transportation, tools, and job retention.

Disclosure

Applicants and apprentices should receive accurate information about voluntary disclosure, confidentiality, medical inquiries, and accommodation procedures.

Universal Design

Accessible online applications, classrooms, worksites, safety materials, tools, and mentoring improve opportunity and program quality.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Earn While Learning

Apprentices receive wages while developing occupational skills, reducing reliance on unpaid education.

Progressive Earnings

The approved wage schedule rewards increasing skill and productivity.

Employer-Relevant Training

Employers shape work processes and instruction around real occupational requirements.

National Credential

Completers receive a portable Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship.

Mentorship

Experienced workers transmit practical knowledge, safety habits, workplace culture, and professional judgment.

Worker Retention

Employers can create internal career pathways and reduce recruitment and turnover costs.

Education Integration

Programs can connect employment with college credit, industry certifications, licenses, and degrees.

Public Accountability

Registration establishes written standards, records, oversight, and completion certification.

Career Access

Apprenticeship can create entry into skilled occupations for workers who cannot pause employment for full-time unpaid study.

Regional Development

Programs can build local talent pipelines for infrastructure, manufacturing, health care, technology, education, and public service.

Limitations and Risks

Limited Openings

Apprenticeships are jobs tied to employer demand. Qualified applicants can face waiting lists or no local opportunity.

Geographic Concentration

Many occupations and sponsors are concentrated in particular states, metropolitan areas, construction markets, military installations, or large employers.

Variable Quality

Registration establishes minimum standards but does not make every mentor, worksite, curriculum, employer, or job assignment equally strong.

Cancellation

Apprentices can leave or be cancelled because of layoffs, performance, attendance, instruction, workplace conflict, relocation, discrimination, or personal barriers.

Supportive-Service Needs

Transportation, childcare, tools, clothing, housing, technology, and reduced entry wages can prevent participation.

Instruction Costs

Sponsors, apprentices, unions, colleges, workforce programs, or public grants can bear related-instruction costs depending on the model.

Employer Administrative Burden

Small employers can struggle with standards, records, mentoring, instruction, registration, and grant compliance without an intermediary.

Credential Portability Limits

The federal certificate is nationally recognized, but employers, unions, licensing boards, colleges, and states can value or interpret it differently.

Data Limitations

National data historically vary in completeness across state agencies, military systems, demographics, occupations, and reporting dates.

Enforcement Gaps

Registration oversight can be constrained by staffing, state variation, infrequent reviews, and the current federal non-enforcement of specialized part 30 requirements.

Major Policy Debates

Scale and Quality

Rapid expansion can open opportunity but strain mentors, instructors, registration staff, data systems, and quality assurance.

Federal Versus State Administration

State agencies can tailor programs to local law and industry, while national sponsors seek consistency and faster multistate registration.

Registration Speed

Employers seek rapid approval; worker advocates emphasize thorough review of wages, ratios, safety, instruction, and selection.

Employer Incentives

Incentive payments can recruit employers but risk subsidizing hiring that would occur without public funds unless tied to measurable additional outcomes.

Completion Rates

Low completion can indicate weak quality, but rates also reflect layoffs, economic cycles, military movement, personal circumstances, and data definitions.

Equal Opportunity

Debate concerns whether universal nondiscrimination law is sufficient or specialized apprenticeship affirmative-action and enforcement mechanisms remain necessary.

Union and Nonunion Models

Joint labor-management programs can provide strong training and benefits, while employer-sponsored nonunion models can expand access and flexibility. Quality depends on standards rather than label alone.

Youth Tracking

Youth apprenticeship can create early opportunity but can also channel disadvantaged students away from academic options without informed choice.

College Integration

Degree-connected models can broaden mobility, while excessive academic requirements can raise cost and exclude capable workers.

Emerging Industries

Technology, teaching, health care, and professional occupations raise questions about occupational standards, licensing, competency assessment, and whether apprenticeship supplements or replaces existing education.

Current Status in 2026

Registered Apprenticeship remains active under the National Apprenticeship Act and 29 C.F.R. part 29.

The Office of Apprenticeship and recognized State Apprenticeship Agencies continue registering programs and apprentices, issuing completion credentials, and providing technical assistance.

The national expansion goal is to reach and surpass one million active apprentices.

The Office of Apprenticeship’s thirty-day registration commitment applies after receipt of complete signed standards in the covered federal processes and is an administrative service commitment rather than a statutory entitlement to approval.

The Department’s July 2026 pay-for-performance awards and 2026 state formula funding support expansion in manufacturing, shipbuilding, defense, technology, telecommunications, transportation, artificial intelligence, semiconductors, nuclear-energy infrastructure, and other targeted sectors.

The Office of Apprenticeship’s Circulars 2026-01 through 2026-03 provide current guidance on program design, state-agency roles, completion rates, and registration transparency.

29 C.F.R. part 30 remains codified, but Circular 2025-03 states that the Department will not enforce its requirements until further notice. Sponsors remain subject to applicable federal and state discrimination law.

National apprenticeship data continue to be updated through RAPIDS, state reporting, and USMAP. Figures should be read with their fiscal year, update date, and population definition.

No enacted law has replaced the National Apprenticeship Act or eliminated the Registered Apprenticeship system as of August 3, 2026.

Key Distinctions

Registered and Unregistered Apprenticeship

Registered programs meet standards accepted by the Office of Apprenticeship or a State Apprenticeship Agency. Unregistered programs do not carry that federal-state validation.

Registered Apprenticeship and Pre-Apprenticeship

Registered Apprenticeship is paid employment under an apprentice agreement. Pre-apprenticeship prepares participants for entry.

Apprenticeship and Internship

An internship is usually shorter and may emphasize exposure. Registered Apprenticeship develops full occupational proficiency through employment, structured learning, instruction, wages, and a completion credential.

Apprenticeship and WIOA On-the-Job Training

WIOA OJT is a funding and training service for an eligible worker. It can support a registered apprentice but does not itself create a Registered Apprenticeship Program.

Apprentice and Student

An apprentice is an employee learning an occupation. The apprentice can also be a college or secondary-school student.

Completion Certificate and Occupational License

The apprenticeship certificate verifies completion of registered standards. A license is government permission to practice a regulated occupation.

Completion Certificate and College Degree

A degree is awarded by an educational institution. A registered apprenticeship certificate is an occupational credential.

Sponsor and Employer

A sponsor administers the registered standards. A participating employer employs and trains apprentices; one entity can perform both roles.

Journeyworker and Mentor

A journeyworker is fully skilled in the occupation. A mentor is the person assigned to guide an apprentice and is often, but not always, a journeyworker.

Federal Registration and State Registration

Both are part of the national Registered Apprenticeship System when the state agency is federally recognized.

Geographic Scope

Registered Apprenticeship operates in every state and across the District of Columbia, territories, Tribal jurisdictions, military installations, federal agencies, and multistate employer systems.

The Office of Apprenticeship directly registers programs in jurisdictions without a recognized State Apprenticeship Agency. State Apprenticeship Agencies register programs in their jurisdictions under federal recognition.

Program availability is highly uneven. Construction and industrial apprenticeships often cluster around metropolitan construction markets, union jurisdictions, military shipyards, manufacturing corridors, utilities, mines, and major infrastructure projects.

Rural communities can face few participating employers, long travel to instruction, limited broadband, and small apprentice cohorts. Intermediaries, community colleges, virtual instruction, and regional consortia can reduce these barriers.

National Program Standards and National Guideline Standards can support employers and affiliates operating across several states, but sponsors must still address local employers, wages, licensing, ratios, and Registration Agency procedures.

USMAP serves active-duty military personnel around the world while operating within the United States Registered Apprenticeship System.

State tax credits, tuition systems, prevailing-wage rules, registration procedures, licensing, and workforce funding differ, making location important even within a nationally recognized system.

Legal Context

National Apprenticeship Act of 1937

The Fitzgerald Act authorizes the Secretary of Labor to formulate and promote apprenticeship labor standards, encourage apprenticeship agreements, bring together employers and labor, and cooperate with state agencies.

29 U.S.C. §§ 50–50c

These provisions codify the National Apprenticeship Act, publication and advisory authority, staffing, and coordination with the Department of Veterans Affairs.

29 C.F.R. Part 29

Part 29 governs apprenticeable occupations, standards, registration, agreements, program review, deregistration, and recognition of State Apprenticeship Agencies.

29 C.F.R. Part 30

Part 30 contains equal-employment-opportunity and affirmative-action requirements. It remains codified but is subject to the Department’s June 2025 interim non-enforcement policy.

Fair Labor Standards Act

Federal minimum-wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and child-labor provisions apply according to their terms.

Occupational Safety and Health Act

Employers must provide workplaces and training consistent with applicable safety standards.

National Labor Relations Act

The Act governs collective bargaining, union representation, protected activity, and labor-management relations in covered private employment.

Davis-Bacon and Related Acts

Prevailing-wage rules govern apprentice wage and ratio treatment on covered federal and federally assisted construction.

Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act

WIOA integrates apprenticeship into career pathways, training services, American Job Centers, youth programs, and Eligible Training Provider Lists.

Higher Education Act

Federal student aid can support eligible related instruction delivered through participating institutions and programs under separate requirements.

Veterans’ Education Laws

Title 38 authorizes GI Bill and related benefits for approved apprenticeship and on-the-job training.

Inflation Reduction Act

Certain energy tax incentives include prevailing-wage and apprenticeship requirements, enforced through tax law and related guidance.

State Law

State apprenticeship, wage, licensing, education, child-labor, civil-rights, and tax laws can add requirements or benefits.

Summary

Registered Apprenticeship is the national regulated employment-and-training system established under the National Apprenticeship Act of 1937.

Programs are registered and overseen by the Department of Labor’s Office of Apprenticeship or a federally recognized State Apprenticeship Agency.

Every Registered Apprenticeship combines paid employment, structured supervised workplace learning, related instruction, progressive wages, mentoring, and a completion credential.

Employers, unions, joint labor-management committees, associations, intermediaries, colleges, community organizations, and public agencies can sponsor programs.

An apprenticeable occupation must be recognized within an industry, learned substantially through supervised work, involve progressive technical skill, require related instruction, and require at least 2,000 hours of learning if taught through a time-based approach.

Programs can be time based, competency based, or hybrid. The Department’s March 2026 guidance clarified that competency-based programs do not have a federal twelve-month minimum, although they must include substantive on-the-job learning.

Federal regulations recommend at least 144 hours of related instruction per apprenticeship year. Instruction can be delivered through colleges, unions, employers, schools, industry providers, or online systems.

The standards include work processes, instruction, wages, ratios, minimum qualifications, probation, safety, evaluation, recordkeeping, selection, and completion procedures.

The apprentice signs a written agreement, earns progressive wages, and receives a Certificate of Completion of Apprenticeship after successful completion.

Registration is not a personal federal benefit and does not guarantee a job. Applicants apply directly to sponsors and employers, and positions depend on employer demand and lawful selection.

Registered Apprenticeship differs from internships, ordinary employer training, WIOA on-the-job training, unregistered apprenticeship, and pre-apprenticeship.

WIOA can support eligible apprentices through related instruction, employer reimbursement, career services, and supportive services. Registered sponsors are automatically eligible for inclusion on state Eligible Training Provider Lists while their programs remain registered and they participate in the state process.

Approved programs can also connect with GI Bill benefits, college credit, degrees, state tax incentives, prevailing-wage rules, and federal or state apprenticeship grants.

The Office of Apprenticeship’s fiscal year 2025 publication reported more than 702,000 active apprentices and more than 26,000 active programs.

Current federal policy seeks to reach and surpass one million active apprentices. The Office of Apprenticeship has announced a thirty-day registration-determination commitment for covered complete submissions.

In 2026, the Department announced approximately $85 million for state expansion and awarded nearly $162 million through five pay-for-performance cooperative agreements in strategic industries.

29 C.F.R. part 30 remains codified, but the Department adopted an interim policy in June 2025 of not enforcing those specialized equal-opportunity requirements until further notice. Other federal and state antidiscrimination laws remain applicable.

Registered Apprenticeship can provide high-quality, paid pathways into skilled work, but equitable results depend on open recruitment, lawful selection, strong mentors, safe worksites, sufficient instruction, progressive wages, supportive services, completion, and access to advancement.

For African American workers and communities, the system’s significance is inseparable from the history of exclusion from unions and skilled trades. Registration creates standards and records, but civil-rights enforcement and community participation remain necessary to prevent old barriers from being reproduced.
