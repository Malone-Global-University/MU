Federal Work-Study Program

The federal campus-based student-aid program subsidizing part-time employment for undergraduate, graduate, and professional students who need earnings to help meet postsecondary education costs.

Overview

The Federal Work-Study Program is a need-based federal student-aid and employment program. Commonly abbreviated FWS, it provides federal funds to participating colleges and career schools that use the money—together with required institutional or employer contributions—to pay eligible students for work actually performed.

Congress created the original College Work-Study Program through the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 as part of the War on Poverty. The program was incorporated into Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965 and renamed the Federal Work-Study Program through the Higher Education Amendments of 1992.

The program’s principal purpose is to provide part-time employment to students who need earnings to help meet the costs of postsecondary education. Federal law also encourages schools to connect work with a student’s educational program or career goals and to employ students in community-service activities.

Federal Work-Study differs from Pell Grants and federal student loans. The federal government does not send a lump-sum FWS grant or loan to the student. A student must obtain an eligible job, perform the work, submit accurate time records, and receive wages through payroll. Unearned portions of an offered work-study amount are not paid to the student.

FWS is a campus-based program. Congress appropriates a national amount, and the Department of Education allocates limited federal funds to participating institutions. Each institution then establishes written student-selection procedures, determines financial need, offers work-study within its allocation, develops or approves jobs, and administers payroll and matching funds.

Because funds and positions are limited, filing a FAFSA, demonstrating need, or receiving FWS in a financial-aid offer does not guarantee a job, a particular position, or the full offered earnings. Students commonly must search, apply, interview, complete employment documents, and remain eligible while working.

Undergraduate, graduate, and professional students can participate. Students generally must complete the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, satisfy federal student-aid eligibility requirements, enroll or be accepted for enrollment at a participating institution, demonstrate financial need, and maintain satisfactory academic progress.

Unlike most federal student loans, FWS does not generally require at least half-time enrollment. A less-than-half-time student may participate when otherwise eligible. Special half-time requirements apply to certain students enrolled solely in teacher-certification coursework.

Financial need limits the amount a student may earn through FWS. A school calculates need using cost of attendance, the Student Aid Index, and other financial assistance. The work-study award, when combined with other need-based aid and resources, cannot exceed the student’s remaining need.

There is no nationally fixed minimum or maximum individual FWS award. The school considers the student’s need, wage rate, expected hours, academic workload, employment period, and other aid. The offered amount is an earnings ceiling for subsidized employment, not a guaranteed payment.

Students may work for the institution, a federal, state, or local public agency, a private nonprofit organization, or a private for-profit organization under federal conditions. Public and nonprofit off-campus employment must be in the public interest. For-profit employment must be academically relevant to the maximum extent practicable.

In most positions, federal funds may pay up to 75 percent of wages and a nonfederal source supplies at least 25 percent. The federal share is limited to 50 percent for private for-profit employment. Certain public-interest positions can qualify for a 90-percent or 100-percent federal share, and eligible Title III institutions can receive a waiver allowing a 100-percent federal share for most positions.

Schools must generally use at least 7 percent of their federal allocation to pay the federal share of wages for community-service jobs. They must also employ at least one FWS student as a reading tutor for children or in family-literacy activities unless the Department grants a waiver.

Community service includes health care, childcare, education, literacy, social services, transportation, housing and neighborhood improvement, public safety, emergency preparedness, recreation, rural development, disability support, mentoring, and other activities improving community life—particularly for low-income residents.

FWS students must receive at least the applicable federal, state, or local minimum wage. As of August 2026, the federal minimum wage for covered nonexempt employment is $7.25 per hour; higher state or local minimums control where applicable. Wage rates must also reflect the work, skills, local labor market, and treatment of comparable non-FWS employees.

Undergraduate students must be paid hourly. Graduate and professional students may be paid hourly or by salary. Students must be paid at least monthly, and the institution remains responsible for ensuring payment even when an off-campus organization handles payroll.

FWS earnings are taxable wages under ordinary income-tax rules. Qualifying students employed by their college or university can be exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes under the student FICA exception when education, rather than employment, is the predominant relationship. The exception does not automatically cover every student or off-campus job.

FWS earnings are excluded from income in the federal student-aid need calculation for a later year. This protects the student from losing future federal aid merely because the student earned federally subsidized work-study wages.

For the 2026–2027 award year, Federal Student Aid authorized approximately $1.215 billion in federal FWS allocations to 2,865 institutions. The ordinary matching requirement increases total potential student wages beyond the federal allocation.

The Department’s latest detailed distributional data show that 481,552 students received approximately $1.100 billion in FWS aid during 2023–2024, with average earnings of approximately $2,284. Private institutions received 44.7 percent of available aid, public four-year institutions 40.8 percent, public two-year institutions 11.9 percent, and proprietary institutions 2.5 percent.

Federal Work-Study can reduce borrowing, provide flexible income, build workplace skills, connect students with faculty and employers, strengthen community organizations, and support persistence. Its effectiveness depends heavily on job quality, wage level, hours, supervision, career relevance, institutional funding, transportation, and whether students can locate and retain positions.

Definitions

Federal Work-Study
The federal need-based program subsidizing part-time employment for eligible undergraduate, graduate, and professional students.
College Work-Study
The original name of the program from 1964 until the Higher Education Amendments of 1992 renamed it Federal Work-Study.
Campus-Based Programs
Federal student-aid programs for which funds are allocated to participating institutions rather than paid automatically for every nationally eligible student. FWS and FSEOG are the principal active Campus-Based programs.
Federal allocation
The amount of federal FWS authority awarded to an institution for a particular award year.
Initial allocation
The institution’s original annual FWS authorization before supplemental funds, releases, transfers, carryover, or reallocation.
Supplemental allocation
Additional FWS funds distributed from amounts released by other institutions or otherwise available for reallocation.
Nonfederal share
The institutional or employer contribution toward FWS wages or authorized program costs.
Federal share
The portion of eligible wages or costs paid from the institution’s federal FWS authorization.
Financial need
For Campus-Based aid, the difference among cost of attendance, Student Aid Index, and other financial assistance under federal need-analysis rules.
Federal Work-Study award
The maximum amount of subsidized FWS compensation a student is authorized to earn during a stated period. It is not a guarantee of wages.
Gross earnings
Total wages earned before taxes, deductions, and job-related expenses.
Net FWS earnings
Gross expected wages minus nonrefundable taxes and reasonable job-related costs for aid-packaging purposes.
Job-related costs
Expenses incurred because of the employment, such as uniforms, meals required at work, or transportation to the job.
On-campus employment
Work performed for the institution or, under qualifying circumstances, for an organization physically located on campus that provides services to students.
Off-campus employment
FWS work performed for a public agency, nonprofit organization, or eligible private for-profit employer under a written institutional agreement.
Public-interest work
Off-campus public or nonprofit employment performed for the welfare of the nation or community rather than the benefit of a restricted private interest or political activity.
Community service
Work identified through consultation with community organizations as improving quality of life—particularly for low-income residents—or addressing community needs.
Reading tutor
An FWS student employed to help preschool or elementary-school children develop reading skills under an institutionally approved project.
Family literacy
Integrated activities intended to improve literacy among children and adult family members and strengthen adults’ ability to support children’s learning.
Civic education and participation
Eligible nonpolitical community-service activity teaching government functions, civic knowledge, emergency preparedness, or lawful participation without supporting candidates, parties, ballot measures, or voting operations.
Job Location and Development Program
The FWS component allowing institutions to use limited federal funds to locate and develop off-campus employment for enrolled students regardless of financial need. The abbreviation JLD is commonly used.
Work-Colleges Program
A separate FWS-related program supporting institutions with comprehensive work-learning-service requirements for resident students.
Community-service waiver
Annual Department approval excusing an institution from the 7-percent expenditure requirement, tutoring and literacy requirement, or both because compliance would cause student hardship.
Fiscal Operations Report and Application to Participate
The annual institutional report and application used to report Campus-Based expenditures and request future funding. The abbreviation FISAP is commonly used.
Administrative Cost Allowance
A limited amount an institution can claim from Campus-Based funds to help cover program-administration costs. The abbreviation ACA is commonly used.
Reallocation
The process through which unused Campus-Based funds released by institutions are redistributed as supplemental awards.
Carry forward
Authority permitting an institution to use a limited share of an allocation in the following award year.
Carry back
Authority permitting limited use of a current allocation for qualifying prior-year summer or other authorized expenditures.
Program Participation Agreement
The contract through which an institution agrees to administer federal student-aid programs according to the Higher Education Act and Department regulations. The abbreviation PPA is commonly used.
Overaward
A condition in which need-based aid and counted resources exceed the student’s financial need.
Satisfactory academic progress
The institution’s federally compliant qualitative, pace, and maximum-time-frame requirements for continued student-aid eligibility.
Student FICA exception
The federal tax exception that can exclude qualifying school employment from Social Security and Medicare taxes when the employee’s relationship with the institution is predominantly educational.

Historical Development

Student Employment Before 1964

Colleges long hired students in dining halls, libraries, laboratories, offices, farms, maintenance departments, and residence halls. These institutional jobs reduced educational cost and supplied labor, but availability depended on local resources.

National Youth Administration

During the Great Depression, the National Youth Administration provided part-time work to students who might otherwise leave school. The experience demonstrated that subsidized employment could combine financial support with education and public service.

War on Poverty

The Economic Opportunity Act created College Work-Study to open postsecondary education to students from low-income families. The program combined self-help, employment, education, and federal investment rather than relying exclusively on grants or loans.

Higher Education Act

Moving the program into the Higher Education Act placed it alongside Educational Opportunity Grants, student loans, and other permanent federal college-access policies.

Community Service

Later amendments required institutions to use a portion of FWS for community needs and to provide tutoring or literacy activity. The program thereby became both a financial-aid mechanism and a federal community-service policy.

Career Relevance

Federal law requires schools, to the maximum extent practicable, to provide employment complementing the student’s educational program or career goals. Modern reform debates seek to shift more positions from routine campus work toward internships, research, apprenticeships, and paid career preparation.

Program Structure and Administration

Federal Student Aid

Federal Student Aid:

  • Calculates institutional Campus-Based allocations
  • Publishes the Federal Student Aid Handbook and operational guidance
  • Administers the Common Origination and Disbursement system
  • Receives FISAP reports and future funding applications
  • Conducts program reviews and resolves audit findings
  • Processes reallocations, waivers, and Title III matching waivers
  • Monitors community-service and fiscal requirements
  • Collects annual student earnings data

Participating Institutions

A participating institution must:

  • Maintain an eligible PPA
  • Establish written and uniformly applied student-selection procedures
  • Determine financial need and prevent overawards
  • Develop, approve, and supervise eligible jobs
  • Set lawful wage rates
  • Provide or secure the nonfederal share
  • Maintain job descriptions and employment agreements
  • Verify time worked and process payroll
  • Pay students at least monthly
  • Reconcile payroll and federal cash monthly
  • Meet community-service obligations
  • Report expenditures, recipients, employers, and earnings

Financial-Aid Office

The financial-aid office commonly determines eligibility and award limits, while a student-employment, human-resources, payroll, career-services, or community-engagement office manages hiring and supervision.

Employer

The employing department or off-campus organization supplies work, supervision, training, safe conditions, accurate time certification, and the required employer share when applicable.

Student

The student obtains the position, completes employment forms, performs assigned duties, records time accurately, monitors the earnings ceiling, communicates schedule changes, and maintains eligibility.

Institutional Responsibility

The school remains accountable for federal funds even when an off-campus organization recruits, supervises, or pays the student.

Campus-Based Allocation System

National Appropriation

Congress appropriates a fixed national amount. This makes FWS availability different from Pell Grants, which are awarded to all eligible applicants at participating schools without an institutional quota.

Institutional Formula

The Department distributes FWS funds under a statutory formula that considers historical institutional funding and measures of student need. The formula is often described as containing a base guarantee and a fair-share component.

Base Guarantee

Longstanding institutional participation and prior allocations protect part of a school’s historical funding. This provides stability but can preserve distributions based on enrollment and institutional patterns from earlier decades.

Fair Share

The fair-share calculation estimates institutional need using eligible student financial need and available appropriations. When funding is insufficient to satisfy every fair-share amount, awards are reduced according to statutory rules.

Application Through FISAP

Institutions report prior operations and apply for future Campus-Based funds through FISAP. A school that misses deadlines or fails to report accurately can lose funding.

Reallocation

Institutions release amounts they cannot spend. The Department can redistribute the money to schools requesting supplemental FWS funds.

Supplemental Community-Service Condition

A school receiving supplemental FWS funds generally must spend the entire supplemental amount on community-service positions unless the Department grants a waiver.

Limited Availability

Students with similar financial need can receive different FWS opportunities depending on the institution’s allocation, spending capacity, matching resources, award policies, and job inventory.

Student Eligibility

FAFSA

A student generally completes the FAFSA for the award year. The institution receives federal eligibility data and calculates financial need.

Financial Need

The student must have remaining need after the Student Aid Index and other financial assistance are considered.

Eligible Student Status

The student must be enrolled or accepted for enrollment as an undergraduate, graduate, or professional student at the institution.

General Title IV Requirements

Applicable requirements include citizenship or eligible noncitizen status, valid identity, qualifying educational credentials or authorized alternatives, satisfactory academic progress, and resolution of federal aid defaults or overpayments when required.

Less-Than-Half-Time Students

A student can receive FWS while enrolled less than half time. If the institution’s federal allocation reflects need among part-time or independent students, it must make a reasonable portion available to those students.

Graduate and Professional Students

Graduate and professional students are eligible for FWS even though they cannot receive Pell Grants under ordinary rules.

Second Undergraduate Degree

A student who already holds a bachelor’s degree can receive FWS while pursuing another eligible undergraduate program if the student demonstrates financial need.

Students with Intellectual Disabilities

An otherwise eligible student enrolled in an approved comprehensive transition and postsecondary program can receive FWS under special Title IV rules even when some ordinary requirements do not apply.

Teacher Certification

A student enrolled solely in required teacher-certification coursework can receive FWS under authorized conditions and generally must be enrolled at least half time.

No Exceptional-Need Priority

Unlike FSEOG, federal law does not require schools to give first priority to students with the lowest Student Aid Index or Pell recipients. Schools establish their own written selection procedures within federal requirements.

Financial Need and Award Packaging

Basic Need Calculation

The general relationship is:

Cost of Attendance − Student Aid Index − Other Financial Assistance = Financial Need

FWS Cannot Exceed Need

The net earnings expected from the FWS award, when added to counted aid and resources, may not exceed remaining need.

Net Rather Than Gross Earnings

For packaging, the institution can subtract nonrefundable taxes and reasonable job-related costs from expected gross wages. This estimates how much employment income is actually available for education expenses.

Award Calculation

The gross work-study authorization is commonly calculated from:

Anticipated hourly wage × planned hours = gross FWS award

No Fixed Federal Individual Limit

Federal law does not prescribe one national award amount. The student’s need and the institution’s resources determine the ceiling.

Overaward Adjustment

If new grants, scholarships, benefits, or other resources create an overaward, the school can reduce unearned FWS hours or adjust other aid according to federal packaging rules. Wages already earned for work performed must be paid.

Estimated Versus Earned Aid

A financial-aid offer may list $3,000 in FWS, but a student who obtains a job late, works fewer hours, leaves the position, or becomes ineligible may earn less than $3,000.

Future FAFSA Treatment

FWS compensation is reported as taxable earnings where required, but the federal need-analysis system excludes qualifying FWS income when determining future federal student aid.

From Financial-Aid Offer to Employment

Step One: Complete FAFSA

The student submits the FAFSA early because Campus-Based funds are limited and institutional deadlines can precede the federal deadline.

Step Two: Receive an Offer

The school determines eligibility and may include an FWS amount in the student’s aid offer.

Step Three: Accept the Award

The institution can require formal acceptance. Acceptance preserves consideration but does not itself create an employment relationship.

Step Four: Locate a Job

The student searches an institutional job system, attends a job fair, receives a placement, contacts a community partner, or applies through a career-services office.

Step Five: Apply and Interview

Employers can use qualifications, interviews, work schedules, and lawful selection criteria. FWS eligibility does not require an employer to hire a particular student.

Step Six: Complete Employment Documents

The student completes Form I-9, tax withholding, payroll, direct-deposit, institutional policy, confidentiality, safety, and other employment requirements.

Step Seven: Begin Work

The student may be paid only for work actually performed, except when a specific disaster-related statutory authority permits otherwise.

Step Eight: Monitor Earnings

The supervisor, payroll office, financial-aid office, and student track hours so subsidized earnings do not exceed the award.

Step Nine: Continue or Convert Employment

When FWS eligibility ends, the employer can continue the student as a regular employee using non-FWS funds, but federal work-study funds cannot subsidize excess earnings.

Jobs and Employers

Eligible Employers

  • The student’s institution
  • Federal public agencies other than the Department of Education
  • State and local public agencies
  • Private nonprofit organizations
  • Private for-profit organizations under stricter rules

Educational and Career Connection

To the maximum extent practicable, employment should complement and reinforce the student’s academic program or career goals.

Suitable Schedule

Work must be compatible with classes, study, health, and academic progress. The job can occur during evenings, weekends, breaks, summer, or nonattendance periods when federal conditions are met.

Written Job Description

The Department strongly recommends a written description identifying the employer, department, location, supervisor, duties, qualifications, wage rate, employment dates, and evaluation procedures.

No Employee Displacement

FWS students cannot replace regular employees, replace workers participating in a strike, fill eliminated full-time positions, or impair existing service contracts.

No Commissions

FWS students cannot be paid by commission or fee in place of lawful hourly wages or salary.

Religious Facilities

FWS cannot fund construction, operation, or maintenance of a portion of a building used for religious worship or sectarian instruction. Eligible secular work at a religiously affiliated institution or organization depends on the duties and location.

Political Activity

FWS cannot support partisan or nonpartisan political activity, campaigns, ballot initiatives, lobbying, voter-registration activity, polling-place assistance, voter hotlines, or service as a poll worker.

On-Campus Employment

Institution as Employer

Common on-campus employers include academic departments, laboratories, libraries, museums, information technology, student services, administrative offices, childcare centers, dining operations, residence life, athletics, facilities, and campus safety.

Academic Employment

Research assistants, laboratory technicians, peer tutors, instructional assistants, archival workers, and faculty project assistants can gain experience directly connected to study.

Student Services

Eligible positions can support financial aid, libraries, peer counselling, job placement, disability services, security, social and health services, and tutoring.

Commercial Organizations on Campus

Employment with an independent commercial firm physically located on campus is eligible only when the work provides services to students and satisfies federal on-campus employment rules.

Institutional Operations

Routine administrative, clerical, maintenance, and service jobs can qualify but may offer less career development unless the institution adds training, progression, and transferable skills.

Institutional Benefit

Federal wage subsidies reduce the cost of student labor to campus departments. This creates a duty to ensure that jobs remain educationally appropriate and do not displace ordinary workers.

Off-Campus Public and Nonprofit Employment

Public Agencies

Students can work for federal, state, county, municipal, Tribal, school, library, park, public-health, transit, housing, court, and other public agencies.

Nonprofit Organizations

Eligible employers can include hospitals, childcare centers, museums, legal-aid organizations, food banks, shelters, conservation organizations, youth programs, cultural organizations, and community-development groups.

Public Interest

The work must benefit the nation or community rather than a restricted membership group, political interest, elected official’s campaign operation, or lobbying effort.

Written Agreement

The institution and employer execute an agreement describing duties, supervision, payroll, federal and employer shares, records, billing, safety, and responsibility for compliance.

School Liability

The institution must pay the student even when an off-campus employer fails to provide its share and must pursue the unpaid employer contribution separately.

Federal Agencies

A federal agency can provide the nonfederal share because its employer contribution is treated as the matching share rather than as FWS federal funds. FWS employment may not be provided by the Department of Education itself.

Transportation

Off-campus work can be inaccessible to students without cars, transit, or time between classes. Transportation cost may be considered a job-related cost in packaging.

Private For-Profit Employment

Purpose

For-profit placements can provide paid career experience in businesses related to a student’s academic or vocational goals.

Academic Relevance

The institution must ensure that the work is academically relevant to the maximum extent practicable.

Federal Share

Federal funds may pay no more than 50 percent of wages. The business supplies at least 50 percent.

Institutional Allocation Limit

A school may use no more than 25 percent of its current-year initial FWS allocation for wages paid to students employed by private for-profit organizations.

No Community-Service Credit

Private for-profit employment cannot satisfy the FWS community-service expenditure requirement.

No Displacement

The business cannot replace regular employees or avoid ordinary payroll cost by substituting subsidized students for existing workers.

Examples

Potential positions include accounting, banking, information technology, engineering, media, health administration, design, manufacturing, hospitality, and business operations when duties are educationally appropriate.

Policy Concern

Critics question using federal student-aid dollars to subsidize private employers. Supporters argue that stronger employer matching and career relevance can create valuable paid internships inaccessible to low-income students.

Apprenticeships and Career-Connected Work

Eligible Apprenticeship Wages

An institution may use FWS funds to pay training wages for an otherwise eligible student employed as an apprentice.

Academic Program Connection

The apprenticeship need not be formally included in the student’s eligible academic program, although the job should complement educational or vocational goals to the maximum extent practicable.

Registered Apprenticeship

A placement can involve a federally or state-registered apprenticeship when the employer and school satisfy FWS and apprenticeship requirements.

Matching Share

The ordinary public, nonprofit, or for-profit federal-share rules apply according to the employer.

Career Services

Career-connected FWS can include internships, cooperative education, undergraduate research, public-service fellowships, clinical support, and other structured work-based learning.

Quality Elements

Strong positions provide supervision, written learning goals, skill development, feedback, progressive responsibility, professional networks, and a relationship to future employment.

Equity

Paid placements allow students with limited family resources to gain experience that otherwise might be available only through unpaid internships.

Community-Service Requirement

Seven-Percent Expenditure Rule

An institution generally must use at least 7 percent of the sum of its initial and supplemental federal FWS allocations to pay the federal share of wages in community-service employment.

Reading or Family Literacy

At least one FWS student must work as a reading tutor for preschool or elementary-school children or perform family-literacy activities.

Supplemental Funds

A school receiving supplemental FWS money generally must spend 100 percent of the supplemental amount in community-service positions when that amount exceeds the ordinary 7-percent calculation.

Community Need

Institutions consult with nonprofit, governmental, and community-based organizations to identify services improving quality of life—particularly for low-income individuals.

Eligible Fields

  • Health care
  • Childcare
  • Literacy and education
  • Welfare and social services
  • Transportation
  • Housing and neighborhood improvement
  • Public safety and crime prevention
  • Emergency preparedness and response
  • Recreation
  • Rural development
  • Disability support
  • Mentoring and career counselling
  • Environmental and community improvement

Direct and Indirect Service

A student need not work face to face with community residents. Research, volunteer coordination, meal preparation, data management, communications, and logistical support can qualify when they advance eligible community services.

Campus as Community

The campus population alone is generally not considered the community at large. On-campus positions qualify only when the service is open and accessible to the broader community or falls within the disability-support exception.

Waiver

The Department can waive one or both requirements when the school documents that compliance would cause student hardship. Difficulty alone is insufficient; the request must demonstrate conditions such as very small allocation, rural transportation barriers, or an unusually intensive specialized program.

Tutoring, Family Literacy, and Civic Service

Reading Tutors

FWS students can read with children, provide individual or small-group tutoring, support classroom literacy, assist libraries, and participate in school or community reading projects.

Mathematics Tutors

Federal funds may pay 100 percent of eligible wages for FWS students tutoring preschool or elementary-school children in mathematics.

Family Literacy

Students can support integrated projects involving adult literacy, parent education, early-childhood education, and joint literacy activity between adults and children.

Federal Share

Qualifying reading, mathematics, and family-literacy positions can receive a 100-percent federal wage share.

Schools and Libraries

Projects can operate through public schools, Head Start programs, libraries, community centers, afterschool programs, and nonprofit organizations.

Civic Education

Eligible projects can teach government structure, public resources, emergency preparedness, disaster response, and nonpartisan civic knowledge.

Political Boundary

FWS cannot support voter registration, campaigns, ballot measures, polling-place assistance, poll work, partisan advocacy, nonpartisan election advocacy, or lobbying.

Training and Safety

Institutions and employers must train tutors and community workers in instructional practice, confidentiality, child safety, mandated reporting, disability access, and other applicable requirements.

Wages, Hours, and Working Conditions

Minimum Wage

Students must receive at least the federal minimum wage or a higher applicable state or local minimum wage.

Comparable Pay

The institution considers the skill required, local wages for similar work, rates paid to comparable non-FWS employees, experience, and legal requirements.

Undergraduate Pay

Undergraduate students must be paid by the hour.

Graduate and Professional Pay

Graduate and professional students may be paid hourly or by salary.

No Federal Weekly Limit

Federal FWS regulations do not establish one universal maximum number of weekly hours. Institutions set limits based on financial need, award amount, academic workload, health, payroll rules, and applicable labor law.

Part-Time Purpose

The program is intended for part-time employment. Schools commonly limit work during enrollment to preserve academic progress.

Overtime

A student can be paid overtime when required by labor law, and eligible FWS funds can support the wage expense when the student remains within the award and other requirements.

Fringe Benefits

Federal and matching FWS funds cannot pay sick leave, vacation, holiday pay, retirement, insurance, workers’ compensation contributions, employer FICA contributions, or other fringe benefits. An employer can pay such costs from separate non-FWS funds.

Breaks and Labor Law

Federal, state, and local laws governing breaks, overtime, youth employment, workplace safety, discrimination, and wage payment apply according to the employment relationship.

Schedule Around Classes

Supervisors should avoid scheduling work during classes and should adjust employment when academic demands, exams, or course schedules change.

Payroll, Taxes, and Payment

Earned Compensation

FWS wages are earned when the student performs the work. An award notice alone does not create a right to payment.

Payment Frequency

The school must ensure payment at least once per month. Many institutions pay weekly or biweekly.

Payment Methods

Students can be paid by check, another negotiable instrument, electronic transfer to a designated account, or credit to the school account with the student’s separate written authorization.

No Coerced Account Credit

A school cannot require the student to apply wages to tuition, fees, housing, or other institutional charges when the match is paid in cash.

Noncash Match

An institution can provide part of its matching share through documented tuition, fees, food, housing, books, supplies, services, or equipment. The school must explain the arrangement in writing and provide an itemized statement.

Income Tax

FWS compensation is earned income and can be subject to federal, state, and local income-tax withholding. Student status alone does not eliminate ordinary income-tax obligations.

Student FICA Exception

Qualifying employment by the student’s college or university can be exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes when the student is enrolled and education predominates. Off-campus employment generally does not qualify under the school-employment exception.

Payroll Records

Payroll vouchers identify the student, job, pay period, hours, wage rate, gross earnings, deductions, net earnings, overtime, noncash compensation, and payment identifier.

Federal Aid Treatment

FWS earnings are excluded from the income used in the federal need calculation for the following FAFSA award cycle.

Ordinary Match

When federal funds pay $3,000 of ordinary wages at a 75-percent share, the school or employer supplies at least $1,000, creating $4,000 in total compensation.

More Than Minimum Match

An institution or employer can contribute more than the minimum, reducing the federal percentage and stretching the institution’s federal allocation across additional jobs or hours.

90-Percent Share

The enhanced share is available case by case for qualifying public or nonprofit organizations that cannot afford the ordinary match. The school cannot own, operate, or control the organization, and no more than 10 percent of the school’s FWS students may benefit from the provision.

Title III Waiver

Institutions meeting federal eligibility criteria related to low educational and general expenditures and enrollment of needy students can apply annually for a waiver of the institutional matching requirement.

Source of Match

The nonfederal share can come from school funds, an off-campus employer, or another lawful nonfederal source. Federal agency employer funds can serve as the matching share when the agency has authority to pay wages.

Summer and Periods of Nonattendance

Work During Enrollment

A student can work during academic terms, summer enrollment, intersessions, and other periods when enrolled and eligible.

Work Before Future Enrollment

A student can work during a period of nonattendance—including the summer before beginning a program—when planning to enroll in the next period and demonstrating need for that future period.

Use of Earnings

Earnings from nonattendance work must be intended to cover expenses for the next enrollment period and are treated as other financial assistance in that period.

Graduating Students

A student generally cannot earn FWS after completing the program unless the student will enroll in another eligible period or program under applicable rules.

After Last Day of Attendance

A school can issue payroll after the student’s last day of attendance for work performed while the student was eligible and attending.

Breaks

Tax treatment, including the student FICA exception, can change during extended breaks when the student is not enrolled.

Job Location and Development Program

Purpose

JLD helps institutions locate and develop off-campus jobs suitable for students’ schedules and educational or vocational goals.

Student Eligibility

Students placed through JLD must be enrolled at the institution, but they do not have to demonstrate FWS financial need. The program is intended to serve both FWS-eligible and non-FWS students.

Funding Limit

A school can use up to the lesser of 10 percent of its FWS allocation and reallocation or $75,000.

Federal Share

Federal JLD funds can pay up to 80 percent of allowable administrative costs. The institution supplies at least 20 percent in cash or services; this match cannot be waived.

Allowable Costs

  • Staff salaries and qualifying fringe benefits
  • Travel related to employer development
  • Printing and mailing
  • Telephone and communications
  • Supplies, equipment, and furniture
  • Employer advertising
  • Student and employer workshops

Prohibited Uses

JLD funds cannot pay student wages, develop on-campus jobs, place graduates, displace workers, impair service contracts, construct facilities, or pay indirect institutional overhead.

Performance Expectation

The total wages earned through developed jobs should exceed the federal funds spent to operate JLD.

Apprenticeship Development

JLD can identify apprenticeship opportunities and help employers develop positions for enrolled students regardless of federal-aid eligibility.

Reporting

Institutions report JLD expenditures, matching funds, students placed, and total student earnings on FISAP.

Work-Colleges Program

Separate Program Component

The Work-Colleges Program is related to FWS but serves a small group of eligible institutions operating comprehensive work-learning-service programs.

Comprehensive Requirement

At a work college, resident students generally participate in an integrated institutional work program as part of education and community life.

Educational Purpose

The model connects work with learning, service, personal responsibility, career development, institutional operation, and reduced educational cost.

Eligible Institutions

A qualifying work college must satisfy statutory institutional, residential, work-program, and public or nonprofit status requirements.

Student Compensation

Students can receive payments or credits toward educational costs through the comprehensive work-learning program.

Distinction from Ordinary FWS

Ordinary FWS is optional employment awarded to financially needy students at thousands of institutions. A Work-College program is a defining institutional educational structure.

Fiscal Management and Reporting

Federal Cash

The institution draws federal FWS funds in connection with payroll and must avoid excess cash.

Monthly Reconciliation

Schools reconcile institutional payroll, student-level earnings, federal cash, COD records, allocation use, and matching contributions at least monthly.

Timesheets

Time records identify dates and hours worked and require certification by the student and supervisor. Electronic signatures must meet federal standards.

Separation of Duties

Strong controls separate hiring, supervision, time approval, payroll processing, federal drawdown, reconciliation, and adjustment functions.

FISAP

The annual report records federal funds spent, institutional and employer shares, administrative allowances, JLD, community service, tutoring, recipients, and earnings.

Administrative Cost Allowance

An institution can claim a limited allowance based on Campus-Based and Pell activity to support administration, including financial-aid, payroll, compliance, and reporting costs.

Unused Funds

Schools return or release funds they cannot spend. Failure to use an allocation can reduce future funding and make money available for reallocation.

Transfers

Subject to federal limits, an institution can transfer part of its FWS allocation to FSEOG when it participates in and makes awards under both programs.

Record Retention

Institutions retain applications, awards, payroll, time records, job descriptions, agreements, matching documentation, reconciliation, and FISAP support for the required federal period.

Program Integrity

Ghost Employees

Institutions must prevent payments to nonexistent students, students who did not work, or individuals using stolen identities.

Falsified Hours

Supervisors and payroll systems verify that reported hours were actually worked and did not conflict impossibly with classes or other employment.

Excess Earnings

Systems should warn when students approach the FWS award ceiling so federal funds do not create an overaward.

Employer Billing

Schools track and collect off-campus employer shares. The institution remains liable for undocumented or unrecovered federal expenditures.

Conflicts of Interest

Hiring and supervision policies address nepotism, personal benefit, private business use, procurement relationships, and misuse of subsidized labor.

Work Actually Performed

Except under a specific emergency authority, federal funds cannot pay students merely for holding an award or remaining available for work.

Program Reviews and Audits

The Department, auditors, and institutional reviewers examine eligibility, packaging, timesheets, wage rates, matching funds, community service, payroll, cash management, and reporting.

Corrective Action

Violations can require repayment, returned allocation, fines, heightened monitoring, limitation, suspension, termination, or referral for civil or criminal enforcement.

Labor Standards and Civil Rights

Fair Labor Standards Act

Applicable minimum-wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and youth-employment protections govern covered FWS jobs.

Occupational Safety and Health

Employers must provide safe work, training, protective equipment, and injury procedures under applicable federal and state law.

Employment Discrimination

Title VII, Title IX, Section 504, the Americans with Disabilities Act, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, state law, and institutional policy can apply according to the employer and circumstances.

Disability Accommodation

Qualified students with disabilities are entitled to nondiscriminatory consideration and reasonable accommodation. Community service can include support services for students with disabilities.

Sexual Harassment

Institutions and employers must respond to employment and educational harassment under applicable civil-rights and workplace policies.

Workers’ Compensation

Applicable workers’ compensation coverage and employer obligations remain in force, although FWS funds cannot pay the employer contribution as part of subsidized wages.

International Students

FWS ordinarily requires citizenship or eligible noncitizen status under Title IV rules. Students studying under common nonimmigrant visa categories generally do not qualify for federal student aid even when other campus employment is authorized.

Equal Access

Institutional selection procedures must be written and uniformly applied. Schools should not reserve the strongest career positions only for students without financial need.

African American Students and Racial Equity

Economic Need

Racial disparities in household income, employment, wealth, home equity, inheritance, and access to family financial support make paid student employment important to many African American students.

College Access

FWS can help cover transportation, food, books, supplies, telephone service, clothing, emergency expenses, and other costs not fully covered by grants.

Historically Black Colleges and Universities

HBCUs use FWS to employ students in academic departments, libraries, research, administration, community service, museums, archives, tutoring, and campus operations. Matching requirements and limited institutional resources can constrain the number of positions.

Historical Allocation Formula

The formula’s protection of historical allocations can advantage older, wealthier private institutions relative to community colleges and minority-serving institutions enrolling larger shares of low-income students today.

Job Quality

Equity depends not only on whether students receive wages but also on access to research, internships, professional networks, technical skills, and career-connected supervision.

Work Burden

Students from families with limited wealth may need more work hours than FWS permits or funds. They can combine FWS with non-FWS employment, increasing risks to study time, health, and completion.

Community Service

FWS can direct college resources toward schools, libraries, health programs, neighbourhood organizations, and community institutions serving Black communities.

Unpaid Internship Replacement

Career-connected FWS can reduce the exclusion created by unpaid internships, which often require family support and therefore favour students with greater wealth.

Data and Accountability

National public data on job quality, career connection, wage progression, employer type, and outcomes by race remain limited. Stronger reporting could reveal whether FWS narrows or reproduces opportunity gaps.

Benefits and Potential Outcomes

Income Without Debt

Students receive wages rather than loans, reducing the amount they must borrow or obtain from family resources.

Flexible Scheduling

Campus employers commonly schedule work around classes, examinations, and academic calendars.

Career Skills

Students can develop communication, teamwork, reliability, technology, research, customer service, supervision, and occupational skills.

Faculty and Staff Relationships

Academic and administrative positions can connect students with mentors, references, research, internships, and institutional knowledge.

Persistence

Moderate employment integrated with campus life can support belonging and persistence. Excessive work hours or poor-quality employment can produce the opposite effect.

Community Capacity

Nonprofits, schools, public agencies, and community groups obtain subsidized workers while students gain service experience.

Career Access

Paid internships and apprenticeships can make professional experience available to students unable to accept unpaid work.

Institutional Operations

Student workers support libraries, laboratories, offices, tutoring, childcare, technology, museums, research, and other services important to college operation.

Limits of Evidence

Outcomes vary with job type, hours, pay, supervision, student characteristics, and institutional context. Selection into FWS complicates efforts to identify the program’s independent effect.

Limitations and Risks

Not Guaranteed

A student can receive an FWS offer but fail to find a job or earn the full amount.

Limited Institutional Funds

National appropriations and institutional allocations restrict the number of students served.

Low Earnings

Average annual earnings are modest relative to tuition, housing, food, transportation, and childcare.

Low Wage Rates

Some positions pay only the applicable minimum wage, requiring many work hours to earn a relatively small award.

Administrative Barriers

Separate financial-aid, hiring, payroll, background-check, and job-search systems can delay employment.

Job Quality

Routine or poorly supervised jobs can provide income without meaningful skill development.

Formula Inequity

Historical base guarantees can direct a disproportionate share of funds toward institutions with older allocations rather than those serving the greatest current need.

Matching Burden

Institutions with limited unrestricted funds may be unable to supply the nonfederal share, even when student need is high.

Off-Campus Access

Transportation, scheduling, background checks, employer billing, and liability concerns can limit community and career placements.

Academic Risk

Students who work too many hours can experience fatigue, lower grades, delayed completion, or withdrawal.

Major Policy Debates

Formula Reform

Reformers seek to direct more funds toward institutions enrolling high shares of Pell recipients and students with unmet need. Established institutions emphasize stability and reliance on historical allocations.

Career Relevance

One approach would require or reward internships, apprenticeships, research, and career-aligned work. Critics warn that rigid relevance standards could reduce flexible jobs or exclude students still exploring majors.

Federal Matching Share

A larger employer share can stretch federal dollars but may cause colleges and nonprofits with limited resources to eliminate jobs. A larger federal share expands access but increases public cost.

Community Service

Supporters value tutoring, health, literacy, public safety, and neighbourhood service. Others argue that scarce aid should focus on students’ career development or direct financial need.

Minimum Wage

Raising FWS wages can improve student income and reduce required hours but causes each institutional allocation to support fewer total hours unless funding rises.

Work Versus Grant Aid

Work-study promotes earnings, responsibility, and experience. Grant aid preserves study time and provides more immediate financial support.

For-Profit Employers

Private businesses can provide career experience and matching funds. Critics question federal subsidy of employers and the risk of replacing regular workers.

Political and Civic Activity

Federal policy distinguishes eligible civic education from prohibited election and political activity. Debate concerns whether voter engagement is public service or an inappropriate use of student-aid funds.

Future Funding

The 2026–2027 program operates with more than $1.2 billion in federal allocations, while the fiscal year 2027 administration request proposes a much smaller amount. Congress will determine whether the program is maintained, reduced, or restructured.

Key Distinctions

Federal Work-Study and Regular Student Employment

FWS requires federal financial need and uses federal wage subsidies. A regular campus job is paid entirely from employer funds and can hire students regardless of federal aid eligibility.

FWS Award and Earned Wages

The award is an earnings ceiling. Wages are paid only after work is performed.

Federal Work-Study and Pell Grant

Pell is grant aid disbursed according to enrollment and eligibility. FWS is taxable compensation earned through employment.

Federal Work-Study and Federal Student Loans

FWS creates wages and no repayment obligation. Loans provide borrowed funds that generally must be repaid with interest.

Federal Work-Study and FSEOG

FWS subsidizes employment for undergraduate and graduate students with need. FSEOG provides limited grants to undergraduates with exceptional need, with priority to Pell recipients.

FWS and JLD

FWS pays eligible student wages. JLD finances institutional efforts to locate and develop off-campus jobs for enrolled students regardless of need.

FWS and Work-Colleges

FWS is optional need-based employment at participating institutions. Work-Colleges operate comprehensive institutional work-learning-service systems.

Community Service and Political Activity

Community service improves public welfare and can include civic education. Campaigns, ballot activity, voter registration, poll work, and lobbying are prohibited.

On-Campus and Off-Campus Employment

On-campus work is performed for the institution or certain campus service providers. Off-campus work requires an eligible external employer and written agreement.

Financial-Aid Eligibility and Job Selection

FWS eligibility permits consideration for subsidized employment. It does not guarantee that a department or employer will select the student.

Geographic Scope

Federal Work-Study operates at participating domestic colleges, universities, community colleges, Tribal colleges, career schools, and other eligible postsecondary institutions throughout the United States and territories.

Institutional availability varies considerably. A student at one college can receive FWS while a similarly needy student at another institution receives no offer because the second school lacks allocation, match, or available positions.

State and local minimum wages create geographic variation in student earnings and in the number of hours an institutional allocation can support.

Rural institutions can have difficulty locating off-campus employers or transportation for community-service placements. Urban institutions can have more employers but face commuting time, transit cost, and schedule barriers.

Public two-year colleges receive a relatively small share of national FWS aid compared with their role in enrolling low-income, adult, and part-time students. Formula reform proposals often focus on this geographic and sectoral mismatch.

Students attending foreign institutions are not eligible for FWS. A domestic student studying abroad ordinarily cannot perform FWS employment at the foreign host institution.

Summary

The Federal Work-Study Program is a need-based federal student-aid program that subsidizes paid employment for undergraduate, graduate, and professional students. Congress created it as College Work-Study through the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 and incorporated it into the Higher Education Act of 1965.

FWS is campus based. The Department allocates limited federal funds to participating institutions, and each institution selects students, determines need, develops jobs, manages employers, pays wages, supplies matching funds, and reports activity.

A work-study offer is not a grant, loan, or guaranteed paycheck. The student must obtain an eligible job and perform work. The award states the maximum subsidized amount the student may earn.

Students generally complete the FAFSA, meet Title IV eligibility rules, demonstrate financial need, and maintain satisfactory academic progress. Undergraduate, graduate, professional, part-time, and certain teacher-certification students can qualify.

Students can work on campus or for eligible public, nonprofit, and private for-profit employers. Jobs should complement academic or career goals to the maximum extent practicable and must not displace workers, support political activity, or violate restrictions on religious facilities.

The ordinary federal share is up to 75 percent of wages. Private for-profit employment is limited to a 50-percent federal share. Certain tutoring, family-literacy, civic-service, public, nonprofit, and Title III positions can receive enhanced federal support.

Institutions generally must use at least 7 percent of their federal allocation for community-service wages and employ at least one reading tutor or family-literacy worker unless the Department grants a hardship waiver.

Students must receive at least the applicable minimum wage. Undergraduates are paid hourly; graduate and professional students can be paid hourly or by salary. Payment occurs at least monthly and is subject to payroll, tax, labour, and recordkeeping requirements.

FWS earnings are taxable wages but are excluded from income in the later federal student-aid need calculation. Qualifying institution-employed students can receive the student FICA exception.

For 2026–2027, Federal Student Aid authorized approximately $1.215 billion for 2,865 institutions. The latest detailed data show 481,552 recipients earning approximately $1.100 billion in 2023–2024.

Federal Work-Study can provide income, reduce borrowing, build career skills, support campus operations, and strengthen community organizations. Its value depends on whether students can obtain positions offering adequate wages, flexible schedules, competent supervision, meaningful work, and career development.

The program’s principal weaknesses are limited funds, unequal institutional allocations, modest earnings, administrative complexity, matching burdens, uneven job quality, and the possibility that students work substantial hours without receiving experience connected to future employment.

The continuing policy question is whether Federal Work-Study should remain primarily a flexible campus employment subsidy or become a more targeted national system of paid internships, apprenticeships, research, public service, and career-connected work for students with financial need.
