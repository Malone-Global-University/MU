Empowerment Zones Program

The federal place-based development initiative that designated selected distressed urban and rural communities for strategic planning, community partnerships, grants, tax incentives, business investment, employment programs, infrastructure, housing, and human services.

Overview

The Empowerment Zones Program was a federal place-based economic and community-development initiative created by the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993. It designated selected high-poverty urban and rural areas for coordinated public, private, nonprofit, and resident-led revitalization.

The program formed part of the broader Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Communities Initiative, commonly abbreviated EZ/EC. Empowerment Zones received the most extensive package of federal assistance. Enterprise Communities received smaller grants and fewer tax benefits but followed a related strategic-planning and partnership model.

The initiative differed from a conventional grant program. Federal law designated geographic zones and attached multiple forms of assistance to those areas. Depending on the designation round, communities received direct grants, employer tax credits, enhanced business deductions, tax-exempt bond authority, hiring incentives, regulatory flexibility, technical assistance, and preferences or coordination across federal programs.

Urban areas were administered principally by the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development. Rural areas were administered principally by the United States Department of Agriculture. The Department of Health and Human Services distributed major first-round Social Services Block Grant funds, while the Department of the Treasury and Internal Revenue Service administered tax provisions.

State and local governments nominated distressed areas through competitive applications. Applicants had to document poverty, unemployment, economic distress, geographic eligibility, community participation, governmental commitment, and a comprehensive strategic plan. Local residents, businesses, community organizations, governments, schools, financial institutions, and other stakeholders were expected to participate in implementation.

The program rested on four recurring principles: economic opportunity, sustainable community development, community-based partnerships, and a strategic vision for change. Local strategic plans combined business development and employment with housing, education, public safety, healthcare, childcare, transportation, infrastructure, social services, environmental improvement, and institutional development.

Congress authorized three designation rounds. The first round ultimately included eleven Empowerment Zones. The second round added twenty zones—fifteen urban and five rural. The third round authorized nine additional zones—seven urban and two rural—bringing the total to forty designations across the three rounds.

The amount and form of federal assistance changed over time. Original first-round zones received substantial grants in addition to tax benefits. Later zones generally received smaller grants or relied primarily on tax incentives, bonds, federal coordination, and local strategic investments. Third-round zones were principally tax-incentive designations.

The Empowerment Zone employment credit became the most visible tax benefit. Qualifying employers could generally claim a credit equal to 20 percent of up to $15,000 in annual qualified wages for an eligible employee who lived in the zone and performed substantially all employment services within it. The maximum annual credit was therefore generally $3,000 per qualified employee.

Local implementation varied considerably. Communities used program resources for business loans, industrial and commercial development, workforce training, childcare, schools, health facilities, affordable housing, homeownership, community centres, water and sewer systems, roads, public safety, technology, small-business assistance, and nonprofit capacity.

Federal evaluations found that many designated communities experienced improvements and completed numerous projects. However, the effects of the program were difficult to isolate from national economic growth, local policy, private-market trends, demographic change, and other federal programs. In 2006, the Government Accountability Office concluded that improvements occurred in the communities studied but that the program’s independent effect remained unclear.

The program was repeatedly extended after its original termination dates. Revenue Procedure 2021-18 automatically extended qualifying Empowerment Zone designations through December 31, 2025 unless a nominating government affirmatively declined the extension.

As of 2026, the designation-based program is no longer active for new tax benefits. The Empowerment Zone employment credit expired for qualified wages paid or incurred after December 31, 2025, and the statutory zone-designation period ended on that date. Prior-year tax claims, carryforwards, outstanding bonds, continuing local organizations, projects, assets, and contractual obligations can remain relevant.

Definitions

Empowerment Zone
A federally designated distressed urban or rural geographic area eligible during its designation period for specified grants, tax incentives, bond authority, federal coordination, technical assistance, and program preferences.
Empowerment Zones Program
The collective federal designation and assistance system authorized principally under Subchapter U of the Internal Revenue Code and related grant, regulatory, and administrative laws.
Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Communities Initiative
The broader federal framework combining Empowerment Zones, Enterprise Communities, strategic plans, local partnerships, tax provisions, grants, and interagency assistance.
Enterprise Community
A designated distressed community receiving smaller grants and a more limited range of tax benefits than an Empowerment Zone while following a related strategic-planning framework.
Urban Empowerment Zone
An Empowerment Zone within an urban area and designated or supervised principally by HUD.
Rural Empowerment Zone
An Empowerment Zone within a qualifying rural area and designated or supervised principally by USDA.
Strategic plan
The community-developed document identifying local conditions, goals, projects, partnerships, resources, benchmarks, governance, and implementation methods for zone revitalization.
Strategic vision for change
A long-term community understanding of how coordinated local action and outside resources would produce measurable economic, social, physical, and institutional improvement.
Economic opportunity
The program principle emphasizing employment, business development, entrepreneurship, access to capital, workforce preparation, wealth creation, and connection to regional markets.
Sustainable community development
The program principle linking economic activity with housing, infrastructure, education, safety, health, environment, transportation, and long-term community capacity.
Community-based partnership
A formal or informal collaboration among residents, governments, businesses, nonprofit organizations, schools, financial institutions, religious organizations, and other local stakeholders.
Nominating government
The state, local, Tribal, or combined governmental authority that submitted an area for federal designation and made commitments concerning implementation.
Coordinating responsible authority
The local entity responsible for coordinating strategic-plan implementation, program reporting, federal communication, tax-benefit outreach, and community governance.
Zone resident
A person whose principal residence was located within an Empowerment Zone during the relevant designation period.
Qualified zone employee
An employee satisfying statutory residence and work-location requirements for purposes of the Empowerment Zone employment credit, subject to exclusions and special rules.
Qualified zone wages
Eligible wages paid or incurred for services performed by a qualified zone employee within an Empowerment Zone, generally limited to the first $15,000 per employee per calendar year.
Empowerment Zone employment credit
A federal general-business credit generally equal to 20 percent of qualified zone wages, producing a maximum annual credit of $3,000 for each qualifying employee.
Enterprise zone business
A qualifying business satisfying statutory requirements concerning location, gross income, property use, employee residence, services, and prohibited activities.
Qualified zone property
Qualifying business property acquired and used within an Empowerment Zone by an eligible enterprise-zone business under federal tax rules.
Section 179 expensing
A federal tax deduction allowing qualifying businesses to deduct eligible property costs immediately rather than recover them solely through depreciation. Empowerment Zone rules historically permitted enhanced expensing for qualifying zone property.
Empowerment Zone facility bond
A tax-exempt bond financing qualifying business facilities and property associated with an Empowerment Zone or Enterprise Community under federal tax law.
Developable site
A qualifying commercial or industrial parcel included within certain later-round zones even when it did not independently satisfy all ordinary poverty criteria, subject to acreage and statutory limitations.
Social Services Block Grant
A federal grant program under Title XX of the Social Security Act used to distribute major first-round EZ and EC grant funds to states for authorized zone activities.
Economic Development Initiative grant
A HUD grant supporting economic and community-development projects, used as part of the supplemental assistance provided to Cleveland, Los Angeles, and selected enhanced communities.
Section 108 Loan Guarantee
A HUD financing mechanism allowing communities to borrow for eligible economic and community-development projects using future Community Development Block Grant funds as security.
Champion Community
A rural applicant recognized by USDA for a strong strategic plan even though it did not receive an Empowerment Zone or Enterprise Community designation.
Benchmark
A measurable milestone used to assess strategic-plan activity, implementation progress, outputs, or outcomes.
Place-based policy
A policy directing benefits and intervention toward a defined geographic area rather than solely toward eligible individuals, firms, or institutions wherever located.
Tax expenditure
Federal revenue forgone through a credit, exclusion, deduction, preferential rate, deferral, or other tax provision intended to advance a public objective.

Historical and Policy Origins

Enterprise-Zone Proposals

The concept drew from British enterprise zones and United States proposals developed during the late 1970s and 1980s. Advocates argued that distressed neighborhoods could attract business through tax relief, regulatory flexibility, land assembly, infrastructure, and local development organizations.

Early federal proposals frequently emphasized private-market incentives. Critics warned that geographically targeted tax relief could subsidize activity that would have occurred anyway, relocate firms from nearby areas, or provide insufficient benefit to residents.

Community-Development Expansion

The Clinton administration combined enterprise-zone incentives with community planning, direct grants, social services, workforce development, housing, resident participation, and interagency support. This broader structure attempted to treat neighborhood distress as an interconnected economic and institutional problem.

Urban and Rural Inclusion

The federal initiative explicitly included rural poverty. USDA-administered zones addressed weak infrastructure, geographic isolation, agricultural transition, limited capital, workforce needs, healthcare access, water and sewer systems, and dispersed settlement patterns.

Post-Industrial and Racial Context

Many urban applicants had experienced deindustrialization, suburban employment growth, population loss, housing abandonment, racial segregation, disinvestment, crime, unemployment, and declining municipal revenue. African American and Latino communities were heavily represented among designated urban areas.

Federalism and Local Planning

The program required state and local nomination rather than unilateral federal selection. Communities were expected to design their own strategic plans and identify local priorities, while federal designation supplied resources and incentives.

Four Program Principles

Economic Opportunity

Local plans were expected to create jobs, support businesses, develop entrepreneurs, connect residents with regional employment, expand access to capital, improve workforce skills, and strengthen local markets.

Sustainable Community Development

Economic activity was linked to housing, transportation, education, health, public safety, childcare, environment, infrastructure, technology, and community institutions. The objective was long-term capacity rather than isolated projects.

Community-Based Partnerships

Residents, governments, businesses, community organizations, schools, banks, religious institutions, foundations, and federal agencies were expected to share responsibility for strategic-plan design and implementation.

Strategic Vision for Change

Applicants were required to establish a coherent local vision, measurable benchmarks, priority activities, committed partners, implementation structures, and an explanation of how projects would reinforce one another.

Interaction of the Principles

The principles were intended to prevent the program from becoming only a tax incentive, only a social-service program, or only a construction initiative. A successful zone would combine economic growth with resident benefit, institutional capacity, physical improvement, and accountable local governance.

Eligibility and Designation

State and Local Nomination

An area could not designate itself. State and local governments—or other qualifying governmental entities under applicable round rules—submitted a formal nomination to HUD or USDA.

Economic Distress

Nominated areas had to demonstrate pervasive poverty, unemployment, general distress, population or employment decline, inadequate housing, weak infrastructure, or related conditions. Census-tract poverty data played a central role.

Geographic Requirements

Federal law limited zone population and land area and generally required a continuous boundary. Later rounds allowed larger zones and qualifying developable sites outside the ordinary poverty tests to support job creation.

Strategic Plan

The application included a comprehensive strategic plan developed with community participation. Reviewers evaluated vision, goals, partnerships, resources, governance, feasibility, resident involvement, and expected results.

Government Commitments

Applicants identified state and local actions involving taxes, fees, zoning, permits, infrastructure, services, procurement, policing, education, business support, land, and regulatory relief.

Private and Nonprofit Commitments

Applications described investments and services expected from businesses, lenders, foundations, universities, hospitals, community-development corporations, religious organizations, and other partners.

Competitive Selection

Applications substantially exceeded available designations. Selection therefore reflected statutory eligibility and federal judgments concerning strategic quality, partnership, need, capacity, innovation, and probability of implementation.

Post-Designation Responsibilities

Designated communities had to maintain governance, implement the strategic plan, report progress, administer grant funds, communicate with businesses, coordinate federal assistance, and comply with designation requirements.

Designation Rounds

Round I

The first round began with six urban and three rural Empowerment Zones designated in December 1994. The urban zones were Atlanta, Baltimore, Chicago, Detroit, New York City, and Philadelphia–Camden. The rural zones were Kentucky Highlands, Mid-Delta Mississippi, and the Rio Grande Valley in Texas.

Cleveland and Los Angeles initially received Supplemental Empowerment Zone status and later received full Round I status. They did not receive the same original Social Services Block Grant package but received substantial Economic Development Initiative grants and Section 108 Loan Guarantees.

Round I included the largest direct-grant component. Original urban zones generally received $100 million each and original rural zones generally received $40 million each in special Social Services Block Grant funding.

Round II

The Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 authorized additional designations. Fifteen urban and five rural zones were announced in January 1999.

The urban zones included Boston; Cincinnati; Columbia–Sumter; Columbus; Cumberland County, New Jersey; El Paso; Gary–East Chicago; Huntington–Ironton; Knoxville–Knox County; Miami–Dade County; Minneapolis; New Haven; Norfolk–Portsmouth; Santa Ana; and St. Louis–East St. Louis.

The rural zones were Cordele, Georgia; Desert Communities in Riverside County, California; Lake Agassiz, North Dakota; Southernmost Illinois; and the Oglala Sioux Tribe’s Pine Ridge area in South Dakota.

Round II zones received smaller direct grants than original Round I zones but gained access to enhanced tax incentives, bonds, federal coordination, and other development tools.

Round III

The Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000 authorized nine additional zones: seven urban and two rural. Designations were announced in December 2001 and generally became effective in 2002.

Round III was principally tax based. It did not provide the large direct-grant packages associated with the original Round I designations.

Urban Round III designations included Fresno, Jacksonville, Oklahoma City, Pulaski County, San Antonio, Tucson, and Yonkers. Rural designations were administered through USDA under the applicable designation process.

Forty Total Zones

Counting the eleven Round I zones, twenty Round II zones, and nine Round III zones, the federal program produced forty Empowerment Zone designations.

Governance and Strategic Plans

Local Governance

Communities used nonprofit corporations, public authorities, municipal offices, intergovernmental boards, resident committees, development corporations, and other structures to administer zone activity.

Board Composition

Boards could include residents, business owners, nonprofit leaders, government officials, clergy, educators, bankers, labor representatives, developers, and community organizations. The proportion and authority of residents varied.

Project Selection

Local entities issued requests for proposals, reviewed project applications, negotiated grants or loans, set priorities, and monitored subrecipients. Some plans preallocated most funds before designation, while others retained flexible funds for later projects.

Benchmarks

Strategic plans identified benchmarks such as jobs created, residents trained, businesses assisted, housing developed, infrastructure completed, children served, crime reduced, or capital leveraged.

Community Participation

Residents participated through public meetings, neighborhood committees, planning sessions, surveys, board seats, project review, and local organizations. Federal reviews found significant participation at some sites and more limited influence at others.

Administrative Capacity

Effective implementation required financial controls, procurement, contracts, data systems, staff, technical expertise, reporting, and coordination among multiple governments and service providers.

Governance Conflict

Zones sometimes experienced conflict between city hall, residents, business leaders, nonprofit organizations, and administering corporations over control of funds, project priorities, representation, accountability, and pace of implementation.

Long-Term Institutional Effects

Even after grant funds were spent, some zones left behind development corporations, revolving loan funds, service networks, community facilities, business partnerships, and experienced local leaders.

Federal Grants and Interagency Assistance

Social Services Block Grant Funding

Round I grants were distributed through Title XX of the Social Security Act. Funds could support a broad range of activities promoting self-sufficiency, employment, services, community development, and implementation of the strategic plan.

Economic Development Initiative Grants

Supplemental zones and selected communities received HUD Economic Development Initiative grants for projects involving economic revitalization, facilities, housing, infrastructure, and business activity.

Section 108 Financing

Section 108 Loan Guarantees enabled communities to finance larger projects using future Community Development Block Grant allocations as security.

USDA Rural Development Assistance

Rural zones coordinated loans, grants, technical assistance, water and wastewater programs, housing, utilities, business development, community facilities, and agricultural or regional-development resources.

Federal Program Preferences

Federal departments could provide preferences, technical support, waivers, outreach, or coordinated access to existing programs. The value of these benefits depended on agency appropriations and local capacity.

Interagency Teams

HUD and USDA coordinated with HHS, Treasury, Labor, Commerce, Justice, Transportation, Education, the Environmental Protection Agency, Small Business Administration, and other agencies.

Leveraging

Communities used federal grants to attract state, local, private, philanthropic, and nonprofit resources. HUD later reported thousands of activities and substantial leveraged investment, although federal reviews identified data-quality problems affecting exact totals.

Grant Flexibility

Broad strategic-plan authority allowed communities to tailor activities to local needs. Flexibility also made national comparison and attribution difficult because zones funded different combinations of projects.

Tax Incentives

Empowerment Zone Employment Credit

The employment credit generally equaled 20 percent of qualified zone wages paid to an eligible employee. Up to $15,000 in wages per employee per year could be considered, producing a maximum credit of $3,000.

The employee generally had to live within an Empowerment Zone and perform substantially all employment services within the employer’s trade or business in the zone. Statutory exclusions applied to specified relatives, owners, certain businesses, and other categories.

Enhanced Section 179 Expensing

Eligible enterprise-zone businesses could deduct an increased amount of qualifying property cost under Section 179. The precise enhancement changed through legislation and inflation adjustments.

Enterprise Zone Facility Bonds

State and local governments could issue tax-exempt bonds for qualifying business property and facilities. The program imposed business, property, employee-residence, use, and bond-volume requirements.

Qualified Small Business Stock

Federal law historically provided an increased exclusion for qualifying gain from certain small-business stock associated with Empowerment Zone businesses, subject to acquisition, holding-period, active-business, and statutory requirements.

Rollover of Qualified Empowerment Zone Assets

Qualifying taxpayers could defer recognition of gain by purchasing replacement Empowerment Zone assets within the required period, subject to tax-law conditions and basis adjustments.

Work Opportunity Tax Credit

Residents of Empowerment Zones and certain summer youth employees could qualify as targeted groups under the Work Opportunity Tax Credit. Employers needed certification from state workforce agencies and had to satisfy separate hiring and wage rules.

Tax-Benefit Awareness

Local zone offices educated businesses and tax professionals, issued boundary information, supported employee certification, and promoted available incentives.

Use and Complexity

GAO found that the employment credit was viewed as the most frequently used original tax benefit. Bond use was more limited because of complexity, issuance cost, workforce requirements, alternative financing, and difficulty assembling eligible projects.

Business Size

Stakeholders reported that larger businesses with specialized tax professionals were more likely to understand and claim benefits. Small businesses with limited profit or accounting capacity could receive less value from tax incentives.

Major Program Activities

Business Development

Zones supported business loans, revolving funds, grants, technical assistance, incubators, commercial property, industrial development, minority-owned businesses, procurement, and entrepreneurship.

Workforce Development

Activities included job training, placement, apprenticeships, youth employment, adult education, employer partnerships, transportation, childcare, and career preparation.

Housing

Communities funded rehabilitation, affordable rentals, homeownership assistance, vacant-property reuse, code enforcement, housing counselling, and neighborhood redevelopment.

Infrastructure

Projects included water systems, sewers, roads, sidewalks, lighting, telecommunications, industrial sites, transit, drainage, community facilities, and environmental improvements.

Education and Youth

Zones supported schools, early childhood education, tutoring, technology, youth centres, after-school programs, scholarships, and school-to-work initiatives.

Health and Human Services

Projects included health clinics, mental-health services, childcare, family support, substance-use treatment, food access, senior services, and coordinated service centres.

Public Safety

Local strategies addressed community policing, youth violence, street lighting, vacant buildings, drug markets, reentry, domestic violence, crime prevention, and neighborhood organization.

Community Institutions

Zones funded nonprofit capacity, resident organizations, faith-based initiatives, community-development corporations, strategic-planning staff, and local data systems.

Environmental and Land Reuse

Activities addressed brownfields, abandoned industrial land, illegal dumping, parks, open space, contamination, and redevelopment of vacant property.

How the Program Worked

Step One: Community Nomination

State and local governments identified a distressed geographic area and organized a competitive federal application.

Step Two: Strategic Planning

Residents and institutional partners assessed local conditions, developed a vision, selected priorities, committed resources, and established benchmarks.

Step Three: Federal Designation

HUD or USDA reviewed eligibility and strategic quality and selected a limited number of zones.

Step Four: Local Governance

The community established or designated an administering organization, board, municipal office, or partnership structure.

Step Five: Grant and Project Implementation

Local entities funded projects, made loans, contracted with service providers, developed facilities, and coordinated government programs.

Step Six: Business Incentives

Employers and investors determined whether activities satisfied federal tax requirements and claimed eligible benefits through federal tax returns.

Step Seven: Federal Coordination

Zone officials sought technical assistance, funding preferences, regulatory support, and program resources from participating agencies.

Step Eight: Reporting and Monitoring

Local entities reported activities and progress to HUD or USDA, while HHS, IRS, state agencies, and other authorities monitored grants or tax provisions within their jurisdiction.

Step Nine: Revision

Strategic plans and project lists changed as local leadership, markets, costs, partners, and community needs evolved.

Step Ten: Legacy and Continuation

After grants and designations ended, some organizations, loan funds, businesses, facilities, and development strategies continued through other resources.

Representative Examples

Baltimore Empowerment Zone

Baltimore used a nonprofit management organization and local partnerships to support business development, employment, housing, human services, and neighborhood initiatives. Implementation demonstrated the potential and difficulty of coordinating multiple neighborhoods and institutions within one zone.

Detroit Empowerment Zone

Detroit directed resources toward business finance, industrial and commercial development, workforce programs, neighborhood organizations, housing, and large-scale economic initiatives within communities affected by deindustrialization and population loss.

New York Empowerment Zone

The New York zone included areas of Upper Manhattan and the South Bronx and used development corporations, loans, grants, cultural investment, commercial development, and business assistance. Its structure reflected the complex relationship among city, state, federal, and community institutions.

Philadelphia–Camden Empowerment Zone

This interstate zone connected distressed neighborhoods in Pennsylvania and New Jersey. Separate local governance structures implemented business, housing, service, and community-development activities under a shared federal designation.

Kentucky Highlands Empowerment Zone

The rural Kentucky zone emphasized regional development, business activity, infrastructure, education, tourism, workforce preparation, and community institutions across a geographically dispersed Appalachian area.

Mid-Delta Mississippi Empowerment Zone

The Mississippi Delta zone addressed persistent rural poverty through economic development, infrastructure, services, business support, workforce activity, and regional partnerships.

Rio Grande Valley Empowerment Zone

The Texas rural zone supported water and public infrastructure, education, community facilities, small-business loans, job training, youth services, and healthcare-related workforce programs.

Oglala Sioux Tribe Empowerment Zone

The Round II rural designation included parts of the Pine Ridge Reservation. It represented the program’s expanded eligibility for Tribal areas and addressed severe poverty, employment, infrastructure, housing, and institutional needs.

Urban and Rural Differences

Population Density

Urban zones commonly included dense census tracts, commercial corridors, industrial sites, public housing, transit, and multiple neighborhoods. Rural zones covered larger territories with dispersed populations and communities.

Infrastructure

Urban priorities often involved vacant buildings, brownfields, commercial districts, housing, public safety, and workforce access. Rural zones frequently emphasized water, sewer, roads, telecommunications, community facilities, healthcare, and regional transportation.

Business Markets

Urban zones could access larger labor and consumer markets but faced disinvestment, land assembly, crime perception, and competition with suburbs. Rural zones faced small markets, distance, limited capital, lower business density, and workforce outmigration.

Governance

Urban zones often involved one or more cities and neighborhood institutions. Rural zones frequently crossed county or municipal boundaries and required regional or Tribal coordination.

Federal Administration

HUD supervised urban designations, while USDA supervised rural designations. Tax provisions operated through the same Internal Revenue Code framework but grant and technical-assistance systems differed.

Evaluation

Rural zones contained fewer census tracts, making some statistical comparison methods less practical. Economic outcomes were also affected by agriculture, migration, regional labor markets, and infrastructure constraints distinct from urban areas.

African American Communities and Racial Context

Geographic Concentration

Many urban Empowerment Zones were located in predominantly African American neighborhoods shaped by residential segregation, deindustrialization, redlining, urban renewal, highway construction, housing abandonment, concentrated poverty, and unequal municipal and private investment.

Capital and Employment

The program sought to attract business investment and employment into communities from which capital and jobs had moved. Business loans, workforce programs, commercial development, and employer credits could expand local opportunity.

Resident Employment Requirement

The employment credit required qualifying workers to live in the zone and work for an eligible zone business. This design attempted to connect business tax relief directly to residents rather than only to property location.

Community Institutions

Grants strengthened some African American community-development corporations, churches, service organizations, business groups, training providers, cultural institutions, and resident organizations.

Control and Representation

Local governance generated debate over whether residents, municipal officials, major businesses, nonprofit leaders, or outside developers should control funds and priorities. Formal participation did not always produce equal decision-making authority.

Gentrification and Displacement

Successful investment could raise property values and improve services. Without housing preservation and resident ownership, revitalization could also increase displacement pressure on low-income renters, homeowners, and small businesses.

Business Benefit

Tax incentives were easier for profitable firms with professional tax advice to use. Smaller minority-owned businesses could have less taxable income, weaker access to capital, or insufficient administrative capacity to capture benefits.

Regional Isolation

Zone boundaries did not remove barriers connecting residents to suburban jobs, transportation, schools, credit, housing markets, and regional political power.

Racial Equity Evaluation

Evaluation requires distinguishing increased investment within a Black community from increased income, ownership, employment, housing security, and institutional power among its existing residents.

Outcomes and Evaluation

Project Activity

Zones implemented thousands of projects involving business assistance, workforce development, housing, education, health, infrastructure, public safety, and community institutions.

Leveraged Investment

HUD reported that federal seed funding and tax incentives attracted billions of dollars in additional public and private commitments. GAO cautioned that inconsistent reporting limited confidence in exact national totals.

Economic Change

Many zones experienced job growth, business growth, higher income, reduced unemployment, reduced poverty, or increased property values during portions of the designation period. Outcomes varied by zone and measure.

Attribution Problem

During the late 1990s, the national economy expanded and unemployment declined. Zone communities were also affected by city policy, state programs, Community Development Block Grants, private investment, demographic change, welfare reform, housing markets, and regional development.

GAO 2006 Finding

GAO found improvements in Round I communities but concluded that the program’s independent effect was unclear. Statistical results varied across poverty, unemployment, businesses, jobs, and comparison methods.

Tax-Use Data

Federal agencies lacked complete information identifying which businesses claimed every incentive and how much benefit reached individual communities. Tax-return confidentiality and form design limited local analysis.

Grant Data

Federal reviews identified inconsistent categories, unsupported amounts, incomplete reporting, and weak reconciliation among HUD, USDA, HHS, and local records.

Local Institutional Effects

Some outcomes were not captured by poverty or employment rates. Strategic planning, partnerships, community organizations, public facilities, loan funds, and local administrative capacity could have continuing value.

Distributional Effects

Aggregate improvement did not establish who benefited. New jobs could go to nonresidents; new businesses could displace older firms; property appreciation could benefit owners while burdening renters; and investment could cluster in selected parts of a zone.

Benefits and Limitations

Community Planning

The application process required distressed communities to create strategic plans, identify assets, build partnerships, and establish priorities.

Flexible Investment

Grant resources could support locally selected combinations of economic development, services, housing, infrastructure, and community capacity.

Resident-Linked Employment Credit

The requirement that qualified employees live and work in the zone sought to connect tax benefits directly to local residents.

Interagency Coordination

Designation gave communities a platform for seeking coordinated support from multiple federal departments.

Urban and Rural Reach

The program addressed both inner-city and rural poverty within one national framework.

Limited Designations

Only a small number of communities received Empowerment Zone status. Comparable distressed areas received weaker Enterprise Community benefits or no designation.

Tax Complexity

Businesses needed to understand boundaries, employee residence, qualified activities, prohibited businesses, property rules, forms, certification, and interactions with other credits.

Unequal Business Capacity

Tax incentives were most useful to firms with taxable income, investment plans, eligible employees, and professional advice. New and very small businesses could benefit less.

Fragmented Oversight

HUD, USDA, HHS, IRS, states, workforce agencies, and local entities administered different components. No single system captured all activity and outcomes.

Temporary Authority

Repeated short-term extensions created uncertainty for businesses, local governments, bond issuers, tax professionals, and employees.

Boundary Effects

Geographic incentives could influence where firms located within a region without necessarily creating net regional economic growth.

Displacement Risk

Rising investment and land values could increase pressure on renters, homeowners, and local businesses unless paired with housing, ownership, and preservation policies.

Major Policy Debates

Place-Based or People-Based Assistance

Supporters argued that distressed places required direct investment in businesses, institutions, infrastructure, and markets. Critics argued that assistance should follow individuals through education, income support, housing mobility, and employment rather than subsidize a location.

Tax Incentives or Direct Spending

Tax incentives can attract private decisions and reduce administrative selection of individual firms. Direct grants can fund infrastructure and services that unprofitable businesses or low-income communities cannot finance through tax savings.

Job Creation or Job Relocation

An employer might create a new job, move a job from outside the zone, or claim a credit for a worker it would have hired anyway. Evaluation must distinguish these effects.

Community Control or Government Administration

Resident participation was a core principle, but governmental and business institutions often controlled legal authority, financing, and implementation.

Comprehensive Strategy or Diffuse Program

Broad eligibility allowed communities to address interconnected needs. It also spread resources across many activities and made it difficult to identify which interventions produced results.

Regional Economy or Zone Boundary

Residents participated in metropolitan and regional labor, housing, and capital markets. Small zone boundaries could not independently change suburban job location, transportation, segregation, or regional governance.

Extension or Replacement

Congress repeatedly extended the program rather than making it permanently active. Later federal policy created Renewal Communities, New Markets Tax Credits, HUBZones, and Opportunity Zones with different incentives and administrative structures.

Economic Growth or Equity

An increase in property value, investment, or business activity does not necessarily improve resident income, ownership, housing security, or political power. Program success depends on both growth and distribution.

Expiration and Current Status

Final Extension

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 extended Empowerment Zone designations through December 31, 2025. IRS Revenue Procedure 2021-18 treated qualifying nominations as automatically amended to use that date unless the nominating government declined.

Designation Expiration

Internal Revenue Code Section 1391 identifies December 31, 2025 as the final designation date. No later extension was in effect as of August 2, 2026.

Employment Credit

The Empowerment Zone employment credit expired for qualified wages paid or incurred after 2025. Employers can still need to address amended returns, prior-year eligibility, examinations, carryforwards, and recordkeeping for periods when the credit was available.

Carryforwards

Unused portions of a prior-year general business credit may remain subject to ordinary carryback or carryforward rules even when new credit generation has expired.

Outstanding Bonds and Property

Expiration does not automatically cancel an existing bond, loan, property covenant, grant contract, development agreement, or prior tax obligation. Each instrument remains governed by its own terms and applicable law.

Local Organizations

Some former zone corporations, development funds, facilities, nonprofit partnerships, and local initiatives continued independently or were absorbed into other organizations.

Geographic Legacy

Former zone boundaries remain relevant to historical research, property records, tax documentation, evaluations, prior investments, and comparison with later place-based programs.

No New Designations

The program does not currently accept applications for new Empowerment Zone designations. Communities seeking current federal place-based assistance must use other programs or any future authority enacted by Congress.

Key Distinctions

Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Communities

Empowerment Zones received broader tax incentives and substantially larger grants. Enterprise Communities received smaller grants and more limited tax benefits under a related planning framework.

Empowerment Zones and Renewal Communities

Renewal Communities were created by the Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000 and relied primarily on a different package of tax incentives and state and local commitments. A Renewal Community designation could replace an overlapping EZ or EC designation.

Empowerment Zones and Opportunity Zones

Empowerment Zones combined strategic planning, grants, employment credits, bonds, community participation, and federal coordination. Opportunity Zones provide capital-gains tax incentives through investment in Qualified Opportunity Funds and do not require the same local strategic plan or resident-employment structure.

Empowerment Zones and HUBZones

HUBZones are a Small Business Administration contracting program providing federal procurement advantages to qualifying small businesses. Empowerment Zones were broader community-development and tax-incentive designations.

Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Zones

“Enterprise zone” is a general term used by many state and local programs. A federal Empowerment Zone was a specific designation under federal law.

Empowerment Zones and New Markets Tax Credits

The New Markets Tax Credit provides investor tax credits for qualified equity investments in Community Development Entities serving low-income communities. Empowerment Zones provided direct business and employment incentives within designated boundaries.

Empowerment Zones and Community Development Block Grants

CDBG is an ongoing formula and state grant program serving a broad range of eligible communities. Empowerment Zones were competitively designated geographic areas receiving special time-limited benefits.

Empowerment Zones and Model Cities

Model Cities combined federal grants, comprehensive planning, municipal administration, and citizen participation during the 1960s and 1970s. Empowerment Zones used a later public-private model with stronger emphasis on business incentives and tax policy.

Designation and Strategic Plan

The designation established eligibility for federal benefits. The strategic plan identified how the local community intended to use designation and other resources.

Program Grant and Tax Credit

Grants were public funds administered through government and local entities. Tax credits reduced qualifying taxpayers’ federal tax liabilities and were claimed through tax returns.

Geographic Scope

The program designated forty Empowerment Zones through three rounds. Zones were located in major cities, midsized cities, multijurisdictional metropolitan areas, rural counties, Tribal lands, the Mississippi Delta, Appalachia, the Rio Grande Valley, and other distressed regions.

Round I urban zones included Atlanta, Baltimore, Chicago, Cleveland, Detroit, Los Angeles, New York City, and Philadelphia–Camden. Round I rural zones included Kentucky Highlands, Mid-Delta Mississippi, and Rio Grande Valley, Texas.

Round II added fifteen urban and five rural areas. Several zones crossed municipal or state boundaries, including Philadelphia–Camden, St. Louis–East St. Louis, and Huntington–Ironton.

Round III added nine tax-oriented zones. Later-round rules allowed specified developable sites that could connect distressed residential communities to industrial or commercial land capable of supporting employment.

Zone boundaries were generally based on census tracts or similar geographic units. Businesses and employees needed to determine whether particular addresses fell inside the legally designated boundary for tax purposes.

The geographic approach concentrated resources but created boundary effects. A business across the street from a zone could be ineligible while a similar business inside qualified. Residents could move out of the zone and lose tax-related eligibility even while remaining in the same metropolitan area.

Legal Context

Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993

Title XIII of Public Law 103-66 created the Empowerment Zones and Enterprise Communities framework, authorized the first designations, established tax incentives, and provided special Social Services Block Grant funding.

Internal Revenue Code Subchapter U

Sections 1391 through 1397D govern designation, eligibility, enterprise-zone bonds, employment credits, definitions, qualified property, rollover treatment, expensing, and other tax rules.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1391

Section 1391 establishes designation procedures, number and type of zones, termination, nomination, strategic-plan requirements, and the authority of HUD and USDA.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1392

Section 1392 establishes geographic, population, poverty, unemployment, and general-distress requirements for nominated areas.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1394

Section 1394 governs tax-exempt enterprise-zone facility bonds, business qualification, bond limitations, and local designation of bond authority.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1396

Section 1396 establishes the Empowerment Zone employment credit, qualified wages, employee eligibility, exclusions, and coordination with other credits.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1397A

Section 1397A historically increased Section 179 expensing for qualified zone property and established recapture rules when property ceased qualifying.

Internal Revenue Code Section 1397B

Section 1397B permits qualifying rollover treatment for gain from certain Empowerment Zone assets when replacement property is acquired within the statutory period.

Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997

Public Law 105-34 authorized Round II zones, revised designation criteria, expanded developable-site authority, and changed the tax-incentive framework.

Community Renewal Tax Relief Act of 2000

Enacted through Public Law 106-554, this law authorized Round III zones and Renewal Communities, conformed tax incentives among EZ rounds, and extended the then-current designation period.

Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021

Public Law 116-260 extended Empowerment Zone designations and related tax provisions through December 31, 2025.

Revenue Procedure 2021-18

The IRS provided an automatic procedure deeming state and local nominations amended to extend qualifying designations through December 31, 2025 unless the nominating government affirmatively declined.

USDA Regulations

Special Social Services Block Grant authority financed major Round I grants and governed state receipt, planning, reporting, and authorized use.

Current Legal Status

Section 1391 identifies December 31, 2025 as the final designation date under current law. The employment credit does not apply to new wages after that date absent subsequent legislation.

Summary

The Empowerment Zones Program was a major federal experiment in place-based economic and community development. Created in 1993, it designated selected distressed urban and rural areas for strategic planning, grants, tax incentives, bonds, business development, employment programs, infrastructure, housing, services, and interagency assistance.

The program attempted to combine four principles: economic opportunity, sustainable community development, community-based partnerships, and a strategic vision for change. Local residents, governments, businesses, nonprofits, schools, lenders, and federal agencies were expected to coordinate their resources around a comprehensive community plan.

Three rounds produced forty Empowerment Zone designations. Round I included the largest direct-grant assistance. Round II expanded the program with smaller grants and enhanced tax tools. Round III relied principally on tax incentives.

The employment credit was the most prominent benefit, generally allowing qualifying employers to claim 20 percent of up to $15,000 in annual wages for an employee who lived and worked within the zone. Other benefits included enhanced expensing, tax-exempt facility bonds, capital-gain provisions, Work Opportunity Tax Credit categories, grants, loan guarantees, and federal program coordination.

Zones implemented thousands of projects involving businesses, jobs, housing, schools, health, childcare, water and sewer systems, public safety, community facilities, and nonprofit capacity. Many communities experienced improvement, but weak tax-use data, inconsistent reporting, national economic change, and other public programs made the program’s independent effects difficult to measure.

The program was especially significant for African American and Latino urban communities and persistently poor rural regions. It directed capital and federal attention toward places affected by segregation, deindustrialization, disinvestment, infrastructure deficits, and limited employment. Its distributional success depended on whether existing residents gained stable employment, ownership, housing security, institutional power, and durable access to investment.

Congress repeatedly extended the designation after its original expiration date. The final extension ended on December 31, 2025. As of 2026, no new Empowerment Zone employment credit can be generated for post-2025 wages and no active federal designation period remains under current law.

The program’s legacy continues through local institutions and later federal policies. Renewal Communities, New Markets Tax Credits, HUBZones, and Opportunity Zones adopted different parts of the geographically targeted development approach, while debates over place-based investment, tax incentives, resident benefit, displacement, and measurable outcomes remain unresolved.
