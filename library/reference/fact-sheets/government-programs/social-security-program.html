Social Security Program

The federal social-insurance system providing retirement, survivor, family, and disability benefits based primarily on a worker’s covered earnings and payroll-tax contributions.

Terminology Note

The formal name of the principal Social Security cash-benefit system is Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance, abbreviated OASDI.

OASDI consists of two legally separate programs and trust funds: Old-Age and Survivors Insurance, or OASI, and Disability Insurance, or DI.

The term Social Security is often used broadly to describe the Social Security Act, the Social Security Administration, Medicare enrollment functions, Supplemental Security Income, and other federal benefits. This fact sheet uses the term principally for the contributory OASDI program.

Supplemental Security Income, or SSI, is a separate means-tested program funded mainly from general federal revenues. It does not require a prior record of Social Security-covered work.

Medicare is a separate federal health-insurance program. Social Security work records, benefit status, and administration can determine or process Medicare eligibility, but Medicare has distinct trust funds, premiums, benefits, and financing.

Social Security is not an individual investment account. Payroll taxes establish insured status and finance current benefits through a social-insurance system governed by federal law.

A worker’s benefit is not simply the worker’s payroll contributions returned with interest. Benefits reflect covered earnings, a progressive formula, family status, disability or death, claiming age, cost-of-living adjustments, and other statutory rules.

Overview

Social Security provides monthly income when a covered worker retires, becomes disabled, or dies.

Workers earn insured status by performing covered employment or self-employment and paying dedicated taxes.

Benefits replace part of a worker’s earnings rather than guaranteeing a fixed poverty-level income or maintaining the worker’s prior standard of living.

The formula is progressive: it replaces a higher proportion of earnings for lower-paid workers while paying larger dollar benefits to workers with higher covered earnings.

Family members can qualify even if they did not personally earn enough credits, because spouse, child, and survivor benefits derive from the insured worker’s record.

Social Security is national, portable among covered employers, adjusted for inflation after entitlement, and generally payable while eligibility continues.

It is a foundation of retirement and disability security rather than a complete retirement plan.

Classification and Scope

This page belongs under Government Programs because Social Security is a continuing federal insurance system with compulsory contributions, insured-status rules, formulas, claims, appeals, trust funds, and annual adjustments.

The Social Security Administration, Treasury, Internal Revenue Service, state Disability Determination Services, and representative-payee organisations belong under Institutions.

The Social Security Act and major amendments belong under Laws and Legislation.

SSI, Medicare, Railroad Retirement, public pensions, unemployment insurance, workers’ compensation, and private retirement plans warrant separate entries.

Great Depression Context

The Great Depression exposed the insecurity of older people dependent on employment, family support, charity, local poor relief, or personal savings.

Bank failures, unemployment, deflation, and business collapse destroyed savings and weakened family support.

State old-age pension systems were uneven, underfunded, and geographically limited.

Popular movements, including the Townsend Plan, increased political pressure for national old-age security.

Committee on Economic Security

President Roosevelt created the Committee on Economic Security in 1934 to develop proposals addressing unemployment, old age, dependency, and health insecurity.

Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins chaired the committee, and Edwin Witte served as executive director.

The committee recommended federal contributory old-age insurance alongside state-administered assistance and unemployment programs.

Social Security Act of 1935

President Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935.

Title II established federal old-age insurance for qualifying workers, with retirement benefits planned for workers age 65 or older.

The broader Act also created federal-state unemployment compensation, old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, maternal and child welfare assistance, and public-health provisions.

The original insurance system did not yet provide monthly spouse, child, survivor, or disability benefits.

Original Coverage Exclusions

The original program excluded many agricultural workers, domestic workers, self-employed people, government employees, nonprofit employees, railroad workers, and other occupations.

Agricultural and domestic exclusions disproportionately affected Black workers and women, especially in the South.

Later amendments extended coverage to most previously excluded occupations, although some state and local government positions remain outside Social Security.

1939 Amendments

The 1939 amendments transformed old-age insurance into a family social-insurance program.

Congress added benefits for wives, minor children, widows, surviving children, and dependent parents.

Monthly benefits began in January 1940, and the formula shifted toward average monthly earnings and family protection.

First Monthly Beneficiary

Ida May Fuller of Vermont received the first recurring monthly Social Security retirement check in January 1940.

Her initial monthly benefit was $22.54.

Her case became a symbol of pooled social insurance rather than a personal account balance.

1950 Coverage and Benefit Expansion

The 1950 amendments substantially increased benefits and extended coverage to millions of additional workers.

Coverage expanded among agricultural workers, domestic workers, self-employed people, nonprofit employees, and some state and local government employees.

Congress continued expanding coverage and benefits throughout the 1950s and 1960s.

Development of Disability Insurance

The 1954 amendments created a disability freeze preventing severe disability from reducing retirement or survivor records.

The 1956 amendments added cash benefits for disabled workers ages 50 through 64 and disabled adult children.

The 1958 amendments added dependent benefits, and in 1960 Congress removed the age-50 restriction.

Early Retirement

Congress allowed women to claim reduced retirement benefits at age 62 beginning in 1956.

Men received the same age-62 option in 1961.

Early claiming permanently reduces the worker’s monthly amount to reflect a longer expected payment period.

Medicare Amendments of 1965

The 1965 Social Security amendments created Medicare and Medicaid.

Social Security records and claims operations became central to Medicare enrollment and premium collection.

Medicare eligibility age generally remained 65 even as Social Security full retirement age later increased.

Automatic Cost-of-Living Adjustments

Before automatic indexing, Congress enacted periodic benefit increases.

The 1972 amendments established automatic cost-of-living adjustments and wage indexing.

Automatic COLAs began in 1975.

A flaw in the original indexing method was corrected by 1977 amendments.

Social Security Amendments of 1983

Congress enacted major bipartisan financing legislation after the program approached an immediate cash crisis.

The amendments accelerated payroll-tax changes, delayed part of the 1983 COLA, covered newly hired federal employees, and subjected some benefits to income tax.

They gradually increased full retirement age from 65 to 67.

Social Security Fairness Act of 2025

The Social Security Fairness Act was signed on January 5, 2025.

It repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset.

Repeal applied to benefits payable after December 2023, producing retroactive payments and higher ongoing benefits for qualifying public employees and others.

The repeal increased program costs and contributed to a somewhat earlier projected OASI depletion date.

Covered Employment

Most wage and salary employment in the United States is covered by Social Security.

Employers report covered wages and withhold employee payroll taxes. The worker’s earnings record determines future insured status and benefit calculations.

Coverage rules differ for domestic work, agricultural work, religious employment, foreign employment, military service, nonprofit work, and state or local government positions.

Self-Employment

Self-employed workers report net earnings on their federal income-tax returns and generally pay both the employee and employer shares through the Self-Employment Contributions Act.

In 2026, the OASDI self-employment rate is 12.4 percent on covered net earnings up to $184,500.

Special computation rules apply to low net earnings, ministers, farm operators, partners, and certain foreign business activity.

Noncovered Employment

Some state and local government employees remain outside Social Security through qualifying public retirement systems.

Certain railroad workers participate in Railroad Retirement rather than ordinary OASDI for the same service.

Some religious workers, students employed by their schools, election workers, foreign government employees, and other categories can be excluded or covered under special rules.

Noncovered employment does not generate Social Security credits unless another coverage provision applies.

FICA and SECA Taxes

The Federal Insurance Contributions Act governs payroll withholding for employees and employers.

The Self-Employment Contributions Act governs taxes on net self-employment income.

In 2026, employees and employers each pay 6.2 percent for OASDI, while self-employed workers pay 12.4 percent.

Medicare payroll taxes are separate and apply to a broader earnings base without the OASDI taxable maximum.

Contribution and Benefit Base

Social Security taxes and benefit computations apply only to covered earnings up to the annual contribution and benefit base.

The 2026 taxable maximum is $184,500.

Earnings above the maximum do not produce additional OASDI tax or enter the worker’s Social Security benefit computation for that year.

The maximum changes with national average wage growth.

Social Security Work Credits

Credits determine whether a worker is insured for retirement, disability, and survivor protection.

In 2026, one credit is earned for each $1,890 in covered earnings.

A worker earns the maximum four credits after $7,560 in annual covered earnings, regardless of when during the year the earnings occur.

Additional credits beyond the insured-status requirement do not directly increase benefit size.

Fully Insured Status

Most workers need forty credits to qualify for retirement benefits.

A worker can become fully insured with fewer credits when dying or reaching retirement age at a younger point under statutory formulas.

Survivor and disability claims can use currently insured or disability-insured standards requiring fewer and more recent credits.

Earnings Record

SSA maintains a lifetime record of wages and self-employment income reported under each Social Security number.

Missing or incorrect earnings can reduce insured status and monthly benefits.

Workers should review their personal Social Security Statement and preserve wage, tax, military, and self-employment records.

Time limits can restrict correction of older earnings records, although statutory exceptions apply.

Average Indexed Monthly Earnings

Retirement benefits generally begin with the worker’s highest thirty-five years of covered earnings.

Earlier earnings are indexed to national wage growth through the year the worker reaches age 60.

SSA selects the highest years, totals them, and converts the amount into average indexed monthly earnings, or AIME.

Fewer than thirty-five years of covered earnings produces zero years in the average.

Primary Insurance Amount

The primary insurance amount, or PIA, is the worker’s basic monthly benefit before early-retirement reduction, delayed-retirement credits, family adjustments, and final rounding.

For workers first eligible in 2026, the formula equals 90 percent of the first $1,286 of AIME, 32 percent from $1,286 through $7,749, and 15 percent above $7,749.

The formula’s progressive structure replaces a larger share of career-average earnings for low-paid workers.

Bend Points

Bend points divide AIME into formula segments.

They are indexed to national average wages and fixed according to the year the worker first becomes eligible through retirement age, disability, or death.

Later wage growth does not substitute a new set of bend points after the eligibility year, although COLAs can increase the resulting PIA.

Maximum Retirement Benefit

The maximum benefit depends on the worker’s historical maximum-taxable earnings and claiming age.

In 2026, the maximum for a worker retiring at full retirement age is $4,152 per month.

Claiming at 62 produces a lower maximum, while delaying to 70 produces a higher maximum.

Few workers receive the maximum because it requires many years of earnings at or above the taxable ceiling.

Retirement Benefit Eligibility

A fully insured worker can ordinarily begin retirement benefits at age 62.

The worker does not have to stop working, although the earnings test can temporarily withhold benefits before full retirement age.

Applying for retirement does not automatically enroll every person in Medicare, and delaying retirement benefits does not necessarily justify delaying Medicare enrollment.

Full Retirement Age

Full retirement age is the age at which the worker can receive the PIA without early-retirement reduction or delayed-retirement increase.

  • Age 65 for people born in 1937 or earlier.
  • Gradually increasing from 65 and two months through 65 and ten months for 1938–1942 births.
  • Age 66 for 1943–1954 births.
  • Gradually increasing from 66 and two months through 66 and ten months for 1955–1959 births.
  • Age 67 for people born in 1960 or later.

Early Retirement Reduction

Benefits claimed before full retirement age are permanently reduced.

The reduction is five-ninths of one percent for each of the first thirty-six months early and five-twelfths of one percent for each additional month.

A worker with full retirement age 67 receives 70 percent of the PIA when claiming at age 62.

Delayed Retirement Credits

Benefits increase when a worker delays claiming beyond full retirement age.

Workers born in 1943 or later generally receive an eight-percent annual increase, calculated monthly, through age 70.

A person born in 1960 or later who delays from age 67 to 70 can receive approximately 124 percent of the PIA.

No additional delayed-retirement credit is earned after age 70.

Claiming-Age Decision

The best claiming age depends on health, longevity, employment, savings, marital status, survivor protection, taxes, insurance, debt, and household needs.

Early claiming provides payments sooner but lowers the monthly amount for life.

Delaying increases longevity insurance and can raise a surviving spouse’s later benefit.

Social Security does not recommend one universal claiming age.

Retirement Earnings Test

The retirement earnings test can withhold benefits when a beneficiary works before full retirement age.

In 2026, SSA withholds one dollar for every two dollars of earnings above $24,480 for a beneficiary under full retirement age throughout the year.

In the year full retirement age is reached, SSA withholds one dollar for every three dollars above $65,160, counting only earnings before the full-retirement-age month.

No earnings test applies beginning with the month full retirement age is reached.

Recomputation after Withheld Benefits

Benefits withheld under the earnings test are not treated as a simple permanent forfeiture.

At full retirement age, SSA adjusts the reduction factor to account for months in which all or part of the benefit was withheld.

Additional covered earnings can also replace lower years in the thirty-five-year computation and increase future benefits.

Special Monthly Earnings Rule

A special first-year rule can pay benefits for months a new retiree is considered retired even when annual earnings exceed the ordinary limit.

The rule is important for people who retire after earning substantial wages earlier in the calendar year.

Self-employment services and wage earnings are evaluated separately under the rule.

Spouse Benefits

A spouse can qualify at age 62 or while caring for the worker’s qualifying child.

At full retirement age, the spouse benefit can equal as much as 50 percent of the worker’s PIA.

Claiming before full retirement age reduces the spouse amount.

The worker generally must be entitled to retirement or disability benefits before the spouse can receive payment.

Dual Entitlement

A person eligible for a retirement benefit on personal earnings and a larger spouse benefit does not ordinarily receive the full amount of both.

SSA pays the worker benefit first and adds only enough spouse benefit to reach the higher combined amount.

Deemed-filing rules generally require people applying for one retirement or spouse benefit to apply for both when eligible, subject to limited exceptions.

Divorced-Spouse Benefits

A divorced person can qualify on a former spouse’s record when the marriage lasted at least ten years and other age, marital-status, and eligibility requirements are met.

If the divorce has lasted at least two years, an independently entitled divorced spouse can sometimes receive benefits even when the worker has not yet applied.

Payments to a divorced spouse do not reduce the worker’s benefit or amounts payable to a current spouse and family.

Benefits for Children

A child can qualify on a retired or disabled parent’s record when unmarried and under age 18, age 18–19 and a full-time elementary or secondary student, or disabled before age 22.

Eligible biological, adopted, stepchildren, grandchildren, and dependent grandchildren can qualify under statutory dependency rules.

A child benefit is generally as much as 50 percent of the living parent’s PIA.

Disabled Adult Child Benefits

An unmarried adult whose disability began before age 22 can qualify on a parent’s retirement, disability, or deceased-worker record.

The beneficiary need not have an independent work record.

The amount can be as much as 50 percent of the parent’s PIA while the parent is living and 75 percent after the parent’s death, subject to family maximums.

Marriage and work can affect continuing eligibility under special rules.

Maximum Family Benefit

The family maximum limits total benefits payable on one worker’s record.

Retirement and survivor family maximums generally range from about 150 to 180 percent of the worker’s full benefit.

The worker’s own benefit is ordinarily paid first; spouse and child amounts are proportionally reduced when necessary.

Divorced-spouse benefits generally do not count toward the family maximum.

Survivor Benefits

Survivor insurance pays eligible spouses, divorced spouses, children, and dependent parents after an insured worker dies.

A worker can provide survivor protection with fewer than forty credits when dying young under currently insured rules.

Survivor benefits protect families from the loss of earnings, caregiving, and retirement income.

Widow and Widower Benefits

A surviving spouse can generally claim as early as age 60, or age 50 when disabled under qualifying rules.

The age-60 rate begins at 71.5 percent of the deceased worker’s benefit and rises to as much as 100 percent at survivor full retirement age.

A surviving spouse caring for the deceased worker’s child under age 16 or disabled can qualify at any age.

Surviving Divorced Spouse

A surviving divorced spouse can qualify when the marriage lasted at least ten years and age, remarriage, and other requirements are met.

Remarriage after age 60, or after age 50 for a disabled surviving spouse, generally does not bar eligibility.

A former spouse’s survivor payment does not reduce benefits for other survivors.

Surviving Children

Eligible children generally receive up to 75 percent of the deceased parent’s PIA.

Family-maximum rules can reduce individual payments when several survivors qualify.

Benefits usually end at age 18, or age 19 for a full-time elementary or secondary student, unless disability began before age 22.

Dependent Parent Benefits

A dependent parent age 62 or older can qualify on a deceased worker’s record when the worker provided at least one-half of the parent’s support and other requirements are met.

One qualifying parent can generally receive 82.5 percent of the PIA, while two qualifying parents can each receive 75 percent, subject to the family maximum.

Lump-Sum Death Payment

Social Security can pay a one-time $255 death benefit to an eligible surviving spouse or, in some cases, an eligible child.

The payment is separate from monthly survivor benefits.

The amount has remained unchanged for decades and covers only a small share of modern funeral expenses.

Switching between Survivor and Retirement Benefits

Survivor benefits are not subject to the same deemed-filing rule as spouse benefits.

An eligible person can sometimes claim a survivor benefit first and switch to a larger personal retirement benefit at age 70.

Another person may claim personal retirement first and later switch to a larger survivor benefit.

Claiming strategy depends on age, earnings, health, and relative benefit amounts.

Social Security Disability Insurance

SSDI pays insured workers who meet Social Security’s strict definition of disability.

It is an earned insurance benefit rather than a means-tested payment.

Savings, property, and a spouse’s income do not ordinarily bar SSDI, although work and other public disability payments can affect benefits.

Federal Definition of Disability

Social Security generally pays only for total, long-term disability.

The claimant must be unable to perform substantial gainful activity because of a medically determinable impairment.

The impairment must have lasted or be expected to last at least twelve continuous months or result in death.

Partial and short-term disability do not qualify by themselves.

Disability Insured Status

SSDI requires both sufficient lifetime covered work and sufficiently recent work.

The ordinary rule for many adults is forty credits, including twenty earned during the ten years ending with disability onset.

Younger workers can qualify with fewer credits.

A person can lose disability-insured status after leaving covered work even though retirement-insured status remains.

Five-Step Disability Evaluation

  1. Current work: Is the claimant performing substantial gainful activity?
  2. Severity: Does the impairment significantly limit basic work activity for the required duration?
  3. Medical listings: Does the impairment meet or medically equal a listed condition?
  4. Past work: Can the claimant perform past relevant work?
  5. Other work: Can the claimant adjust to other work considering residual capacity, age, education, and experience?

Substantial Gainful Activity

SSA uses earnings guidelines to evaluate whether work is substantial and gainful.

In 2026, the ordinary nonblind SGA amount is $1,690 per month and the statutory-blind amount is $2,830.

Self-employment, subsidies, impairment-related expenses, unsuccessful work attempts, and special conditions can alter the evaluation.

Disability Waiting Period and Retroactivity

SSDI ordinarily has a five-full-month waiting period after the established disability onset date.

Benefits can be paid for as many as twelve months before the application date when all requirements are met.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is exempt from the ordinary five-month cash-benefit waiting period under current law.

Medicare after Disability Entitlement

Most SSDI beneficiaries become entitled to Medicare after twenty-four months of disability-benefit entitlement.

ALS beneficiaries receive Medicare beginning with disability-benefit entitlement without the ordinary twenty-four-month wait.

End-stage renal disease uses separate Medicare eligibility rules.

Continuing Disability Reviews

SSA periodically reviews whether a beneficiary remains medically disabled.

Review frequency depends on the expected likelihood of medical improvement.

Benefits can cease after medical improvement related to ability to work, failure to cooperate, work activity, or another statutory event.

Beneficiaries have appeal rights and can sometimes elect benefit continuation during review.

Disability Work Incentives

SSDI rules encourage attempts to return to work without immediate loss of all benefits.

The trial work period permits nine service months within a rolling sixty-month period.

In 2026, a month generally counts when earnings exceed $1,210 or self-employment activity meets applicable standards.

Extended eligibility, expedited reinstatement, impairment-related work expenses, subsidies, and continued Medicare can also apply.

Workers’ Compensation and Public Disability Offset

SSDI can be reduced when combined with workers’ compensation or certain public disability benefits exceeding a statutory percentage of prior earnings.

State reverse-offset laws and lump-sum settlements can affect the calculation.

Private disability insurance and veterans’ disability compensation generally do not trigger this offset.

Cost-of-Living Adjustment Calculation

The annual COLA is based on the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers, or CPI-W.

SSA compares the average index for the third quarter of the current measuring year with the third-quarter average from the last year a COLA became effective.

If the measured increase is positive, benefits rise by that percentage after statutory rounding.

If there is no increase, benefits do not receive a negative COLA.

2026 Cost-of-Living Adjustment

Social Security benefits increased 2.8 percent for payments beginning in January 2026.

The estimated average retired-worker payment rose from $2,015 to $2,071.

The estimated average disabled-worker payment rose from $1,586 to $1,630.

Medicare premiums, tax withholding, garnishment, or recovery can cause the net bank deposit to increase by less than the gross COLA.

Wage Indexing

National average wage growth affects the taxable maximum, credit amount, benefit-formula bend points, family maximum, and indexed earnings.

Wage indexing operates differently from the CPI-based COLA.

Wage indexing generally applies before initial eligibility, while COLAs protect benefits after eligibility.

Federal Income Taxation of Benefits

Social Security benefits can be included in federal taxable income when combined income exceeds statutory thresholds.

Combined income generally includes adjusted gross income, tax-exempt interest, and one-half of Social Security benefits.

As much as 50 percent of benefits can be taxable above $25,000 for an individual or $32,000 for a married couple filing jointly.

As much as 85 percent can be taxable above $34,000 for an individual or $44,000 for a joint return.

“Eighty-five percent taxable” does not mean an 85-percent tax rate or that 85 percent is withheld automatically.

Temporary Enhanced Deduction for Seniors

Federal tax law effective for tax years 2025 through 2028 provides an additional deduction of as much as $6,000 for a qualifying taxpayer age 65 or older.

A married couple can receive as much as $12,000 when both spouses qualify.

The deduction phases out above modified adjusted gross income of $75,000 for an individual and $150,000 for a joint return.

The deduction can eliminate federal income tax for many Social Security beneficiaries, but it did not repeal the statutory calculation that includes up to 85 percent of benefits in income.

State Taxation

State treatment of Social Security benefits varies.

Most states do not tax Social Security benefits, while some apply income limits, deductions, or partial taxation.

State rules can change independently of federal benefit-taxation law.

Voluntary Federal Tax Withholding

A beneficiary can request federal income-tax withholding from monthly Social Security payments.

Available withholding rates are 7, 10, 12, or 22 percent.

Beneficiaries can instead make estimated tax payments when appropriate.

Social Security versus SSI

OASDI is based on insured work and covered earnings.

SSI is based on age, blindness, or disability together with limited income and resources.

SSI uses a federal payment standard and resource limits rather than the worker’s PIA.

A person can receive both SSDI or retirement benefits and SSI when the Social Security payment and other resources are sufficiently low.

Social Security versus Medicare

Social Security pays cash benefits; Medicare pays or arranges health-insurance coverage.

Medicare Part A is financed principally through Hospital Insurance payroll taxes, while Parts B and D rely heavily on premiums and general revenues.

Claiming retirement before 65 does not begin Medicare early, and delaying Social Security after 65 can create a need to enroll in Medicare separately.

Relationship to Railroad Retirement

Railroad Retirement provides retirement, survivor, and disability benefits for qualifying railroad service.

The Railroad Retirement Board and SSA coordinate records and a statutory financial interchange.

A worker with railroad and Social Security employment can receive coordinated or separate components under specialised rules.

Public Pensions and Noncovered Work

Some public employees earn pensions through jobs that do not withhold Social Security taxes.

Before 2025, WEP could reduce a worker’s own Social Security benefit and GPO could reduce spouse or survivor benefits.

The Social Security Fairness Act repealed both provisions effective for benefits payable after December 2023.

Repeal does not create Social Security credits for noncovered work; a worker must still qualify through covered employment.

International Social Security Agreements

Totalization agreements coordinate Social Security coverage and benefits between the United States and partner countries.

They can prevent dual payroll taxation and allow workers to combine coverage periods to meet minimum eligibility requirements.

Each country pays its own proportional benefit under its law.

Payments outside the United States

Many United States citizens and qualifying noncitizens can receive Social Security payments while living abroad.

Citizenship, country of residence, benefit type, treaty status, and duration outside the United States affect payment.

SSI generally cannot be paid during extended residence outside the United States.

Noncitizens

Lawfully present noncitizens can earn Social Security coverage and receive benefits when they satisfy work, status, residency, and payment rules.

Unauthorized work, invalid identifying information, removal, residence abroad, and bilateral agreements can complicate payment.

Payroll contributions alone do not override every immigration or payment restriction.

Applying for Retirement Benefits

Retirement applications can generally be submitted online, by telephone, or through a Social Security office.

An applicant can usually file as many as four months before the desired benefit-start month.

SSA verifies age, work history, citizenship or lawful status, marriage, military service, and other relevant information.

The beneficiary chooses the start month subject to age and retroactivity rules.

Applying for Disability Benefits

A disability claimant provides medical conditions, treatment sources, medications, work history, education, daily-function information, and supporting records.

SSA determines nonmedical insured status and forwards medical development to a state Disability Determination Services agency.

Medical sources, consultative examiners, vocational evidence, and SSA adjudicators contribute to the determination.

Severe illnesses may receive expedited processing through Compassionate Allowances or Quick Disability Determination.

Applying for Survivor Benefits

Survivor claims are generally initiated by telephone or through a Social Security office rather than a complete online survivor application.

Funeral homes frequently report deaths, but survivors should contact SSA promptly to discuss monthly and lump-sum benefits.

Required evidence can include death, marriage, divorce, birth, dependency, disability, and the deceased worker’s identity.

Personal Social Security Account

A personal online account allows users to review earnings, estimate benefits, request replacement documents, manage direct deposit, obtain tax forms, and monitor claims.

Identity verification and account security are important because Social Security records can be targeted for fraud.

People should use official government systems rather than commercial websites imitating SSA.

Payment Schedule and Direct Deposit

Social Security benefits are generally paid one month after the month for which they are due.

Payment dates depend on birth date, benefit history, and whether the beneficiary also receives SSI.

Federal payments are ordinarily made through direct deposit or an approved electronic payment card.

Representative Payees

SSA appoints a representative payee when a beneficiary cannot manage or direct the management of benefits.

Payees can be family members, friends, guardians, public agencies, financial organisations, or qualified service organisations.

The payee must use funds for the beneficiary’s current needs, conserve remaining money, maintain records, and report changes.

Appointment as representative payee does not by itself create general legal guardianship.

Overpayments

Overpayments arise when SSA pays more than federal law permits.

Causes include unreported work, incorrect earnings estimates, death, marriage, custody, incarceration, medical improvement, duplicate payments, or agency error.

SSA can recover through withholding, repayment, tax-refund offset, or other collection.

A beneficiary can dispute the fact or amount of the debt and request waiver when without fault and recovery would defeat program purposes or be inequitable.

Underpayments

SSA pays underpayments when benefits were withheld, delayed, or calculated below the lawful amount.

Underpayments can result from corrected earnings, appeal decisions, WEP or GPO repeal, delayed records, or posthumous adjustments.

When a beneficiary dies, unpaid amounts may be distributed to eligible family members or the estate under a statutory priority order.

Administrative Appeals

A claimant can challenge an initial determination through reconsideration, an administrative-law-judge hearing, Appeals Council review, and federal court.

Deadlines generally run from receipt of the notice.

Disability claims often involve new medical evidence and testimony at the hearing stage.

Some determinations use specialised review procedures, including continuing-disability and overpayment processes.

Federal Court Review

After exhausting administrative remedies, a claimant can seek judicial review in federal district court.

Courts review whether SSA applied the correct law and whether findings are supported under the governing evidentiary standard.

Courts can affirm, reverse, or remand for further proceedings.

Program Integrity

SSA uses earnings matching, death records, incarceration data, medical reviews, identity verification, employer reports, and data exchanges to protect program funds.

The agency’s Office of Inspector General investigates fraud, identity theft, representative-payee misuse, false disability claims, and employee misconduct.

Program integrity must be balanced with due process, privacy, accessibility, and timely payment.

Identity Theft and Benefit Fraud

Criminals can redirect benefits, create false accounts, impersonate SSA, steal Social Security numbers, or submit false wage and disability information.

SSA generally does not threaten immediate arrest or demand payment through gift cards, cryptocurrency, or cash courier.

Beneficiaries should protect login credentials and verify communications through official channels.

Administrative Cost

Social Security operates at comparatively low administrative cost relative to total benefit payments.

In 2025, administration cost approximately $7 billion, or about 0.4 percent of total expenditures.

Low administrative spending can coexist with service problems when staffing, technology, field-office access, disability backlogs, and telephone capacity are inadequate.

Administrative Structure

SSA is an independent federal agency headed by a commissioner.

Field offices, teleservice centres, processing centres, hearing offices, and online systems handle public claims and records.

State Disability Determination Services make initial medical disability decisions under federal standards and federal financing.

Treasury disburses benefits and holds trust-fund securities, while the IRS collects most payroll taxes.

Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund

The OASI Trust Fund receives taxes and other income allocated for retirement and survivor benefits.

It pays benefits and associated administrative costs.

OASI is legally separate from the Disability Insurance Trust Fund.

Congress can change allocation rates between the funds but cannot administratively treat them as one account without statutory authority.

Disability Insurance Trust Fund

The DI Trust Fund finances disabled-worker and related family benefits.

Disability incidence, allowance rates, recovery, mortality, workforce trends, and tax allocation affect its finances.

The 2026 Trustees project DI reserves to remain positive throughout the seventy-five-year projection period.

Trust-Fund Securities

Social Security reserves are invested in interest-bearing United States Treasury securities backed by the federal government.

Trust-fund assets represent legal claims on Treasury rather than cash stored in a vault or private stocks and bonds.

Treasury uses incoming funds within the unified federal financial system and later redeems securities to pay benefits when program cost exceeds current income.

Sources of Program Income

OASDI receives payroll and self-employment taxes, federal income taxes on benefits, interest on trust-fund securities, reimbursements, and other small transfers.

In 2025, combined income included approximately $1.32 trillion in net payroll contributions, $58 billion from taxation of benefits, and $69 billion in interest.

Payroll taxes remain the dominant recurring revenue source.

Pay-As-You-Go and Reserves

Current tax income finances current beneficiaries, with surpluses added to reserves and deficits financed by redeeming trust-fund securities.

Social Security is therefore primarily pay-as-you-go with a substantial reserve component.

Workers do not own individually earmarked shares of the trust funds.

Calendar Year 2025 Finances

Combined OASI and DI income was approximately $1.45 trillion.

Combined expenditures were approximately $1.61 trillion.

Trust-fund reserves declined by approximately $160 billion to $2.56 trillion.

Social Security paid about $1.60 trillion in benefits to approximately seventy million beneficiaries at year-end.

Current Solvency Status

Social Security is paying full scheduled benefits in 2026.

Current tax income and trust-fund reserves are sufficient for scheduled payments during the near term.

Reserve depletion is a future financing problem, not evidence that the program currently lacks money or has ceased operation.

2026 Trustees’ Projection

The OASI Trust Fund is projected to deplete reserves in the fourth quarter of 2032.

At that point, continuing OASI income would equal approximately 78 percent of scheduled benefits.

If OASI and DI were considered together, combined reserves would deplete in the third quarter of 2034, with 83 percent payable.

DI alone is projected to remain adequately financed throughout the seventy-five-year projection period.

Meaning of Trust-Fund Depletion

Depletion does not mean payroll taxes stop or every benefit becomes zero.

Continuing tax revenue would finance a substantial majority of scheduled benefits.

Current law does not provide a clear administrative mechanism for paying full scheduled benefits after reserves are exhausted when income is insufficient.

Congress can change taxes, benefits, retirement ages, transfers, formulas, or other provisions before depletion.

Seventy-Five-Year Actuarial Deficit

The 2026 Trustees estimate a combined OASDI actuarial deficit of 4.42 percent of taxable payroll over 2026–2100.

The measure compares projected income and reserves with projected cost and a target ending reserve.

It is not the same as an annual federal budget deficit or the amount of trust-fund securities outstanding.

Policy Options for Solvency

Common proposals include:

  • raising or eliminating the taxable maximum;
  • increasing payroll-tax rates;
  • dedicating other tax revenue;
  • changing the benefit formula;
  • raising retirement ages;
  • changing COLA measurement;
  • reducing benefits for higher earners;
  • creating minimum-benefit increases;
  • crediting caregiving or low-wage years;
  • changing taxation of benefits;
  • investing reserves differently; and
  • combining revenue increases and benefit changes.

Demographic and Economic Pressures

Population aging, lower birth rates, longer retirements, wage distribution, immigration, disability trends, productivity, unemployment, and interest rates shape solvency.

The ratio of covered workers to beneficiaries has declined substantially since the early program.

Growth of earnings above the taxable maximum can weaken revenue relative to total national earnings.

Why “Bankrupt” Is Misleading

Social Security has dedicated taxes, legal trust funds, and continuing revenue.

The projected problem is insufficient income and reserves to pay every scheduled benefit after depletion, not commercial bankruptcy.

The term can obscure the difference between trust-fund solvency, federal borrowing, and the general budget.

Role in Retirement Security

Social Security provides inflation-adjusted lifetime income and is the principal retirement income source for many older households.

It protects against longevity risk, low lifetime earnings, disability before retirement, and loss of a spouse.

Because benefits continue for life and receive COLAs, they differ from savings that can be exhausted.

Benefits alone may not meet all housing, healthcare, food, caregiving, and long-term-care needs.

Poverty Reduction

Social Security substantially reduces poverty among older adults, widows, disabled workers, and children.

Its anti-poverty effect depends on benefit adequacy, household composition, other income, taxes, housing costs, and the poverty measure used.

Low earners receive a higher replacement rate, but short careers and uncovered work can still produce inadequate benefits.

Women and Social Security

Women rely heavily on Social Security because they live longer on average, are more likely to outlive spouses, and historically earned lower wages and accumulated smaller pensions.

Spouse and survivor benefits were originally designed around a male breadwinner and dependent wife.

Increased women’s employment has made retired-worker benefits more important, while caregiving interruptions continue to lower career-average earnings.

Widow benefits provide critical protection but can leave a household with only one monthly benefit after a spouse dies.

African American Workers and Families

Original agricultural and domestic exclusions denied or delayed coverage for many Black workers.

Later coverage expansion made Social Security an important source of retirement, survivor, disability, and child income for Black families.

Black workers face lower average lifetime earnings, higher disability incidence, lower private pension wealth, and shorter average life expectancy, shaping contributions and benefits.

Survivor and disability protections can be especially important where workers are less likely to reach or enjoy long retirements.

Latino Workers and Families

Social Security provides portable coverage across employers and states, an important feature for workers in agriculture, construction, hospitality, domestic services, and other mobile occupations.

Lower wages, interrupted work, immigration status, informal employment, language access, and missing earnings records can reduce eligibility or benefit amounts.

Large multigenerational families can receive retirement, disability, child, and survivor protection through one worker’s record.

Native American and Territorial Communities

Social Security applies nationally, including in Tribal and territorial communities, but access can be affected by remoteness, employment patterns, language, documentation, and limited field-office services.

Seasonal, informal, subsistence, and self-employment earnings may not produce accurate covered records without proper reporting.

Disability and survivor benefits can be especially significant where private pensions and financial assets are limited.

Low-Wage Workers

The progressive formula replaces a larger share of earnings for low-paid workers.

Low wages still produce lower dollar benefits, and workers with fewer than thirty-five years of earnings receive zero years in the average.

Early claiming caused by unemployment, disability, caregiving, or physically demanding work can permanently reduce benefits.

Caregivers

Social Security does not generally provide earnings credits for unpaid caregiving.

Years outside paid employment can lower AIME and worker benefits.

Spouse, divorced-spouse, survivor, and child-in-care benefits provide partial protection, but depend on another worker’s record and marital or family status.

Same-Sex Couples

Married same-sex couples can qualify for spouse and survivor benefits under the same federal standards applied to other married couples.

Historical inability to marry can affect duration-of-marriage requirements and earlier benefit access.

SSA applies federal law, state marital law, and court decisions to marriage and survivor claims.

Children and Family Income

Social Security is a major children’s insurance program because a parent’s disability, retirement, or death can trigger monthly payments.

Child benefits support food, housing, education, clothing, healthcare, and caregiving.

Benefits generally end after secondary school unless the child has a qualifying disability beginning before age 22.

Economic Stabilisation

Social Security sends regular federal payments to every state and most communities.

Benefits support consumer spending during recessions because payments continue when employment and investment income decline.

Automatic eligibility and national financing reduce dependence on varying state fiscal capacity.

Statutory Entitlement and Property Rights

Social Security benefits are statutory entitlements payable under current federal law.

In Flemming v. Nestor, the Supreme Court held that covered work and contributions do not create an accrued contractual property right preventing Congress from changing future benefit rules.

Claimants nevertheless possess due-process rights in benefit determinations and termination procedures.

Helvering v. Davis

In 1937, the Supreme Court upheld the federal old-age benefits tax structure.

The Court recognised broad congressional authority under the General Welfare Clause to address national economic insecurity.

The decision helped secure the constitutional foundation of Social Security.

Goldberg v. Kelly and Due Process

Although Goldberg v. Kelly concerned public assistance rather than OASDI, it influenced the modern law of due process in benefit termination.

Later Social Security cases distinguished disability-benefit procedures while recognising protected interests and administrative-review rights.

Program Achievements

  • Created national contributory retirement insurance.
  • Expanded protection to spouses, children, survivors, and disabled workers.
  • Reduced old-age poverty and dependence on local poor relief.
  • Provided portable benefits across covered employers and states.
  • Protected families after a worker’s death or disability.
  • Established inflation-indexed lifetime income.
  • Covered approximately 185 million workers paying taxes in 2025.
  • Paid approximately $1.60 trillion to roughly seventy million beneficiaries in 2025.
  • Operated at low administrative cost relative to total payments.
  • Extended coverage to occupations originally excluded.
  • Provided disability-related Medicare access.
  • Supported children through parent-based benefits.
  • Repealed WEP and GPO through the 2025 Fairness Act.
  • Created a national earnings and benefit record spanning working life.

Limitations and Failures

  • Original exclusions disproportionately harmed Black workers and women.
  • Low lifetime earnings produce low dollar benefits despite the progressive formula.
  • Unpaid caregiving years receive no general earnings credit.
  • Early claiming permanently reduces benefits.
  • The retirement earnings test is widely misunderstood.
  • Family maximums can reduce spouse and child payments.
  • The $255 death payment has lost most of its original value.
  • The disability standard excludes partial and short-term disability.
  • Disability decisions and appeals can take substantial time.
  • Most SSDI beneficiaries wait twenty-four months for Medicare.
  • Taxation thresholds are not indexed for inflation.
  • Some public employment remains outside coverage.
  • Benefit formulas and family rules are difficult to understand.
  • Service barriers affect people with disabilities, limited English, rural residence, or limited digital access.
  • Current financing is insufficient for full scheduled long-range benefits.

Major Controversies

Earned Right versus Welfare

Supporters emphasise contributions and insured status, while critics debate redistribution and federal responsibility.

Payroll Tax Regressivity

The tax applies at a flat rate only to earnings below the taxable maximum, while the progressive benefit formula offsets part of that structure.

Retirement Age

Raising retirement age improves financing but disproportionately affects workers with shorter life expectancy, physically demanding jobs, unemployment, or disability.

Trust-Fund Accounting

Debate frequently confuses Treasury securities, federal debt, trust-fund reserves, payroll taxes, and personal savings accounts.

Benefit Adequacy

Proposals to reduce growth conflict with concerns about poverty, private pension decline, and longer retirements.

Privatisation

Individual accounts could create investment ownership but would shift market risk and require financing of existing promised benefits.

Disability Adjudication

Policymakers debate standards, delays, work incentives, medical evidence, fraud control, and treatment of older workers.

WEP and GPO Repeal

Repeal increased benefits for affected public employees but also increased OASI costs and renewed debate about benefit-formula equity.

Documented Outcomes

Social Security became the principal income source for many retired households and substantially reduced poverty among older adults.

Survivor insurance provides continuing income after millions of worker deaths, while child benefits protect minors and disabled adult children.

SSDI provides insured income to workers with severe long-term disabilities and supports family members.

The program paid approximately $1.60 trillion in benefits during 2025.

Long-range financing remains unresolved despite current full payment of benefits.

Historical Significance

Social Security transformed old age from a primarily private and local responsibility into a national social-insurance obligation.

The 1939 amendments made family protection central to the program.

Disability insurance extended the system from old age and death to severe loss of work capacity.

Automatic indexing made the benefit system responsive to wage growth before retirement and inflation afterward.

The program remains one of the largest and most politically durable institutions created by the New Deal.

Research Cautions

  • Social Security is not SSI. OASDI is contributory insurance; SSI is means-tested assistance.
  • Social Security is not Medicare. Cash and health benefits use distinct financing and eligibility rules.
  • Credits determine eligibility, not benefit amount. Earnings history determines the monthly amount.
  • The benefit is not an individual account balance. Taxes finance pooled social insurance.
  • Full retirement age is not Medicare age. Medicare generally begins at 65; retirement FRA can be 67.
  • Early retirement reduction is generally permanent. Earnings-test withholding is different and can later be recomputed.
  • Spouse benefits are not added fully to personal retirement. Dual-entitlement rules generally pay the higher combined amount.
  • Trust-fund depletion is not zero benefits. Continuing taxes would finance most scheduled payments.
  • OASI and DI are legally separate. Combined projections are analytical unless Congress changes the law.
  • Up to 85 percent taxable is not an 85-percent tax rate.
  • The enhanced senior deduction did not repeal benefit taxation.
  • WEP and GPO were repealed, but noncovered work still does not earn credits.
  • Annual amounts change. Verify the year for COLAs, credits, taxable maximums, earnings tests, and disability thresholds.

Definitions

Average indexed monthly earnings
The monthly average derived from a worker’s highest indexed covered earnings years, generally thirty-five years for retirement.
Beneficiary
A person entitled to a monthly Social Security payment.
Covered earnings
Wages or self-employment income subject to Social Security coverage and reporting.
Credit
The basic unit used to determine insured status; one credit requires $1,890 in covered earnings in 2026.
Delayed retirement credit
An increase for delaying retirement benefits after full retirement age and before age 70.
Disability Insurance
The OASDI component paying insured disabled workers and eligible family members.
Full retirement age
The age at which the worker’s retirement benefit equals the PIA without early reduction or delayed credit.
Insured status
The work-credit status required for retirement, disability, or survivor benefits.
Old-Age and Survivors Insurance
The trust-fund program paying retirement, spouse, child, and survivor benefits.
Primary insurance amount
The worker’s basic monthly benefit calculated from AIME before claiming-age adjustments.
Representative payee
A person or organisation appointed to manage benefits for someone unable to do so.
Social Security Statement
A personal record showing reported earnings, estimated benefits, and insured status.
Substantial gainful activity
A level of work activity and earnings used in disability eligibility.
Taxable maximum
The annual amount of covered earnings subject to OASDI tax and included in the benefit record.
Trust-fund depletion
The point when reserves are exhausted and continuing program income is insufficient for full scheduled benefits.

Summary

Social Security is the federal Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance program created by the Social Security Act of 1935.

It pays retirement, spouse, child, survivor, and disability benefits based principally on covered work and earnings.

Employees and employers each pay 6.2 percent of covered wages, while self-employed workers pay 12.4 percent.

The 2026 taxable maximum is $184,500, and one work credit requires $1,890 in covered earnings.

Retirement eligibility generally requires forty credits. Benefits can begin at 62, full retirement age reaches 67 for people born in 1960 or later, and delayed-retirement credits end at 70.

The benefit formula uses indexed earnings and progressive 90-, 32-, and 15-percent factors. The 2026 bend points are $1,286 and $7,749.

Spouses can receive as much as half of the worker’s PIA, surviving spouses as much as the full amount, children as much as half on a living parent’s record, and surviving children as much as 75 percent.

SSDI requires recent covered work and a severe impairment preventing substantial gainful activity for at least twelve months or expected to result in death.

The 2026 COLA is 2.8 percent. The estimated average retired-worker benefit is $2,071, and the average disabled-worker benefit is $1,630.

The Social Security Fairness Act repealed WEP and GPO for benefits payable after December 2023.

Social Security is separate from SSI and Medicare, although the programs interact.

Social Security paid approximately $1.60 trillion to roughly seventy million beneficiaries in 2025 and held approximately $2.56 trillion in combined reserves at year-end.

The 2026 Trustees project OASI depletion in late 2032 and analytical combined depletion in 2034. Continuing revenue would cover 78 percent of OASI scheduled benefits and 83 percent of combined scheduled benefits at those dates.

The program is not currently bankrupt or unable to pay benefits, but Congress must change financing or scheduled benefits to avoid automatic shortfalls after reserve depletion.

Social Security’s historical importance lies in nationalising retirement and family social insurance, reducing poverty, protecting workers against disability and death, and creating a durable foundation of American economic security.
