Maternal and Child Health Services Program

The federal-state public-health partnership established under Title V of the Social Security Act to improve the health of mothers, infants, children, adolescents, and children and youth with special health care needs.

What Is the Maternal and Child Health Services Program?

The Maternal and Child Health Services Program is the federal-state partnership authorised by Title V of the Social Security Act. Its principal modern component is the Maternal and Child Health Services Block Grant, which provides formula funding to states and jurisdictions for maternal, infant, child, adolescent, and special-health-care-needs priorities.

Title V is not an insurance programme and does not create one nationally uniform benefit package. States use the block grant to strengthen public-health systems, coordinate services, collect and analyse data, support specialised programmes, improve quality, address gaps, and provide selected direct services when other payment sources are unavailable.

The programme can support prenatal and postpartum initiatives, infant-mortality prevention, newborn screening, injury prevention, adolescent health, oral health, nutrition, family support, care coordination, medical homes, disability services, workforce development, and many other activities.

Title V’s role differs by state. One jurisdiction may emphasise maternal mortality and rural maternity access; another may prioritise infant mortality, adolescent mental health, childhood injury, children with medical complexity, developmental screening, or transition to adult care.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the Maternal and Child Health Services Program as the Title V Maternal and Child Health Services Block Grant and associated federal activities under Sections 501 through 509 of the Social Security Act.

It covers the programme’s historical origins, 1981 restructuring, federal-state financing, state applications, population groups, service levels, required set-asides, Medicaid coordination, performance measurement, civil-rights obligations, direct and systems services, health-equity significance, limitations, and policy context through August 2, 2026.

It does not determine whether an individual qualifies for a particular state service. Eligibility, referral procedures, service availability, and direct-care policies differ by state, locality, programme component, and funding source.

Historical Foundations Before 1935

Maternal and child-health policy developed from Progressive Era public-health reform, the settlement-house movement, visiting-nurse programmes, infant-welfare campaigns, vital-statistics development, school health, child-labour reform, and growing recognition that maternal and infant deaths were preventable public problems.

Congress established the United States Children’s Bureau in 1912. The bureau investigated infant mortality, child labour, maternal health, child welfare, and conditions affecting children and families.

The Sheppard-Towner Maternity and Infancy Protection Act of 1921 provided federal matching grants for state maternal and infant-health work. States used funds for prenatal education, public-health nursing, infant-welfare clinics, home visits, and professional training.

Sheppard-Towner expired in 1929 amid disputes over federalism, professional authority, cost, and the federal role in health. Its administrative experience nevertheless established a precedent for federal-state maternal and child-health grants.

Creation Under the Social Security Act of 1935

President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935. Title V authorised grants to states for maternal and child-health services, services for children with serious physical conditions then described in historical statutory language as “crippled children,” and child-welfare services.

The maternal and child-health provisions supported state public-health agencies, local health units, prenatal and child-health clinics, public-health nursing, professional training, rural services, and care for children requiring specialised treatment.

Title V was established alongside old-age assistance, unemployment compensation, Aid to Dependent Children, and other foundational social programmes.

Unlike retirement insurance, Title V operated through grants to states. This structure made it one of the earliest permanent federal-state public-health partnerships.

Early Programme Development

States used Title V to build maternal and child-health divisions, expand county and local health services, train nurses and physicians, develop specialty clinics, and extend care into rural communities.

The programme supported children with congenital conditions, orthopaedic disabilities, heart disease, cleft palate, hearing loss, and other conditions requiring specialised services that many families could not afford.

World War II increased attention to maternal health, nutrition, workforce mobilisation, and infant care. The Emergency Maternity and Infant Care Program provided maternity and infant services for wives and infants of lower-ranking service members.

Postwar developments included newborn screening, regional specialty systems, hospital growth, disability services, and expanded public-health research.

Great Society and Medicaid Era

During the 1960s, Congress expanded maternal and child-health project grants, training, research, maternity and infant-care projects, services for children and youth, and specialised systems.

Medicare and Medicaid were created in 1965. Medicaid became a major payer for low-income pregnant women and children, while Title V retained a systems, coordination, gap-filling, and public-health role.

Federal law later required coordination between Title V and Medicaid’s Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment benefit.

The era also strengthened community health centres, family planning, nutrition programmes, disability policy, and civil-rights enforcement, creating a broader network within which Title V operated.

Conversion to a Block Grant in 1981

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 substantially revised Title V. It consolidated several categorical maternal and child-health programmes and replaced the earlier structure with the Maternal and Child Health Services Block Grant.

The change reflected the Reagan administration’s broader policy of reducing categorical federal control, consolidating grants, lowering federal expenditure, and increasing state discretion.

States gained greater authority to select priorities and organise services, but federal law retained purposes, application requirements, spending floors, matching, reporting, nondiscrimination, and financial accountability.

The reform divided funding between state formula allotments and federal activities addressing regional and national priorities, research, training, genetic services, systems development, and emerging issues.

Federal-State Structure

Title V operates through cooperative federalism. Congress establishes the programme and appropriates funding. HHS and HRSA administer federal policy, formula allotments, guidance, data systems, technical assistance, special projects, research, training, and oversight.

States conduct needs assessments, identify priorities, design strategies, finance activities, coordinate programmes, monitor contractors, report performance, and contribute nonfederal funds.

Local health departments, hospitals, universities, community health centres, schools, family organisations, nonprofit agencies, tribal entities, and other institutions frequently carry out state-funded activities.

This model allows adaptation to state need but produces variation in organisational structure, service availability, spending, priorities, and outcomes.

Participating States and Jurisdictions

Fifty-nine states and jurisdictions participate in the modern Title V state block-grant process.

The group includes the fifty states, the District of Columbia, United States territories, and participating Pacific jurisdictions recognised under programme rules and appropriations.

Each participating jurisdiction submits a standardised application and annual report through the Title V Information System.

Territorial and Pacific programmes often face small populations, geographic isolation, workforce shortages, transportation cost, limited specialty care, and dependence on off-island referral.

Statutory Purposes

Title V authorises funding to enable states to:

  • assure mothers and children access to quality maternal and child-health services, especially people with low incomes or limited local services;
  • reduce infant mortality;
  • reduce preventable disease and disabling conditions among children;
  • reduce avoidable inpatient and long-term care;
  • increase childhood immunisation;
  • increase health assessments and follow-up treatment for low-income children;
  • provide prenatal, delivery, and postpartum care for low-income and high-risk pregnant women;
  • promote preventive and primary care for low-income children;
  • provide specified rehabilitation services when Medicaid does not cover them; and
  • develop family-centred, community-based, coordinated systems for children with special health care needs.

Population Groups

Women and maternal-health populations
People requiring preconception, prenatal, delivery, postpartum, preventive, mental-health, and related services.
Infants
Children from birth through the first year of life, including newborn-screening, safe-sleep, nutrition, developmental, and mortality-prevention priorities.
Children
Children requiring preventive, primary, developmental, oral-health, injury-prevention, immunisation, and related services.
Adolescents
Young people requiring preventive care, mental-health support, injury and violence prevention, reproductive health, nutrition, transition planning, and positive development.
Children and youth with special health care needs
Children and youth who have or face increased risk for chronic physical, developmental, behavioural, or emotional conditions and require health and related services beyond those generally required.
Families
Parents, caregivers, and family members participating in care, systems design, navigation, support, and decision-making.

Children and Youth with Special Health Care Needs

Services for children and youth with special health care needs are a central Title V responsibility.

State programmes support family-centred systems connecting primary care, specialists, hospitals, therapies, schools, early intervention, behavioural health, durable equipment, home care, insurance, and community services.

Core goals include access to a medical home, adequate insurance, early and continuous screening, easy-to-use community services, shared decision-making, and successful transition to adult health care.

Title V may finance care coordination, family navigation, specialty clinics, genetic services, transition initiatives, data systems, and gap-filling services not covered by Medicaid or insurance.

Family Partnership and Lived Experience

Modern maternal and child-health practice treats families as partners in programme planning, service delivery, evaluation, and policy.

States use advisory councils, family delegates, youth representatives, family-to-family organisations, surveys, focus groups, and paid consultation.

Participation should include decision-making authority, accessible material, language support, disability accommodation, compensation, and feedback regarding how recommendations were used.

Family engagement can become symbolic when institutions seek personal testimony without sharing power or compensating participants.

Five-Year Needs Assessment

Every state conducts a comprehensive statewide maternal and child-health needs assessment at least once every five years.

The assessment examines maternal and women’s health, perinatal and infant health, child health, adolescent health, children with special health care needs, and cross-cutting systems.

States analyse vital records, surveys, Medicaid data, hospital records, programme data, workforce information, community input, geographic access, disparities, and emerging conditions.

The needs assessment identifies priority needs that guide the state’s five-year action plan, performance measures, evidence-based strategies, and annual spending.

A high-quality assessment distinguishes population need from the interests of existing providers and examines groups whose needs may be hidden in statewide averages.

Annual Application and Report

A state must submit an annual application in a standardised federal form to receive its allotment.

The application includes:

  • priority needs;
  • goals and objectives;
  • a five-year action plan;
  • planned strategies and activities;
  • geographic and population targets;
  • performance measures;
  • financial data;
  • coordination arrangements;
  • public input; and
  • the prior year’s annual report.

The application must be developed by or in consultation with the state maternal and child-health agency and made public to facilitate comment.

Federal Allocation Formula

Federal law distributes state allotments through a statutory formula rooted partly in each state’s historical fiscal year 1983 allotment and partly in its share of the national population of low-income children.

The structure preserves a historical funding base while distributing increases according to low-income child population.

HRSA describes the amount as reflecting population size and need. The formula does not respond fully or immediately to current maternal mortality, health-care prices, fiscal capacity, rural distance, or newly emerging conditions.

Unused or unavailable allotments can be redistributed among qualifying states under statutory rules.

State Matching and Maintenance of Effort

States and jurisdictions must provide at least three dollars in nonfederal maternal and child-health funds for every four federal dollars received.

The match can include qualifying state and local funds and other allowable resources under federal guidance.

States must also maintain state-only maternal and child-health spending at least at the fiscal year 1989 level.

Matching and maintenance of effort are intended to prevent federal funds from replacing existing state investment. Because the statutory baseline is historical, it does not guarantee that state spending keeps pace with population, inflation, medical cost, or present need.

Required State Spending Set-Asides

Federal law generally requires each state to allocate:

  • at least 30 percent of federal Title V payments to preventive and primary-care services for children; and
  • at least 30 percent to services for children with special health care needs.

HHS may waive the percentages when a state demonstrates an extraordinary unmet need and the alternative distribution advances Title V purposes.

The remaining funds can support maternal health, infant health, adolescent health, cross-cutting systems, and other authorised priorities.

The set-asides protect child-health and special-needs functions while limiting complete state discretion.

Administrative Limit

No more than 10 percent of a state’s federal allotment may be used for administration of the federal funds.

Administrative activity can include grant management, fiscal control, reporting, contracting, personnel, and general programme oversight.

Planning, evaluation, surveillance, quality improvement, and systems development may be programmatic rather than purely administrative when properly structured and documented.

The distinction matters because public-health systems require data and coordination, but excessive overhead can reduce services and community investment.

Permitted Uses of Funds

States may use Title V funds for health services and related activities consistent with the approved application.

  • planning and needs assessment;
  • public-health surveillance and data linkage;
  • prenatal, postpartum, infant, child, and adolescent initiatives;
  • preventive and primary-care services;
  • care coordination and case management;
  • children with special health care needs programmes;
  • family navigation and support;
  • workforce development;
  • health education;
  • quality improvement;
  • provider consultation;
  • screening and follow-up systems;
  • regional specialty networks;
  • toll-free information lines;
  • evaluation;
  • technical assistance; and
  • selected direct health services.

Generally Prohibited Uses

Federal allotment funds generally may not be used for:

  • ordinary inpatient services, except authorised services for children with special health care needs, high-risk pregnant women, infants, or other approved circumstances;
  • cash payments to intended recipients;
  • land purchase;
  • building purchase or construction;
  • permanent facility improvement beyond minor remodelling, unless a statutory waiver applies;
  • major medical equipment;
  • meeting another programme’s required nonfederal match;
  • research or training grants to a for-profit entity; or
  • nonemergency services from federally excluded providers.

HHS can waive selected facility limitations in extraordinary circumstances when doing so advances Title V.

The Maternal and Child Health Services Pyramid

States commonly describe Title V activity through four service levels.

Direct services
Clinical preventive, primary, or specialty services delivered to an individual when another payment source is unavailable.
Enabling services
Transportation, interpretation, outreach, case management, eligibility assistance, health education, and other support helping people obtain care.
Population-based services
Newborn screening, immunisation systems, safe-sleep campaigns, injury prevention, surveillance, emergency planning, and other interventions directed toward entire populations.
Infrastructure-building services
Workforce, data systems, standards, policy, quality improvement, programme coordination, needs assessment, and systems development.

The pyramid illustrates why Title V cannot be measured solely by clinic visits. Much of its value lies in systems affecting people who never receive a service billed directly to the grant.

Maternal Health

Title V supports strategies addressing maternal mortality, severe maternal morbidity, prenatal care, postpartum care, perinatal regionalisation, chronic disease, mental health, substance use, nutrition, intimate-partner violence, and maternity-care access.

State programmes can support maternal mortality review, quality collaboratives, patient-safety bundles, community-based care, provider training, rural referral systems, doula initiatives, risk assessment, and postpartum follow-up.

Title V funding is generally insufficient to finance comprehensive maternity care directly. Its comparative advantage is coordinating institutions, identifying gaps, testing strategies, and aligning Medicaid, hospitals, public health, and community organisations.

Infant and Perinatal Health

Infant-health priorities include prematurity, low birth weight, birth defects, neonatal care, safe sleep, breastfeeding, newborn screening, congenital conditions, injury prevention, and infant mortality.

States use vital records and linked data to identify geographic, racial, economic, and clinical patterns.

Interventions can include regional perinatal systems, bereavement support, home visiting, neonatal follow-up, maternal risk reduction, safe-sleep education, and coordination with Medicaid and hospitals.

Infant mortality is both a clinical outcome and a broader indicator of social conditions, health access, housing, environment, income, and institutional inequality.

Child Health

Child-health strategies address preventive visits, immunisation, oral health, developmental screening, school readiness, nutrition, physical activity, injury, violence, environmental exposure, asthma, obesity, and mental health.

Title V can help states establish standards, train providers, link data, coordinate referrals, support local programmes, and improve access for children who lack adequate insurance or providers.

At least 30 percent of federal state-allotment funds generally must support preventive and primary-care services for children.

Title V coordination with Medicaid EPSDT is essential because EPSDT creates an individual coverage entitlement, while Title V strengthens the systems needed to make screening and treatment function.

Adolescent Health

Modern Title V planning treats adolescence as a distinct health domain.

Priorities can include preventive visits, mental health, suicide prevention, substance use, sexual and reproductive health, nutrition, physical activity, violence, motor-vehicle injury, bullying, school connectedness, and transition to adult care.

Adolescents frequently experience fragmented systems divided among schools, paediatrics, behavioural health, juvenile justice, child welfare, and adult medicine.

Title V can align these institutions and support youth participation in programme design.

Newborn Screening

Title V has a historic and continuing relationship to newborn screening for metabolic, endocrine, blood, hearing, heart, and other conditions.

State systems collect specimens or screening results, operate laboratories, notify providers and families, conduct diagnostic follow-up, and connect infants with treatment.

Federal and state law, fees, laboratory systems, and specialised grants finance different components.

Screening is effective only when positive results lead quickly to confirmation, treatment, care coordination, and long-term follow-up.

Coordination with Medicaid

Title V and Medicaid serve overlapping populations but have different legal functions.

Medicaid
Provides individual health coverage and payment for covered services under federal and state rules.
Title V
Builds public-health systems, coordinates services, fills gaps, supports population interventions, and finances selected services that insurance does not cover adequately.

Federal law requires Title V agencies to coordinate with Medicaid, including EPSDT standards, provider information, care arrangements, and assistance enrolling eligible pregnant women and infants.

States maintain interagency agreements or memoranda of understanding defining roles, data sharing, referrals, reimbursement, and programme coordination.

Weak coordination can cause duplication, uncovered gaps, delayed treatment, and disputes over which programme should pay.

Coordination with Other Programmes

Title V coordinates with:

  • Medicaid and CHIP;
  • WIC;
  • community health centres;
  • Title X family planning;
  • Maternal, Infant, and Early Childhood Home Visiting;
  • Head Start and Early Head Start;
  • Individuals with Disabilities Education Act programmes;
  • child welfare;
  • schools and education departments;
  • developmental-disability systems;
  • mental-health and substance-use programmes;
  • injury-prevention programmes;
  • hospitals and perinatal systems;
  • tribal health programmes; and
  • nutrition, housing, transportation, and social-service agencies.

Coordination is a core programme function because maternal and child health crosses agency boundaries.

Performance Measurement Framework

Title V uses a national performance framework to connect needs, strategy, activity, and outcome.

National Outcome Measures
Population-level indicators describing maternal, infant, child, adolescent, special-needs, and family outcomes.
National Performance Measures
Priority indicators selected by states and linked to evidence-informed action.
Evidence-Based or Informed Strategy Measures
State-defined measures tracking implementation of strategies intended to improve performance.
State Performance Measures
Measures developed to address state priorities not captured adequately by national measures.
Standardised Measures
Common programme and systems indicators used for reporting and comparison.

Performance systems can strengthen accountability, but data lag, small sample sizes, changing definitions, and broad programme influence complicate causal evaluation.

Title V Information System

The Title V Information System, commonly called TVIS, is the federal platform for state applications, annual reports, financial data, performance measures, narratives, contacts, and public information.

TVIS allows comparison across states and years and supports transparency regarding priorities, activities, and spending.

National data sources include vital statistics, the National Survey of Children’s Health, Medicaid and programme records, surveys, and state-linked datasets.

Public reporting is strongest when states provide clear geographic, racial, ethnic, disability, and socioeconomic detail without compromising privacy.

Special Projects of Regional and National Significance

A federal portion of Title V funding supports Special Projects of Regional and National Significance, commonly called SPRANS.

SPRANS grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements address emerging issues, research, training, demonstrations, genetics, haemophilia, newborn screening, family information, workforce, and specialised systems.

These projects allow federal leadership beyond the state formula structure and can develop models later adopted nationally or by states.

The federal set-aside also creates tension between direct state flexibility and national investment in innovation, workforce, and rare conditions.

Community Integrated Service Systems

Federal Title V authority also supports Community Integrated Service Systems activities.

These activities have included home visiting, rural maternal and child-health projects, integrated service-delivery systems, maternal and child-health centres, provider participation, and community-based services for children otherwise dependent on institutional care.

Statutory preferences can direct selected projects toward areas with high infant mortality.

Maternal and Child-Health Workforce

Title V supports a multidisciplinary workforce including public-health professionals, nurses, physicians, midwives, social workers, nutritionists, dentists, therapists, epidemiologists, data analysts, family leaders, community health workers, and programme administrators.

Federal training programmes support leadership education, nutrition, developmental-behavioural paediatrics, adolescent health, disability, and other specialised fields.

Workforce shortages affect obstetrics, midwifery, neonatology, paediatrics, child psychiatry, dentistry, home nursing, therapy, public-health epidemiology, and rural care.

Title V can develop capacity but cannot by itself correct national payment, training, licensing, migration, housing, or employment conditions driving shortages.

Civil-Rights Requirements

Section 508 of Title V treats funded programmes as recipients of federal financial assistance for specified civil-rights purposes.

Applicable protections include:

  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
  • section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973;
  • the Age Discrimination Act of 1975;
  • Title IX sex-discrimination protections where applicable;
  • the Americans with Disabilities Act;
  • section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act where applicable;
  • language-access requirements; and
  • the express Title V prohibitions concerning sex and religion.

Civil-rights compliance affects programme access, communication, disability accommodation, geographic distribution, contracting, service quality, and data analysis.

Significance for African American Communities

Title V developed in a health system marked by segregation, unequal hospitals, limited prenatal care, racial exclusion from medical professions, poverty, and severe regional differences in maternal and infant mortality.

The programme financed state and local maternal and child-health infrastructure that reached Black communities through public-health nursing, clinics, specialty programmes, newborn screening, infant-mortality initiatives, and care coordination.

Federal reporting law requires selected maternal and child-health information to be compiled by racial and ethnic group, including infant mortality, low birth weight, deliveries, and service coverage.

Modern Title V programmes play a major role in maternal mortality review, community-informed maternal health, infant mortality reduction, safe sleep, preterm-birth prevention, perinatal regionalisation, and care for children with chronic conditions.

The programme has not eliminated racial inequality. Black women continue to face disproportionate pregnancy-related mortality and morbidity, while Black infants experience higher mortality and preterm-birth risk than national averages.

Effective Title V administration requires more than race-neutral statewide programming. It requires disaggregated data, community partnership, investment in Black-led institutions, accountability for hospital quality, geographic targeting, workforce diversity, and correction of discriminatory systems.

American Indian and Alaska Native Significance

American Indian and Alaska Native families face distinctive maternal and child-health conditions involving rural distance, underfunded health systems, jurisdiction, data quality, workforce, transportation, and federal trust responsibilities.

State Title V agencies coordinate with tribes, tribal epidemiology centres, Indian Health Service, urban Indian organisations, Medicaid, and community providers.

State consultation must respect tribal sovereignty and avoid treating tribal governments solely as ordinary local stakeholders.

Federal Title V activities also support family-to-family information capacity for Indian tribes under specialised statutory authority.

Rural and Frontier Significance

Rural families can face maternity-unit closure, long travel distance, ambulance scarcity, specialist shortages, limited home care, weak broadband, and difficulty obtaining therapy or paediatric services.

Title V supports regional referral systems, telehealth, transportation, rural workforce, mobile services, risk-appropriate maternity care, and coordination with critical-access hospitals and community health centres.

Rural systems require more than equal per-person funding because low population density increases the cost of maintaining access.

Disability Rights and Community-Based Care

Title V’s historical special-needs programme evolved from a medical-treatment model toward family-centred, community-based, coordinated systems.

Modern practice incorporates disability rights, family partnership, inclusion, community living, education, transition, and access to ordinary paediatric and preventive care.

Title V can reduce institutional dependence through care coordination, home- and community-based support, family information, and outpatient specialty systems.

Families still encounter waiting lists, inadequate home nursing, insurance denials, fragmented agencies, inaccessible facilities, and poor transition to adult care.

Health Equity

Maternal and child-health outcomes vary by race, ethnicity, income, disability, geography, language, immigration, insurance, housing, environment, and access to care.

Title V agencies use data and community partnership to identify disparities and select evidence-informed strategies.

Equity can involve universal systems with targeted resources, culturally responsive services, disability access, language assistance, rural investment, and support for community-based organisations.

Measurement must distinguish equal programme availability from equal ability to benefit.

Direct Services and Payer of Last Resort

Title V can pay for direct clinical services, but states generally seek Medicaid, CHIP, private insurance, and other responsible payers first.

Direct services may fill gaps for uninsured patients, uncovered treatments, specialised diagnostics, transportation, care coordination, or services unavailable within ordinary networks.

When charges are imposed for Title V-assisted health services, states must use a public fee schedule, exempt low-income mothers and children, and adjust charges for income, resources, and family size.

Title V is best understood as a safety-net and systems payer rather than a substitute for comprehensive insurance.

Information and Referral

States must provide toll-free telephone access and other appropriate methods for parents to obtain information about Title V, Medicaid, and relevant health providers.

Information systems can assist families with eligibility, providers, specialty care, developmental services, insurance, transportation, and care coordination.

Websites and directories must remain current, accessible, multilingual, and usable by people without reliable broadband.

Audits and Oversight

States submit annual reports documenting activities, people reached, services, expenditures, outcomes, and progress.

States must conduct independent expenditure audits at least every two years and make reports and audits available for public inspection.

HHS can require repayment, offset future allotments, withhold funds, or take other action when expenditures violate federal law.

Federal officials and the Government Accountability Office have access to relevant records for evaluation and review.

Programme Integrity

Programme-integrity risks include unsupported costs, inaccurate service counts, weak contractor monitoring, duplicate payment, improper matching, unallowable construction, undisclosed conflicts, and failure to meet spending set-asides.

States use fiscal controls, contracts, audits, data validation, procurement, subrecipient monitoring, corrective action, and public reporting.

Oversight must account for the fact that many Title V outcomes result from systems activity rather than individually billed services.

Major Achievements

  • Created the nation’s oldest continuing federal-state public-health programme.
  • Built state maternal and child-health agencies and professional capacity.
  • Expanded prenatal, infant, child, adolescent, and disability-related public-health systems.
  • Supported development of specialty services for children with complex conditions.
  • Institutionalised five-year state needs assessments and public planning.
  • Required substantial state matching and maintenance of effort.
  • Protected child preventive-care and special-health-care-needs spending through federal set-asides.
  • Strengthened coordination with Medicaid and EPSDT.
  • Supported newborn screening and follow-up systems.
  • Developed population data and performance measurement across states.
  • Supported family-centred and community-based systems.
  • Financed national research, training, genetics, workforce, and demonstration projects.
  • Reached tens of millions of mothers, infants, children, adolescents, and families annually.
  • Provided a flexible platform for emerging maternal and child-health priorities.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Federal funding has not kept pace consistently with medical cost, population need, or programme expectations.
  • The allocation formula remains tied partly to historical funding patterns.
  • State flexibility produces unequal services and priorities.
  • A fiscal year 1989 maintenance-of-effort baseline does not guarantee contemporary state investment.
  • Title V does not create an individual entitlement to services.
  • States may report people reached through broad population services, making the programme appear larger than its direct-service footprint.
  • Performance measures do not always establish that Title V caused an observed outcome.
  • Small jurisdictions can face data instability and workforce scarcity.
  • Administrative and reporting complexity can burden state and local agencies.
  • Coordination with Medicaid, education, disability, hospitals, and public health can remain fragmented.
  • Racial and geographic disparities persist despite universal programme goals.
  • Families may lack meaningful influence over priorities and spending.
  • Direct-service gaps remain when Medicaid and private insurance fail to provide access.
  • Short-term federal projects can be difficult to sustain after grant expiration.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Maternal Mortality and Severe Maternal Morbidity

States continue using Title V data, quality collaboratives, review committees, community partnerships, and hospital-safety strategies to reduce preventable pregnancy-related death and injury.

Fiscal Year 2026 Funding

Fiscal year 2026 enacted budget authority for the Maternal and Child Health Block Grant account was approximately $818.7 million.

Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Request

The presidential request proposed approximately $767.25 million, including about $593.3 million for state formula awards and $163.7 million for Special Projects of Regional and National Significance. Congress determines final funding.

Federal Organisational Change

Fiscal year 2027 budget documents presented maternal and child-health activities within the proposed Administration for a Healthy America structure, while current HRSA and Maternal and Child Health Bureau programme systems remained operational in 2026.

Maternity-Care Deserts

Rural hospital closure, obstetric-unit closure, workforce loss, transportation, and insurance economics leave many communities without nearby maternity care.

Children’s Mental Health

States increasingly prioritise suicide prevention, behavioural health, school connection, crisis systems, and paediatric mental-health consultation.

Children with Medical Complexity

Families continue to face home-nursing shortages, equipment delays, fragmented insurance, caregiver burden, and difficult transition to adult services.

Data Modernisation

States are improving linkage among birth, death, Medicaid, hospital, newborn-screening, survey, and programme data while addressing privacy, interoperability, and timeliness.

Health Equity

Title V agencies face pressure to convert disparity measurement into resource allocation, institutional accountability, and durable community partnership.

Climate and Emergency Preparedness

Heat, wildfire, hurricanes, flooding, infectious disease, and infrastructure failure create distinctive risks for pregnant people, infants, disabled children, and medically dependent families.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Title V
The maternal and child-health title of the Social Security Act.
Block grant
Federal funding covering a broad policy field with substantial state discretion within statutory requirements.
Maternal and child health
The public-health field addressing women, pregnancy, birth, infancy, childhood, adolescence, special health care needs, and family systems.
Children and youth with special health care needs
Children and youth who have or face increased risk for chronic conditions and require services beyond those generally required.
Needs assessment
A systematic examination of population health, services, systems, assets, gaps, disparities, and priorities.
Maintenance of effort
A requirement to preserve a specified level of state-only spending.
Matching funds
Nonfederal contributions required as a condition of receiving federal funds.
Direct service
A clinical preventive, primary, or specialty service furnished to an individual.
Enabling service
A support such as transportation, interpretation, case management, or outreach that facilitates access.
Population-based service
An intervention directed to an entire group or community rather than a single patient.
Infrastructure-building service
Data, workforce, policy, quality, standards, planning, and systems activity supporting public-health capacity.
Care coordination
Organisation and use of resources to assure access to comprehensive services.
Medical home
An accessible, continuous, comprehensive, family-centred, coordinated, compassionate, and culturally effective model of care.
EPSDT
Medicaid’s Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment benefit for qualifying children and youth.
SPRANS
Special Projects of Regional and National Significance funded through the federal Title V set-aside.
CISS
Community Integrated Service Systems activities supporting coordinated maternal and child-health delivery.
TVIS
The Title V Information System used for state applications, reports, performance, and financial data.

Summary

The Maternal and Child Health Services Program is the federal-state public-health partnership authorised under Title V of the Social Security Act. Congress created Title V in 1935, making it the nation’s oldest continuing federal public-health grant programme.

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 transformed the earlier programme into the modern Maternal and Child Health Services Block Grant. States gained substantial flexibility while retaining federal purposes, matching, maintenance-of-effort, spending set-asides, planning, reporting, audit, and civil-rights requirements.

Title V serves pregnant people, mothers, infants, children, adolescents, and children and youth with special health care needs. It finances direct and enabling services, population interventions, care coordination, data, workforce, quality improvement, family partnership, and public-health infrastructure.

States conduct a comprehensive needs assessment every five years and submit annual applications and reports. At least 30 percent of state allotments generally supports child preventive and primary care, and at least 30 percent supports children with special health care needs.

In 2024, Title V-supported systems reached an estimated 61 million people. Fiscal year 2026 enacted funding was approximately $818.7 million, while the fiscal year 2027 presidential request proposed approximately $767.25 million.

Title V’s long-term value lies in its ability to coordinate institutions and address population needs that no single insurer, hospital, clinic, or agency can manage alone. Its effectiveness depends on state investment, Medicaid coordination, family leadership, workforce, strong data, civil-rights enforcement, and sustained federal funding.
