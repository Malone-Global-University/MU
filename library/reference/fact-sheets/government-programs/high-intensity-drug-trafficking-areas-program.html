High Intensity Drug Trafficking Areas Program

The ONDCP-administered regional program created in 1988 to coordinate federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial agencies; share intelligence; deconflict investigations; disrupt drug-trafficking and money-laundering organizations; respond to overdose; and direct federal assistance to nationally significant trafficking areas.

HIDTA is ONDCP’s principal long-running law-enforcement assistance program and one of the federal government’s largest institutional frameworks for coordinating drug investigations across federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial boundaries.

What Is the HIDTA Program?

The High Intensity Drug Trafficking Areas Program designates counties and regions where drug production, importation, transportation, distribution, money laundering, or related harm creates a nationally significant threat.

Designation allows participating agencies to organize regional initiatives, share intelligence, deconflict investigations, obtain specialized analysis and training, and receive federal funding for approved activities.

A HIDTA is not a new federal police agency. It is a regional coordination and funding structure governed by participating law-enforcement leaders.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains the program’s creation, legal authority, regional designation, county petitions, Executive Boards, grant administration, threat assessments, strategies, initiatives, intelligence centres, event and case deconfliction, task forces, prosecution, highway enforcement, money-laundering investigations, overdose-response partnerships, prevention, treatment, performance measures, current regions, fiscal year 2026 funding, civil-rights issues, oversight concerns, and relationship to other federal drug programs.

Geographic Scope

The national program includes thirty-three regional HIDTAs serving designated counties in every state, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the United States Virgin Islands.

Regional boundaries do not follow one standard pattern. Some HIDTAs are concentrated around one metropolitan area, while others cover large multistate, border, rural, or island regions.

County designation is the usual geographic unit, but independent cities, parishes, boroughs, tribal lands, territories, and equivalent jurisdictions can require special treatment.

Historical Context

Congress created HIDTA during the late-1980s War on Drugs, amid concern about cocaine and crack markets, trafficking corridors, organized criminal networks, money laundering, violent drug crime, and fragmented law-enforcement authority.

Federal agencies possessed national and international jurisdiction, while state and local agencies held detailed knowledge of local markets. HIDTA sought to combine those capacities without creating a new national police force.

Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988

The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988 created ONDCP and authorized the Director of National Drug Control Policy to designate areas of the United States as high-intensity drug-trafficking areas.

The original authority permitted increased federal assistance, temporary reassignment of federal personnel, and coordination with state and local officials.

Initial HIDTAs

ONDCP funded the first five HIDTAs in fiscal year 1990.

The early program concentrated on major importation, distribution, and money-laundering centres and established the model of geographically focused interagency cooperation.

National Expansion

The number and geographic reach of HIDTAs expanded during the 1990s and 2000s as trafficking routes, methamphetamine production, rural distribution, border activity, and regional drug markets changed.

Expansion increased access to coordination resources but also created pressure to divide limited appropriations among more regions and counties.

1998 Reauthorization

The Office of National Drug Control Policy Reauthorization Act of 1998 expanded ONDCP authority, strengthened performance requirements, and gave the HIDTA program a more permanent institutional position.

The 2006 Statutory Framework

The ONDCP Reauthorization Act of 2006 established the current comprehensive version of 21 U.S.C. 1706.

It formalized the program’s purposes, designation petition process, Executive Board balance, use-of-funds rules, annual budget justification, emerging-threat authority, evaluation requirements, task-force assessment, intelligence-sharing assessment, and methamphetamine reporting.

The SUPPORT Act of 2018

The SUPPORT for Patients and Communities Act reauthorized ONDCP and HIDTA through fiscal year 2023 and strengthened public-health and overdose-related authorities.

Current law permits up to 5 percent of HIDTA appropriations to support prevention and treatment programs.

Authorization and Appropriations

The most recent explicit statutory authorization covered fiscal years 2018 through 2023 at $280 million annually.

Expiration of an authorization period does not automatically terminate a program when Congress continues to appropriate funds.

HIDTA remained fully active and received a record fiscal year 2026 allocation.

Four Statutory Purposes

  1. Facilitate cooperation among federal, state, local, and tribal law-enforcement agencies.
  2. Enhance law-enforcement intelligence sharing.
  3. Provide reliable intelligence needed to design effective strategies and operations.
  4. Support coordinated strategies that maximize available resources to reduce illegal drug supply in designated areas and nationally.

Office of National Drug Control Policy

ONDCP administers the national program, designates counties and regions, allocates appropriations, issues policy and budget guidance, reviews annual strategies and budgets, monitors performance, conducts audits, approves discretionary projects, and reports to Congress.

ONDCP’s location in the Executive Office of the President allows it to coordinate multiple cabinet departments without placing HIDTA inside one law-enforcement agency.

National HIDTA Program Office

ONDCP’s national HIDTA staff review designation petitions, regional strategies, budgets, Executive Board compliance, initiative performance, financial controls, and national projects.

The national office balances regional discretion with national policy, statutory requirements, and congressional appropriations.

Regional HIDTA Structure

Each regional HIDTA has:

  • defined designated jurisdictions;
  • an Executive Board;
  • an Executive Director and administrative staff;
  • an annual threat assessment;
  • an annual strategy;
  • a budget proposal;
  • approved initiatives;
  • an intelligence and information-sharing capacity; and
  • performance and financial reporting.

Executive Board

The Executive Board governs the regional HIDTA and directs its priorities.

It establishes goals, approves strategies, reviews initiative proposals, allocates funds, monitors results, approves reports, and selects officers and senior regional management.

Federal and Nonfederal Voting Balance

Federal law requires equal voting power between federal representatives and representatives of state, local, tribal, and territorial agencies.

When equal in-person attendance is impractical, proxy or weighted voting must preserve the balance.

This structure is intended to prevent federal agencies or local agencies from controlling the program unilaterally.

Executive Board Membership

Members commonly include senior officials from DEA, FBI, ATF, Homeland Security Investigations, Customs and Border Protection, the United States Coast Guard, United States Attorneys’ Offices, state police, sheriffs, municipal police, prosecutors, tribal law enforcement, and other participating agencies.

Membership varies according to regional threats and institutions.

Board Officers

The Executive Board designates a chair, vice chair, and other officers it considers necessary.

Many boards rotate leadership or pair federal and nonfederal officers to reinforce balanced governance.

Executive Director

The Executive Director manages daily regional operations under the Executive Board.

Responsibilities include strategy, budgets, initiative oversight, personnel, compliance, coordination, reporting, training, communications, and liaison with ONDCP and other HIDTAs.

HIDTA Is Not a Separate Federal Agency

Federal law states that HIDTA eligibility and funding do not create an agency relationship between an individual HIDTA and the federal government.

Participating officers remain employees of their own agencies, and legal authority derives from those agencies, deputations, task-force agreements, and applicable law.

Fiduciary or Grantee Agency

ONDCP grant funds are generally awarded through eligible state, local, or tribal governmental entities or other approved fiduciaries that manage funds for the regional HIDTA.

The fiduciary handles accounting, payments, procurement, reimbursements, records, and audit requirements but does not replace the Executive Board’s strategic authority.

No General Statutory Match Requirement

HIDTA does not operate through a uniform statutory dollar-for-dollar matching requirement.

Designation criteria nevertheless require state, local, and tribal agencies to demonstrate that they have committed substantial personnel and resources to the trafficking problem.

HIDTA Designation

The Director of National Drug Control Policy may designate a specified area after consultation with the Attorney General, Secretary of the Treasury, Secretary of Homeland Security, relevant national drug-control agencies, and the governor of each affected state.

Most modern petitions seek to add one or more counties to an existing regional HIDTA rather than establish an entirely new regional organization.

Four Designation Criteria

  1. The area is a significant centre of illegal drug production, manufacturing, importation, or distribution.
  2. State, local, and tribal agencies have committed resources and shown determination to respond.
  3. Drug-related activity has a significant harmful impact locally and elsewhere in the country.
  4. A significant increase in federal resources is necessary to respond adequately.

Petition Process

A coalition of interested law-enforcement agencies can petition ONDCP for designation.

ONDCP accepts county-based petitions on an ongoing basis and reviews petitions at least annually.

There is no single preset application form; petitioners organize evidence around statutory criteria and federal guidance.

Evidence Supporting a Petition

A petition can document:

  • drug seizures and laboratory data;
  • trafficking routes and distribution networks;
  • overdose deaths and nonfatal overdoses;
  • money laundering and asset movement;
  • violence, firearms, gangs, and exploitation;
  • connections to other regions;
  • existing task forces and agency resources;
  • intelligence gaps;
  • federal-resource needs; and
  • support from the relevant regional Executive Board.

Designation Does Not Guarantee a Fixed Award

County designation provides eligibility for participation and approved resources but does not create a statutory per-county payment.

The regional Executive Board and ONDCP determine which initiatives receive funds within available appropriations.

Most Recent County Designations

ONDCP designated St. Joseph County, Indiana, and Rogers County, Oklahoma, in September 2025.

St. Joseph County joined the Indiana HIDTA, and Rogers County joined the Texoma HIDTA.

Annual Planning Cycle

Each Executive Board annually:

  1. assesses the regional drug threat;
  2. develops a strategy;
  3. establishes objectives and measures;
  4. designs or revises initiatives;
  5. proposes a budget;
  6. reviews performance; and
  7. submits required materials to ONDCP.

Drug Threat Assessment

The threat assessment analyses drug production, importation, transportation, wholesale and retail distribution, trafficking organizations, money laundering, violence, overdose, and emerging substances.

It combines law-enforcement intelligence with seizure, laboratory, public-health, treatment, mortality, and community information where available.

Regional Strategy

The strategy identifies priority threats and explains how regional initiatives will address them.

It should connect the threat assessment to resources, operations, intelligence, prevention, treatment, training, and measurable objectives.

HIDTA Initiative

An initiative is an approved operational or support component receiving HIDTA resources.

Initiatives can be task forces, intelligence centres, prosecution units, training programs, laboratories, prevention projects, treatment partnerships, administration, or national support activities.

Seven Initiative Categories

  1. Enforcement.
  2. Intelligence and information sharing.
  3. Prosecution.
  4. Management and coordination.
  5. Prevention.
  6. Treatment.
  7. Support.

Enforcement Initiatives

Enforcement initiatives investigate drug-trafficking and money-laundering organizations, violent drug groups, production, importation, transportation, distribution, fugitives, and related criminal enterprises.

Most operate as multijurisdictional task forces with personnel from several agencies.

Intelligence and Information-Sharing Initiatives

Intelligence initiatives collect, evaluate, collate, analyse, and disseminate criminal information.

Services include threat assessments, investigative analysis, link charts, target packages, case support, alerts, database access, event deconfliction, and case or subject deconfliction.

Prosecution Initiatives

Prosecution initiatives assign federal, state, or local attorneys and support personnel to complex investigations.

They can assist with warrants, wiretaps, charging, grand juries, asset forfeiture, witness issues, trial, appeals, and interjurisdictional case strategy.

Management and Coordination Initiatives

Management initiatives support the Executive Board, Executive Director, grants, strategy, budgeting, compliance, reporting, records, communications, and regional coordination.

Support Initiatives

Support initiatives can provide training, information technology, surveillance equipment, forensic laboratories, communications, administrative service, research, facilities, and specialized technical capability.

Prevention Initiatives

Prevention initiatives connect public safety with schools, coalitions, health institutions, faith organizations, families, youth programs, and community groups.

They can address nonmedical drug use, counterfeit pills, fentanyl, cannabis, methamphetamine, safe medication practices, social norms, and community protective factors.

Treatment Initiatives

Treatment initiatives coordinate law enforcement, courts, corrections, healthcare, and community providers to improve access to substance-use-disorder treatment and reduce repeated harm.

The statutory 5-percent combined ceiling limits the scale of direct prevention and treatment spending within an enforcement-centred program.

Investigative and Intelligence Support Centers

Every HIDTA must maintain at least one intelligence and information-sharing initiative with co-located federal and nonfederal participants.

These centres provide neutral analytical and coordination capacity for regional agencies that otherwise use separate databases, jurisdictions, and command structures.

Required DEA Representation

The ONDCP Director, in consultation with the Attorney General, must ensure that a DEA representative is included in each HIDTA Intelligence Support Center.

Analytical Support

Analysts can identify organizational structures, financial relationships, communications, travel, addresses, vehicles, firearms, precursor sources, distribution nodes, and links between cases.

Analytical products can support investigations and prosecution but must distinguish intelligence leads from evidence admissible in court.

Information Sharing

HIDTA seeks to overcome institutional barriers that prevent agencies from recognizing overlapping targets, trafficking routes, and criminal networks.

Sharing remains subject to classification, source protection, grand-jury secrecy, privacy, criminal-intelligence rules, agency policy, and technical interoperability.

Event Deconfliction

Event deconfliction alerts agencies when raids, surveillance, arrests, undercover activity, or other operations may occur in the same location and time period.

It reduces the danger that officers from different agencies unknowingly confront one another or compromise investigations.

HIDTAs performed more than 335,000 event deconflictions in 2025.

Case, Subject, and Target Deconfliction

Case deconfliction identifies when separate agencies are investigating the same person, address, organization, telephone number, vehicle, or enterprise.

Agencies can coordinate, preserve independent cases, combine resources, or resolve conflicts according to law and operational need.

Evidence and Information Connections

ONDCP reported that HIDTA systems helped agencies share more than one million pieces of evidence or investigative information in 2025.

The number measures information exchange rather than the accuracy or legal significance of every item.

Criminal-Intelligence Rules

HIDTA intelligence systems must comply with applicable law, including 28 C.F.R. Part 23 where it applies to federally funded multijurisdictional criminal-intelligence systems.

The regulation requires reasonable suspicion, relevance, security, review, dissemination controls, and periodic purging of information.

Drug-Enforcement Task Forces

Task forces combine personnel, authority, intelligence, equipment, funding, and jurisdiction from multiple agencies.

A task force can be HIDTA funded, participate without direct funding, or coordinate with HIDTA while receiving support from another federal program.

Participating Agencies

Participants can include DEA, FBI, ATF, HSI, CBP, Coast Guard, IRS Criminal Investigation, United States Marshals Service, United States Attorneys, state police, sheriffs, municipal police, prosecutors, tribal police, National Guard counterdrug personnel, corrections, and regulatory agencies.

State and Local Role

State and local agencies supply most HIDTA participants and much of the street-level intelligence, investigative authority, informant management, surveillance, arrest, and prosecution capacity.

Federal funding supplements rather than replaces their personnel and institutional resources.

Tribal Participation

Tribal law-enforcement agencies can serve on Executive Boards, participate in initiatives, petition for designation support, receive resources, and coordinate investigations involving Indian country.

Effective participation requires respect for sovereignty, treaty rights, jurisdictional complexity, and tribal control of sensitive information.

Territorial Participation

Puerto Rico and the United States Virgin Islands operate through a regional HIDTA addressing Caribbean trafficking, maritime routes, airports, money laundering, and local distribution.

Territorial agencies participate alongside federal partners while operating under distinct legal and geographic conditions.

National Guard Counterdrug Support

National Guard counterdrug personnel can provide analysis, communications, engineering, aviation, training, and other support consistent with law and Department of Defense policy.

Military support does not transfer ordinary civilian arrest authority to military personnel.

Drug-Trafficking Organizations

A drug-trafficking organization is a coordinated group involved in production, importation, transportation, wholesale or retail distribution, financial operations, or related criminal activity.

HIDTA measures distinguish organizations disrupted from organizations dismantled.

Disruption and Dismantlement

Disruption generally means materially impairing an organization’s operations.

Dismantlement means the organization is no longer capable of operating in its prior form because leadership, structure, finances, supply, or distribution capability has been eliminated.

These classifications depend partly on law-enforcement judgement and reporting standards.

Money-Laundering Organizations

HIDTA initiatives investigate bulk cash, financial accounts, shell companies, trade-based laundering, virtual assets, money services, real estate, casinos, fraud, and international movement of proceeds.

Financial investigations can produce evidence, forfeiture, sanctions, tax cases, and disruption of trafficking logistics.

Asset Forfeiture

Federal and state forfeiture laws can seize money and property connected to trafficking or laundering.

Supporters view forfeiture as a way to remove criminal profit; critics cite due-process concerns, innocent-owner burdens, and financial incentives for enforcement agencies.

Firearms and Drug Trafficking

HIDTA investigations can address firearms used to protect trafficking, enforce debts, commit violence, or move from the United States to criminal organizations abroad.

Current national strategy emphasizes disrupting both drug flow and related firearms trafficking.

Human Trafficking and Exploitation

Drug-trafficking organizations can overlap with human smuggling, human trafficking, labor exploitation, sexual exploitation, kidnapping, and extortion.

HIDTA involvement depends on a credible drug nexus or authorized regional priority.

Violent Crime

Regional initiatives can investigate homicide, assault, robbery, kidnapping, intimidation, and gang violence when connected to drug trafficking or production.

HIDTA is not a general violent-crime grant and should preserve the statutory drug-trafficking purpose.

Illicit Fentanyl

Illicitly manufactured fentanyl and counterfeit pills are central current threats because small quantities can produce large numbers of doses and severe overdose risk.

HIDTA initiatives target precursor supply, international organizations, clandestine production, border movement, wholesale distribution, online sales, money laundering, and local networks.

Methamphetamine

Methamphetamine remains a major national trafficking threat.

Federal law requires ONDCP to report on HIDTA methamphetamine investigations, seizures, listed chemicals, clandestine laboratories, and trafficking trends.

Cocaine

Cocaine trafficking involves international production, maritime and land importation, regional distribution, money laundering, and conversion into crack cocaine.

ONDCP reported more than 211 metric tons of cocaine seized and disposed of through HIDTA activity in 2025.

Heroin

Heroin remains present in regional markets and can be sold alone or mixed with fentanyl or other substances.

ONDCP reported approximately 1.6 metric tons seized and disposed of in 2025.

Illicit Marijuana

HIDTA addresses illicit cultivation, interstate diversion, unlawful production, environmental damage, labor exploitation, violence, and trafficking that remains outside state-regulated systems.

The program should distinguish legal state activity, federal prohibition, unlicensed commerce, youth use, and organized trafficking.

Emerging Synthetic Drugs

HIDTA analysts monitor xylazine, medetomidine, nitazenes, novel synthetic opioids, designer benzodiazepines, synthetic cannabinoids, cathinones, and changing adulterants.

Rapid laboratory identification and information sharing are necessary because traditional reporting can lag behind market changes.

Online Drug Trafficking

Drug sales can use social media, encrypted communications, darknet markets, online advertising, postal systems, delivery services, and digital payment.

Investigations raise jurisdiction, privacy, platform, cybersecurity, and international-evidence issues.

Domestic Highway Enforcement

Domestic Highway Enforcement is a national HIDTA information-sharing and coordination platform addressing trafficking on highways and associated transportation corridors.

It supports intelligence exchange, interdiction coordination, officer safety, trend analysis, and links among seizures.

Highway Interdiction Concerns

Traffic stops and highway interdiction can identify drugs, money, firearms, and trafficking patterns.

They also raise Fourth Amendment, racial-profiling, consent-search, property-seizure, pretext, and data-retention concerns.

National Strategic Analysis Initiative

In 2024, HIDTA combined several national analytical functions into the National Strategic Analysis Initiative.

NSAI includes Domestic Highway Enforcement, the Drug Threat Analysis Group, and the Marijuana Impact Group.

It produces national analysis, strategic intelligence, policy information, and shared platforms.

Drug Threat Analysis Group

The Drug Threat Analysis Group collects and analyses regional and national information concerning current and emerging drugs.

Its products support policymakers, regional strategies, and operational planning.

Marijuana Impact Group

The Marijuana Impact Group disseminates information concerning illicit cultivation, trafficking, public safety, and health effects associated with marijuana.

Its work operates within a changing landscape of federal prohibition and state legalization.

National HIDTA Assistance Center

The National HIDTA Assistance Center is located in the Miami area and provides nationwide training, event support, multimedia services, financial-system support, administration, and assistance to regional HIDTAs.

NHAC received $4,086,770 in the fiscal year 2026 allocation.

HIDTA Directors Committee

The HIDTA Directors Committee brings regional Executive Directors together to coordinate national policy, shared initiatives, training, operational issues, and recommendations to ONDCP.

Financial Administration Systems

NHAC historically supported the HIDTA Financial Management System.

ONDCP launched a new grants-management platform and Financial Administration for the HIDTA Program system, with transition beginning for the 2025 program year.

Training

HIDTA-funded training can cover intelligence, investigations, financial crime, surveillance, cybercrime, officer safety, informants, prosecution, overdose response, prevention, leadership, and grant management.

Training should be connected to regional strategy and evaluated beyond attendance counts.

Overdose Response Strategy

ONDCP launched the Overdose Response Strategy with HIDTA discretionary funding in 2015 and expanded it nationally in 2021.

ORS pairs drug-intelligence officers with public-health analysts to connect trafficking information with overdose prevention and response.

ORS Staffing

The 2026 report to Congress described sixty-one full-time HIDTA-funded drug-intelligence-officer positions and full-time CDC-supported public-health analysts serving all thirty-three regions.

Felony Arrest Notifications

ORS drug-intelligence officers can notify the home jurisdiction when a person is arrested for a felony drug offense elsewhere.

The information can support trafficking investigations, but use must follow legal, relevance, retention, and civil-rights safeguards.

Overdose Detection Mapping Application Program

ODMAP is a free, restricted-access platform developed by the Washington/Baltimore HIDTA for near-real-time reporting and mapping of suspected fatal and nonfatal overdoses.

Agencies can identify spikes, clusters, cross-jurisdictional patterns, and opportunities for coordinated response.

ODMAP’s Current Scale

By February 2026, approximately 5,647 agencies in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico were using ODMAP.

The system contained more than 3.4 million suspected-overdose events and had more than 38,000 users.

ODMAP Privacy and Interpretation

ODMAP uses limited incident data, participation agreements, access controls, and agency policies.

Suspected-overdose entries are operational signals, not final medical diagnoses, and incomplete participation can affect geographic comparisons.

Public Health and Public Safety Framework

HIDTA’s current framework links enforcement, overdose surveillance, naloxone, prevention, treatment coordination, and community partnerships.

The approach recognizes that disrupting supply and preventing death require different professional expertise and measures.

Naloxone

HIDTA regions can support naloxone education, distribution partnerships, officer training, overdose-response protocols, and analysis of reversal activity.

Naloxone prevents opioid-overdose death but does not replace treatment, prevention, or trafficking intervention.

HIDTA National Prevention Strategy

The prevention strategy seeks to establish evidence-based prevention in all HIDTA regions, use assessment and research to guide activity, and sustain regional prevention capacity.

It promotes collaboration among law enforcement, education, health, social services, coalitions, civic groups, and faith organizations.

A Division for Advancing Prevention and Treatment

ADAPT is associated with the Washington/Baltimore HIDTA and supports evidence-informed prevention, training, resource development, and public-health collaboration.

It represents the program’s expansion beyond conventional task-force activity while remaining within the statutory spending ceiling.

Five-Percent Prevention and Treatment Ceiling

Federal law limits the combined amount used for treatment and prevention to 5 percent of program appropriations.

The ceiling preserves HIDTA’s law-enforcement focus but limits direct investment in community and clinical responses to drug harm.

Counterterrorism Assistance

HIDTA resources can assist terrorism investigations when activity is also related to drug trafficking.

Counterterrorism assistance must remain incidental to the drug mission, and significant resources cannot be redirected exclusively to terrorism except temporarily under extraordinary circumstances.

Homeland Security Task Forces

Between 2025 and 2026, more than 100 HIDTA initiatives were designated as or integrated with Homeland Security Task Forces.

Current policy connects HIDTA resources to investigations of cartels, transnational criminal organizations, fentanyl networks, money laundering, online trafficking, firearms, and related national-security threats.

Mission-Dift Risk

Expansion into terrorism, immigration, human trafficking, firearms, and general organized crime can improve whole-of-government coordination when there is a genuine drug nexus.

It can also dilute the statutory mission or use drug funding for broader enforcement priorities.

Program Goal One

The first national performance goal is to disrupt the illegal-drug market by disrupting or dismantling drug-trafficking and money-laundering organizations.

Program Goal Two

The second national performance goal is to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of HIDTA initiatives.

Measures can include information sharing, deconfliction, training, analytical service, coordination, cost avoidance, and initiative performance.

Outputs and Outcomes

Seizures, arrests, cases, deconflictions, reports, and organizations targeted are outputs.

Reduced drug availability, reduced trafficking capacity, fewer overdoses, improved safety, and durable organizational disruption are outcomes.

Large outputs do not automatically establish population-level impact.

Return-on-Investment Measure

ONDCP calculates a return-on-investment measure using the estimated wholesale value of seized drugs and cash relative to program funding.

The reported 2025 ratio was $82.78 for every dollar budgeted.

This is an enforcement-value metric, not a conventional financial return or proof of reduced drug use.

Valuation of Seized Drugs

Estimated drug value depends on quantity, purity, dosage assumptions, location, market level, and price estimates.

Seized-drug value should therefore be treated as an analytical estimate rather than realised government revenue or verified trafficker profit.

Reported 2025 Seizures

ONDCP reported that HIDTA initiatives seized and disposed of more than:

  • 211 metric tons of cocaine;
  • 137 metric tons of methamphetamine;
  • 1,508 metric tons of marijuana;
  • 1.6 metric tons of heroin; and
  • 7 metric tons of fentanyl.

Reported 2025 Organizational Results

ONDCP reported more than 3,383 drug-trafficking organizations disrupted or dismantled during 2025.

Approximately 51 percent were described as international or multistate operations.

Reported Fugitive Apprehensions

The July 2026 White House allocation announcement stated that the regional HIDTAs helped apprehend approximately 42,000 fugitives during the preceding year.

The figure includes varied warrants and should not be interpreted as 42,000 leaders of major trafficking organizations.

Annual Evaluation

ONDCP evaluates each regional HIDTA’s purposes, goals, objectives, initiatives, performance measures, financial controls, and reported accomplishments.

The annual report to Congress combines national assessment with individual regional profiles.

Performance and Financial Audits

ONDCP conducts or commissions program reviews, performance audits, financial audits, and compliance work.

Reviews can require correction of inaccurate data, unsupported costs, weak controls, procurement problems, or initiative-management deficiencies.

Early GAO Criticism

GAO’s early reviews found that program effectiveness was uncertain and that ONDCP needed stronger collaboration, analytical foundations, financial management, and performance measurement.

These findings helped shape later annual strategies, measures, and statutory evaluation requirements.

GAO’s 1998 Review

GAO examined rapid expansion, administration, allocation methods, performance measures, and communication of lessons among HIDTAs.

The review reflected concern that adding regions could dilute resources and complicate national oversight.

Recent GAO Oversight

GAO reported in 2022 that ONDCP conducted audits and evaluations of HIDTA regions and made recommendations concerning performance-data accuracy and financial internal controls during fiscal years 2019–2021.

ONDCP reported that the individual regions implemented those recommendations.

Limits of Effectiveness Claims

Trafficking markets adapt through replacement personnel, alternative routes, new substances, changed communications, and decentralized distribution.

Seizure and arrest trends can reflect increased enforcement, increased supply, improved reporting, or changing prices.

It is therefore difficult to isolate HIDTA’s causal effect on national drug availability or overdose.

Funding History

HIDTA began with $25 million in fiscal year 1990 and expanded as additional regions were designated.

The 2006 law authorized increasing amounts through fiscal year 2011, and the 2018 reauthorization authorized $280 million annually through fiscal year 2023.

Actual appropriations have not always matched authorization levels.

Fiscal Year 2026 Record Allocation

On July 9, 2026, the White House announced $277,491,415 for the program, describing it as the largest HIDTA investment in program history.

Regional base allocations total $273,404,645, and the National HIDTA Assistance Center receives $4,086,770.

How Regional Funding Is Determined

HIDTA funding is not distributed by one published population-and-crime formula.

ONDCP reviews regional threats, strategies, initiative requests, historical funding, performance, national priorities, emerging threats, and available appropriations.

Annual budget justifications must explain the rationale for each region’s proposed amount.

Emerging-Threat Response Authority

ONDCP can use up to 10 percent of appropriated funds to address an emerging trafficking threat, establish a new HIDTA, or expand an existing region.

The Director must consider whether the expenditure can reduce national trafficking or prevent a threat from spreading.

Neighborhood Safety and Witness Protection

Current law directs at least $7 million of annual program appropriations toward HIDTA areas with severe neighborhood-safety and illegal-distribution problems.

Authorized uses include witness protection and assistance connected to drug-distribution cases.

Personnel and Overtime

HIDTA funds can support approved overtime, analysts, coordinators, contracted personnel, equipment, travel, facilities, technology, training, and operational costs.

Permanent sworn personnel are often contributed by participating agencies rather than entirely paid through HIDTA grants.

Equipment and Technology

Regional initiatives can purchase or lease approved surveillance, communications, analytical, information-technology, forensic, safety, and operational equipment.

Equipment must be necessary, properly procured, inventoried, secured, maintained, and disposed of according to award terms.

Procurement

Fiduciaries and participating entities must follow applicable federal grant standards and state or local procurement rules.

Competition, conflicts of interest, cost reasonableness, vendor screening, records, and cybersecurity are important controls.

Financial Controls

HIDTA grant administration requires accurate accounting, segregation of duties, expenditure approval, time records, travel documentation, inventory, reimbursement controls, and audit trails.

Balanced strategic governance does not eliminate the fiduciary’s legal responsibility for federal funds.

Privacy and Civil Liberties

HIDTA systems can contain criminal intelligence, personal identifiers, locations, communications, associates, vehicles, financial information, overdose data, and law-enforcement-sensitive material.

Collection and sharing require relevance, legal authority, security, retention limits, access controls, audit capability, correction procedures, and protection from unauthorized secondary use.

Racial Profiling

Drug interdiction and task-force activity have historically produced racial disparities in stops, searches, arrests, prosecution, sentencing, and forfeiture.

Intelligence, geography, association, and travel patterns cannot lawfully substitute for individualized reasonable suspicion or probable cause where those standards apply.

Search and Seizure

HIDTA participation does not alter Fourth Amendment requirements.

Warrants, traffic stops, consent searches, electronic surveillance, tracking, home entries, and seizures must have lawful authority and satisfy applicable constitutional and statutory rules.

Confidential Informants

Multijurisdictional investigations often use confidential informants and cooperating witnesses.

Agencies require procedures concerning reliability, authorization, payments, criminal activity, safety, disclosure, supervision, and avoidance of coercion.

Electronic Surveillance

Wiretaps, pen registers, location data, device searches, online accounts, and communications interception can be important in complex trafficking investigations.

Such methods require judicial process, statutory compliance, minimization, data security, and disclosure obligations.

Use of Force and Tactical Operations

Task forces conduct high-risk arrests, warrant service, surveillance, and interdiction.

Written command, use-of-force policy, body-worn-camera requirements where applicable, medical response, deconfliction, and post-operation review are essential.

Task-Force Accountability

Multiagency structures can obscure responsibility when policies differ among agencies.

Memoranda of understanding should identify command, supervision, evidence handling, complaints, discipline, disclosure, use of force, vehicle pursuits, informants, and public-record responsibility.

Prosecution and Federalism

HIDTA investigations can be prosecuted federally, by a state, by a tribe, by a territory, or through coordinated cases.

Forum selection affects penalties, discovery, forfeiture, plea practices, incarceration location, and local policy.

Mass Incarceration

HIDTA developed during a period of mandatory drug sentencing and rapid incarceration growth.

Major trafficking investigations can incapacitate violent organizations, while broad enforcement against lower-level participants can deepen incarceration without eliminating supply.

Evaluation should distinguish organizational leadership from replaceable or coerced participants.

Public Health and Enforcement Tensions

Public-health agencies prioritize treatment, trust, confidentiality, overdose prevention, and service access.

Law-enforcement agencies prioritize investigation, evidence, prosecution, and disruption.

Partnership requires clear rules so health information and life-saving services are not undermined by unrelated enforcement.

Harm Reduction

Naloxone, overdose alerts, drug checking, syringe services, and low-barrier treatment can reduce death and disease.

HIDTA’s public-health partnerships can support some of these functions, although regional and administration policy may affect terminology and emphasis.

Cannabis Legalization and Federal Law

State legalization does not change federal controlled-substance law.

HIDTA regions must distinguish state-licensed activity from illicit cultivation, diversion, unlicensed sales, trafficking, environmental crime, exploitation, and violence.

Rural Communities

Rural HIDTA activity can address methamphetamine, fentanyl, trafficking corridors, limited treatment, sparse law enforcement, tribal jurisdiction, and long distances.

Regional funding can provide analytical and task-force capacity unavailable to small agencies independently.

Border Regions

Border HIDTAs address ports of entry, areas between ports, maritime routes, tunnels, commercial cargo, passenger traffic, precursor movement, cash, firearms, and cross-border organizations.

Drug enforcement can overlap immigration, customs, trade, and national-security authorities.

Transportation Hubs

Airports, seaports, rail, parcel facilities, highways, warehouses, and distribution centres can be central to regional strategies.

Interdiction must balance public safety with constitutional rights, commerce, and non-discriminatory screening.

Community Impact

Major trafficking can produce overdose, violence, corruption, exploitation, unsafe housing, environmental damage, and economic harm.

Enforcement can also create disruption, family separation, property loss, distrust, and unequal burdens.

Community impact should be measured alongside seizures and prosecutions.

A State Can Contain Designated and Nondesignated Counties

National coverage does not mean every county is designated.

A state can participate through one or more regional HIDTAs while only selected counties receive formal designation.

Distinction from DEA

DEA is a federal law-enforcement agency with independent investigative authority.

HIDTA is an ONDCP-administered coordination and grant structure in which DEA is a major participant.

Distinction from OCDETF

The Organized Crime Drug Enforcement Task Forces Program is a Department of Justice program targeting major criminal organizations through federal investigations and prosecution.

HIDTA supports regional coordination, intelligence, state and local participation, and approved initiatives.

The programs overlap and are legally required to coordinate intelligence.

Distinction from Byrne JAG

Byrne JAG is a formula grant to states and local jurisdictions for broad criminal-justice purposes.

HIDTA is a regionally governed, threat-based drug-trafficking program with designated counties and approved initiatives.

Designation Applies to Areas, Not People

A county or region is designated as a High Intensity Drug Trafficking Area.

The term does not classify every resident, neighborhood, business, or person in the region as involved in drug trafficking.

Not a General Community Drug Grant

HIDTA is principally a law-enforcement coordination program.

Community coalitions seeking broad youth-prevention support are generally better aligned with the Drug-Free Communities Support Program.

Current Status as of August 3, 2026

HIDTA is active, nationally operational, and funded at a record level.

The program includes thirty-three regional HIDTAs, more than 600 designated counties, 1,032 initiatives, public-health partnerships in every region, and more than 100 initiatives aligned with Homeland Security Task Forces.

Current priorities emphasize illicit fentanyl, cartels, transnational organizations, money laundering, online trafficking, firearms, overdose reduction, and coordinated intelligence.

Major Achievements

  • Created a durable federal-state-local-tribal coordination structure.
  • Expanded to every state and major United States territory within its statutory scope.
  • Placed most program participants outside the federal government.
  • Established balanced regional Executive Boards.
  • Institutionalized event and case deconfliction.
  • Built regional intelligence and analytical centres.
  • Supported complex investigations of trafficking and money laundering.
  • Created national highway, threat-analysis, overdose, and training networks.
  • Developed ODMAP as a nationwide overdose early-warning tool.
  • Connected public health and public safety through ORS.
  • Reported substantial drug, cash, organizational, and fugitive results.
  • Allowed regional adaptation to changing drug threats.

Limitations and Failures

  • Seizures and arrests do not prove reduced drug availability or overdose.
  • Drug-value estimates can overstate economic impact.
  • Trafficking organizations can replace personnel and routes.
  • Expansion divides finite resources among more counties and initiatives.
  • Regional discretion creates uneven practice and transparency.
  • Task-force structures can blur command and accountability.
  • Intelligence systems create privacy and accuracy risks.
  • Highway interdiction can produce racial-profiling and forfeiture concerns.
  • Federal prosecution can intensify sentencing and incarceration.
  • Prevention and treatment are limited to 5 percent of funding.
  • Public-health data can be undermined if used for unrelated enforcement.
  • Mission expansion can dilute the statutory drug-trafficking focus.

Major Controversies

War on Drugs
The program is a major institution of supply-reduction policy and inherits broader debates over prohibition, incarceration, and racial disparity.
Effectiveness measurement
Large seizures and organization counts demonstrate activity but do not establish long-term market reduction.
Intelligence databases
Information sharing improves coordination but risks inaccurate, outdated, or overly broad personal data.
Forfeiture
Asset seizure can remove criminal profit while creating due-process and incentive concerns.
Highway stops
Interdiction can uncover trafficking while increasing pretextual-stop and racial-profiling risks.
Public-health partnership
Overdose coordination can save lives, but health information must remain protected from inappropriate investigative use.
Mission expansion
Cartels, terrorism, immigration, firearms, human trafficking, and organized crime can expand HIDTA beyond its original core.
Funding equity
Designation and historical base funding can advantage established regions over emerging threats and underserved areas.

  • a regional HIDTA versus a designated county;

  • the Executive Board versus the fiduciary agency;
  • HIDTA personnel versus employees of participating agencies;
  • enforcement initiatives versus intelligence or support initiatives;
  • event deconfliction versus case deconfliction;
  • drug value seized versus realised government assets;
  • organizations disrupted versus dismantled;
  • outputs versus population-level outcomes;
  • HIDTA grants versus agency-contributed resources;
  • regional base funding versus national and emerging-threat projects;
  • 2024 complete report data versus 2025 current impact figures;
  • authorization levels versus actual appropriations; and
  • HIDTA versus DEA, OCDETF, Byrne JAG, and DFC.

Why the HIDTA Program Matters

HIDTA created a durable method for coordinating law-enforcement agencies that possess different jurisdictions, data systems, priorities, and resources.

Balanced Executive Boards give state, local, tribal, and territorial leaders formal power alongside federal agencies, while intelligence centres and deconfliction reduce duplication and officer danger.

The program has adapted from cocaine and crack-era enforcement to methamphetamine, illicit fentanyl, online markets, money laundering, overdose surveillance, public-health partnerships, and transnational organizations.

HIDTA also demonstrates the limits of supply-reduction measurement: seizures and organizational cases can be substantial even while drug markets, addiction, and overdose continue.

Its continuing importance lies both in operational coordination and in the debate over how enforcement, prevention, treatment, civil liberties, and public health should be balanced in national drug policy.

Summary

The High Intensity Drug Trafficking Areas Program is an ONDCP-administered regional coordination and grant program created through the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988.

Its statutory purpose is to reduce drug trafficking and production by improving cooperation, intelligence sharing, analytical support, and coordinated enforcement among federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial agencies.

The program currently contains thirty-three regional HIDTAs operating in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the United States Virgin Islands. More than 600 counties are designated, and nearly two-thirds of the United States population lives in a designated county.

Each region is governed by an Executive Board with balanced federal and nonfederal voting power. The board prepares an annual threat assessment, strategy, budget, and set of initiatives tailored to regional conditions.

The national program includes 1,032 enforcement, intelligence, prosecution, management, prevention, treatment, and support initiatives. The most recent complete report identified 23,037 participants, 68 percent of whom came from nonfederal agencies.

Required intelligence centres provide analysis, threat assessments, information sharing, and event and case deconfliction. HIDTA also supports national highway enforcement, strategic analysis, overdose mapping, the Overdose Response Strategy, prevention, treatment coordination, training, and grant administration.

Current law limits prevention and treatment spending to 5 percent and allows up to 10 percent for emerging threats or geographic expansion.

ONDCP reported that HIDTA initiatives seized approximately $22 billion in drugs and cash and disrupted or dismantled more than 3,383 trafficking organizations in 2025. These figures document enforcement output but do not independently prove reduced drug availability, use, or overdose.

Fiscal year 2026 funding totals $277,491,415—the largest announced investment in program history. Of that amount, $273,404,645 supports regional HIDTAs and $4,086,770 supports the National HIDTA Assistance Center.

HIDTA remains active as of August 3, 2026. Its central achievement is durable interagency coordination; its central challenge is demonstrating long-term public impact while protecting civil rights, privacy, public-health trust, and regional accountability.
