Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act Programs

The federal infrastructure system established and expanded by the 2021 Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, including transportation, broadband, water, energy, environmental cleanup, resilience, domestic manufacturing, and public-works programs.

Overview

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, commonly abbreviated IIJA, is a wide-ranging federal infrastructure law that reauthorized major surface-transportation programs and created or substantially expanded programs for roads, bridges, public transit, passenger and freight rail, airports, ports, roadway safety, electric-vehicle charging, broadband, drinking water, wastewater, electric-grid modernization, energy demonstrations, environmental cleanup, drought response, wildfire resilience, cybersecurity, and other public infrastructure.

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. signed the Act on November 15, 2021. It became Public Law 117-58 and is also widely known as the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, or BIL. The law emerged from a bipartisan Senate agreement and passed the Senate by 69–30 and the House by 228–206.

The Act combines several different forms of legislation. It provides a five-year reauthorization of federal highway, transit, highway-safety, motor-carrier, and passenger-rail programs; establishes new grant and formula programs; directly appropriates supplemental funds; modifies taxes and fees; creates the Build America, Buy America Act; and revises environmental review, permitting, broadband, energy, water, and public-works statutes.

Public descriptions commonly characterize the legislation as approximately $1.2 trillion in total authorized infrastructure activity over several years, including roughly $550 billion in spending above previously expected or baseline levels. These figures are useful summaries but are not interchangeable with appropriations, budget authority, obligations, outlays, or the amount available to any one class of recipient.

The Department of Transportation administers the largest share. The Government Accountability Office reports that IIJA authorized and appropriated more than $551 billion to the department for transportation investments. The law also provided more than $62 billion to the Department of Energy, more than $50 billion through the Environmental Protection Agency for drinking-water and wastewater infrastructure, $65 billion for broadband-related programs, and more than $28 billion over fifteen years for Department of the Interior initiatives.

The law does not operate as one single grant program. It contains hundreds of accounts, authorizations, appropriations, set-asides, formula distributions, competitive grants, loans, loan subsidies, contracts, direct federal projects, technical-assistance programs, research programs, and regulatory provisions.

Some funds flow automatically or semi-automatically to states, territories, Tribes, transit agencies, water programs, and other eligible recipients under statutory formulas. Other funds require competitive applications, benefit-cost analysis, planning, environmental review, matching funds, technical documentation, public engagement, or federal selection.

Major transportation programs include the National Highway Performance Program, Surface Transportation Block Grant Program, Bridge Formula Program, Bridge Investment Program, National Infrastructure Project Assistance program, Infrastructure for Rebuilding America grants, Rural Surface Transportation grants, RAISE grants, Safe Streets and Roads for All, public-transit formula programs, Capital Investment Grants, Federal-State Partnership for Intercity Passenger Rail, Amtrak grants, airport grants, port grants, and railroad-crossing programs.

The Act created the $42.45 billion Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment Program, known as BEAD; provided $1 billion for middle-mile broadband; established $2.75 billion in Digital Equity Act programs; added support for Tribal broadband and rural deployment; and appropriated $14.2 billion for the Affordable Connectivity Program.

The Affordable Connectivity Program provided monthly broadband discounts to eligible households but exhausted its appropriated funds. The Federal Communications Commission stopped accepting new applications in February 2024, and the household benefit ended June 1, 2024. The program remains an important example of the difference between building broadband infrastructure and making service affordable after construction.

The Environmental Protection Agency received more than $50 billion for water infrastructure between fiscal years 2022 and 2026. Major components include general Drinking Water and Clean Water State Revolving Fund capitalization, $15 billion dedicated to lead-service-line replacement, and funding for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and other emerging contaminants.

EPA also administers $5 billion for the Clean School Bus Program and approximately $5.4 billion for Superfund cleanup, brownfields revitalization, recycling infrastructure, recycling education, and pollution-prevention activities.

The Department of Energy received more than $62 billion for grid reliability, transmission, batteries, critical minerals, clean hydrogen, carbon capture, direct-air capture, nuclear-energy support, hydropower, weatherization, energy efficiency, demonstration projects, manufacturing, and related research and deployment.

Department of the Interior programs address western water and drought, Indian water-rights settlements, ecosystem restoration, fish passage, wildfire, orphaned oil and gas wells, abandoned mine lands, Tribal infrastructure, and resilience. The law provides $4.7 billion for orphaned-well work and $11.3 billion over fifteen years for abandoned-mine-land reclamation.

Infrastructure funding generally reaches projects in stages. Congress first provides legal authority and budget authority. Agencies then issue apportionments, notices, regulations, guidance, or funding opportunities. Recipients prepare plans and applications. Agencies select or approve awards, establish grant agreements, and obligate funds. Recipients conduct design, permitting, procurement, property acquisition, construction, reimbursement, and closeout. Outlays therefore occur years after enactment.

The Government Accountability Office reported in April 2025 that fifteen agencies identified approximately $711.8 billion in IIJA funding available for grants to Tribes, states, localities, and territories across more than one hundred programs. Of that amount, $131.2 billion was scheduled to become available for obligation in fiscal year 2026.

As of December 31, 2024, agencies had obligated $275.1 billion and outlayed $119.4 billion of the $580.6 billion that had become available through fiscal year 2025 for those recipient groups. Obligation means the government has entered a legal commitment; outlay means money has actually been paid.

A July 2026 GAO review of the Department of Transportation, Environmental Protection Agency, Department of the Interior, and National Telecommunications and Information Administration found that those agencies had received approximately $574.7 billion in IIJA budget authority for fiscal years 2022 through 2025, obligated about 76 percent, and disbursed about 54 percent of the obligated amount.

Implementation changed after January 2025. Executive Order 14154 directed agencies to review certain IIJA and Inflation Reduction Act disbursements for consistency with new administration policy. OMB clarified that the pause applied to programs, projects, or activities implicated by the order rather than every IIJA payment.

GAO reported in July 2026 that selected agencies had approved approximately 9,500 IIJA and Inflation Reduction Act awards valued at about $128 billion during the reviews, cancelled around 800 awards valued at approximately $17.8 billion, and had more than 2,500 awards valued at approximately $33.6 billion awaiting decisions as of the agencies’ reporting dates.

The IIJA includes the Build America, Buy America Act. Federal agencies generally must apply a domestic-content preference to iron, steel, manufactured products, and construction materials used in infrastructure projects receiving federal financial assistance, unless an authorized waiver applies.

Most construction projects funded or assisted through the Act are also covered by Davis-Bacon prevailing-wage requirements because the funds flow through existing Davis-Bacon Related Acts, new programs were placed under covered statutes, or the IIJA expressly applied the labor standards.

Infrastructure projects remain subject to other federal and state requirements, which can include the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, National Historic Preservation Act, Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act, civil-rights laws, accessibility requirements, procurement rules, disadvantaged-business requirements, prevailing-wage laws, and occupational-safety standards.

The law’s original implementation was closely associated with the Biden administration’s Justice40 Initiative and environmental-justice policies. Executive Order 14154 revoked Executive Order 14008 in January 2025, and agencies revised or reviewed program criteria. Statutory civil-rights, nondiscrimination, Tribal-consultation, accessibility, and disadvantaged-community provisions continue according to the terms of each law and program.

The Act has major racial and economic significance. Black neighborhoods have often experienced highway displacement, transit underinvestment, lead exposure, sewer failures, flooding, industrial pollution, unreliable broadband, high energy burdens, and exclusion from infrastructure contracting and skilled trades. IIJA programs can address these conditions, but benefits depend on project selection, local governance, affordability, displacement safeguards, labor access, contractor participation, maintenance, and accountability.

IIJA programs continue in 2026, but the law is not an unlimited permanent fund. Many supplemental appropriations were divided among fiscal years 2022 through 2026, and some programs have obligation deadlines. Other amounts are no-year funds or remain available for longer periods, including the fifteen-year abandoned-mine-land appropriation.

The law’s long-term effect will depend not merely on announced awards but on completed assets, service quality, affordability, safety, maintenance, climate and disaster performance, community access, economic development, and whether benefits reach populations historically excluded from public investment.

Definitions

Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act
Public Law 117-58, the 2021 federal law reauthorizing and funding transportation and numerous other infrastructure programs.
Bipartisan Infrastructure Law
The common executive-branch and public name for the IIJA.
Authorization
Legal permission for a program or specified funding level; authorization does not always provide budget authority.
Appropriation
A law providing budget authority that allows a federal agency to incur obligations and make payments.
Budget authority
Authority provided by federal law to enter obligations that will result in immediate or future government outlays.
Obligation
A legally binding federal commitment, such as a grant agreement, contract, or project authorization.
Outlay
A federal payment made to satisfy an obligation.
Apportionment
Distribution of formula funds, often by statutory calculation, among states or other eligible recipients.
Allocation
Assignment of funds to a recipient, project, category, or purpose under a statute or agency decision.
Formula grant
Funding distributed according to statutory or regulatory factors rather than project-by-project national competition.
Discretionary grant
Funding awarded through agency selection among competing applications.
Cooperative agreement
Federal financial assistance in which substantial federal involvement is anticipated during performance.
State Revolving Fund
A federal-state financing structure using capitalization grants, state contributions, loans, principal forgiveness, repayments, and recycling of funds for water projects.
Federal share
The portion of eligible project cost paid by the federal program.
Nonfederal match
The state, local, Tribal, private, or other contribution required by a federal program.
Advance appropriation
Funding enacted in one law but becoming available in a later fiscal year.
No-year funding
Budget authority remaining available until expended rather than expiring at the end of a stated fiscal period.
Period of availability
The time in which an agency may obligate appropriated funds.
BEAD
The Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment Program, a $42.45 billion state and territory grant program.
Middle mile
Network infrastructure connecting local last-mile networks to major internet backbones, data centers, or exchange points.
Last mile
The final broadband connection between the network and the household, business, or community institution.
Digital equity
The condition in which individuals and communities have the technology, skills, support, and affordable access needed to participate fully in society and the economy.
State Revolving Fund
A long-term water-financing program capitalized by federal and state contributions.
Lead service line
A pipe made of lead or containing lead components connecting a water main to a building’s plumbing.
Emerging contaminant
A chemical or substance, including PFAS, presenting potential health or environmental risk and requiring monitoring or treatment.
National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Program
A $5 billion formula program supporting charging along designated alternative-fuel corridors and a national charging network.
Charging and Fueling Infrastructure Program
A $2.5 billion competitive program divided between corridor and community charging or alternative-fueling projects.
PROTECT
The Promoting Resilient Operations for Transformative, Efficient, and Cost-Saving Transportation program.
Build America, Buy America
The IIJA domestic-content requirements for iron, steel, manufactured products, and construction materials used in covered infrastructure.
Davis-Bacon Related Act
A statute applying prevailing-wage requirements to federally assisted construction.
Environmental review
The process of evaluating environmental effects, alternatives, consultation duties, mitigation, and legal compliance before project approval.
Justice40 Initiative
A Biden-administration policy seeking to direct forty percent of overall benefits from certain federal investments to disadvantaged communities; its founding executive order was revoked in January 2025.
Infrastructure asset
A long-lived physical facility or network such as a road, bridge, pipe, rail line, grid facility, port, airport, broadband network, or treatment system.
Operations and maintenance
Ongoing staffing, repair, energy, inspection, replacement, and management needed after construction.
Life-cycle cost
The total cost of planning, construction, operation, maintenance, rehabilitation, and disposal over an asset’s useful life.

Structure of the Law

Baseline Reauthorization and Supplemental Investment

IIJA combines continuation and expansion of longstanding programs with newly appropriated supplemental funds. Statements about “new spending” generally compare the Act with a baseline in which existing programs continued at expected levels.

Five-Year Surface Transportation Law

The Act authorizes federal highway, transit, highway-safety, motor-carrier, and passenger-rail programs principally for fiscal years 2022 through 2026.

Division J Appropriations

A major portion of the law directly appropriates supplemental amounts to transportation, energy, water, broadband, Interior, Agriculture, FEMA, EPA, Commerce, and other agencies.

Annual Tranches

Many programs receive equal or specified annual amounts for fiscal years 2022 through 2026. Agencies may not obligate a later-year tranche before it becomes available.

Longer Availability

Some funds remain available until expended or for multiple years. Abandoned-mine-land funding extends over fifteen years.

Mandatory and Discretionary Budget Treatment

The Act contains direct spending, contract authority, advance appropriations, discretionary authorizations, user-fee and tax provisions, and rescissions. Budget estimates therefore vary depending on the period and category measured.

Federal Credit

Some provisions support loans or loan subsidies through programs such as TIFIA, Railroad Rehabilitation and Improvement Financing, WIFIA, and energy loan authorities.

Recipient-Level Funding

A project’s federal support may combine several programs, but recipients must prevent duplication of payment for the same cost and maintain auditable cost allocation.

Leverage

Federal funds often combine with state bonds, local revenue, utility rates, private capital, philanthropic support, or other federal programs.

Not Every Authorization Was Pre-Funded

Some authorized programs depend on later annual appropriations and should not be treated as guaranteed five-year cash.

Highways, Bridges, and Major Projects

Federal-Aid Highway Formula Programs

The Act increases the core federal-aid highway program and continues state apportionments for system condition, local flexibility, freight, congestion, safety, planning, Tribal transportation, and federal lands.

Bridge Formula Program

The new formula program distributes $26.675 billion over five years for bridge replacement, rehabilitation, preservation, protection, and construction, with attention to off-system and Tribal bridges.

Bridge Investment Program

Competitive grants support large bridges and other significant projects requiring substantial federal participation.

Megaprojects

The National Infrastructure Project Assistance program supports large, complex projects that are difficult to finance through ordinary formula programs.

RAISE and INFRA

RAISE supports multimodal local and regional projects, while INFRA emphasizes nationally significant freight and highway infrastructure.

Reconnecting Communities

The program funds planning and capital work addressing barriers created by highways, rail lines, or other transportation facilities.

Carbon and Resilience

Carbon Reduction and PROTECT funds support lower-emission strategies and transportation adaptation to flooding, fire, heat, storms, erosion, and other hazards.

State Discretion

Federal law establishes eligibility and broad purposes, but states retain substantial authority over project selection and transfer of some funds.

Public Transportation

Formula Growth

Urbanized Area, Rural Area, State of Good Repair, Seniors and Individuals with Disabilities, and other formula programs receive increased five-year funding.

Capital Investment Grants

The Act provides supplemental appropriations for New Starts, Small Starts, and Core Capacity projects while retaining the multiyear project-development and evaluation process.

Bus Programs

Bus and Bus Facilities and Low or No Emission programs finance fleet replacement, facilities, workforce development, and zero- or low-emission vehicles.

Accessibility

The All Stations Accessibility Program addresses inaccessible legacy rail stations built before modern disability standards.

Rural and Tribal Transit

Dedicated formulas and competitive grants support transit service in rural and Tribal communities.

Operating Challenge

Most IIJA transit funds are capital funds. Agencies still require stable fare revenue, state and local support, and operating assistance to provide service.

Passenger and Freight Rail

Federal-State Partnership

The $36 billion program supports intercity passenger-rail capital projects, including Northeast Corridor and state-supported corridors.

Amtrak

Direct grants support the Northeast Corridor and National Network fleet, stations, facilities, bridges, tunnels, and state-of-good-repair work.

CRISI

Consolidated Rail Infrastructure and Safety Improvements grants support safety, reliability, capacity, grade crossings, short-line railroads, and passenger-rail development.

Railroad Crossing Elimination

The new program supports grade separation, closure, relocation, and other measures reducing delay and collisions.

Corridor Identification

The Act supports planning and development of potential new or improved intercity passenger-rail corridors.

Long Timelines

Rail megaprojects require complex environmental review, property, utility, railroad, labor, engineering, and funding agreements and often extend beyond the Act’s five-year funding window.

Airports, Ports, Ferries, and Waterways

Airports

The law provides $15 billion for airport infrastructure, $5 billion for terminal grants, and additional funding for FAA facilities and equipment.

Terminals

Airport Terminal Program grants can improve capacity, energy performance, accessibility, passenger movement, and aging terminal facilities.

Ports

Port Infrastructure Development grants support cargo, intermodal, safety, resilience, emissions, and supply-chain projects.

Ferries

Programs support ferry boats, terminals, rural ferry service, passenger ferries, and low- or zero-emission vessels.

Army Corps

Separate appropriations support inland waterways, ports, flood-risk projects, ecosystem restoration, and other water-resources infrastructure.

Community Burdens

Ports and airports provide economic benefits but can impose noise, truck traffic, air pollution, displacement, and land-use burdens on nearby communities.

Transportation Safety

Safe Streets and Roads for All

Local, regional, and Tribal grants support comprehensive safety action plans and implementation projects intended to prevent roadway deaths and serious injuries.

Highway Safety Improvement

States use crash and roadway data to identify and address high-risk locations and systemic hazards.

Rail Crossings

Federal funding supports grade separation, warning devices, closures, and corridor projects.

Motor Carrier and Pipeline Safety

The law expands commercial-vehicle safety and provides grants to modernize natural-gas distribution infrastructure.

Vulnerable Road Users

Pedestrian and bicycle safety receives increased attention through state planning, formula programs, and local grants.

Measurement

Safety evaluation should distinguish changes caused by project design, enforcement, travel behavior, vehicle technology, and broader trends.

Electric Vehicles and Clean Transportation

NEVI

The National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Formula Program provides $5 billion for charging along alternative-fuel corridors and a national network.

Charging and Fueling Infrastructure

The $2.5 billion competitive program supports corridor charging and community charging or alternative-fueling facilities.

Clean School Buses

EPA’s $5 billion program funds zero-emission and clean school buses, charging, and related project costs.

Low-No Transit Buses

FTA grants support low- and zero-emission transit buses and facilities.

Ferries

Dedicated programs support electric or low-emitting ferries.

2025 Review

Executive Order 14154 specifically identified EV-charging funds for review. Program implementation and guidance changed under the current administration.

Grid and Siting

Charging deployment depends on electric-service upgrades, utility coordination, real estate, permitting, reliability, payment systems, and maintenance.

Equity and Access

Public benefit depends on station location, pricing, uptime, apartment access, rural coverage, vehicle affordability, and non-EV transportation alternatives.

PFAS

Dedicated emerging-contaminant programs support testing, treatment, source response, and system improvements addressing PFAS and other substances.

Principal Forgiveness

A substantial portion of supplemental SRF capitalization is provided as grants or principal forgiveness to qualifying disadvantaged communities.

Inventory

Replacement depends on accurate service-line inventories and coordination between public and privately owned portions.

Household Cost

Programs must address replacement without shifting unaffordable costs to residents or leaving partial lead lines in service.

Workforce

Large-scale replacement requires plumbers, operators, engineers, excavators, laboratories, inspectors, outreach staff, and restoration crews.

Public Health

Infrastructure funding works alongside drinking-water standards, corrosion control, sampling, public notification, and health policy.

Energy Program Implementation

Grid Resilience

DOE and states support grid hardening, microgrids, wildfire mitigation, undergrounding, sensors, storage, advanced conductors, and restoration capability.

Transmission

Planning, financing, and capacity agreements seek to expand long-distance transmission, but siting and cost allocation remain major barriers.

Batteries and Critical Minerals

Manufacturing and processing awards support domestic supply chains for battery materials, components, cells, recycling, and minerals.

Hydrogen Hubs

Regional hubs combine production, transportation, storage, and end-use projects with different feedstocks and emissions profiles.

Carbon Management

Programs support carbon capture demonstrations, carbon transport, geologic storage, utilization, and direct-air-capture hubs.

Nuclear Energy

The Civil Nuclear Credit Program supports qualifying reactors facing economic closure, while other provisions support advanced-reactor and fuel infrastructure.

Weatherization

Supplemental funding expands residential efficiency improvements for eligible low-income households.

Demonstration Risk

First-of-a-kind projects face technology, construction, market, feedstock, offtake, permitting, and cost-overrun risk.

2025–2026 Reviews

Energy projects and awards are subject to current administration policy, statutory requirements, grant terms, and agency review.

Legacy Pollution, Brownfields, and Recycling

Superfund

EPA received $3.5 billion for remedial work at National Priorities List sites, allowing construction at sites with delayed or unfunded cleanup work.

Brownfields

The law provides $1.5 billion for assessment, cleanup, revolving-loan, multipurpose, state and Tribal response, and job-training activities.

Recycling

Solid Waste Infrastructure for Recycling and education programs support collection, processing, data, markets, and public information.

Orphaned Wells

Interior’s $4.7 billion program funds states, Tribes, and federal land managers to plug wells, remediate sites, measure methane, and restore land.

Abandoned Mine Lands

$11.3 billion over fifteen years supports reclamation of hazards and pollution from historic coal mining.

Economic Revitalization

Cleanup can return land to productive use, reduce hazards, support recreation, and create employment.

Liability and Standards

Federal funding does not automatically eliminate private liability or replace cleanup standards under environmental law.

Community Role

Residents need access to site data, cleanup alternatives, reuse plans, health information, and contractor opportunities.

Resilience, Disaster Mitigation, and Natural Resources

FEMA Programs

The law supplements Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities, Flood Mitigation Assistance, dam safety, cybersecurity, and emergency-management programs.

Transportation Resilience

PROTECT formula and competitive programs address flood, wildfire, heat, erosion, sea-level, storm, and other transportation risks.

Wildfire

Interior and Agriculture investments support fuels reduction, burned-area recovery, fire science, workforce, community protection, and ecosystem restoration.

Western Water

Bureau of Reclamation programs address drought, storage, conveyance, water recycling, desalination, dam safety, ecosystem restoration, and Colorado River sustainability.

Ecosystems

Funding supports fish passage, habitat restoration, invasive-species response, watershed health, and coastal resilience.

Cybersecurity

State and Local Cybersecurity Grants and related investments support planning, risk assessment, protection, and resilience of public systems.

Climate Policy Change

The law’s resilience authorities remain, while federal climate-policy framing changed after January 2025.

Risk-Based Planning

Effective resilience investment uses future hazard, vulnerability, consequence, redundancy, and life-cycle analysis rather than rebuilding identical exposure.

Tribal Nations, Rural Communities, and Territories

Direct Tribal Investment

The Act includes Tribal transportation, broadband, sanitation, orphaned-well, water-rights, resilience, and public-land programs and makes Tribes eligible for many national competitions.

Indian Water Rights

Interior received $2.5 billion to help fulfill previously enacted Indian water-rights settlements.

Sanitation

Indian Health Service funding addresses water, sewer, solid-waste, and sanitation backlogs.

Tribal Transportation

Road, bridge, transit, safety, and high-priority-project programs support mobility on Tribal lands.

Rural Broadband

BEAD, ReConnect, Middle Mile, and Tribal programs address high-cost and low-density areas.

Rural Transportation

Dedicated rural surface grants, ferry programs, airports, transit, and bridge funds address long distances and limited alternatives.

Territories

Statutory minimums and special allocations support broadband, highways, water, airports, transit, energy, and resilience in territories.

Capacity

Small governments need technical assistance, flexible match, project development, procurement support, and long-term operations planning.

Consultation and Sovereignty

Federal agencies must respect Tribal sovereignty, treaty rights, consultation duties, cultural resources, and self-governance.

Implementation and Governance

Federal Coordination

Implementation is distributed across at least fifteen departments and agencies. The largest include Transportation, Energy, EPA, Commerce, Interior, Agriculture, Homeland Security, the Federal Communications Commission, the Army Corps of Engineers, and the Indian Health Service.

State Role

States receive major formula funds, prepare plans, select projects, administer subawards, provide matching funds, conduct procurement, enforce requirements, and report performance.

Local Role

Cities, counties, transit agencies, ports, airports, utilities, metropolitan planning organizations, school districts, and public authorities develop projects and apply directly or through state programs.

Tribal Governments

Tribes can receive direct allocations or competitive grants, participate in state programs, consult on federal actions, and administer infrastructure under self-determination and self-governance authorities.

Private and Nonprofit Entities

Depending on the program, telecommunications providers, electric utilities, railroads, manufacturers, nonprofits, cooperatives, colleges, and public-private partnerships can participate.

Planning Before Construction

Many awards initially finance planning, preliminary engineering, environmental review, design, permitting, stakeholder engagement, or right-of-way acquisition rather than immediate construction.

Grant Agreement

A public announcement or selection does not always equal an obligation. Agencies and recipients may need to negotiate scope, milestones, budget, conditions, and environmental requirements before executing an award.

Reimbursement

Many programs reimburse eligible costs after the recipient incurs and documents them, requiring cash management or interim financing.

Performance Period

Recipients must complete work within the grant period or obtain authorized extensions.

Closeout

Final reimbursement, asset records, audits, property management, performance reports, and retention of records continue after construction.

Formula and Competitive Funding

Formula Advantages

Formula funding provides predictability, broad geographic distribution, and continuity for state and local capital programs.

Formula Limitations

Statutory factors can reflect historic population, lane miles, usage, or program structure rather than current need, racial inequity, project readiness, or national significance.

Competitive Advantages

Competitive grants allow agencies to select high-impact projects, reward planning, support national priorities, and assist recipients that lack a large formula share.

Competitive Burdens

Applications can require engineering, benefit-cost analysis, environmental documentation, matching funds, public engagement, and specialized grant-writing capacity.

Planning Grants

Programs such as Safe Streets and Roads for All, Reconnecting Communities, broadband, and resilience can fund planning to help applicants develop implementation-ready projects.

Rural and Tribal Barriers

Small governments can struggle with staff, data, procurement, grant management, and match. Technical assistance and reduced-match provisions can improve access.

Suballocation

Some state formula programs require portions to be suballocated to metropolitan areas, local governments, or specified uses.

Transferability

Certain transportation funds can be transferred among programs within statutory limits, affecting the amount ultimately spent for the originally named purpose.

Build America, Buy America

Statutory Rule

For covered federal financial assistance used for infrastructure, agencies generally must ensure that all iron and steel, manufactured products, and construction materials incorporated into the project are produced in the United States.

Iron and Steel

All manufacturing processes for iron and steel products generally must occur in the United States, from the initial melting stage through application of coatings, subject to the governing standards and waivers.

Manufactured Products

The product must be manufactured in the United States and meet the applicable domestic-component-cost standard.

Construction Materials

Covered materials include categories such as nonferrous metals, plastic and polymer-based products, glass, lumber, and drywall under government-wide regulations.

Waivers

An agency can issue a public-interest, nonavailability, or unreasonable-cost waiver after required review and public notice.

Interaction with Older Buy America Rules

Transportation, water, and other programs may have preexisting domestic-content laws. Agencies determine how the older requirements and BABA apply together.

Project Effects

Domestic preferences can support manufacturing and supply-chain investment but can also create procurement delays, documentation burdens, price changes, or product-availability disputes.

Current Implementation

Made in America waiver notices and agency guidance remain central sources for determining whether a particular product or project is covered.

Labor Standards and Workforce Development

Davis-Bacon Coverage

The Department of Labor states that a vast majority of IIJA-authorized federal funding requires prevailing wages on covered construction.

Prevailing Wage

Contractors and subcontractors must pay covered laborers and mechanics at least the locally prevailing wage and fringe benefits listed in the applicable federal wage determination.

Certified Payroll

Covered contractors maintain and submit payroll records documenting classifications, hours, wages, and deductions.

Apprentices

Apprentices may be paid approved apprentice rates when individually registered in a qualifying apprenticeship program and employed within applicable ratios and standards.

Workforce Development

Programs can support registered apprenticeship, pre-apprenticeship, community-college training, on-the-job training, supportive services, and construction-career outreach.

Local Hire

Transportation law and program guidance can permit local or economic hiring preferences under applicable federal rules.

Disadvantaged Business Enterprises

DOT-assisted contracts continue to use the Disadvantaged Business Enterprise program, while the IIJA also funds training and assistance.

Worker Safety

Occupational Safety and Health Act standards, state plans, workers’ compensation, and industry-specific safety requirements apply.

Job Quality

Wage rules improve pay on covered construction, but employment duration, benefits, scheduling, worker voice, advancement, and post-construction jobs vary by project.

Environmental Review and Permitting

National Environmental Policy Act

Federal agencies assess environmental effects and alternatives through categorical exclusions, environmental assessments, or environmental impact statements depending on the project.

One Federal Decision

The Act codified and revised procedures intended to coordinate federal environmental review and establish schedules for major projects.

Permitting Council

The law made the Federal Permitting Improvement Steering Council permanent and amended the FAST-41 process for covered projects.

Clean Water and Wetlands

Projects can require permits for discharges, wetlands, dredge-and-fill activity, wastewater, stormwater, and water-quality certification.

Historic Preservation

Section 106 consultation evaluates effects on historic properties and can require Tribal, state, local, and public participation.

Endangered Species

Federal agencies consult concerning listed species and critical habitat when a project may affect them.

Environmental Justice

Environmental review can analyze disproportionate burdens even though the federal executive-policy framework changed in 2025.

Project Acceleration

Supporters seek faster, coordinated decisions. Critics warn that compressed review can reduce public participation, alternatives analysis, and protection of affected communities.

Litigation

Permits, environmental documents, procurement decisions, and grant terms can be challenged administratively or in court, affecting schedules and cost.

Civil Rights, Accessibility, and Public Participation

Title VI

Recipients of federal financial assistance may not discriminate based on race, color, or national origin under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Section 504 and the ADA

Infrastructure and transportation programs must provide nondiscrimination and accessibility for people with disabilities under applicable federal law.

Environmental Justice History

The original implementation emphasized disadvantaged communities and Justice40. The executive policy changed in 2025, but statutory nondiscrimination and program-specific requirements remain.

Limited English Proficiency

Recipients must provide meaningful access to people with limited English proficiency under applicable Title VI policy and law.

Public Participation

Planning and environmental-review processes should provide timely, understandable, and accessible opportunities for affected communities.

Displacement

Projects acquiring property can trigger relocation assistance, replacement-housing requirements, compensation, and procedural safeguards.

Contracting

Disadvantaged-business, small-business, labor, and civil-rights programs seek to broaden access to contracts and jobs, but participation depends on implementation and enforcement.

Data

Agencies and recipients use demographic, geographic, environmental, accessibility, and service data to evaluate effects, though data quality and policy priorities differ.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Highway Displacement

Urban expressways destroyed or divided many Black neighborhoods. Reconnecting Communities planning and capital grants can remove, retrofit, cap, redesign, or mitigate some transportation barriers.

Transit Dependence

Black households are disproportionately represented among public-transit riders in many metropolitan areas. Formula, state-of-good-repair, accessibility, bus, and capital grants can improve reliability and access.

Lead Exposure

Older housing and water systems in Black and low-income neighborhoods are more likely to contain lead service lines or lead plumbing. Dedicated replacement funds can reduce exposure when programs locate and replace lines equitably.

Water Affordability

Capital grants and principal forgiveness can reduce debt burdens, but new infrastructure can still lead to higher utility rates if operating costs, local match, and long-term maintenance are not addressed.

Flooding and Sewer Failure

Combined sewers, undersized drainage, pavement, subsidence, and historical land-use decisions create severe flooding and sewage exposure in many Black communities.

Industrial Pollution

Superfund, brownfields, orphaned wells, abandoned mines, port electrification, clean buses, and pollution-prevention programs can reduce legacy burdens.

Digital Divide

BEAD and middle-mile construction can improve availability, while the expiration of the Affordable Connectivity Program illustrates that infrastructure access does not guarantee household affordability.

Energy Burden

Weatherization, grid reliability, efficiency, and community-energy investments can reduce costs and outages, but program eligibility, landlord participation, and utility-rate design affect results.

Contracting Opportunity

Infrastructure spending can expand work for Black contractors and workers, but bonding, capital, procurement scale, payment delay, certification, networking, and discrimination remain barriers.

Skilled Trades

Prevailing wages, apprenticeships, pre-apprenticeships, community-benefit agreements, supportive services, and local hiring can widen access to construction and technical careers.

Gentrification Risk

Improved transit, parks, broadband, waterfronts, and environmental cleanup can increase property values and displacement unless paired with affordable housing, tenant protection, community ownership, and anti-displacement policy.

Governance

Equity depends on whether affected Black residents influence project selection, design, construction mitigation, contracting, operations, and performance measurement.

Maintenance

Communities harmed by disinvestment need sustained operations and repair, not only ribbon-cutting projects.

Accountability

Meaningful measures include travel time, service reliability, water quality, household cost, flood reduction, pollution, job retention, contract participation, displacement, and long-term asset condition.

Oversight, Spending Data, and Accountability

Government Accountability Office

GAO reviews obligations, outlays, grant management, agency risk, performance reporting, project reviews, fraud controls, and program-specific implementation.

Inspectors General

Agency inspectors general audit program design, awards, procurement, eligibility, data, controls, fraud risks, and project performance.

USAspending.gov

Federal award data can be searched by recipient, agency, account, program, location, and award, but users must distinguish obligations from outlays and IIJA from other funding.

Agency Dashboards

DOT, EPA, DOE, NTIA, Interior, and other agencies maintain program lists, state allocations, award announcements, maps, or performance data with varying definitions and update schedules.

Recipient Audits

State and local recipients are subject to the Single Audit Act, Uniform Guidance, internal controls, procurement rules, and recordkeeping.

Payment Integrity

Large, new, and rapidly expanded grant programs face risks involving improper payments, ineligible costs, duplicate funding, fraud, conflicts of interest, and weak subrecipient monitoring.

Performance Reporting

GAO found variation among agencies in the availability and maturity of public performance information.

Announcement Versus Completion

Award announcements measure federal commitments or selections, not construction completion or delivered public benefit.

Cancellation and Review

The 2025–2026 agency reviews produced approvals, modifications, cancellations, and pending decisions. Recipients can face legal, contractual, financing, and schedule consequences when awards change.

Expiration

Funds can expire or be reallocated when statutory deadlines are missed. GAO reported several hundred million dollars of DOT IIJA balances expiring or becoming unavailable to original recipients in 2025.

Current Status in 2026

Final Major Formula Year

Fiscal year 2026 is the final scheduled tranche for many five-year transportation, water, energy, broadband, and resilience appropriations.

Construction Continues Beyond 2026

Obligation and construction periods can extend after the year in which budget authority becomes available. Many large rail, bridge, transit, broadband, grid, and water projects will continue for years.

Selected Agency Funding

GAO reported that DOT, EPA, Interior, and NTIA had approximately $574.7 billion in IIJA budget authority for fiscal years 2022 through 2025 and had obligated approximately 76 percent.

Disbursement

The same GAO review found that the four agencies had disbursed approximately 54 percent of obligated IIJA funds.

Award Reviews

The agencies reviewed awards for consistency with administration policy, approving most reviewed projects but cancelling or leaving pending significant amounts.

BEAD

BEAD implementation continues under the June 2025 restructuring, with revised final proposals, technology-neutral selection, savings calculations, and early 2026 connections in approved states.

Affordable Connectivity Program

The infrastructure law’s principal household broadband subsidy is concluded because Congress did not replenish the fund.

EPA

Water, cleanup, recycling, and Clean School Bus programs continue awarding, obligating, and disbursing IIJA funds.

Transportation

Formula programs continue, while large discretionary projects remain in grant-agreement, environmental-review, design, procurement, or construction stages.

Energy

Grid, transmission, battery, hydrogen, carbon-management, nuclear, weatherization, and demonstration projects continue under DOE program rules and current administration review.

Statutory Continuity

Executive orders can change priorities and administration but cannot repeal statutory appropriations or program requirements without lawful authority or congressional action.

Long-Term Evaluation

Definitive evaluation will require completed-project data, asset condition, service outcomes, household affordability, economic effects, and maintenance performance.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Asset Repair

The law provides resources for deferred maintenance and replacement of roads, bridges, transit fleets, rail infrastructure, airports, water systems, and other aging assets.

New Capacity

Programs can add passenger rail, broadband, transmission, charging, port, transit, water-treatment, and resilience capacity.

Safety

Bridge, road, rail-crossing, pipeline, motor-carrier, transit, airport, water, and environmental programs can reduce injuries, failures, exposure, and operational risk.

Public Health

Lead replacement, PFAS treatment, wastewater upgrades, school buses, Superfund cleanup, brownfields, and pollution prevention can improve health.

Economic Productivity

Reliable transportation, freight, energy, broadband, and water systems support business operations, labor access, trade, and private investment.

Employment

Planning, design, manufacturing, construction, operations, and maintenance create jobs and training demand.

Domestic Manufacturing

Build America, Buy America and sector investments can strengthen domestic production of construction materials, buses, rail equipment, chargers, batteries, grid components, and other products.

Resilience

Projects can reduce damage from flood, fire, drought, heat, storms, erosion, outages, and other hazards.

Rural and Tribal Investment

Dedicated programs and eligibility changes expand access for rural communities, territories, and Tribal Nations.

Planning Capacity

Technical assistance and planning grants can help communities develop projects that were previously unfunded or incomplete.

Limitations and Risks

Construction Inflation

Rapid demand for labor, equipment, steel, cement, electrical components, transformers, and specialized services can reduce purchasing power.

Project Readiness

Some recipients lack completed design, environmental documents, permits, right-of-way, match, or procurement capacity.

Workforce Shortages

Engineers, grant managers, skilled trades, inspectors, operators, and specialized contractors are constrained in some regions.

Administrative Burden

Hundreds of programs with different rules, deadlines, matches, and reporting systems create complexity.

Unequal Capacity

Large states, utilities, universities, consultants, and metropolitan governments can compete more effectively than small or under-resourced communities.

Operations and Maintenance

Federal construction grants do not always provide sufficient long-term operating revenue, staffing, replacement reserves, or maintenance.

Affordability

A new network or facility can remain unaffordable to households through fares, tolls, utility rates, connection charges, energy bills, or broadband prices.

Displacement

Infrastructure improvements can increase land value and accelerate residential or commercial displacement.

Environmental Effects

Construction can disturb habitat, increase emissions, induce development, or create localized burdens despite broader public benefits.

Domestic-Content Constraints

BABA can support manufacturing but also produce documentation disputes and delays where domestic products are unavailable.

Federal Policy Change

Executive review, revised priorities, grant termination, litigation, and administrative turnover create uncertainty.

Measurement

Announcements and obligations do not measure completed infrastructure, service quality, or distribution of benefits.

Fragmentation

Transportation, housing, land use, broadband, energy, water, and economic development are administered by separate institutions that may not coordinate.

Debt and Fiscal Tradeoffs

The law increases federal spending and requires future governments to manage debt, maintenance, and program continuation.

Major Policy Debates

Total Size and Deficit Effect

Supporters view the Act as overdue investment; critics question federal borrowing, offsets, project selection, and the definition of infrastructure.

Formula Versus Competition

Formula funds provide certainty, while competitive grants can target national priorities but reward applicants with greater capacity.

Repair Versus Expansion

Policy debate concerns whether funds should prioritize maintenance and state of good repair or create new capacity and corridors.

Highways Versus Multimodal Investment

States can devote large amounts to roads, while transit, rail, walking, bicycling, and demand management compete for attention and match.

Climate Policy

The prior administration emphasized decarbonization and resilience; the current administration emphasizes energy dominance, cost, speed, technology neutrality, and statutory limits.

EV Charging

Debate concerns consumer demand, charging technology, corridor placement, federal authority, state plans, and the speed of deployment.

Broadband Technology

Fiber offers high capacity and longevity, while fixed wireless, cable, low-earth-orbit satellite, and other technologies can lower cost or speed deployment in some areas.

Broadband Affordability

The end of ACP created a gap between capital investment and low-income household affordability.

Permitting

Project sponsors seek faster approvals, while communities and environmental advocates seek meaningful review, alternatives, and mitigation.

Domestic Content

Manufacturing policy can create jobs and supply security but may conflict with project schedules and cost when domestic capacity is limited.

Labor Standards

Prevailing wage and apprenticeship can improve job quality, while contractors and some states argue that requirements raise cost or restrict labor supply.

Environmental Justice

Debate concerns whether infrastructure should explicitly correct historic racial and environmental inequities or use facially neutral cost and performance criteria.

Local Control

States and localities understand local conditions, but national funding creates federal interests in civil rights, safety, environment, labor, and accountability.

Grant Cancellation

The 2025–2026 reviews raise questions about executive discretion, reliance interests, appropriations law, contract rights, and congressional control of spending.

Key Distinctions

IIJA and the Inflation Reduction Act

IIJA primarily funds physical infrastructure, transportation, broadband, water, grid, and demonstrations. The Inflation Reduction Act relies more heavily on tax incentives, climate programs, health provisions, and additional energy investment.

IIJA and the CHIPS and Science Act

The CHIPS and Science Act focuses on semiconductor incentives, research, science agencies, and technology policy, although its manufacturing goals overlap with infrastructure supply chains.

Authorization and Appropriation

Authorization permits a program; appropriation provides budget authority. IIJA contains both.

Award Announcement and Obligation

An announced selection may still require a completed grant agreement before the government is legally obligated.

Obligation and Outlay

An obligation is a legal commitment. An outlay is an actual payment.

Formula and Discretionary Grant

Formula funds are distributed by statutory factors. Discretionary grants are selected competitively or by agency judgment.

Allocation and Project Completion

An allocation reserves or assigns funds; it does not mean construction has begun or ended.

Infrastructure Availability and Affordability

A broadband, transit, water, or energy service may physically exist but remain unaffordable.

Capital and Operations

Capital funds build or rehabilitate assets. Operating funds pay for staffing, energy, routine maintenance, service, and administration.

Build America, Buy America and Buy American Act

BABA applies to covered federal financial assistance for infrastructure. The Buy American Act principally governs federal procurement.

Davis-Bacon and Minimum Wage

Davis-Bacon sets locally prevailing wages for covered construction, which can be higher than general federal or state minimum wages.

Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and Bipartisan Safer Communities Act

They are separate statutes addressing different subjects despite similar informal names.

Geographic Scope

IIJA programs operate throughout the fifty states, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, United States territories, Tribal lands, federal lands, interstate corridors, navigable waterways, and coastal areas.

Formula programs distribute funding broadly, but amounts vary according to population, lane miles, vehicle miles, transit service, bridge condition, unemployment, broadband need, water-system data, statutory minimums, and other factors.

Competitive awards cluster around project readiness, eligibility, cost, national significance, benefit-cost performance, statutory criteria, agency priorities, matching resources, and application quality.

Territories receive transportation, broadband, water, energy, disaster, and technical-assistance funding under program-specific rules and minimum allocations.

Tribal programs include transportation, broadband, sanitation, water-rights settlements, orphaned wells, roads, bridges, transit, resilience, and competitive access to many national programs.

Rural areas benefit from dedicated rural transportation, broadband, water, electric, airport, ferry, and technical-assistance programs but can face limited staff, long distances, small markets, and high unit costs.

Urban areas receive transit, rail, reconnecting, port, airport, water, lead, brownfields, bus, safety, and broadband investment but face displacement, congestion, land cost, and complex permitting.

Western states receive major drought, water-storage, conveyance, ecosystem, wildfire, and Colorado River-related investments.

Appalachian and other legacy-energy regions receive abandoned-mine, orphaned-well, water, broadband, transportation, and economic-redevelopment funding.

Project effects can cross jurisdictional boundaries, requiring interstate compacts, metropolitan planning, watershed coordination, utility agreements, corridor planning, and federal land consultation.

Legal Context

Public Law 117-58

The enacted Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act is the controlling statutory text.

Title 23

Federal highway programs, planning, safety, bridges, resilience, carbon reduction, and EV infrastructure are principally codified in Title 23 of the United States Code.

Title 49

Transit, rail, aviation, motor-vehicle, motor-carrier, pipeline, freight, and transportation-safety provisions appear throughout Title 49.

Clean Water Act

The law expands Clean Water State Revolving Fund and related water-quality programs.

Safe Drinking Water Act

The law expands Drinking Water State Revolving Fund, lead-service-line, and emerging-contaminant assistance.

National Telecommunications and Information Administration statutes

Division F establishes and funds major broadband and digital-equity programs.

Energy Policy Act and Department of Energy Organization Act

The Act creates or expands energy research, demonstration, deployment, grid, hydrogen, nuclear, carbon, and efficiency authorities.

Build America, Buy America Act

Title IX creates a government-wide domestic-content preference for covered infrastructure financial assistance.

National Environmental Policy Act

IIJA amends project-review and permitting procedures and leaves projects subject to environmental analysis according to law.

Davis-Bacon and Related Acts

Prevailing-wage requirements apply to most covered IIJA construction through existing or newly incorporated statutory provisions.

Summary

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act is a comprehensive 2021 federal law reauthorizing transportation programs and funding infrastructure across transportation, broadband, water, energy, environmental cleanup, resilience, and public works.

President Joseph R. Biden Jr. signed Public Law 117-58 on November 15, 2021. The statute is commonly called the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law.

The Act is often described as approximately $1.2 trillion in total activity, including roughly $550 billion above baseline, but budget authority, grants, obligations, outlays, and project costs are distinct measures.

Transportation receives the largest share. DOT administers more than $551 billion through highways, bridges, transit, rail, aviation, ports, safety, ferries, EV charging, and major-project programs.

The law invests $65 billion in broadband, including $42.45 billion for BEAD, $14.2 billion for the Affordable Connectivity Program, $2.75 billion for Digital Equity, and $1 billion for middle mile.

The Affordable Connectivity Program ended June 1, 2024 after its appropriation was exhausted, demonstrating that network construction and household affordability require different policies.

EPA administers more than $50 billion for drinking water and wastewater, including $15 billion dedicated to lead-service-line replacement and major funding for PFAS and emerging contaminants.

DOE administers more than $62 billion for grid, transmission, batteries, hydrogen, carbon management, nuclear energy, weatherization, efficiency, manufacturing, and demonstrations.

Interior administers orphaned-well, abandoned-mine, drought, western-water, Tribal, wildfire, and ecosystem programs, including $4.7 billion for orphaned wells and $11.3 billion over fifteen years for abandoned mine lands.

The Act uses formula grants, competitive grants, loans, cooperative agreements, contracts, direct federal projects, and technical assistance.

The Build America, Buy America Act applies domestic-content preferences to covered infrastructure financial assistance. Most IIJA-assisted construction is also subject to Davis-Bacon prevailing-wage rules.

Projects remain subject to environmental review, civil rights, accessibility, procurement, relocation, safety, auditing, and program-specific requirements.

GAO identified approximately $711.8 billion available for grants to Tribes, states, localities, and territories across more than one hundred programs, including $131.2 billion becoming available in fiscal year 2026.

A July 2026 GAO review found that four major agencies had obligated approximately 76 percent of their fiscal year 2022–2025 IIJA budget authority and disbursed about 54 percent of the obligated amount.

Implementation shifted after Executive Order 14154 directed review of selected programs and awards. GAO reported approvals, cancellations, and thousands of pending award decisions during the review process.

Fiscal year 2026 is the final scheduled funding tranche for many programs, but design, procurement, construction, reimbursement, and operation will continue well beyond 2026.

For African American communities, IIJA programs can address highway displacement, transit underinvestment, lead exposure, flooding, sewer failures, pollution, broadband gaps, energy burden, and barriers to contracting and skilled employment.

Equitable benefit is not automatic. Outcomes depend on local project selection, affordability, anti-displacement safeguards, civil-rights enforcement, contracting access, workforce pathways, public participation, maintenance, and measurement.

The Act’s ultimate success must be judged by completed infrastructure and durable public outcomes rather than the number or dollar value of announced awards alone.
