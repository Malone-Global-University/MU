COINTELPRO

The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s 1956–1971 counterintelligence and covert-action programs designed to disrupt, discredit, expose, or neutralize selected domestic political organizations, movements, leaders, and individuals.

What Was COINTELPRO?

COINTELPRO was the FBI’s internal name for a series of secret counterintelligence programs that applied covert-action methods to domestic political organizations and individuals.

The Bureau did not merely watch targeted groups. It attempted to alter events by weakening organizations, generating suspicion, destroying alliances, discouraging membership, disrupting leadership, damaging reputations, and provoking conflict.

The term is now also used more broadly in public discussion for related FBI domestic-intelligence abuses, although not every abusive FBI operation was formally captioned COINTELPRO.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines COINTELPRO’s Cold War origins, program structure, target categories, methods, relationship to surveillance, the campaigns against Black freedom movements and other political organizations, the Martin Luther King Jr. investigation, the Black Panther Party, Fred Hampton, the Ku Klux Klan, Puerto Rican independence activism, the New Left, the Media burglary, Freedom of Information litigation, Church Committee findings, civil cases, reforms, archival access, current debates, and the proposed 2026 disclosure legislation.

Geographic Scope

COINTELPRO was directed from FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C. and implemented through field offices across the United States.

Field offices proposed, executed, and reported local actions. Headquarters approved many proposals, circulated successful techniques, imposed quotas or expectations, and coordinated national objectives.

FBI Vault files demonstrate activity in major cities, smaller communities, universities, prisons, political conventions, and territories including Puerto Rico.

Historical Context

COINTELPRO developed during the Cold War, the Red Scare, decolonization, the Civil Rights Movement, the Vietnam War, urban rebellions, the rise of Black Power, student protest, and widespread conflict over race, class, war, and political authority.

Federal officials faced genuine foreign espionage, political violence, bombings, and organized white supremacist terror. The FBI also treated lawful dissent, ideological advocacy, racial protest, and challenges to established institutions as security problems.

Cold War Anticommunism

The first formal program was created in 1956 against the Communist Party USA, which the FBI viewed as connected to Soviet intelligence, propaganda, and political influence.

The Bureau argued that court decisions and declining membership made ordinary prosecution insufficient and that covert disruption was needed to contain the party.

Supreme Court and Legal Constraints

The Church Committee found that COINTELPRO began partly from FBI frustration with Supreme Court decisions limiting overt government action against dissident organizations.

Rather than treating constitutional limits as controlling boundaries, Bureau officials sometimes regarded covert action as an alternative means of achieving goals that could not lawfully be reached through prosecution.

Domestic Intelligence Before COINTELPRO

The FBI maintained domestic-security, racial-matters, Communist-infiltration, and political-intelligence files before 1956.

COINTELPRO formalized covert disruption within a larger intelligence system that included informants, wiretaps, physical surveillance, mail monitoring, records collection, liaison reporting, and public-source monitoring.

J. Edgar Hoover

Hoover served as FBI Director from 1924 until his death in 1972.

He exercised unusually centralized control, cultivated congressional and media relationships, and viewed communism, Black militancy, antiwar activism, and challenges to the Bureau as threats to national order and FBI authority.

Headquarters directives issued under Hoover shaped the targets, tone, and aggressiveness of COINTELPRO.

William C. Sullivan

Sullivan directed important FBI domestic-intelligence operations and was a senior official associated with COINTELPRO and the campaign against Martin Luther King Jr.

His later testimony and records helped congressional investigators reconstruct the Bureau’s decision-making and internal conflicts.

Headquarters and Field Offices

FBI headquarters opened programs, defined objectives, approved many proposals, evaluated field-office performance, and distributed examples of techniques.

Field offices gathered local intelligence, proposed actions, used informants, contacted institutions, created anonymous communications, and reported results.

Why Program Counts Differ

Federal records schedules describe seven separate counterintelligence programs: five directed at domestic groups and two foreign-counterintelligence programs.

FBI Vault collections separately identify White Hate Groups, New Left, Puerto Rican Groups, Black Extremist, Hoodwink, Cuba, Socialist Workers Party, and Espionage Programs.

The differences reflect changing captions, archival organization, overlapping operations, and uncertainty about what should be counted as a separate formal program rather than a project or file series.

Five Principal Domestic Programs

  1. Communist Party, USA, 1956–1971.
  2. Socialist Workers Party, 1961–1970.
  3. White Hate Groups, 1964–1971.
  4. Black Extremists, 1967–1971.
  5. New Left, 1968–1971.

These dates and categories follow the National Archives records schedule describing the five principal domestic programs.

Foreign-Counterintelligence Programs

The same National Archives schedule identifies two foreign-counterintelligence programs: Espionage or Soviet-Satellite Intelligence, 1964–1971, and Special Operations, 1967–1971.

Their inclusion demonstrates that the FBI used the COINTELPRO label across both domestic-political and foreign-intelligence contexts.

Communist Party USA Program

The 1956 program sought to weaken the Communist Party USA, exploit internal disputes, discourage recruitment, discredit leaders, interfere with finances, and expose or deepen divisions.

The Bureau justified the program as a response to Soviet influence and espionage, but actions also affected lawful political advocacy and association.

Socialist Workers Party Program

The Socialist Workers Party program began in 1961 and targeted the party, its youth organization, and people or organizations connected to demonstrations the party helped sponsor.

The Church Committee found that the program reached people outside the party who joined antiwar or civil-rights activity.

White Hate Groups Program

The FBI opened the White Hate Groups program in 1964 against Ku Klux Klan organizations and related white supremacist groups.

Tactics included infiltration, exploiting leadership disputes, exposing members, encouraging defections, disrupting meetings, and supplying information for prosecution.

Some actions helped weaken violent Klan organizations, but the same secret and extralegal framework raised constitutional and accountability problems.

Black Extremist Program

The FBI opened the program captioned “Black Nationalist–Hate Groups” or “Black Extremist” in 1967.

It targeted organizations ranging from the Black Panther Party and Nation of Islam to SNCC, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, Black student groups, and local community organizations.

The category collapsed major ideological differences among revolutionary organizations, nonviolent civil-rights groups, religious organizations, student associations, and community programs.

New Left Program

The New Left program began in 1968 and targeted Students for a Democratic Society, antiwar organizations, student newspapers, alternative schools, campus groups, professors, clergy, and other critics of the Vietnam War and established institutions.

The breadth of the category made lawful protest, speech, publication, and association vulnerable to covert disruption.

Puerto Rican Independence Groups

FBI archives preserve an extensive COINTELPRO Puerto Rican Groups series concerning independence organizations and activists.

Operations sought intelligence, exposed or deepened factional disputes, discouraged membership, and disrupted political activity in Puerto Rico and mainland communities.

The campaign must be understood within Puerto Rico’s colonial status, independence movement, political violence, and federal territorial authority.

Cuba-Related Operations

FBI Vault holdings include a Cuba COINTELPRO series involving organizations and individuals connected to Cuba, travel, solidarity activity, or perceived Cuban influence.

These files overlapped foreign counterintelligence, domestic political activity, and Cold War restrictions concerning Cuba.

Operation Hoodwink

FBI archives preserve a COINTELPRO series known as Hoodwink.

The operation attempted to encourage conflict between organized-crime figures and Communist-linked targets by transmitting deceptive information.

It illustrates the Bureau’s willingness to manipulate dangerous nonstate actors rather than rely only on lawful investigation.

What Were the Purposes of COINTELPRO?

The Church Committee identified three broad purposes:

  1. Protecting national security.
  2. Preventing violence.
  3. Maintaining the existing social and political order by disrupting or neutralizing perceived threats.

The third purpose allowed political disagreement and social change to be treated as security threats even without evidence of criminal activity.

“Disrupt” and “Neutralize”

FBI directives frequently used terms such as disrupt, expose, discredit, misdirect, isolate, or neutralize.

These terms did not require arrest or prosecution. Success could mean a cancelled speech, lost job, broken alliance, damaged marriage, reduced donations, leadership dispute, public suspicion, or organizational collapse.

First Amendment Activity as a Target

The Church Committee found direct attacks on speaking, teaching, writing, meeting, organizing, publishing, and political association.

Many targets had not committed violent crimes. Their inclusion rested on ideology, organizational affiliation, rhetoric, protest activity, or perceived influence.

National-Security Claims

Genuine foreign intelligence and espionage concerns were used to justify some Communist Party operations.

The rationale expanded into actions against domestic groups whose primary activities were political advocacy, protest, racial justice, or opposition to war.

Violence-Prevention Claims

The FBI argued that disruption could prevent riots, bombings, armed conflict, and political violence.

Congressional investigators found that the Bureau sometimes increased danger by encouraging hostility, spreading false accusations, or exploiting violent factional conflict.

Informants

Informants attended meetings, reported membership and plans, supplied documents, identified leaders, described personal relationships, and sometimes influenced internal decisions.

Informants varied from passive sources to active participants. Their actions could affect organizational direction and create disputes about entrapment, provocation, credibility, and agency responsibility.

Undercover Agents

Undercover agents or covert sources entered organizations under false identities.

Infiltration can be lawful for investigating crime, but COINTELPRO used infiltration to manipulate lawful political activity and relationships in addition to collecting evidence.

Anonymous Letters

The FBI sent anonymous or pseudonymous letters to leaders, spouses, employers, universities, newspapers, donors, clergy, and rival organizations.

Letters could accuse targets of betrayal, sexual misconduct, financial dishonesty, police cooperation, ideological deviation, or personal disloyalty.

Forged and Deceptive Correspondence

Agents created communications designed to appear as if written by members, rivals, community residents, or independent critics.

Forgery allowed the government to conceal its role while causing recipients to act on false information.

Poison-Pen Letters

The Church Committee documented degrading anonymous letters intended to break marriages and personal relationships.

Such tactics bore little connection to gathering evidence or preventing imminent crime and instead used private vulnerability as a weapon.

False Informant Labels

The FBI sometimes falsely suggested that a person was a police or FBI informant.

In organizations where suspected informants faced assault or death, such deception created severe physical danger.

Exploitation of Factional Conflict

Agents identified ideological, regional, racial, generational, personal, and leadership disputes and attempted to intensify them.

Tactics included anonymous accusations, forged documents, selective disclosures, and communications designed to provoke mistrust or retaliation.

Encouraging Violent Conflict

The Church Committee found examples of the FBI encouraging gang warfare or hostility among violent organizations.

Such conduct placed targeted people, organization members, and uninvolved community residents at risk and blurred the line between intelligence work and government-instigated violence.

Media Manipulation

The FBI supplied information, encouraged articles, contacted editors and reporters, circulated negative material, and used public-relations relationships to shape coverage.

Recipients were not always told that material originated from a covert government campaign.

Friendly Media Contacts

The Bureau maintained relationships with journalists and publishers who could circulate information or themes useful to FBI objectives.

These relationships allowed secret intelligence claims to enter public debate without transparent sourcing or an opportunity for targets to challenge the evidence.

Employer Pressure

FBI offices contacted employers or transmitted derogatory information to threaten jobs and professional standing.

Employment consequences punished political activity without criminal charge, trial, or meaningful opportunity to respond.

Schools and Universities

The Bureau sent information to university administrators, school officials, governing boards, and donors to discredit students, professors, speakers, publications, and campus organizations.

Actions interfered with academic freedom, student speech, employment, and institutional autonomy.

Speaking Engagements

COINTELPRO actions attempted to prevent speakers from appearing at colleges, churches, public events, and political meetings.

Techniques included anonymous complaints, derogatory information, pressure on venues, and warnings to sponsors.

Meetings and Demonstrations

The FBI sought to disrupt meetings, conventions, demonstrations, conferences, and coalition activity.

Actions could generate internal conflict, discourage attendance, create logistical problems, or encourage authorities and private institutions to deny access.

Financial Disruption

COINTELPRO actions targeted donations, bank relationships, employment, legal-defense funds, speaking income, and organizational finances.

Financial pressure could weaken lawful organizations without the government proving any crime.

Tax and Regulatory Referrals

The FBI shared information with tax, licensing, immigration, military, and other government bodies.

Some referrals addressed legitimate legal issues; others used administrative power to burden political targets.

Selective Law-Enforcement Pressure

The Bureau supplied information to police or prosecutors, encouraged arrests or investigations, and searched for legal violations that could be used against targeted organizations.

Law enforcement becomes politically abusive when investigative pressure is driven by ideology rather than neutral legal criteria.

Harassment and Repeated Police Contact

Targeted individuals could face repeated interviews, stops, subpoenas, visits, surveillance, and contact with associates.

Even when each act appeared minor, accumulated pressure could discourage political participation and isolate targets.

Surveillance

COINTELPRO depended on physical surveillance, informants, records, telephone information, mail-related intelligence, electronic surveillance, photographs, and reports from other agencies.

Surveillance supplied information used to design disruption and measure whether actions succeeded.

Electronic Surveillance

FBI domestic-intelligence operations used wiretaps, microphones, and other electronic methods, sometimes without judicial warrants under the legal assumptions of the era.

Not every electronic surveillance was a COINTELPRO action, but surveillance and covert disruption were institutionally connected.

Surreptitious Entries

FBI domestic-security operations used warrantless surreptitious entries, sometimes called black-bag jobs, to photograph or obtain records.

These operations were generally concealed from courts, targets, and the public and became a central subject of post-Watergate intelligence reform.

Mail Monitoring and Opening

Federal intelligence agencies monitored mail covers and, in some programs, opened mail without ordinary judicial process.

Mail programs overlapped broader domestic-intelligence activity examined by the Church Committee, although they were not all formally part of COINTELPRO.

Publications and Newspapers

Underground newspapers, movement publications, student papers, newsletters, and alternative media were monitored and sometimes targeted for disruption.

The Bureau attempted to influence distribution, advertisers, printers, institutional support, and public reputation.

Disinformation

Disinformation is deliberately false or misleading information circulated to influence behaviour.

COINTELPRO used disinformation to create mistrust, manipulate decisions, discredit leaders, and distort public understanding.

Propaganda and Public Relations

FBI officials used information campaigns to present targets as dangerous, dishonest, foreign controlled, immoral, violent, or un-American.

Government propaganda becomes especially problematic when the source and evidentiary basis are concealed.

Reporting “Tangible Results”

Field offices were expected to report tangible results such as cancelled events, adverse publicity, resignations, internal disputes, reduced participation, employment consequences, or law-enforcement action.

This performance culture rewarded disruption even when the affected activity was constitutionally protected.

Pressure on Field Offices

Headquarters pressed field offices to generate proposals and demonstrate initiative.

Organizational pressure could encourage offices to treat aggressive action as a measure of productivity rather than ask whether the target posed a lawful investigative threat.

Black Freedom Movement

COINTELPRO targeted Black political organizations during a period of lawful civil-rights protest, racist violence, urban unrest, police brutality, armed self-defense, revolutionary politics, and community institution building.

The FBI’s broad categories frequently treated demands for racial justice, Black autonomy, socialism, anti-imperialism, self-defense, and revolutionary violence as parts of one security threat.

The “Messiah” Objective

A 1968 FBI directive identified preventing the rise of a Black nationalist leader capable of unifying and electrifying the movement as a counterintelligence objective.

The directive considered several prominent leaders and demonstrates that the Bureau sought to shape political leadership, not merely investigate specific crimes.

Preventing Coalitions

The FBI sought to prevent alliances among Black organizations and between Black activists, antiwar groups, students, white radicals, Puerto Rican activists, and other movements.

Cross-movement solidarity was treated as a threat because it could increase political influence.

Preventing Organizational Growth

Objectives included discouraging recruitment, reducing membership, preventing youth participation, weakening community support, and stopping organizations from gaining respectability.

This purpose directly targeted lawful political development.

Black Panther Party

The Black Panther Party became one of the most intensively targeted organizations under the Black Extremist program.

The FBI monitored chapters, leaders, finances, publications, travel, community programs, weapons, international contacts, legal cases, and factional disputes.

The Party engaged in armed confrontations and revolutionary rhetoric, while also operating free-breakfast, health, education, and community-service programs. COINTELPRO targeted both its militant and community-building capacities.

Free Breakfast for Children Program

FBI records monitored Black Panther free-breakfast programs and treated successful community services as a source of political influence.

The attention demonstrates that peaceful social programs could be viewed as threatening when they increased legitimacy, membership, and community support.

Huey P. Newton

Black Panther cofounder Huey P. Newton was the subject of extensive FBI files, surveillance, and counterintelligence attention.

The Bureau monitored his criminal cases, writings, leadership disputes, travel, and relations with other Party figures.

Eldridge Cleaver

The FBI exploited and intensified conflict between Eldridge Cleaver’s faction and the Oakland leadership.

Anonymous and deceptive communications contributed to suspicion and fragmentation within a movement already divided by ideology, exile, personality, and strategy.

Fred Hampton

Fred Hampton was chairman of the Illinois Black Panther Party and a major organizer of the multiracial Rainbow Coalition in Chicago.

FBI informant William O’Neal infiltrated the Chicago Panthers and supplied information, including a floor plan of Hampton’s apartment, to the FBI.

Hampton and Mark Clark were killed during a December 4, 1969 raid conducted by officers working for the Cook County State’s Attorney. The relationship among FBI intelligence, local planning, the raid, and later official accounts became the subject of extensive litigation and investigation.

William O’Neal

O’Neal served as an FBI informant inside the Chicago Black Panther Party and became the chapter’s security chief.

His role illustrates how an informant could gain sensitive internal authority rather than remain a passive observer.

Hampton Civil Litigation

Survivors and families pursued federal civil litigation alleging conspiracy and constitutional violations involving local and federal officials.

After years of proceedings, government defendants and local entities agreed to a settlement without a final trial judgment resolving every disputed factual and legal issue.

US Organization and Panther Conflict

The FBI exploited hostility between the Black Panther Party and the US Organization in Southern California.

Anonymous communications and false accusations were designed to worsen conflict in an environment where members had already been killed.

The case is among the clearest examples of covert action creating foreseeable risk of violence.

Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee

SNCC and its leaders were targeted as the organization moved from nonviolent direct action toward Black Power and opposition to the Vietnam War.

FBI attention included Stokely Carmichael, H. Rap Brown, organization communications, travel, speeches, and relationships with other groups.

Southern Christian Leadership Conference

The Southern Christian Leadership Conference was a nonviolent civil-rights organization led by Martin Luther King Jr.

Its inclusion within Black nationalist counterintelligence targeting demonstrates how the FBI’s categories extended far beyond groups advocating armed revolution.

Nation of Islam

The Nation of Islam and leaders including Elijah Muhammad and Malcolm X were monitored and targeted within broader FBI domestic-security and counterintelligence operations.

The Bureau tracked internal disputes, religious activity, speeches, membership, publications, and political influence.

Malcolm X

Malcolm X was the subject of extensive FBI surveillance before and after his separation from the Nation of Islam.

His case involved multiple agencies, organizations, threats, and internal conflicts. Public claims about COINTELPRO responsibility for his 1965 assassination require careful distinction between documented surveillance and unresolved or contested allegations concerning culpability.

Martin Luther King Jr.

The FBI conducted an extraordinary surveillance and discrediting campaign against Martin Luther King Jr., his advisers, family, organization, and reputation.

The Bureau initially justified attention through allegations of Communist influence, then continued intrusive surveillance and personal attacks after evidence failed to support claims that King was controlled by Communists.

King and the COINTELPRO Label

Much of the campaign against King was conducted under Communist-infiltration, security, or individual investigative captions rather than the formal Black Extremist COINTELPRO caption.

During Church Committee hearings, investigators emphasized that some of the most vicious acts they found were not formally labelled COINTELPRO.

The distinction prevents an archival label from obscuring the broader system of covert domestic abuse.

Electronic Surveillance of King

The FBI used wiretaps and microphones against King and his associates with executive-branch authorization under the domestic-security practices of the era.

Surveillance collected political, organizational, and intensely private information that was later used for efforts to discredit and pressure him.

Anonymous Package Sent to King

In 1964, the FBI prepared an anonymous package containing a recording and a hostile letter intended to pressure King before he received the Nobel Peace Prize.

The letter has commonly been interpreted as urging suicide, although the government concealed its authorship and King’s associates did not act as the Bureau intended.

Media Campaign Against King

The FBI supplied derogatory information to journalists and attempted to prevent honours, speaking opportunities, publications, and public recognition.

A later congressional assassination inquiry concluded that FBI manipulation of the media contributed to hostility toward King and what he represented.

The 2025 Martin Luther King Jr. Records Release

The National Archives released 243,496 pages of records related to the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. on July 21, 2025 pursuant to Executive Order 14176.

The collection includes FBI assassination-investigation records and other agency material. It is not identical to a complete COINTELPRO archive and should not be described as releasing every FBI file concerning King.

Muhammad Ali

FBI records include files concerning Muhammad Ali’s political, religious, and antiwar activity.

His surveillance illustrates how opposition to the draft, membership in the Nation of Islam, celebrity, and Black political influence drew federal attention.

Angela Davis

Angela Davis was the subject of FBI files connected to Communist Party activity, Black political movements, support committees, speeches, travel, and her criminal case.

Her records illustrate overlap among Communist, Black liberation, student, and New Left investigations.

Stokely Carmichael

Stokely Carmichael, later known as Kwame Ture, was targeted because of leadership in SNCC, advocacy of Black Power, international activity, and relationships with other movements.

The FBI sought to isolate him from allies and portrayed his influence as a national security threat.

Anti-Vietnam War Movement

Antiwar organizations, draft resisters, clergy, students, veterans, professors, publications, and demonstration sponsors were monitored and targeted.

Opposition to government policy was frequently conflated with foreign influence, revolutionary intent, or disorder.

Students for a Democratic Society

SDS was a principal New Left target.

The FBI monitored conventions, chapters, factions, leaders, publications, campus alliances, demonstrations, and the organization’s fragmentation.

Universities and Alternative Schools

The Church Committee found targets ranging from Antioch College to alternative universities and campus organizations.

The FBI treated educational institutions as locations where dissident ideas, antiwar activity, civil-rights organizing, and youth movements could grow.

Underground Press

Underground newspapers and alternative publications were monitored as political institutions capable of connecting local movements.

Counterintelligence actions sought adverse publicity, advertiser pressure, internal disputes, and distribution problems.

Women’s and LGBTQ Movements

Individuals associated with women’s liberation and gay-rights activity appeared in broader New Left and domestic-intelligence files.

Public descriptions sometimes overstate the existence of separate formal COINTELPRO programs for every social movement; many targets were reached through overlapping categories and individual investigations.

White Supremacist Organizations

COINTELPRO was not directed only against left-wing or Black organizations.

The White Hate Groups program targeted the Ku Klux Klan and related organizations responsible for terrorism, murder, intimidation, church bombings, and attacks on civil-rights workers.

Klan Infiltration

FBI informants penetrated Klan organizations and supplied information concerning membership, violence, weapons, and plans.

Intelligence contributed to prosecutions and disruption, but later controversies showed that informant relationships could also shield criminals, complicate accountability, or fail to prevent violence.

Different Targets, Same Secret Structure

The existence of a Klan program does not make tactics against civil-rights and antiwar movements constitutionally neutral.

Each action must be evaluated by the target’s conduct, the government’s lawful authority, the method used, necessity, proportionality, and oversight.

Media, Pennsylvania Burglary

On March 8, 1971, activists entered the FBI resident agency in Media, Pennsylvania and removed more than 1,000 documents.

They mailed selected records to journalists, members of Congress, and organizations under the name Citizens’ Commission to Investigate the FBI.

The burglary was illegal, but the documents exposed government activity that had escaped meaningful public and congressional oversight.

Citizens’ Commission to Investigate the FBI

The Citizens’ Commission was the underground group responsible for the Media burglary.

Members remained publicly unidentified for decades. The FBI investigated the burglary but did not prosecute the participants.

Publication of the Media Documents

Journalist Betty Medsger received and reported on the stolen files for the Washington Post.

Publication revealed political surveillance, informant programs, and the unfamiliar term COINTELPRO, leading reporters and lawyers to seek further records.

Carl Stern and Freedom of Information Litigation

NBC journalist Carl Stern pursued Freedom of Information Act litigation seeking FBI records explaining the COINTELPRO caption.

The litigation helped force the Bureau to release documents and widened public understanding of the program.

Termination in April 1971

The Church Committee traced the termination decision to a memorandum dated April 27, 1971 and a headquarters letter to field offices dated April 28.

The Bureau cited security concerns following exposure rather than a concluded determination that covert disruption was unlawful or improper.

Exceptional Individual Actions After Termination

The termination communication stated that field offices could submit recommendations in exceptional cases when counterintelligence action remained warranted.

Congressional investigators concluded that formal termination did not create a clear boundary between discontinued COINTELPRO actions and comparable tactics conducted under other case captions.

Death of J. Edgar Hoover

Hoover died on May 2, 1972, ending nearly forty-eight years as Bureau Director.

His death, Watergate, the Media disclosures, litigation, and congressional investigations created conditions for a broad reassessment of FBI power.

Watergate and Intelligence Scandal

COINTELPRO became publicly understood during the same period as Watergate, CIA domestic activity, military surveillance, IRS political use, and other executive-branch abuses.

These scandals reinforced concern that secret government institutions could be used against political opposition.

Church Committee

The Senate created the Church Committee on January 27, 1975 to investigate intelligence activities and government abuse.

Senator Frank Church chaired the bipartisan committee, and Senator John Tower served as vice chairman.

Church Committee Investigation

The committee held 126 full committee meetings and forty subcommittee hearings, interviewed approximately 800 witnesses, and reviewed roughly 110,000 documents.

It examined the FBI, CIA, National Security Agency, Internal Revenue Service, military intelligence, mail programs, electronic surveillance, covert action, and executive-branch control.

COINTELPRO Staff Study

The detailed COINTELPRO report reviewed more than 20,000 pages of Bureau documents, deposed agents involved in operations, and interviewed targets.

It described more than 2,000 approved actions and emphasized that the examples discussed represented only a fraction of the total.

Church Committee Summary Finding

The staff report described COINTELPRO as a sophisticated vigilante operation directed against American citizens and designed to prevent the exercise of First Amendment rights.

It found that many techniques would have been intolerable even against violent targets and that numerous targets were not involved in violence.

Protection of the Existing Social and Political Order

The committee concluded that an unexpressed premise was that the FBI had a duty to do whatever it considered necessary against perceived threats to the existing social and political order.

That premise substituted institutional judgement for law, judicial process, democratic accountability, and constitutional rights.

Lack of Effective Oversight

Congress, Attorneys General, presidents, courts, and the public did not receive complete or accurate information about many operations.

Officials sometimes approved broad surveillance authorities without knowing how the FBI used collected information for covert disruption.

Presidential and Attorney General Awareness

Presidents and Attorneys General knew varying amounts about FBI domestic intelligence and particular investigations.

The record does not support one simple claim that every COINTELPRO action was specifically ordered by the White House or personally approved by the Attorney General.

Weak supervision and broad political demands nevertheless helped create the environment in which the programs operated.

Congressional Responsibility

Members of Congress praised aggressive anticommunist and anti-extremist action, requested intelligence, and failed to create effective oversight.

The Church Committee emphasized that intelligence excesses developed across parties and administrations rather than arising from one individual alone.

FBI Defenses of the Program

FBI officials argued that the nation expected the Bureau to respond to espionage, terrorism, riots, bombings, Klan violence, and political instability while Congress supplied few clear guidelines.

The Bureau has also noted that COINTELPRO represented a small fraction of its workload.

Scale does not resolve whether particular actions violated constitutional rights or caused serious harm.

Current FBI Acknowledgment

The FBI’s historical website states that some tactics went too far and that Congress and the American people rightly criticized the program for abridging First Amendment rights and other reasons.

The Bureau also presents COINTELPRO within a larger history that includes legitimate civil-rights, espionage, and violent-crime investigations.

Privacy Act of 1974

The Privacy Act established rules governing federal systems of personal records, access, correction, disclosure, and recordkeeping.

DOJ identifies Watergate and COINTELPRO surveillance scandals as part of the law’s historical context and its effort to limit what government can know and maintain about citizens.

Freedom of Information Act Reform

The 1974 FOIA amendments strengthened judicial review and public access following Watergate and intelligence scandals.

FOIA became a principal mechanism through which targets, journalists, lawyers, and historians obtained COINTELPRO records.

Levi Guidelines

Attorney General Edward Levi issued domestic-security investigation guidelines in 1976.

The guidelines required factual predicates, established limits and approval procedures, and attempted to prevent investigation based solely on protected political activity.

Successive Attorneys General revised domestic-investigation standards, producing continuing debate over security, discretion, and civil liberties.

Permanent Senate Intelligence Committee

The Senate established the Select Committee on Intelligence in 1976 to conduct continuing oversight of intelligence activities.

Permanent oversight was one of the Church Committee’s most important institutional legacies.

Permanent House Intelligence Committee

The House established its Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence in 1977.

The two congressional intelligence committees receive classified information, authorize programs, oversee legality and performance, and report legislation.

Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978

FISA created a statutory judicial process for specified electronic surveillance and physical searches conducted for foreign-intelligence purposes.

The law responded in part to warrantless surveillance abuses exposed during the Church Committee era.

FISA did not regulate every domestic intelligence technique or eliminate controversy over political surveillance.

Attorney General Guidelines

Modern FBI domestic operations are governed by statutes, constitutional law, Attorney General guidelines, FBI policy, inspector-general review, courts, and congressional oversight.

Critics argue that later rules still permit broad assessments, informants, online monitoring, and intelligence collection that can burden lawful movements.

Criminal Investigation Versus Intelligence

Criminal investigation seeks evidence of specified legal violations for possible prosecution.

Intelligence investigation seeks information about threats, capabilities, networks, or foreign influence and may not be tied immediately to prosecutable conduct.

COINTELPRO showed how intelligence authority can become political intervention when standards are vague and covert action lacks legal limits.

First Amendment

Speech, press, religion, assembly, petition, and association receive constitutional protection even when ideas are unpopular or radical.

Government can investigate crime and foreign-agent activity, but it cannot lawfully punish or suppress people merely for protected viewpoints and associations.

Fourth Amendment

Electronic surveillance, physical searches, tracking, and seizures implicate the Fourth Amendment.

Several domestic-intelligence techniques of the era operated without ordinary warrants or meaningful judicial review.

Due Process

Anonymous government attacks deprived targets of notice, evidence, neutral decision-makers, and opportunities to answer accusations.

Employment, education, reputation, liberty, and organizational survival could be affected without formal legal process.

Equal Protection and Racial Discrimination

The Black Extremist program treated Black political leadership and organizing as a distinct security category.

Racialized assumptions concerning militancy, unrest, nationalism, and community influence shaped targeting and imposed disproportionate burdens on Black movements.

Political Neutrality

Law-enforcement agencies must not manipulate political competition, leadership, protest, or public opinion to preserve an incumbent social order.

COINTELPRO blurred national security with institutional and ideological preference.

Freedom of Association

Covert infiltration, membership exposure, anonymous accusations, and employer pressure can deter people from joining lawful organizations.

The Supreme Court has recognized that compelled disclosure and government harassment can burden association even without direct prohibition.

Chilling Effect

A chilling effect occurs when fear of surveillance, retaliation, job loss, stigma, or investigation discourages lawful speech and association.

COINTELPRO created chilling effects beyond direct targets because entire communities learned that protest could trigger secret government action.

Provocation and Entrapment Concerns

Informants who encourage conflict or unlawful activity raise questions about government causation and entrapment.

Entrapment is a criminal-defense doctrine with specific elements; unethical or dangerous provocation can exist even when the technical defence is unavailable.

State and Local Cooperation

The FBI shared intelligence with state and local police and sometimes worked through local agencies.

Local departments also operated their own political-intelligence units, creating overlapping surveillance and disruption that cannot always be attributed solely to formal COINTELPRO.

Military Intelligence

Military intelligence monitored civilian protest during the same era.

The Church Committee and related investigations examined military, CIA, NSA, IRS, and FBI activity as a broader system rather than treating COINTELPRO as the only source of domestic surveillance.

Distinction from CIA Operation CHAOS

Operation CHAOS was a CIA program collecting information concerning domestic antiwar and dissident activity, often through foreign-intelligence authorities.

It was separate from FBI COINTELPRO, although agencies shared information and targeted overlapping movements.

Distinction from NSA Watchlisting

NSA watchlisting and communications-intelligence programs collected or selected communications associated with domestic figures during the same period.

These operations were institutionally separate but contributed to the domestic-intelligence abuses examined by the Church Committee.

Internal Revenue Service Activity

The IRS maintained political and ideological projects and received referrals concerning organizations and individuals.

Tax administration can become a political weapon when selection is based on viewpoint rather than neutral enforcement standards.

Socialist Workers Party Litigation

The Socialist Workers Party and Young Socialist Alliance pursued long-running federal litigation challenging FBI surveillance, infiltration, and disruption.

A federal district court found constitutional violations and awarded damages, creating one of the principal judicial records concerning COINTELPRO’s unlawful effects.

Hobson v. Wilson

Activists in Washington, D.C. sued federal and local officials for New Left COINTELPRO operations.

The litigation addressed conspiracies to disrupt lawful political activity and helped establish that individual officials could face civil liability for covert constitutional violations.

Hampton v. Hanrahan

Litigation arising from the raid that killed Fred Hampton and Mark Clark alleged federal and local conspiracy, falsification, and constitutional violations.

Appellate proceedings revived claims after a lengthy trial, and the case ended in a multimillion-dollar settlement shared among federal, county, and city defendants.

Morris Starsky FOIA Appeal

In 1974, DOJ’s Office of Legal Counsel recommended administrative release of COINTELPRO-New Left files concerning Arizona State University professor Morris Starsky.

The records concerned an anonymous FBI letter used to influence university action against him and became an important example of FOIA exposing covert political interference.

Limited Criminal Accountability

COINTELPRO exposure produced investigations, civil litigation, policy reform, and reputational consequences, but few officials faced criminal prosecution for designing or executing the programs.

Secrecy, statutes of limitation, evidentiary difficulties, immunity, institutional defence, and the legal assumptions of the era limited accountability.

Official Apology and Acknowledgment

The FBI acknowledges the program’s abuses in historical materials, but the federal government has not enacted one comprehensive COINTELPRO apology, compensation statute, or truth commission.

Individual settlements and record releases have provided partial remedies.

Archival Records

COINTELPRO records are dispersed among FBI headquarters files, field-office files, individual subject files, Department of Justice records, congressional collections, court records, presidential libraries, and personal papers.

A file’s absence from the COINTELPRO caption does not prove that comparable covert action did not occur.

FBI Vault

The FBI Vault publishes scanned FOIA releases organized by program and subject.

The collection includes thousands of pages but can be difficult to search because many documents are image scans, duplicated, redacted, fragmented, or divided into numerous parts.

National Archives

The National Archives preserves transferred FBI and Department of Justice records and provides finding aids for Black Power, civil-rights, and other subject files.

Access depends on transfer status, archival processing, legal restrictions, classification review, privacy, and the condition of original records.

Missing and Destroyed Records

Some intelligence records were destroyed under records schedules, routine practices, or decisions made before historical significance was fully understood.

Other actions may never have been documented completely or may appear only in individual case files, informal communications, or records of another agency.

Redactions and Withholding

Public files can contain redactions protecting living informants, personal privacy, grand-jury information, law-enforcement techniques, national security, and other legally protected interests.

Redaction can be necessary while also limiting historical accountability and the ability of targets to learn what occurred.

COINTELPRO Full Disclosure Act of 2026

Representative Summer Lee of Pennsylvania introduced H.R. 7220 on January 22, 2026 with Representative Jonathan Jackson of Illinois as an original cosponsor.

The bill would require public disclosure of COINTELPRO records, create a centralized COINTELPRO Records Collection at the National Archives, establish a five-member review board, direct agencies to identify records, and create standards for postponing disclosure.

Status of H.R. 7220

The bill was referred to the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform and the Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure.

As of August 3, 2026, the official GovInfo record listed January 22, 2026 as its latest action. It had not passed either chamber and was not law.

Proposed Records Collection

H.R. 7220 would direct the Archivist to create an indexed, searchable COINTELPRO Records Collection preserving provenance and physical integrity.

The proposal reflects concern that existing records are dispersed across agencies, archives, court seals, congressional files, and archival series.

Proposed Records Review Board

The bill proposes a five-member board appointed by the President with Senate confirmation.

The board would include expertise in history, archives, law, and COINTELPRO records and would have authority to review agency decisions, obtain records, receive public information, hold hearings, and issue subpoenas subject to judicial enforcement.

Current Status as of August 3, 2026

The formal FBI COINTELPRO programs remain terminated.

The FBI publicly acknowledges that the program abridged First Amendment rights and that some tactics went too far.

Records continue to be released and reexamined through FOIA, archives, litigation, declassification, congressional activity, and scholarship.

H.R. 7220 represents the most current federal proposal for a centralized disclosure process but remains pending legislation.

Claims of a “New COINTELPRO”

Activists and commentators sometimes describe later surveillance of racial-justice, environmental, antiwar, religious, Indigenous, socialist, anarchist, or other movements as a new COINTELPRO.

The comparison can identify recurring risks—ideological targeting, infiltrators, disinformation, and weak oversight—but should not substitute for evidence concerning a specific modern program, authority, official, or action.

How to Evaluate Modern Analogies

A modern allegation should identify:

  • the responsible agency;
  • the legal authority;
  • the target-selection standard;
  • the investigative predicate;
  • the use of informants or undercover agents;
  • whether government sought information or political disruption;
  • the approving officials;
  • oversight and audit records;
  • harm to protected activity; and
  • documentary or testimonial evidence.

Security and Liberty

COINTELPRO does not establish that domestic intelligence is never necessary.

It establishes that genuine threats do not justify secret political warfare without lawful predicates, proportional methods, oversight, and accountability.

Claimed Government Achievements

  • Developed intelligence concerning Communist foreign influence and espionage.
  • Penetrated violent Ku Klux Klan organizations.
  • Contributed information to prosecutions and disruption of violent actors.
  • Identified planned violence and organizational relationships.
  • Provided government officials with information during periods of unrest.

These claimed benefits must be separated from unlawful disruption of protected activity and do not validate every method or target.

Documented Abuses

  • Targeting based on political belief and association.
  • Anonymous and forged communications.
  • Efforts to break marriages and personal relationships.
  • False accusations of informant status.
  • Manipulation of journalists and public opinion.
  • Interference with employment and education.
  • Disruption of speeches, meetings, and publications.
  • Exploitation of violent factional conflict.
  • Targeting of nonviolent civil-rights and antiwar organizations.
  • Use of intimate surveillance information for political pressure.
  • Concealment from courts, Congress, targets, and the public.
  • Continuation of comparable actions under labels other than COINTELPRO.

Limits of the Historical Record

  • Records are incomplete and dispersed.
  • Many documents remain redacted.
  • Some actions were conducted under other captions.
  • FBI proposals do not always prove execution or effect.
  • Field reports can exaggerate success.
  • Targets and officials sometimes gave conflicting accounts.
  • Modern summaries can collapse separate agencies and programs.
  • Archival labels used offensive historical terminology that should not be adopted uncritically.
  • Claims connecting COINTELPRO to particular deaths require case-specific evidence.

Major Controversies

National security
Supporters emphasized espionage and violence; investigators found that covert action expanded far beyond those threats.
Race
Black movements were treated as a distinct security category, and community influence itself could trigger disruption.
Violence
The FBI claimed to prevent violence while some actions foreseeably intensified dangerous conflict.
Martin Luther King Jr.
The most notorious anti-King actions were closely related to COINTELPRO but often filed under other program labels.
Fred Hampton
FBI infiltration and intelligence were connected to the events leading to the fatal raid, while precise legal responsibility was contested and resolved through civil settlement rather than a definitive merits judgment on every claim.
Ku Klux Klan
Action against violent white supremacists produced public-safety benefits but did not make secret extralegal methods universally lawful.
Records
The public archive is extensive but fragmented, redacted, and incomplete.
Modern comparisons
The term COINTELPRO is sometimes applied broadly to later surveillance without sufficient program-specific evidence.

How COINTELPRO Should Be Researched

Research should identify the exact program caption, field office, headquarters file, individual file, date, proposal, approval, implementation report, and claimed result.

Important distinctions include:

  • surveillance versus covert action;
  • formal COINTELPRO captions versus related domestic-intelligence operations;
  • proposal versus approved action;
  • approved action versus proven implementation;
  • implementation versus documented effect;
  • FBI activity versus state, local, CIA, NSA, military, or IRS activity;
  • lawful investigation versus political disruption;
  • violent conduct versus protected ideology;
  • historical FBI terminology versus neutral modern description;
  • archival evidence versus inference or allegation;
  • government acknowledgment versus independent verification; and
  • the 1956–1971 programs versus modern analogies.

Why COINTELPRO Matters

COINTELPRO demonstrates how a law-enforcement agency can transform intelligence collection into secret political action when threat categories are broad, oversight is weak, and institutional leaders treat dissent as danger.

It damaged organizations and individuals that differed greatly in ideology and conduct, from violent white supremacists to nonviolent civil-rights leaders, student groups, socialists, antiwar activists, religious organizations, and community programs.

Its exposure helped establish permanent intelligence oversight, privacy protections, domestic-investigation guidelines, and judicial procedures for foreign-intelligence surveillance.

The continuing archival and legislative debate shows that accountability remains incomplete when records are fragmented, labels obscure related operations, and many affected people never received notice or remedy.

Summary

COINTELPRO was a series of secret FBI counterintelligence and covert-action programs operating from 1956 through April 1971.

The first program targeted the Communist Party USA. Later programs targeted the Socialist Workers Party, Ku Klux Klan organizations, Black political movements, civil-rights groups, student and antiwar organizations, and other domestic and foreign-linked targets.

COINTELPRO went beyond surveillance. It used informants, anonymous and forged letters, disinformation, media contacts, employer and school pressure, selective enforcement, organizational infiltration, and exploitation of personal and factional conflict.

The Black Extremist program targeted organizations ranging from the Black Panther Party and Nation of Islam to SNCC, SCLC, and Black student groups.

The FBI conducted an especially abusive campaign against Martin Luther King Jr. Much of that campaign was filed under labels other than formal COINTELPRO, demonstrating that archival captions do not define the full scope of domestic covert action.

On March 8, 1971, the Citizens’ Commission to Investigate the FBI removed records from the FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania and sent them to journalists and public officials.

The FBI terminated the formal programs on April 27–28, 1971 for security reasons after exposure, while allowing exceptional individual counterintelligence proposals.

The Church Committee reviewed more than 20,000 pages for its COINTELPRO staff study and described more than 2,000 approved actions. It concluded that the FBI conducted a sophisticated vigilante operation aimed at preventing First Amendment activity.

Exposure contributed to the Privacy Act, stronger FOIA access, Attorney General guidelines, permanent congressional intelligence committees, and FISA.

The FBI now acknowledges that COINTELPRO was rightly criticized for abridging First Amendment rights and that some tactics went too far.

Records remain extensive but fragmented. H.R. 7220, introduced on January 22, 2026, would create a centralized National Archives collection and review board, but it had not become law as of August 3, 2026.
