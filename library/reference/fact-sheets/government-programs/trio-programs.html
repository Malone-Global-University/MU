TRIO Programs

The federal college-access and student-support system serving low-income individuals, potential first-generation college students, students with disabilities, veterans, and other eligible participants from middle school through doctoral preparation.

Overview

The Federal TRIO Programs are a group of competitive federal grant programs designed to identify, recruit, prepare, advise, and support individuals who face economic, educational, disability-related, military-transition, or other barriers to postsecondary education. The programs assist participants at different points along the educational pipeline—from middle school and secondary school through undergraduate completion and preparation for doctoral study.

TRIO is not an acronym. The name arose because the federal system originally consisted of three programs: Upward Bound, Talent Search, and Special Services for Disadvantaged Students, now called Student Support Services. Congress and the executive branch later added additional programs, but the historical name remained.

The Department of Education currently describes TRIO as eight programs:

  1. Talent Search
  2. Upward Bound
  3. Upward Bound Math-Science
  4. Veterans Upward Bound
  5. Student Support Services
  6. Educational Opportunity Centers
  7. Ronald E. McNair Postbaccalaureate Achievement
  8. Training Program for Federal TRIO Programs Staff

The eight-program count differs from the six principal discretionary-grant lines sometimes used in federal budgets. Upward Bound is one statutory grant program containing three project types—regular Upward Bound, Upward Bound Math-Science, and Veterans Upward Bound. Counting those project types separately produces the commonly stated total of eight.

Seven TRIO programs provide services to students or prospective students. The eighth—the Training Program—supports the directors and staff who operate TRIO projects.

TRIO is primarily an institutional and organizational grant system rather than a direct federal benefit claimed by any eligible individual. The Department awards grants to institutions of higher education, public agencies, nonprofit organizations, secondary schools, and other eligible entities according to the governing program. Grantees recruit and select participants within approved target areas, schools, campuses, institutions, or communities.

Meeting a federal eligibility definition does not create an individual entitlement to services. Each project has limited funded capacity, geographic boundaries, selection priorities, performance objectives, and local recruitment procedures. A person can be eligible for TRIO but unable to participate because no project serves the area, the project is full, or the applicant does not meet the project’s specific selection criteria.

Most participant-serving programs require that at least two-thirds of participants satisfy a combined disadvantage standard. In Talent Search, Upward Bound, Veterans Upward Bound, Upward Bound Math-Science, and Educational Opportunity Centers, the two-thirds group generally consists of individuals who are both low income and potential first-generation college students. Student Support Services uses a related rule involving low-income first-generation students and students with disabilities. McNair uses a distinct doctoral-preparation eligibility structure.

A low-income individual generally comes from a family whose taxable income for the preceding year did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level. The Department publishes annual TRIO low-income levels. The 2026 levels became effective January 13, 2026.

A potential first-generation college student generally is an individual whose natural or adoptive parents did not complete a four-year bachelor’s degree. For a student who regularly lived with and received support from only one parent, the definition ordinarily looks to whether that parent completed a bachelor’s degree.

The programs provide different combinations of academic instruction, tutoring, advising, mentoring, test preparation, financial-aid assistance, college applications, campus visits, summer residential experiences, career exploration, financial literacy, transfer assistance, disability support, research, internships, veteran services, graduate-school preparation, and doctoral mentoring.

TRIO projects do not award admission to a college and do not guarantee financial aid, enrollment, transfer, graduation, employment, or entry into graduate school. Their purpose is to increase the knowledge, academic preparation, navigation capacity, and institutional support participants need to reach those outcomes.

Federal law and regulations establish grant competitions, allowable services, participant eligibility, prior-experience points, minimum participant numbers, project periods, performance reporting, fiscal requirements, and civil-rights obligations. Most direct-service grants are awarded for five-year project periods, subject to appropriations and continued performance.

TRIO received $1.191 billion in fiscal year 2025, the latest total identified as an enacted actual amount in the Department’s current budget materials. The Department’s fiscal year 2024 project data show that grants were funded to serve approximately 869,000 participants across the seven direct-service programs. This figure represents funded participant commitments rather than a verified count of unique individuals actually served, and a participant can interact with more than one TRIO program over time.

For fiscal year 2024, Talent Search had the largest funded participant count at 341,744, while Student Support Services and Educational Opportunity Centers were each funded to serve more than 200,000 participants. McNair was the smallest and most intensive student-serving program, with 5,935 funded participants and the highest average federal cost per participant.

In 2026, the Departments of Education and Labor began co-administering new TRIO grant competitions under an interagency partnership. The Talent Search and Educational Opportunity Centers competitions were managed through Department of Labor systems on behalf of the Department of Education, while the programs remained authorized under the Higher Education Act.

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 budget request proposed eliminating federal TRIO funding. A presidential budget request is a policy proposal, not enacted law. As of August 2, 2026, TRIO remained authorized, existing grants continued, and fiscal year 2026 competitions were active.

TRIO has major civil-rights and racial-equity significance. The programs emerged during the Great Society and Civil Rights eras in response to unequal educational preparation, poverty, racial exclusion, and limited access to college information. African American, Latino, Native American, rural, veteran, disabled, and first-generation students have been central participant populations, although most eligibility rules are based on income, parental education, disability, veteran status, and educational need rather than race alone.

Definitions

Federal TRIO Programs
The collective federal outreach, preparation, student-support, graduate-preparation, and staff-training programs authorized in Chapter 1 of the Higher Education Act’s early outreach and student services provisions.
TRIO
A historical label derived from the original three programs. It is not an abbreviation formed from program words.
Low-income individual
An individual whose family taxable income for the preceding year did not exceed 150 percent of the federal poverty level, subject to the governing statute, regulations, and annual Department table.
Potential first-generation college student
An individual whose natural or adoptive parents did not complete a bachelor’s degree, with special treatment when the individual regularly lived with and received support from only one parent.
Individual with a disability
A person meeting the applicable federal disability definition and otherwise satisfying the eligibility requirements of the relevant TRIO program.
Participant
An eligible individual selected by a funded TRIO project and receiving services documented under program rules.
Potential participant
An individual contacted, screened, or considered by a project who has not necessarily been selected and served as an official participant.
Target area
The geographic community, school attendance area, campus, institution, or service region identified in a project’s approved application.
Target school
A secondary school or other educational institution from which a precollege TRIO project recruits and serves participants.
Institution of higher education
An eligible postsecondary institution meeting the applicable Higher Education Act definition. The abbreviation IHE is commonly used.
Prior experience
Points awarded in a grant competition to an applicant that has recently operated the same TRIO program and met required project objectives.
Need
The documented educational, economic, demographic, geographic, institutional, or service gap supporting a grant application or participant selection.
Competitive preference priority
A Department priority for which an applicant can earn additional review points by proposing specified activities or serving specified needs.
Absolute priority
A priority an application must address to be eligible for consideration in a competition.
Project period
The multiyear period for which a federal grant is approved, ordinarily five years for most direct-service TRIO projects.
Annual performance report
The project report documenting participant characteristics, services, progress, outcomes, expenditures, and attainment of approved objectives. The abbreviation APR is commonly used.
Standard objectives
Program-specific performance commitments concerning persistence, graduation, enrollment, transfer, completion, graduate study, or other participant outcomes.
Postsecondary education
Education or training beyond secondary school, including eligible colleges, universities, career schools, and other qualifying programs.
Rigorous secondary school program of study
A challenging course sequence designed to prepare a student for postsecondary education, commonly including advanced academic coursework.
Summer bridge
A short program helping recent secondary-school graduates transition into postsecondary study before fall enrollment.
Residential program
A structured academic and college-exposure experience in which participants live on a campus during part of the summer or another program period.
Grant aid
Direct financial assistance that an eligible Student Support Services project may provide to qualifying Pell Grant recipients under statutory limits.
Underrepresented in graduate education
A category historically used in McNair eligibility for specified groups with low doctoral participation. Its current administration is affected by a December 2025 Office of Legal Counsel opinion.
Disconnected student
A broad program term that can include students experiencing homelessness, foster care, limited English proficiency, disability, educational interruption, or other barriers to postsecondary access.
Federal outreach program
A program that identifies and assists eligible individuals who may not independently receive adequate information, preparation, or institutional support.

Why the Programs Are Called TRIO

Upward Bound

Created in 1964, Upward Bound established an intensive college-preparation model involving academic instruction, advising, campus exposure, and summer experiences.

Talent Search

Created in 1965, Talent Search used outreach, counseling, financial-aid information, and application assistance to connect disadvantaged students with postsecondary opportunities.

Special Services

Created in 1968, Special Services for Disadvantaged Students supported enrolled college students. The program was later renamed Student Support Services.

A Trio of Programs

Once all three programs operated, federal officials and the college-access field referred to them collectively as the “TRIO” programs. The label remained after Congress expanded the system.

Not Three Programs Today

Modern TRIO includes eight commonly listed programs. The name records institutional history rather than current program count.

Common Participant Eligibility Framework

Low Income

The Department publishes an annual table equal to 150 percent of the federal poverty guidelines. Projects ordinarily use family taxable income from the preceding calendar year and maintain documentation supporting eligibility.

First Generation

Potential first-generation status focuses on whether the participant’s parents completed a four-year bachelor’s degree. The definition does not require that no relative ever attended college.

Disability

Students with disabilities are central to Student Support Services and can participate in other TRIO programs when they meet applicable eligibility and need requirements.

Educational Need

Programs generally require a need for academic support, counseling, preparation, information, transition assistance, or other project services. Income and parental education alone do not always establish every required element.

Citizenship and Residency

Participant rules commonly require United States citizenship, permanent residency, or another qualifying status recognized in program regulations.

Two-Thirds Rule

Most projects must ensure that at least two-thirds of participants fall within the combined category prescribed for that program. Projects may use the remaining share to serve other individuals meeting an authorized single criterion or special need.

No Individual Entitlement

A project can impose lawful selection priorities based on grade, target school, service area, academic need, readiness, available capacity, and ability to benefit from project services.

Avoiding Duplication

Projects coordinate with other TRIO, GEAR UP, school, college, workforce, veterans, disability, and community programs and document which services each program provides.

The Department’s 2026 TRIO low-income table became effective January 13, 2026 and remains in effect until replaced. The figures equal 150 percent of the 2026 federal poverty guidelines.

Low-income status is determined using the table effective when the project makes the eligibility determination, subject to program-specific recordkeeping and continuing-participant rules.

Talent Search

Purpose

Talent Search identifies individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds who have the potential to succeed in postsecondary education. It helps participants complete secondary school, enter or reenter education, apply for aid, enroll in postsecondary programs, and complete those programs.

Participants

Projects commonly serve students beginning in middle school, secondary-school students, secondary-school dropouts, veterans, and adults seeking educational reentry. At least two-thirds generally must be both low income and potential first-generation college students.

Services

  • Academic, career, and financial counseling
  • Course-selection advice
  • College and career exploration
  • Admissions and financial-aid applications
  • FAFSA assistance
  • Test preparation
  • Financial and economic literacy
  • Secondary-school reentry and equivalency options
  • Campus visits and cultural exposure
  • Mentoring and tutoring

Service Intensity

Talent Search serves large participant numbers at a lower average cost per participant than intensive residential and research programs.

Fiscal Year 2024

The program had approximately $196.2 million in funding, 545 awards, 341,744 funded participants, and an average federal cost of $574 per participant.

Fiscal Year 2026 Competition

The Departments of Education and Labor announced more than $175 million for the 2026 competition. Applications closed May 1, 2026.

Performance Measures

Projects report secondary persistence, graduation, rigorous coursework, postsecondary enrollment, and completion outcomes.

Upward Bound

Purpose

Regular Upward Bound provides intensive academic preparation and motivation for secondary-school students who face barriers to postsecondary education.

Participant Eligibility

Participants generally have completed the eighth grade, are between thirteen and nineteen years old, and need academic support to pursue postsecondary education. At least two-thirds ordinarily must be both low income and potential first-generation college students.

Academic-Year Component

Projects provide tutoring, instruction, counseling, mentoring, financial literacy, career exploration, college applications, financial-aid assistance, and school-year enrichment.

Summer Component

Many projects operate six-week summer instructional or residential programs simulating college study and campus life.

Summer Bridge

Recent high-school graduates can receive transitional coursework and support before beginning postsecondary education.

Core Instruction

Projects commonly provide mathematics, laboratory science, composition, literature, foreign language, and other academic coursework.

Stipends

Projects may pay limited participant stipends under federal statutory and regulatory ceilings. Stipends support participation but do not constitute wages or general income assistance.

Fiscal Year 2024

Regular Upward Bound had approximately $379.9 million in funding, 1,026 awards, 73,519 funded participants, and an average federal cost of $5,167 per participant.

Program Intensity

Upward Bound’s lower participant-to-project ratio reflects repeated contact, instruction, summer programming, campus exposure, and multiyear service.

Upward Bound Math-Science

Purpose

Upward Bound Math-Science strengthens mathematics and science preparation and encourages participants to complete postsecondary degrees in science, technology, engineering, mathematics, and related fields.

Participant Base

Projects serve eligible secondary-school students with educational need and interest or potential in mathematics and science.

Required Elements

  • Intensive mathematics and science instruction
  • Laboratory experience
  • Computer and technology exposure
  • Scientific research or inquiry
  • Six-week summer instructional experience
  • Summer bridge for qualifying graduates
  • Career and postsecondary exploration
  • Mentoring by educators, students, and professionals

Institutional Partnerships

Projects can connect participants with universities, laboratories, engineering programs, hospitals, research institutions, museums, and STEM employers.

Fiscal Year 2024

The program had approximately $79.4 million in funding, 251 awards, 15,555 funded participants, and an average cost of $5,070 per participant.

Equity Significance

UBMS addresses unequal access to advanced mathematics, laboratory science, research, technology, and STEM professional networks.

Veterans Upward Bound

Purpose

Veterans Upward Bound prepares eligible veterans to enter and succeed in postsecondary education by refreshing academic skills, explaining benefits and financial aid, and coordinating veteran-support resources.

Participant Eligibility

Participants must be veterans under the applicable program definition and demonstrate a need for academic support. The ordinary Upward Bound age restriction does not govern Veterans Upward Bound.

Services

  • Instruction in reading, writing, mathematics, science, and other subjects
  • College entrance examination preparation
  • Admissions and financial-aid assistance
  • Information concerning veterans’ education benefits
  • Secondary completion and equivalency guidance
  • Career and academic counseling
  • Financial literacy
  • Referral to Department of Veterans Affairs, state, local, and nonprofit services

Transition Challenges

Veterans may need assistance translating military experience into academic credit, adjusting to civilian institutions, addressing service-connected disability, and coordinating multiple benefit systems.

Fiscal Year 2024

The program had approximately $22.3 million in funding, 68 awards, 8,973 funded participants, and an average cost of $2,480 per participant.

Program Distinction

Veterans Upward Bound provides preparation before or during entry into postsecondary education. It differs from campus veteran centers and Department of Veterans Affairs education benefits, although projects coordinate with both.

Student Support Services

Purpose

Student Support Services helps enrolled postsecondary students persist, transfer, graduate, and achieve academic success. It also seeks to create institutional climates supportive of students with disabilities, students experiencing homelessness, and foster youth.

Eligible Grantees

Institutions of higher education and eligible consortia of institutions operate SSS projects.

Participant Eligibility

Students must be enrolled or accepted for enrollment, need academic support, and meet an authorized low-income, first-generation, or disability criterion. At least two-thirds must generally be low-income first-generation students or individuals with disabilities, with additional requirements concerning low-income students with disabilities.

Required Services

  • Academic tutoring
  • Advice and assistance in postsecondary course selection
  • Information concerning financial aid
  • Assistance completing financial-aid applications
  • Financial and economic literacy
  • Transfer assistance for two-year college students
  • Support for applying to graduate and professional programs

Additional Services

Projects can provide mentoring, counseling, cultural activities, career services, disability support, temporary housing referral, peer support, academic coaching, and individualized planning.

Grant Aid

SSS projects may provide direct grant aid to current participants who receive Pell Grants and generally are within their first two years of postsecondary education, subject to statutory and project limits.

Fiscal Year 2024

SSS had approximately $379.6 million in funding, 1,156 awards, 208,343 funded participants, and an average federal cost of $1,822 per participant.

Fiscal Year 2025 Competition

The 2025 appropriation supported a major new-award competition with approximately $375.1 million designated for new SSS awards and additional continuation funding.

Performance

The Department reported that 49.9 percent of the relevant two-year-institution cohort completed an associate degree at the original institution or transferred to a four-year institution within three years in 2024. It reported a 61.2-percent six-year bachelor’s completion rate for the defined four-year SSS cohort at the original grantee institution.

Educational Opportunity Centers

Purpose

Educational Opportunity Centers provide adults and other eligible individuals with information, counseling, financial-aid assistance, and application support needed to enter or continue postsecondary education.

Participants

EOC generally serves individuals age nineteen or older. A younger person can be served when an appropriate Talent Search project is unavailable or unable to provide the needed service.

Services

  • Postsecondary opportunity information
  • Admissions assistance
  • FAFSA and financial-aid application assistance
  • Financial and economic literacy
  • Career counseling
  • Academic advice and course selection
  • Secondary reentry and equivalency guidance
  • Test preparation
  • Public-information campaigns
  • Referral to education, workforce, veteran, and community resources

Adult Reentry

EOC can serve adults who stopped out of secondary or postsecondary education, displaced workers, veterans, people affected by incarceration, parents, and individuals needing help navigating unfamiliar college systems.

Fiscal Year 2024

The program had approximately $65.1 million in funding, 179 awards, 215,303 funded participants, and an average cost of $302 per participant.

Fiscal Year 2026 Competition

The Department announced more than $52 million for the 2026 competition. The Department of Labor’s Employment and Training Administration managed competition activities on behalf of the Department of Education.

Performance

The Department reported a 57.2-percent postsecondary enrollment rate for the defined EOC outcome cohort in 2024.

Ronald E. McNair Postbaccalaureate Achievement

Purpose

McNair prepares eligible undergraduates for graduate education and doctoral study through research, faculty mentoring, academic development, and assistance with graduate admissions and financing.

Namesake

Ronald E. McNair was an African American physicist and NASA astronaut who died in the 1986 Challenger disaster. The program reflects his scientific achievement and the national goal of broadening access to advanced study.

Required Activities

  • Research or other scholarly activity
  • Summer internships
  • Seminars preparing students for doctoral study
  • Tutoring
  • Academic counseling
  • Graduate-admission assistance
  • Graduate financial-aid assistance

Additional Activities

Projects can provide faculty mentoring, conference participation, research presentations, graduate-school visits, test preparation, financial literacy, cultural activities, and writing support.

Participant Structure

Historically, at least two-thirds of participants have been required to be both low income and first generation, while the remaining share could include students from groups underrepresented in graduate education.

Current Legal Issue

A December 2025 Office of Legal Counsel opinion concluded that specified race-based classifications in the McNair statute and regulations violate the Fifth Amendment’s equal-protection component. The opinion binds executive-branch legal administration unless modified, but it does not itself rewrite the United States Code. Current project implementation should be understood in light of Department guidance, future rulemaking, litigation, and congressional action.

Fiscal Year 2024

McNair had approximately $60.2 million in funding, 216 awards, 5,935 funded participants, and an average cost of $10,138 per participant.

Performance

The Department reported that 60.7 percent of the relevant 2024 McNair cohort enrolled in graduate school within three years of college graduation and that 80.7 percent of graduate enrollees persisted into the second year.

Training Program for Federal TRIO Programs Staff

Purpose

The Training Program develops the knowledge and skills of TRIO project directors and staff and individuals preparing for such employment.

Eligible Grantees

Institutions of higher education and public or private nonprofit institutions and organizations can apply.

Training Methods

  • Conferences
  • Seminars
  • Workshops
  • Internships
  • Online training
  • On-site technical assistance
  • Manuals and operational publications

Priority Topics

Competitions can address program law and regulation, financial aid, project design, educational technology, assessment, evaluation, fiscal management, serving hard-to-reach populations, and improving participant outcomes.

New Directors

Federal priorities ordinarily require training opportunities for new TRIO project directors.

Fiscal Year 2024

The Training Program had approximately $4.49 million in funding, 13 awards, and a funded training commitment of 3,703 personnel.

Distinct Function

Training grants do not recruit students into college. They strengthen the projects and personnel that provide direct services.

Common Services Across TRIO

Academic Support

Tutoring, supplemental instruction, study skills, academic planning, test preparation, summer coursework, and progress monitoring address differences in prior educational opportunity.

College Knowledge

Projects explain admissions, course requirements, credentials, majors, transfer, campus systems, academic calendars, and institutional expectations.

Financial Aid

Participants receive information and assistance concerning FAFSA, Pell Grants, scholarships, loans, work-study, veterans’ benefits, state aid, institutional aid, and responsible borrowing.

Career Development

Career inventories, occupational information, employer exposure, internships, mentoring, apprenticeships, and graduate pathways connect education with employment.

Financial Literacy

Projects address budgeting, credit, debt, banking, college costs, aid offers, repayment, fraud, taxes, and financial decision-making.

Mentoring

Students interact with teachers, counselors, college staff, faculty, peers, alumni, professionals, veterans, researchers, and community leaders.

Cultural and Campus Exposure

College visits, museums, lectures, performances, laboratories, conferences, and residential experiences broaden academic and social familiarity.

Application Assistance

Projects help participants complete admissions, financial-aid, scholarship, transfer, graduate-school, and testing forms.

Advocacy and Navigation

Staff help participants interpret institutional rules, obtain accommodations, communicate with offices, understand deadlines, and persist through administrative barriers.

Grant Competitions and Project Selection

Competitive Awards

TRIO funds are not distributed automatically to every community or institution. Eligible applicants submit detailed proposals during program-specific competitions.

Need Section

Applicants document poverty, parental education, educational attainment, school performance, enrollment gaps, disability needs, veteran needs, graduate participation, and service shortages in the proposed target area.

Objectives

Each application commits to measurable participant outcomes such as persistence, graduation, postsecondary enrollment, transfer, degree completion, graduate enrollment, or doctoral attainment.

Project Design

Reviewers examine services, intensity, recruitment, staffing, partnerships, budget, evaluation, institutional commitment, and ability to meet participant needs.

Prior Experience Points

Existing grantees can receive points based on documented success in recent project years. Prior-experience policy supports continuity but can make entry more difficult for new applicants.

Geographic Distribution

Federal officials consider statutory requirements, competition scores, available funds, and program rules. High-need areas can remain unserved when applications are absent or insufficiently ranked.

Peer Review

Qualified nonfederal reviewers score applications using published selection criteria. The Department resolves technical, eligibility, budget, and policy issues.

Continuation Awards

Projects generally receive annual continuation funding during the approved period when Congress appropriates funds and the grantee makes substantial progress and complies with requirements.

Project Administration

Project Director

The director manages personnel, services, participant eligibility, partnerships, budget, reporting, evaluation, institutional coordination, and federal compliance.

Advisers and Counselors

Staff provide case management, academic planning, application assistance, financial-aid support, career guidance, and referrals.

Instructors and Tutors

Upward Bound and related projects employ teachers, faculty, graduate students, and tutors to provide academic instruction and enrichment.

Institutional Commitment

Grantees contribute facilities, faculty participation, access to laboratories and libraries, data systems, administrative support, transportation, technology, and campus programming.

Participant Records

Projects document eligibility, recruitment, services, attendance, academic progress, enrollment, completion, and other outcomes.

Annual Performance Reports

Each program uses a separate APR system aligned with its statutory objectives and participant pathway.

Data Privacy

Projects must protect education, tax, financial-aid, disability, veteran, foster-care, housing, and personally identifiable information under applicable law and agreements.

Coordination

Projects coordinate with schools, colleges, financial-aid offices, disability offices, veteran agencies, workforce programs, community organizations, and other TRIO projects.

Institution Types

Public four-year institutions operated large shares of Upward Bound and McNair funding. Public two-year institutions received approximately half of Student Support Services funding. Nonprofit and other organizations operated substantial shares of Talent Search and Educational Opportunity Centers.

Program Intensity

Cost differences reflect fundamentally different service models. EOC and Talent Search often provide broad counseling and application assistance, while McNair and Upward Bound deliver intensive, repeated, research, instructional, or residential services.

Performance and Evaluation

Annual Objectives

Each project reports against program-specific objectives. Failure to meet objectives can reduce prior-experience points in the next competition.

Department Measures

The Department tracks college enrollment for Talent Search, Upward Bound, and EOC; persistence and completion for SSS; and graduate enrollment and persistence for McNair.

Fiscal Year 2024 Enrollment Outcomes

The Department reported college-enrollment rates of 71.5 percent for the defined Talent Search cohort, 78.6 percent for the defined Upward Bound cohort, and 57.2 percent for the defined EOC cohort.

Student Support Services Outcomes

The Department reported a 49.9-percent associate-degree completion or transfer outcome for the defined two-year SSS cohort and a 61.2-percent six-year bachelor’s completion outcome for the defined four-year cohort.

McNair Outcomes

The Department reported 60.7-percent graduate enrollment within three years and 80.7-percent second-year persistence among the relevant graduate enrollees.

Evaluation Challenges

Participants are selected because they need and can benefit from services, and projects differ in intensity and local context. Comparing participants with nonparticipants without strong research design can produce misleading conclusions.

Past Studies

Research has found positive results for some programs and outcomes, limited or mixed effects for others, and variation according to participation intensity, service design, cohort, and comparison method.

Current Study

The Institute of Education Sciences began a descriptive and feasibility study in 2025. It will survey grantees, examine use of funds and service costs, and propose options for a future impact evaluation. The first report is expected in 2027.

Budget Debate

The fiscal year 2027 budget justification argues that TRIO failed many federal performance targets and cites mixed evaluation evidence. Program advocates dispute using target attainment alone as proof of ineffectiveness and emphasize completion, mobility, institutional access, and the difficulty of serving high-need populations.

Civil Rights and Nondiscrimination

Federal Grant Conditions

TRIO grantees must comply with civil-rights laws governing federally assisted education and employment.

Title VI

Title VI prohibits discrimination based on race, color, or national origin in federally funded programs.

Section 504 and ADA

Projects must provide nondiscriminatory access and reasonable accommodations to qualified individuals with disabilities.

Title IX

Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in covered education programs, subject to current law and regulation.

Language Access

Projects serving individuals with limited English proficiency must consider meaningful access to recruitment, information, counseling, and services.

Religious Institutions

Religiously affiliated colleges and organizations can participate when they meet eligibility rules and use federal funds for lawful program purposes.

Selection Procedures

Projects use written criteria and maintain records supporting eligibility and selection. They cannot disguise unlawful discrimination as local discretion.

McNair Constitutional Issue

The 2025 Office of Legal Counsel opinion specifically challenges race-based beneficiary classifications in McNair. The broader TRIO system continues to rely principally on income, parental education, disability, veteran status, and educational need.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Civil Rights Era Origins

TRIO developed while African American students and civil-rights organizations were challenging segregated schools, discriminatory admissions, unequal college preparation, and exclusion from federal and institutional opportunity.

Income and Wealth

Racial disparities in income, family wealth, housing, employment, school finance, and access to college-educated networks increase the importance of first-generation and low-income support.

Historically Black Colleges and Universities

HBCUs operate many TRIO projects and serve as target institutions, summer campuses, graduate-preparation sites, research partners, and institutional homes for participants and staff.

Black Colleges as Access Institutions

TRIO funding can strengthen HBCU advising, tutoring, research, retention, veteran services, graduate preparation, and community outreach. Limited institutional resources and federal competition can constrain capacity.

School Segregation

Many precollege participants attend high-poverty and racially isolated schools with limited counselors, advanced coursework, laboratories, college visits, and financial-aid expertise.

First-Generation Navigation

TRIO helps families interpret admissions, financial aid, academic language, institutional deadlines, housing, transfer, and graduate education without assuming prior family experience.

McNair’s Symbolism

The McNair Program recognizes the scientific achievement of a Black astronaut and historically sought to increase doctoral preparation among students excluded from advanced education.

Racially Neutral Eligibility and Unequal Conditions

Most TRIO eligibility standards are formally race neutral but respond to economic and educational conditions that are not evenly distributed by race.

Equity Evaluation

Racial-equity analysis should examine recruitment, service intensity, completion, transfer, graduate entry, research access, institutional type, geographic coverage, and whether Black communities participate in program governance and staffing.

Benefits and Public Purposes

Early Identification

Talent Search and Upward Bound identify students before application deadlines and academic decisions close postsecondary options.

Academic Preparation

Instruction, tutoring, summer programs, research, and mentoring can address unequal access to advanced learning.

Information

Participants learn how education systems, credentials, financial aid, transfer, and graduate admissions operate.

Institutional Belonging

Repeated contact with staff, peers, campuses, faculty, and alumni can make higher education more understandable and socially accessible.

Application Completion

Projects help individuals convert aspiration into completed admissions, FAFSA, scholarship, transfer, and graduate applications.

Persistence and Graduation

Student Support Services provides continuing academic and personal support after enrollment, when financial and institutional barriers can cause withdrawal.

Veteran Transition

Veterans Upward Bound connects military-experienced adults with academic refreshment, civilian campuses, benefits, disability services, and advising.

Doctoral Preparation

McNair provides research and mentoring ordinarily more available to students with faculty networks and institutional privilege.

Institutional Capacity

TRIO grants create professional expertise, data systems, partnerships, and support structures that can benefit campuses and communities beyond individual participants.

Limitations and Risks

Limited Coverage

Federal funding serves only a fraction of potentially eligible individuals. Many communities and institutions have no TRIO project.

Competitive Instability

A project can lose funding after a competition, disrupting staff, relationships, participant continuity, and local knowledge.

Prior-Experience Advantage

Prior-experience points reward effective existing grantees but can create a barrier for new applicants and unserved communities.

Administrative Burden

Eligibility documentation, participant records, annual reports, grant competitions, fiscal rules, and performance tracking require substantial capacity.

Uneven Service Quality

Projects differ in staffing, intensity, curriculum, mentoring, institutional support, transportation, technology, and partnerships.

Outcome Attribution

Participants receive services from schools, colleges, families, financial-aid programs, community organizations, and TRIO simultaneously. Isolating the federal grant’s effect is difficult.

Geographic Boundaries

Target-school and service-area rules can exclude an otherwise eligible individual living or studying outside a project’s approved boundaries.

Institutional Dependence

A project can be weakened by campus leadership changes, staff turnover, weak data access, insufficient facilities, or lack of institutional commitment.

Policy Volatility

Appropriations, competition priorities, interagency administration, civil-rights interpretation, and program regulations can change across administrations.

Major Policy Debates

Expansion or Elimination

Supporters seek increased funding to serve more eligible students. The fiscal year 2027 administration request proposes ending federal funding and leaving similar services to states and institutions.

Evidence Standard

Critics argue that mixed evaluation results do not justify continued spending without stronger evidence. Advocates argue that program objectives, participant disadvantage, service intensity, and long-term outcomes require more nuanced evaluation.

Prior Experience

Existing grantees view prior-experience points as recognition of performance and continuity. New applicants argue the system can entrench the current map of projects.

Income and First-Generation Definitions

Some proposals would modernize eligibility to reflect wealth, family complexity, regional cost, foster care, homelessness, or current income. Others favor administratively clear national definitions.

College Versus Workforce Pathways

Traditional TRIO emphasizes postsecondary education and degrees. Current 2026 competitions place greater emphasis on high-quality training and registered apprenticeships alongside college pathways.

Federal or State Administration

Supporters of federal grants emphasize national access and civil-rights purposes. Critics argue that states and institutions can design more flexible local programs.

Race and McNair

Debate concerns whether and how federal policy may address underrepresentation in doctoral education while complying with constitutional equal-protection requirements.

Service Depth or Breadth

Intensive programs can produce deeper support for fewer participants. Broad outreach can reach more people but provide less individualized service.

Interagency Management

The 2026 Education–Labor partnership may improve coordination with workforce systems, but grantees can face uncertainty concerning authority, grants platforms, oversight, and long-term administration.

Key Distinctions

TRIO and GEAR UP

TRIO consists of multiple programs serving selected individuals across several educational stages. GEAR UP commonly serves cohorts of students or priority students through state and partnership grants beginning no later than middle school.

TRIO and Federal Student Aid

TRIO provides outreach and support services. Pell Grants, work-study, and federal loans provide financial assistance under separate Title IV programs.

TRIO Eligibility and Entitlement

Eligibility permits selection by a funded project. It does not guarantee services or money.

Talent Search and Upward Bound

Talent Search generally provides broad outreach and counseling to larger numbers. Upward Bound provides more intensive academic and summer programming to smaller cohorts.

Talent Search and Educational Opportunity Centers

Talent Search focuses heavily on secondary students and younger participants. EOC focuses principally on adults and educational reentry.

Upward Bound and Upward Bound Math-Science

Regular Upward Bound provides broad college preparation. UBMS concentrates on mathematics, science, research, and STEM pathways.

Upward Bound and Veterans Upward Bound

Regular Upward Bound generally serves secondary-school students. VUB serves veterans preparing to enter or resume postsecondary education.

Student Support Services and Other TRIO Programs

SSS serves students already enrolled at the grantee institution. Precollege and outreach programs generally prepare individuals to enter postsecondary education.

McNair and Graduate Fellowships

McNair prepares undergraduates for doctoral study. It is not itself a graduate fellowship or guarantee of graduate-school funding.

Training Program and Student Services

The Training Program serves project personnel rather than students or prospective students.

Geographic Scope

TRIO projects operate in every region of the United States, including states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and participating territories.

Projects serve urban neighborhoods, rural communities, Tribal areas, migrant communities, military-connected populations, suburban districts, correctional reentry populations, community colleges, regional universities, research institutions, and minority-serving institutions.

National availability is uneven. Some communities contain several overlapping projects, while other high-poverty areas have no local TRIO provider.

Transportation, broadband access, school distance, college location, and geographic isolation influence recruitment and participation. Residential summer programs and virtual advising can reduce some barriers but introduce cost, technology, and supervision challenges.

Alaska and Hawaii use separate low-income levels. Projects in United States territories follow Department procedures concerning the applicable poverty standard and documentation.

The federal footprint map records project locations but does not show every school, campus, county, or individual reached by a grantee.

Summary

The Federal TRIO Programs are the principal national outreach and student-support system serving low-income individuals, potential first-generation college students, students with disabilities, veterans, and other eligible individuals facing barriers to postsecondary education.

The name “TRIO” comes from the original three programs: Upward Bound, Talent Search, and Student Support Services. The modern system contains eight commonly listed programs, seven serving students or prospective students and one training project personnel.

Talent Search and Educational Opportunity Centers provide broad outreach, counseling, and application assistance. Upward Bound, UBMS, and VUB provide intensive preparation. Student Support Services assists enrolled college students. McNair prepares undergraduates for doctoral study. The Training Program strengthens project staff.

TRIO operates through competitive grants to institutions and organizations. An eligible individual does not possess an automatic right to participate. Projects have limited spaces, target areas, selection procedures, approved objectives, and federal reporting duties.

Most direct-service projects must ensure that at least two-thirds of participants satisfy the applicable combined low-income and first-generation or disability-related standard. For 2026, the low-income level equals 150 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.

Services can include instruction, tutoring, advising, mentoring, summer programs, financial literacy, FAFSA assistance, college visits, career exploration, transfer support, disability assistance, veteran services, research, internships, graduate admissions, and doctoral mentoring.

TRIO received $1.191 billion in fiscal year 2025. Fiscal year 2024 grants were funded to serve approximately 869,000 participants across the seven direct-service programs, although this is not a verified unduplicated count of individuals actually served.

The programs vary greatly in intensity. Educational Opportunity Centers cost approximately $302 per funded participant in fiscal year 2024, while McNair cost approximately $10,138.

TRIO emerged from the War on Poverty and Civil Rights eras and remains important to African American communities, HBCUs, community colleges, rural areas, veterans, students with disabilities, and first-generation families.

In 2026, the Departments of Education and Labor began co-administering selected grant competitions. The administration also proposed eliminating TRIO funding in fiscal year 2027, but the proposal has not been enacted.

The future of TRIO depends on congressional appropriations, grant administration, evaluation evidence, institutional capacity, legal interpretation, and the continuing national debate over whether college access and completion support should be a federal, state, institutional, or shared responsibility.
