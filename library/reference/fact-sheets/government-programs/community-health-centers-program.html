Community Health Centers Program

The federal primary-care programme rooted in the War on Poverty that supports locally governed health centres serving medically underserved communities and providing care regardless of a patient’s ability to pay.

What Is the Community Health Centers Program?

The Community Health Centers Program is the nationwide system of nonprofit, public, tribal, and community-based organisations operating under the federal Health Center Program. These organisations deliver primary and preventive health services in communities designated as medically underserved or to populations with distinctive barriers to care.

“Community health centre” is widely used as a descriptive term, but not every clinic using that name participates in the federal programme. A federally supported health centre must receive a Section 330 award, operate as a qualifying subrecipient, or receive HRSA look-alike designation.

A health centre is also not identical to one clinic building. A single corporate grantee may operate numerous fixed clinics, school-based sites, mobile units, shelters, public-housing sites, dental clinics, pharmacies, and seasonal service locations within an approved scope of project.

The programme is designed to supplement—not replace—insurance revenue and local resources. Federal grant funds help centres provide care to uninsured and underinsured patients, maintain services that are poorly reimbursed, and support enabling services needed to make medical care practically accessible.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet examines the federal Health Center Program and the institutions commonly called community health centres. It covers the programme’s War on Poverty origins, Section 330 authority, eligibility, service areas, required services, sliding-fee discounts, patient governance, FQHC reimbursement, look-alike status, Federal Tort Claims Act protection, 340B participation, workforce programmes, civil-rights significance, financial structure, performance reporting, limitations, and policy conditions through August 2, 2026.

It does not determine whether a particular organisation is a health centre, whether a specific clinic accepts new patients, or how an individual service will be billed. Those determinations depend on current HRSA designation, approved scope of project, payer contracts, clinical capacity, and local operations.

Historical Context

Before the modern health-centre movement, low-income communities depended on public hospitals, charity clinics, local health departments, segregated medical institutions, maternal and child-health programmes, and private physicians willing to provide uncompensated care.

Access was especially limited in rural areas, segregated Southern communities, migrant-labour regions, public-housing developments, and urban neighbourhoods isolated from hospitals and private medical practices.

Federal hospital-construction support under the Hill-Burton Act expanded medical infrastructure after 1946, but hospital construction did not guarantee access to routine primary care. Segregation, inability to pay, physician shortages, transportation, and exclusionary admission practices remained major barriers.

The Civil Rights Movement and War on Poverty reframed health care as part of a larger struggle involving poverty, political power, food, housing, sanitation, education, employment, and racial equality.

Physicians H. Jack Geiger and Count D. Gibson Jr. drew on community-oriented primary-care models developed in South Africa and proposed neighbourhood health centres that would combine clinical care with community organisation and action on the social conditions producing illness.

Creation of the Neighborhood Health Centers

In 1965, the Office of Economic Opportunity approved a demonstration grant supporting two pioneering neighbourhood health centres affiliated with Tufts University.

The Columbia Point Health Center opened in a Boston public-housing community in December 1965. The Delta Health Center developed in Mound Bayou, Mississippi, and began serving the rural Mississippi Delta during the following period.

The centres were designed as more than outpatient medical offices. They provided primary care, prevention, home visits, health education, nutrition assistance, sanitation initiatives, employment, transportation, and community participation.

In Mississippi, the model confronted extreme poverty, racial exclusion, hunger, inadequate water and sanitation, and a shortage of physicians. The centre used health services as an entry point for broader community development.

The Office of Economic Opportunity expanded the demonstration to additional urban and rural communities. By the early 1970s, administrative responsibility moved from the antipoverty agency into the federal health system.

Permanent Section 330 Authority

Congress established the Community Health Center Program within the Public Health Service Act through the Health Revenue Sharing and Health Services Act of 1975.

Section 330 provided a permanent health-programme framework for grants serving medically underserved populations. It formalised requirements concerning comprehensive services, community need, access regardless of ability to pay, sliding fees, and governing-board participation.

Subsequent legislation integrated community, migrant, homeless, and public-housing health-centre authorities into the modern Section 330 structure.

The Health Centers Consolidation Act of 1996 reorganised the separate statutory authorities into Section 330 and adopted the broader federal term “health center.”

Section 330 Service Categories

Section 330(e): Community Health Centers
Supports organisations serving medically underserved areas or populations in urban and rural communities.
Section 330(g): Migratory and Seasonal Agricultural Workers
Supports services for migratory and seasonal agricultural workers and their families, addressing mobility, occupational exposure, transportation, language, housing, and continuity-of-care barriers.
Section 330(h): Health Care for the Homeless
Supports care for people experiencing homelessness and requires substance-use-disorder services in addition to ordinary required health-centre services.
Section 330(i): Public Housing Primary Care
Supports services for residents of public housing and people living in areas immediately accessible to public-housing communities.

A health-centre award can include one or several categories. Target populations and service sites must appear within the HRSA-approved scope of project.

Medically Underserved Areas and Populations

The programme directs resources to geographic areas and population groups with inadequate access to primary care.

Federal designation methods consider factors such as poverty, infant mortality, population age, and the supply of primary-care physicians. Special population designations recognise barriers affecting groups that may not be captured fully by geography.

Medically Underserved Area or Population status is distinct from a Health Professional Shortage Area designation, although the same community can hold both.

A health centre must conduct or update a needs assessment that describes the population, barriers, health conditions, available providers, and unmet service needs in its service area.

Eligible Organisations

Health Center Program applicants must be public or nonprofit entities meeting federal organisational and programme requirements.

  • private nonprofit corporations;
  • state, territorial, county, city, or municipal governments;
  • public health departments;
  • tribes and tribal organisations;
  • urban Indian organisations;
  • public agencies operating with a compliant co-applicant governing board; and
  • qualifying organisations in United States-affiliated jurisdictions.

The applicant must own and control the health-centre project rather than function as a subsidiary whose programme authority is controlled by another organisation. HRSA evaluates corporate independence, governance, assets, contracts, and decision-making authority.

Funded Health Centres and Look-Alikes

Health Center Program award recipient
An organisation receiving federal grant funding under Section 330.
Health Center Program subrecipient
An organisation receiving Section 330 funds through a formal subaward and meeting applicable programme requirements.
Health Center Program look-alike
An organisation designated by HRSA as meeting Health Center Program requirements but not receiving a Section 330 operating grant.

Look-alikes can receive FQHC Medicare and Medicaid payment, participate in the 340B Drug Pricing Program, access National Health Service Corps recruitment benefits, and qualify for certain other federal advantages.

Look-alikes do not receive Section 330 grant funding and do not receive health-centre Federal Tort Claims Act malpractice protection merely because of look-alike status.

HRSA-Approved Scope of Project

A health centre’s approved scope of project identifies the sites, services, service area, target population, and service-delivery methods that constitute the federally recognised programme.

HRSA records this information through programme forms covering sites, services, and other project details. A centre generally must obtain approval before making material scope changes.

Scope affects grant allowability, FQHC status, FTCA coverage, 340B participation, reporting, and compliance review.

A service can be clinically useful yet remain outside the federal project if it is not included in the approved scope.

Required Primary Health Services

Section 330 requires health centres to provide comprehensive primary health services directly or through written contracts and cooperative arrangements.

  • basic health services related to family medicine, internal medicine, paediatrics, obstetrics, and gynaecology;
  • diagnostic laboratory and radiology services;
  • preventive health services, including prenatal, perinatal, well-child, immunisation, family-planning, cancer-screening, communicable-disease, and other preventive care;
  • emergency medical services and after-hours coverage;
  • pharmaceutical services as appropriate;
  • referrals to other providers and institutions;
  • patient case management;
  • services enabling patients to use the centre effectively;
  • health education concerning disease prevention and appropriate use of health services; and
  • additional services required for special statutory populations.

A health centre does not have to employ every specialist or operate every service at every site. It must ensure access through its own staff, contracts, referrals, or cooperative arrangements consistent with federal requirements.

Additional Health Services

Health centres may provide additional services needed by their population and approved by HRSA.

  • oral health and dental care;
  • behavioural and mental-health treatment;
  • substance-use-disorder treatment;
  • vision and optometric services;
  • pharmacy services;
  • specialty medical care;
  • home health;
  • nutrition services;
  • physical and occupational therapy;
  • school-based services;
  • mobile medical and dental care;
  • telehealth;
  • community health workers;
  • infectious-disease prevention and treatment;
  • maternal-health programmes; and
  • chronic-disease management.

Service breadth depends on community need, workforce, payer coverage, facility capacity, grants, and partnerships.

Enabling Services

Enabling services address nonclinical barriers that prevent patients from obtaining or completing care.

  • case management;
  • outreach;
  • transportation;
  • interpretation and language assistance;
  • eligibility and insurance-enrolment assistance;
  • health education;
  • housing and social-service referral;
  • community health-worker support;
  • care coordination; and
  • assistance obtaining medicines, food, and other resources.

Conventional insurance frequently reimburses these activities poorly or not at all. Section 330 grant support helps centres provide them because access to a clinician has limited value when a patient cannot travel, communicate, afford medication, or navigate the health system.

Access Regardless of Ability to Pay

Health centres must serve all people within their service area or target population regardless of ability to pay.

This rule does not mean every service is free. Centres establish fee schedules, bill third-party payers, assess patient income, and collect affordable amounts under written policies.

A person lacking insurance, lawful immigration status, documents, or immediate payment cannot be excluded solely for inability to pay when seeking services within the centre’s approved project.

Centres may use reasonable collection practices but must avoid barriers that undermine access. Policies should consider patient income, family size, hardship, service urgency, and the cost of collection.

Sliding-Fee Discount Programme

Every health centre must operate a sliding-fee discount programme applying to required and additional services within the approved scope for which the centre charges distinct fees.

At or below 100 percent of the federal poverty guidelines
The centre must provide a full discount or may charge only a nominal amount designed not to create a barrier to care.
Above 100 percent and at or below 200 percent
The centre must provide partial discounts through at least three graduated pay classes.
Above 200 percent
The centre is not federally required to provide a sliding discount, although it may adopt other assistance policies.

Eligibility must be based only on income and family size. The centre evaluates whether contracted or referred services apply the required discount and whether nominal charges remain affordable.

The governing board approves the fee and discount schedules and periodically evaluates whether the programme is reducing financial barriers.

Patient-Directed Governance

Community governance is one of the programme’s defining features. In the standard model, at least 51 percent of governing-board members must be patients currently served by the centre.

Patient members should collectively represent the demographic characteristics of the people served, including race, ethnicity, sex, and other relevant factors.

The remaining members should contribute expertise in community affairs, government, finance, law, health care, business, labour, social services, or other fields.

The board selects, evaluates, and can remove the chief executive; approves the budget; establishes policy; reviews services and hours; adopts fee schedules; evaluates quality; and exercises authority over the federal project.

Tribal and qualifying urban Indian health centres are exempt from specified federal board requirements. Special-population centres not funded under the ordinary community-health-centre authority may request approved waivers of patient-majority composition.

Significance of Patient Governance

The patient-majority board requirement distinguishes health centres from hospital-owned clinics, private physician groups, and many charitable providers.

It gives patients formal authority over executive leadership, finances, services, hours, priorities, and quality rather than limiting community participation to advisory consultation.

Governance can increase accountability and trust, but legal authority alone does not guarantee meaningful participation. Boards require accessible meetings, training, accurate information, independent judgment, and freedom from executive domination.

Recruiting active patients can be difficult when members face work, childcare, transportation, disability, language, or health barriers. Centres must build governance capacity rather than treat patient membership as a numerical compliance exercise.

Hours, Emergencies, and Continuity of Care

Health centres must provide hours responsive to patient need and ensure professional coverage for medical emergencies during and after regular operating hours.

After-hours coverage can include health-centre clinicians, cooperative arrangements, telephone triage, nurse call lines, extended-hour sites, urgent-care arrangements, and referral to emergency departments.

The centre must ensure continuity of care, hospital admission when medically necessary, and follow-up after hospital or emergency treatment.

Merely directing every caller to dial emergency services without clinical assessment ordinarily does not satisfy the full after-hours requirement.

Clinical Staffing and Credentials

A health centre must maintain the clinical staff necessary to provide services within its approved scope and service area.

Staff can include physicians, nurse practitioners, physician associates, certified nurse-midwives, nurses, dentists, dental hygienists, pharmacists, psychologists, social workers, counsellors, community health workers, and other licensed or trained personnel.

Centres must verify licensure, education, training, competence, health status, and other credentials and must grant clinical privileges consistent with each professional’s qualifications.

Contracts do not eliminate centre responsibility. The health centre must ensure that contracted clinicians and partner organisations satisfy applicable credentialing, quality, and access expectations.

Quality Improvement and Risk Management

Health centres must maintain ongoing quality-improvement and quality-assurance systems covering clinical services and patient safety.

These systems review medical records, clinical outcomes, patient complaints, incidents, infection control, credentialing, referral completion, medication safety, and other risk areas.

Federal Tort Claims Act deeming requires additional risk-management, claims-management, credentialing, and quality documentation.

HRSA recognises centres for quality achievement, health-information technology, access, value, and reduction of disparities through annual performance-recognition programmes.

Uniform Data System

Health Center Program award recipients and look-alikes report operational and performance data annually through the Uniform Data System.

UDS reporting covers:

  • patient demographics;
  • income and insurance status;
  • special populations;
  • service use;
  • staffing;
  • clinical quality measures;
  • costs and revenue;
  • managed-care participation;
  • delivery sites; and
  • selected health outcomes.

HRSA uses the data for oversight, funding, research, performance comparison, service-area analysis, and quality improvement.

UDS is one of the largest standardised primary-care datasets in the United States, but it generally reports aggregated health-centre information rather than complete person-level longitudinal records.

Current Scale and Reach

HRSA’s latest complete national data show that more than 32.4 million people used funded health centres in 2024.

The programme includes nearly 1,400 health-centre organisations and more than 16,200 service-delivery sites across urban, suburban, rural, frontier, island, agricultural, public-housing, school, shelter, and mobile settings.

Health-centre patients include children, working-age adults, older adults, Medicaid beneficiaries, Medicare beneficiaries, privately insured patients, and people without insurance.

The programme’s scale gives it a national role in vaccination, cancer screening, maternal health, chronic-disease management, oral health, behavioural health, substance-use treatment, HIV prevention, and public-health emergency response.

Financing

Community health centres operate through blended financing rather than one federal appropriation.

Section 330 Grants

Federal grants support approved operating costs, uninsured care, enabling services, population-specific activities, quality, infrastructure, and access expansion. Awards are designed to supplement other revenue.

Medicaid

Medicaid is a major payer because many health-centre patients qualify for the programme. Federal law establishes prospective-payment protections for FQHC services, while states can adopt approved alternative payment methodologies.

Medicare

Medicare pays qualifying FQHC services through a national prospective payment system adjusted for geography and selected visit characteristics.

Private Insurance and Patient Revenue

Centres contract with commercial insurers and collect patient responsibility under sliding-fee and collection policies.

Other Public and Private Resources

State grants, local appropriations, foundation support, Ryan White funding, behavioural-health grants, capital awards, 340B-related revenue, and philanthropy supplement operations.

Federal Appropriation Structure

The federal Health Center Program has been financed through annual discretionary appropriations and mandatory appropriations from the Community Health Center Fund.

The Affordable Care Act created the mandatory fund in 2010 to expand health-centre capacity and related primary-care workforce programmes. Congress has repeatedly extended the fund through health-programme legislation.

Short-term extensions and continuing resolutions can complicate hiring, construction, service expansion, and multiyear planning even when funding ultimately continues.

The fiscal year 2027 presidential budget request proposed $1.9 billion in discretionary funding for health centres and described continued support for approximately 1,400 organisations and more than 16,000 sites. A presidential budget is a proposal to Congress and does not itself set final programme funding.

Federally Qualified Health Centre Status

“Federally qualified health centre” is a Medicare and Medicaid payment designation created by the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1989.

The category generally includes:

  • organisations receiving Section 330 grants;
  • qualifying entities receiving Section 330 funds through a grant recipient;
  • HRSA-designated look-alikes; and
  • certain tribal and urban Indian health organisations treated as FQHCs under federal law.

FQHC status creates payment and coverage consequences but does not mean the federal government owns the centre.

Most centres are independent nonprofit corporations governed locally.

Medicaid Payment

Federal law requires state Medicaid programmes to reimburse FQHCs through a prospective payment system or a qualifying alternative payment methodology agreed to by the centre.

The prospective rate is generally based on historical costs, adjusted over time, with provisions for changes in service scope.

When a Medicaid managed-care organisation pays less than the amount due under the FQHC methodology, the state may make a supplemental “wraparound” payment.

Payment disputes commonly involve visit definitions, managed-care data, scope changes, behavioural health, dental services, telehealth, inflation updates, and timely supplemental payments.

Adequate Medicaid payment is critical because Section 330 grants are not designed to replace insurance reimbursement for covered patients.

Medicare Payment

Medicare pays FQHC services under a prospective payment system implemented in 2014 pursuant to the Affordable Care Act.

Payment is based on a national encounter rate adjusted for geographic differences and certain qualifying visits. Medicare generally pays 80 percent of the lesser of the FQHC charge or the adjusted prospective-payment amount, subject to preventive-service and beneficiary cost-sharing rules.

Covered encounters can involve physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, certified nurse-midwives, clinical psychologists, clinical social workers, and other authorised practitioners.

Medicare payment rules are separate from Section 330 grant requirements and are administered by CMS and Medicare contractors.

340B Drug Pricing Program

Health Center Program award recipients, qualifying subrecipients, and look-alikes can participate as covered entities in the 340B Drug Pricing Program.

The programme requires drug manufacturers participating in Medicaid to offer covered outpatient drugs to eligible entities at statutorily discounted prices.

Health centres can dispense through in-house pharmacies or eligible contract-pharmacy arrangements, subject to federal registration, patient, diversion, duplicate-discount, and audit requirements.

The difference between acquisition cost and reimbursement can support pharmacy operations, medication access, uninsured care, and other health-centre services.

340B policy remains contested among covered entities, manufacturers, pharmacies, states, and federal agencies concerning contract pharmacies, rebates, duplicate discounts, transparency, patient definition, and programme authority.

Federal Tort Claims Act Protection

The Federally Supported Health Centers Assistance Acts of 1992 and 1995 permit qualifying Section 330-funded health centres to obtain federal medical-malpractice protection.

A health centre must apply annually and demonstrate compliance with credentialing, privileging, risk management, claims management, quality improvement, and other requirements.

When HRSA approves deeming, the centre and covered governing-board members, officers, employees, and specified contractors are treated as Public Health Service employees for covered medical, surgical, dental, and related functions.

Claims are handled under the Federal Tort Claims Act rather than ordinary private malpractice litigation against the deemed individual or centre, when statutory conditions are met.

Deeming reduces malpractice-insurance expense and allows more resources to support patient care. It does not cover every act, site, person, service, or time period. Look-alikes do not receive this protection through designation alone.

National Health Service Corps

Health centres frequently serve as approved sites for the National Health Service Corps.

The Corps provides scholarships and loan repayment to clinicians who agree to work in Health Professional Shortage Areas.

Eligible disciplines include primary-care physicians, dentists, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, nurse-midwives, behavioural-health clinicians, and other authorised professionals.

The relationship helps centres recruit clinicians but does not eliminate competition with hospitals, private practices, telehealth companies, and other employers.

Teaching Health Centers

Many health centres participate in community-based medical and dental education.

The Teaching Health Center Graduate Medical Education Program, created by the Affordable Care Act, funds residency training in community-based ambulatory settings.

Most participating programmes are located in community health centres, rural health clinics, and tribal health centres.

Training clinicians in underserved settings can strengthen recruitment and retention, increase exposure to population health, and shift graduate medical education away from exclusive reliance on teaching hospitals.

Workforce Challenges

Workforce scarcity is one of the programme’s most persistent constraints.

  • primary-care physician shortages;
  • dental and behavioural-health shortages;
  • competition for nurses, medical assistants, and pharmacists;
  • high vacancy and turnover;
  • rural recruitment barriers;
  • burnout and administrative workload;
  • limited specialty referral capacity;
  • housing and childcare costs for employees;
  • licensing and credentialing delays; and
  • salary competition with hospital systems and private employers.

Centres respond through team-based care, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, community health workers, telehealth, residencies, loan repayment, training partnerships, and expanded support roles.

Civil-Rights Context

Community health centres emerged from the Civil Rights Movement and the federal struggle against segregated and exclusionary medical care.

Section 330 organisations are subject to applicable federal civil-rights laws, which can include:

  • Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
  • section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973;
  • the Age Discrimination Act of 1975;
  • the Americans with Disabilities Act;
  • section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act;
  • federal language-access requirements; and
  • other federal, state, territorial, tribal, and local protections.

Compliance can require nondiscriminatory access, effective communication, language assistance, physical accessibility, reasonable accommodation, equal employment practice, and fair administration of fees and services.

Significance for African American Communities

The health-centre movement is inseparable from African American civil-rights history. Its formative rural centre was established in Mound Bayou, Mississippi, a historically Black town surrounded by Delta communities experiencing extreme poverty, plantation labour systems, medical scarcity, racial violence, and political exclusion.

The early model treated poor health as connected to hunger, unsafe water, inadequate housing, unemployment, transportation, education, and power. Centre staff therefore combined clinical care with nutrition, sanitation, employment, outreach, and community organisation.

Health centres later became major providers in Black urban neighbourhoods affected by hospital segregation, redlining, disinvestment, public-housing isolation, industrial decline, environmental hazards, and physician shortages.

Patient governance offered a structural alternative to institutions in which Black communities received services but possessed little decision-making authority.

The programme does not eliminate racial disparities. Black patients can still encounter maternal-health inequity, specialty delays, hospital closures, underfunded facilities, clinician bias, medical mistrust, unstable insurance, and unequal exposure to chronic disease.

Programme performance should therefore be evaluated by access, quality, trust, community control, leadership, employment, procurement, health outcomes, and the centre’s ability to strengthen local institutions.

Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Workers

Migrant Health Centers predate the permanent Community Health Center statute. Congress created federal migrant-health authority in 1962.

Migratory and seasonal agricultural workers face occupational injury, pesticide exposure, heat, transportation barriers, temporary housing, language differences, limited insurance, and interrupted continuity of care.

Health centres serving this population use mobile units, seasonal sites, outreach workers, evening hours, interpretation, employer and community partnerships, and portable records.

The statutory category includes qualifying family members and permits services adapted to migratory work patterns.

Health Care for the Homeless

Health Care for the Homeless projects serve people living in shelters, streets, vehicles, temporary arrangements, and other unstable conditions recognised by federal law.

Services can include primary care, mental-health treatment, substance-use services, outreach, street medicine, mobile clinics, case management, respite care, and housing coordination.

Section 330(h) requires substance-use-disorder services in addition to ordinary primary health services.

Health centres can continue serving certain formerly homeless patients for a limited period after permanent housing placement and can serve specified children, youth, and veterans at risk of homelessness.

Public Housing Primary Care

Public Housing Primary Care projects address barriers experienced by residents of public housing and nearby communities.

Common needs include chronic disease, maternal and child health, behavioural health, environmental exposure, disability, transportation, violence, food access, and limited nearby medical services.

Health centres can operate clinics within housing developments, nearby community facilities, schools, mobile units, or broader delivery networks.

Successful programmes coordinate with residents, housing authorities, tenant councils, schools, social-service agencies, and local government without compromising health-centre governance or patient confidentiality.

Rural and Frontier Significance

Community health centres are central institutions in rural areas where private practices, pharmacies, maternity units, dental providers, and hospitals may be distant or financially unstable.

Rural centres commonly operate satellite clinics, mobile units, school sites, telehealth, pharmacy services, dental programmes, and transportation assistance across large geographic areas.

Challenges include workforce recruitment, low patient density, travel distance, broadband limitations, emergency transport, hospital closure, ageing facilities, and limited specialty networks.

A health centre can preserve primary-care access after other providers leave, but it cannot substitute fully for an emergency department, inpatient hospital, surgeon, obstetric unit, or advanced diagnostic facility.

Tribal and Urban Indian Health Centres

Tribes, tribal organisations, and urban Indian organisations can participate in the Health Center Program under specialised authorities.

Federal law exempts qualifying tribal and urban Indian centres from specified patient-majority board requirements in recognition of sovereign and statutory governance structures.

These centres may coordinate Section 330, Indian Health Service, Medicaid, Medicare, 340B, and tribal resources.

Programme participation does not replace the federal trust responsibility or Indian Health Service obligations.

Public-Health Role

Although health centres are primary-care providers rather than ordinary public-health departments, their community reach gives them an important public-health function.

  • vaccination;
  • infectious-disease testing and treatment;
  • cancer screening;
  • maternal and infant care;
  • chronic-disease surveillance and management;
  • HIV prevention and treatment;
  • opioid and substance-use treatment;
  • nutrition services;
  • emergency response;
  • community outreach; and
  • health education.

Partnerships with local health departments, hospitals, schools, emergency-management agencies, and community organisations are essential because no single institution controls every public-health function.

COVID-19 Response

During the COVID-19 pandemic, health centres provided testing, vaccination, treatment, outreach, telehealth, and continuity of primary care in communities experiencing high infection and mortality.

Congress and the executive branch supplied supplemental funds for operations, workforce, vaccination, testing, construction, and technology.

The emergency demonstrated the programme’s capacity to reach uninsured, rural, immigrant, homeless, and medically underserved populations.

It also intensified workforce burnout, deferred preventive care, financial volatility, supply shortages, and behavioural-health demand.

Facilities and Capital Development

Health centres require clinical space, dental equipment, laboratories, pharmacies, information systems, mobile units, and accessible facilities.

Section 330 operating awards do not automatically finance major construction. Capital projects can use supplemental federal awards, state and local funds, New Markets Tax Credits, philanthropy, loans, bonds, Community Development Block Grants, and other financing.

HRSA operates a Health Center Facility Loan Guarantee Program that can reduce lender risk for qualifying capital projects.

Construction projects can trigger environmental review, historic-preservation, procurement, labour, accessibility, and federal-interest requirements.

Health Information Technology

Health centres use electronic health records, patient portals, telehealth, health-information exchange, data warehouses, registries, and quality-reporting systems.

Technology supports care coordination, population management, prescription safety, billing, UDS reporting, and remote access.

Barriers include cybersecurity risk, vendor cost, interoperability, broadband, digital literacy, staff training, privacy, and duplicate reporting requirements.

Digital expansion must not replace accessible in-person, telephone, language, and disability-accommodated service channels.

Federal Oversight

HRSA assesses programme eligibility and compliance through applications, progress reports, Uniform Data System reports, audits, conditions of award, document review, and operational site visits.

The Health Center Program Compliance Manual is the principal consolidated resource explaining programme requirements.

A health centre found noncompliant can receive conditions requiring corrective action. Continued failure can lead to progressive enforcement, shortened project periods, suspension, termination, or loss of look-alike designation.

FTCA, 340B, Medicare, Medicaid, federal grants, and civil-rights requirements have separate oversight systems in addition to ordinary Section 330 review.

Programme Integrity

Programme-integrity risks include unsupported grant costs, inaccurate patient data, conflicts of interest, procurement violations, improper discounts, billing errors, diversion of 340B drugs, duplicate discounts, credentialing failures, and weak board oversight.

Health centres must maintain internal controls, independent audits, written contracts, conflict policies, financial systems, inventory controls, billing procedures, and governing-board review.

Oversight must protect federal resources without creating administrative burdens that divert scarce staff from patient care.

Relationship to Hospitals and Health Systems

Health centres coordinate with hospitals for emergency care, inpatient admission, diagnostics, specialists, labour and delivery, and post-discharge follow-up.

Some hospitals support centres through referrals, specialists, laboratory services, grants, or shared programmes.

A health centre must retain independent governance and control of its federal project. Hospital partnerships cannot override the authority assigned to the health-centre board.

Hospital consolidation or closure can increase demand on health centres while simultaneously reducing referral and specialty capacity.

Health Centres and Free Clinics

Community health centres and free clinics both serve people with limited access, but they are institutionally different.

Health centre
Operates under Section 330 requirements, bills insurers, uses sliding fees, reports UDS data, and maintains patient-directed governance.
Free clinic
Usually relies heavily on volunteers and charitable support and may limit services to uninsured or low-income patients under its own eligibility policies.

Free clinics can obtain separate federal malpractice protection for qualifying volunteer activity, but they are not automatically FQHCs or Health Center Program participants.

Health Equity

Health centres are structured to reduce disparities by locating services in underserved communities, adapting care to local need, providing language and enabling services, and incorporating patients into governance.

Equity strategies include community health workers, mobile care, extended hours, culturally responsive care, transportation, disability access, interpretation, and targeted chronic-disease programmes.

Equal access to a health centre does not guarantee equal health outcomes. Housing, wealth, education, environmental exposure, food, employment, violence, insurance, and specialty access remain powerful determinants.

Effective equity evaluation requires disaggregated quality data, patient experience, referral completion, leadership diversity, geographic access, and analysis of who remains underserved.

Major Achievements

  • Created a national primary-care network rooted in medically underserved communities.
  • Institutionalised access regardless of ability to pay.
  • Established patient-majority governance as a federal programme standard.
  • Expanded medical, dental, behavioural-health, pharmacy, and enabling services.
  • Served more than 32 million people annually.
  • Created care access in rural, migrant, homeless, public-housing, island, and urban communities.
  • Provided a stable institutional platform for Medicaid and Medicare primary care.
  • Supported discounted medicines through 340B participation.
  • Reduced malpractice-insurance expense through FTCA deeming.
  • Supported clinician recruitment through the National Health Service Corps.
  • Expanded community-based residency and professional training.
  • Provided vaccination, testing, and treatment during public-health emergencies.
  • Developed a national primary-care performance dataset through UDS.
  • Created community employment and leadership opportunities.

Limitations and Criticisms

  • Demand frequently exceeds appointment and workforce capacity.
  • Specialty, hospital, maternity, dental, and behavioural-health referrals can remain difficult.
  • Federal grants cover only part of operating cost.
  • Centres remain exposed to Medicaid policy, insurance changes, and federal funding extensions.
  • Workforce vacancies and burnout constrain service expansion.
  • Sliding fees do not eliminate all affordability barriers, especially for medicines, diagnostics, and outside referrals.
  • Short-term grants can create programmes that are difficult to sustain.
  • Complex compliance systems consume administrative capacity.
  • Patient-majority boards can become formal rather than substantively empowered.
  • Centre quality and service breadth vary.
  • Rural distance and weak broadband can limit access despite federal designation.
  • Health centres cannot replace hospitals or full specialty networks.
  • 340B and payer-policy disputes can create financial uncertainty.
  • Community need can grow faster than facilities and grant funding.
  • Broader racial, economic, and environmental health disparities persist.

Current Policy Issues Through August 2026

Federal Funding Stability

The programme continues to depend on discretionary appropriations and extensions of mandatory health-centre funding. Temporary funding measures complicate long-term workforce and capital planning.

Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Proposal

The administration’s fiscal year 2027 budget proposed $1.9 billion in discretionary Health Center Program funding and described a broader federal health-agency reorganisation. Congress determines final appropriations and statutory organisation.

Workforce Capacity

Recruitment and retention of primary-care, dental, nursing, pharmacy, and behavioural-health staff remain leading operational constraints.

Behavioural Health and Substance Use

Centres continue integrating mental-health and substance-use treatment into primary care while facing workforce, reimbursement, and referral shortages.

Nutrition and Chronic Disease

HRSA encouraged health centres in 2026 to expand nutrition services and food-related interventions as part of chronic-disease prevention and management.

340B Policy

Manufacturer restrictions, contract-pharmacy arrangements, rebates, duplicate-discount controls, and programme transparency remain contested.

Medicaid Change

Eligibility and financing changes can alter patient insurance, health-centre revenue, uncompensated care, and administrative burden.

Cybersecurity and Technology

Centres must modernise information systems and telehealth while managing ransomware, privacy, interoperability, cost, and digital-access risks.

Capital and Facility Needs

Ageing facilities, population growth, dental and behavioural-health expansion, and emergency preparedness create capital needs beyond ordinary operating awards.

Community Governance

The programme continues to balance professional management, federal compliance, corporate complexity, and genuine patient control.

Definitions and Key Distinctions

Health Center Program
The federal programme authorised under Section 330 of the Public Health Service Act.
Community health centre
A common term for a Section 330-funded organisation, although the phrase can also be used informally for unrelated clinics.
Federally qualified health centre
A Medicare and Medicaid payment category including Section 330 grantees, qualifying subrecipients, look-alikes, and certain tribal entities.
Look-alike
An organisation meeting Health Center Program requirements and receiving federal designation without a Section 330 operating grant.
Service area
The geographic community in which the health centre provides services and assesses need.
Target population
A statutorily recognised population served under migrant, homeless, public-housing, or other programme authority.
Scope of project
The HRSA-approved sites, services, service area, target populations, and delivery methods constituting the federal project.
Medically underserved area
A geographic area designated as having insufficient access to primary care under federal criteria.
Medically underserved population
A population group facing economic, cultural, linguistic, geographic, or other access barriers.
Sliding-fee discount
A reduction in charges based on patient income and family size.
Nominal charge
A small charge permitted for patients at or below 100 percent of the federal poverty guidelines when structured not to create an access barrier.
Enabling services
Nonclinical services such as transportation, interpretation, outreach, eligibility assistance, and case management that support access.
Prospective payment system
A method paying a predetermined amount for a qualifying encounter or service.
Wraparound payment
A state Medicaid supplemental payment covering the difference between managed-care payment and the FQHC payment obligation.
FTCA deeming
Annual federal approval treating qualifying health-centre personnel as Public Health Service employees for covered malpractice claims.
340B covered entity
An eligible organisation authorised to purchase covered outpatient drugs at statutory discounts.
Uniform Data System
The annual national reporting system for health-centre patients, services, staffing, finances, and quality.
Operational Site Visit
A structured HRSA review of Health Center Program compliance.

Summary

The Community Health Centers Program, officially the federal Health Center Program, grew from two War on Poverty neighbourhood health-centre demonstrations launched in 1965. The model linked comprehensive primary care, prevention, enabling services, and community participation in places experiencing poverty and medical exclusion.

Section 330 of the Public Health Service Act now supports nearly 1,400 health-centre organisations operating more than 16,200 sites. More than 32.4 million people received care through funded centres in 2024.

Health centres must serve patients regardless of ability to pay, operate sliding-fee discount programmes, provide required primary health services, maintain after-hours coverage, and preserve patient-directed governance.

Their financial model combines federal grants with Medicaid, Medicare, private insurance, patient revenue, 340B participation, state and local support, and supplemental grants. FQHC payment, FTCA malpractice protection, and National Health Service Corps participation strengthen institutional capacity.

The programme’s historical significance extends beyond clinical care. It created community-controlled institutions within the broader Black freedom, antipoverty, migrant-health, rural-health, and health-equity movements.

Its future depends on stable federal funding, adequate Medicaid and Medicare payment, workforce growth, capital investment, meaningful patient governance, civil-rights compliance, and coordination with hospitals, specialists, public health, housing, transportation, and social services.
