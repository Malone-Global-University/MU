HBCU Title III Strengthening Program

The federal institutional-development program providing formula-based grants to eligible historically Black colleges and universities for academic programs, fiscal and administrative capacity, instructional facilities, student services, faculty development, endowment growth, teacher preparation, community outreach, and long-term institutional stability.

Terminology Note

The formal program name is the Strengthening Historically Black Colleges and Universities Program. It is commonly described as the HBCU Title III Program, Title III Part B, Strengthening HBCUs, or the HBCU Formula Grant Program.

Title III of the Higher Education Act contains several institutional-development programs. Title III Part B Strengthening HBCUs should not be confused with the general Title III Part A Strengthening Institutions Program, the Strengthening Historically Black Graduate Institutions Program, or the Title III Part C HBCU Capital Financing Program.

Eligible HBCUs commonly receive two parallel formula awards: annual discretionary funding under Title III Part B, section 323, and permanent annual mandatory funding under Title III Part F, section 371. The two streams use closely related institutional data and purposes but remain legally distinct appropriations.

The term formula grant means that eligible institutions with approved applications receive amounts calculated under statutory factors. It does not mean that funds arrive automatically without an application, required data, accreditation, an approved development plan, and continuing grant compliance.

HBCU status is based on an institution’s history, founding mission, legal authority, and accreditation. The federal definition does not require a current minimum percentage of Black students, and HBCUs admit students without limiting enrollment to one race.

In September 2025, the Department of Education ended discretionary funding for several minority-serving institution programs based on current racial or ethnic enrollment percentages. The Department did not list the Title III Part B HBCU program among those discontinued. The distinction reflects the HBCU program’s historical-status definition rather than a current student-body quota.

Overview

The HBCU Title III Strengthening Program is the principal recurring federal grant program devoted to the institutional capacity of historically Black colleges and universities.

It supports the colleges and universities themselves rather than providing individual student grants directly.

Institutions use the funding to modernise academic programs, renovate instructional facilities, purchase scientific equipment, improve administrative and financial systems, develop faculty, strengthen libraries, provide tutoring and counselling, improve teacher preparation, expand outreach, build fundraising capacity, and support approved endowments.

The program reflects a congressional finding that HBCUs have made distinctive contributions to educational equality while operating with historically limited financial resources and restricted access to public and private capital.

The grant formula rewards service to Pell Grant recipients, successful completion, and progression into graduate or professional fields where Black Americans are underrepresented.

The funding is flexible compared with narrowly targeted academic grants, but every expenditure must appear within an approved application and comprehensive development plan.

Annual continuation funding depends on congressional appropriations, mandatory funding law, accreditation, required formula data, satisfactory progress, performance reporting, and federal grant compliance.

Classification and Scope

This subject belongs under Government Programs because it is a continuing federal grant system with institutional eligibility, statutory formulas, allowable activities, applications, performance periods, funding restrictions, audits, and enforcement.

The Department of Education, Office of Postsecondary Education, and Institutional Service belong under Institutions.

The Higher Education Act of 1965, its 1986 Title III restructuring, the Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008, the Student Aid and Fiscal Responsibility Act, and the FUTURE Act belong under Laws and Legislation.

HBCUs as institutions and the historical development of Black higher education warrant separate entries.

Federal Definition of an HBCU

A Part B institution is a historically Black college or university established before 1964 whose principal mission was, and is, the education of Black Americans.

The institution must be accredited by a nationally recognised accrediting agency or association or be making reasonable progress toward accreditation according to an agency recognised for preaccreditation.

The historic mission reflects the era in which Black students were excluded from or severely restricted within most American colleges and universities.

The statutory definition does not limit admission to Black students or require the institution to maintain a particular current racial enrollment percentage.

Historical Context

Most HBCUs were created after the Civil War to educate formerly enslaved people and their descendants in a society that denied Black Americans equal access to elementary, secondary, professional, and collegiate education.

Public HBCUs often received unequal state appropriations, facilities, land, and academic programs under segregated dual systems.

Private HBCUs relied heavily on churches, missionary organisations, philanthropy, tuition, and alumni contributions from communities with limited accumulated wealth.

Segregation restricted access to graduate and professional education, research funding, libraries, laboratories, hospitals, and public infrastructure.

The civil-rights movement and desegregation did not eliminate historic capital deficits, small endowments, deferred maintenance, underfunding, and the need to serve large numbers of first-generation and low-income students.

Title III institutional aid was designed to strengthen colleges whose limited resources affected their ability to provide equal educational opportunity.

Higher Education Act of 1965

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Higher Education Act on November 8, 1965.

Title III created federal assistance for developing institutions with limited financial resources and large numbers of students needing educational support.

HBCUs became central beneficiaries of the original institutional-aid structure even before Congress created the modern HBCU-specific Part B formula.

Early grants supported faculty development, curriculum, libraries, administration, cooperative arrangements, student services, and institutional planning.

Special Needs Program before 1986

Before the modern Part B structure, Title III included a Special Needs Program serving institutions with exceptional institutional-development needs.

HBCUs received substantial assistance through this earlier authority.

The current regulation preserves eligibility for specified institutions or merged campuses that received Special Needs grants before fiscal year 1986.

Historical references to Title III Part B before 1986 may therefore describe a different organisational structure from the current HBCU formula program.

1986 Title III Restructuring

The Higher Education Amendments of 1986 reorganised Title III and created a dedicated Part B for strengthening historically Black colleges and universities.

Congress separated HBCU assistance from the general Part A institutional-development program.

The new structure codified the historical definition, institutional formula, eligible activities, application requirements, graduate-institution program, audits, and reporting.

The dedicated program reduced the need for HBCUs to compete against other developing institutions for the same limited grant pool.

1992 and 1998 Amendments

Later Higher Education Act reauthorisations revised the formula, application data, allowable activities, minimum grants, underrepresented disciplines, endowment provisions, and general institutional-development authorities.

Congress increasingly connected formula funding with Pell Grant enrollment, graduation, and participation in graduate or professional education.

Amendments also expanded technology, teacher education, community outreach, student services, financial management, and development-office uses.

Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008

The Higher Education Opportunity Act revised the HBCU minimum-allotment structure and other Title III provisions.

Under the current statute, an institution whose formula amount is greater than $250,000 but less than $500,000 receives $500,000.

An institution whose formula amount is $250,000 or less receives $250,000.

The Act also addressed interaction with other HBCU and minority-serving institution grants, endowment use, data requirements, and institutional accountability.

Origins of the Mandatory Funding Stream

Congress created additional mandatory funding for HBCUs and other minority-serving institutions through the College Cost Reduction and Access Act of 2007.

The funding was placed in Title III Part F and used for activities that strengthen academic quality, institutional management, fiscal stability, and educational capacity.

The Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010, through the Student Aid and Fiscal Responsibility Act provisions, extended the mandatory stream through fiscal year 2019.

Institutions commonly administer the Part B and Part F grants as related but distinct projects with separate grant numbers, budgets, performance reports, and legal authority.

FUTURE Act of 2019

President Donald J. Trump signed the Fostering Undergraduate Talent by Unlocking Resources for Education Act on December 19, 2019.

The FUTURE Act made annual Title III Part F funding permanent rather than requiring periodic expiration and reauthorisation.

The statute provides $255 million annually across HBCUs and several other minority-serving institution programs.

Eighty-five million dollars of the annual statutory amount is designated for strengthening HBCUs.

The mandatory funding supplements rather than replaces the annual discretionary Part B appropriation.

HBCU PARTNERS Act of 2020

The HBCU Propelling Agency Relationships Towards a New Era of Results for Students Act placed the federal HBCU initiative and agency-planning requirements in statute.

Federal agencies must prepare annual plans describing efforts to strengthen HBCU participation in federal programs and initiatives.

The law is broader than Title III Part B but reinforces the federal policy of institutional capacity building.

Institutional Eligibility

An institution must:

  • meet the federal definition of a Part B HBCU;
  • have been established before 1964;
  • maintain the historic principal mission of educating Black Americans;
  • be legally authorised by its state or jurisdiction;
  • operate as a junior or community college or provide a bachelor’s-degree program;
  • be accredited or preaccredited by a nationally recognised agency;
  • submit the required application and comprehensive development plan;
  • submit complete formula data;
  • maintain federal grant eligibility; and
  • satisfy annual continuation and performance requirements.

Accreditation and Preaccreditation

Accreditation is a continuing eligibility condition rather than a one-time application requirement.

The accrediting body must be nationally recognised by the Secretary of Education as a reliable authority concerning educational quality.

An institution making reasonable progress toward accreditation may qualify through recognised preaccreditation or candidacy status.

Loss of accreditation causes termination of unexpended grant funds under the program regulation.

Accreditation probation, show-cause action, financial responsibility concerns, or governance problems can also affect grant risk and institutional stability even before formal loss.

State Legal Authorisation

The institution must remain legally authorised in its state or jurisdiction to operate as an eligible junior or community college or to provide a bachelor’s-degree program.

Closure, merger, loss of degree authority, or withdrawal of state authorisation can affect eligibility.

The regulation contains limited legacy provisions for specified merged institutions associated with the pre-1986 Special Needs Program.

Howard University and the University of the District of Columbia

Howard University and the University of the District of Columbia receive special statutory or regulatory treatment because each has separate federal or District funding authority.

Each institution is eligible for a Part B award only when the calculated HBCU grant exceeds the amount scheduled under the specified separate appropriation or governmental authority for that fiscal year.

The special comparison prevents duplicative treatment of federal institutional support while preserving access when the formula amount is greater.

HBCU Lists and Current Designation

The Department of Education maintains statutory and programmatic HBCU lists.

The current eCFR includes a historic list containing former institutional names and some institutions that later merged, closed, or changed status.

Researchers should use the current Department eligibility matrix, NCES identifiers, accreditation records, and award lists rather than treating the old regulatory list as a current operating directory.

Inclusion in an eligibility matrix does not by itself establish receipt of an award in a particular fiscal year.

Application Process

  1. Confirm HBCU and accreditation status. The institution verifies that it meets the Part B definition and current designation requirements.
  2. Collect Phase I data. The institution submits Pell recipient, graduate, and graduate-school attendance information used in the formula.
  3. Assess institutional needs. Leadership evaluates academic, fiscal, administrative, facility, technology, student-service, and development weaknesses.
  4. Prepare the comprehensive development plan. The institution establishes goals, measurable objectives, timeframes, methods, and institutionalisation strategies.
  5. Select legislatively allowable activities. Proposed projects must fit statutory and regulatory uses.
  6. Prepare budgets and assurances. The institution addresses supplement-not-supplant, construction limits, endowment matching, reporting, audit, and state-plan consistency.
  7. Submit the Phase II application. The Department reviews the institutional plan and proposed activities.
  8. Receive formula calculations. The Department calculates Part B and Part F amounts from appropriations and institutional data.
  9. Accept the grant award notification. The institution establishes accounts, internal controls, staff, and project governance.
  10. Complete annual continuation requirements. Future budget periods depend on performance, appropriations, data, accreditation, and compliance.

Phase I Formula Data

Phase I data provide the quantitative basis for each institution’s allocation.

The submission includes:

  • the number of Pell Grant recipients during the relevant preceding school year;
  • the number of graduates during the relevant preceding school year;
  • the number of qualifying graduates attending graduate or professional school;
  • the disciplines in which those students are enrolled;
  • the relevant five-year graduating cohorts used in the denominator;
  • institutional identifiers and accreditation information; and
  • certifications concerning accuracy and eligibility.

Because the formula compares every applicant with all other applicants, a data change at one institution can affect the award amounts of every participating HBCU.

Phase II Institutional Application

Phase II describes the projects, budgets, objectives, personnel, implementation schedule, evaluation, and institutional-development strategy.

The Department determines whether proposed activities are legislatively allowable and connected to the program purpose.

Formula entitlement does not permit use of funds for activities omitted from the approved application.

Institutions often coordinate Part B and Part F activities while maintaining separate budgets and grant records.

Comprehensive Development Plan

The comprehensive development plan is the central institutional strategy required by Part 608.

It must:

  • assess strengths and weaknesses in financial management;
  • assess strengths and weaknesses in academic programs;
  • establish institutional goals based on the assessment;
  • create measurable objectives for each goal;
  • identify timeframes;
  • describe implementation methods and procedures;
  • explain how improvements will become institutionalised; and
  • connect funded activities to long-term growth and self-sufficiency.

Institutionalisation of Improvements

Title III is not intended to create temporary projects that disappear when a grant ends.

The development plan must explain how new practices, systems, curricula, services, technology, and staff capacity will be incorporated into ordinary institutional operations.

Institutionalisation may require permanent policies, trained employees, recurring budgets, maintenance plans, fundraising, integration with accreditation, and continuing assessment.

A grant can purchase equipment, but long-term success depends on staffing, maintenance, replacement, curriculum use, and operating funds.

Five-Year Grant Structure

The Secretary may award a Part B grant for up to five academic years.

The initial application establishes a multi-year project period.

Each annual budget period receives a continuation award subject to appropriations, formula recalculation, progress, reports, accreditation, and grant conditions.

A five-year project is not an unconditional promise of the same annual amount.

Formula data and congressional appropriations can cause award amounts to rise or fall from one year to the next.

The 50–25–25 Allocation Formula

The Department divides available HBCU formula funds into three components.

  • 50 percent: Pell Grant recipients.
  • 25 percent: graduates.
  • 25 percent: graduate or professional school participation in underrepresented disciplines.

The three amounts are calculated separately and added to produce the preliminary institutional allocation.

Minimum-allotment, interaction, eligibility, insufficient-funding, and reallotment rules can then modify the result.

Pell Grant Recipient Component

Fifty percent of available funds is distributed according to the institution’s share of Pell Grant recipients among all applicant HBCUs.

Pell participation serves as a proxy for enrollment of students with financial need.

Larger institutions and institutions serving more Pell recipients generally receive a larger share of this component.

The factor rewards service to low-income students but does not directly measure the depth of each student’s need, institutional cost, or completion.

Graduate-Production Component

Twenty-five percent of funds is distributed according to each institution’s share of graduates among all applicants.

The factor rewards degree completion and institutional productivity.

The Department uses the relevant preceding school-year data submitted by the institution.

Institutions with larger graduating classes generally receive more through this component.

The measure does not by itself adjust for program length, transfer students, part-time enrollment, student preparation, or institutional mission.

Graduate and Professional Education Component

Twenty-five percent is distributed according to the institution’s percentage of graduates entering graduate or professional programs in disciplines where Black Americans are underrepresented.

The numerator uses qualifying graduates who attended such programs within the statutory period after completing a bachelor’s degree.

The denominator uses the institution’s relevant graduating cohorts.

The Department identifies underrepresented disciplines after consultation with the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

This factor encourages preparation for advanced study and entry into professions with limited Black representation.

Disciplines in Which Black Americans Are Underrepresented

A discipline is treated as underrepresented when the percentage of Black Americans in that professional or academic field is lower than the Black percentage of the general United States population under the governing standard.

Relevant areas can include selected science, technology, engineering, mathematics, health, law, business, education, and other professional fields.

The official list used for formula purposes is determined through federal notice and should not be inferred from a general statement that a field lacks diversity.

Minimum-Allotment Rules

The current Higher Education Act establishes a two-tier minimum structure.

  • A formula amount greater than $250,000 but less than $500,000 is increased to $500,000.
  • A formula amount equal to or less than $250,000 is increased to $250,000.

If appropriations are insufficient to fund required minimums, the minimum allotments are reduced proportionally and restored proportionally if additional funds become available.

The current eCFR text continues to state a general $500,000 minimum, while the later-amended statute and current Department budget descriptions recognise the two-tier structure. The statute and annual Department calculation control.

Formula Data Eligibility Conditions

Statutory amendments restrict an institution from receiving an allotment when required formula data demonstrate no qualifying activity in the relevant category.

An institution can lose formula eligibility for a fiscal year if it reports no Pell recipients, no graduates, or—where applicable—no qualifying graduates entering underrepresented graduate or professional disciplines.

The institution must submit complete and timely data in the form required by the Department.

Data verification protects the formula but can create difficulty for small institutions or institutions undergoing system changes.

Reallotment of Unneeded Funds

Funds the Secretary determines will not be required by one grantee during the relevant period may be reallotted to other eligible institutions.

Reallotment follows the statutory formula rather than a new open competition.

Institutions should promptly identify funds they cannot use so that federal resources can be redirected before expiration.

Discretionary Part B Funding

Congress provides annual discretionary appropriations for Strengthening HBCUs under section 323.

Although the program is labelled a discretionary grant program in federal assistance systems, individual awards are formula based rather than scored against other HBCUs in a conventional competitive review.

Annual appropriations determine the size of the total pool.

In fiscal year 2024, the Department reported $400.966 million in discretionary Part B funding.

Mandatory Part F Funding

Title III Part F supplies a second annual funding stream for strengthening HBCUs.

The FUTURE Act made the statutory appropriation permanent.

The Department distributes HBCU Part F awards using formula-based institutional data and administers them through separate grant records.

The statutory annual designation is $85 million for HBCUs, although reported net award totals may reflect administrative reservations or formula implementation.

In fiscal year 2024, the Department reported $80.155 million in Part F awards to ninety-eight institutions.

Fiscal Year 2024 Award Structure

The Department reported:

  • $397.966 million in Part B continuation awards to ninety-eight institutions;
  • $3 million in Part B supplemental awards to thirteen institutions;
  • $400.966 million in total discretionary Part B funding;
  • $80.155 million in Part F continuation awards to ninety-eight institutions; and
  • an average Part B continuation award of approximately $4.06 million.

The Department’s public page contains an inconsistent line labelling total award funding; researchers should calculate and cite Part B and Part F streams separately.

Fiscal Year 2025 Funding Context

In September 2025, the Department announced a one-time additional investment in HBCUs and tribally controlled colleges and universities.

The Department stated that total fiscal year 2025 support for HBCUs across Department programs would exceed $1.34 billion.

That figure covers multiple HBCU-related programs and should not be cited as the Part B appropriation alone.

The Department simultaneously ended several discretionary minority-serving institution programs tied to current racial or ethnic enrollment percentages, but it did not identify Strengthening HBCUs Part B as a terminated program.

Current Status in 2026

The official program page lists the application status as closed and identifies fiscal year 2022 as the last full competition.

Institutions remain within a five-year project cycle and receive annual formula-based continuation awards subject to appropriations, Part F mandatory funding, data, accreditation, and compliance.

The Department publishes an FY 2026 Eligibility Matrix and statutory HBCU lists.

Current award amounts should be verified against grant award notifications and the Department’s annual award tables rather than estimated from a presidential budget request.

Academic Program Development

Institutions may create, revise, strengthen, or expand academic programs connected to their comprehensive development plans.

Activities can include:

  • curriculum design;
  • program review;
  • accreditation preparation;
  • laboratory integration;
  • online and hybrid instruction;
  • general education reform;
  • high-demand degree development;
  • academic assessment;
  • licensure-examination preparation;
  • internship and experiential learning systems;
  • advising redesign; and
  • faculty support for new or revised programs.

STEM and Underrepresented Fields

Part B expressly permits academic instruction in disciplines in which Black Americans are underrepresented.

HBCUs use Title III funds to strengthen science, technology, engineering, mathematics, health professions, computer science, agriculture, education, and other fields.

Funding can support faculty, laboratories, curriculum, equipment, tutoring, advising, and pathways to graduate education.

The program does not require every activity to be a STEM project; the institution’s approved development plan governs its priorities.

Scientific and Laboratory Equipment

Institutions may purchase, rent, or lease scientific and laboratory equipment for instructional or research purposes.

Equipment projects should address identified academic weaknesses and include faculty preparation, student access, maintenance, safety, calibration, cybersecurity, and replacement planning.

A purchase is not effective institutional development when equipment remains unused, lacks trained staff, or cannot be maintained after federal funding ends.

Faculty Development and Exchanges

Allowable activities include faculty exchanges, professional development, and fellowships enabling faculty members to attain advanced degrees in their fields.

Institutions may use development activities to improve pedagogy, research capacity, accreditation credentials, technology use, assessment, and disciplinary expertise.

Faculty support must connect to the approved institutional-development strategy rather than operate as unrelated personal assistance.

Libraries and Educational Materials

Part B funds can purchase library books, periodicals, microfilm, electronic resources, databases, instructional materials, and telecommunications-related educational content.

Library projects may improve accreditation compliance, research support, information literacy, archival preservation, digital access, and shared resources.

Institutions must plan for recurring subscription costs and long-term access after grant support ends.

Tutoring, Counselling, and Student Services

Funds may support tutoring, counselling, academic advising, retention, early-alert systems, supplemental instruction, career development, and other services designed to improve academic success.

Services can be targeted to gateway courses, first-year students, transfer students, adult learners, students with disabilities, or programs with completion gaps.

Part B institutional services should not be confused with an unrestricted scholarship program or individual entitlement.

Financial and Administrative Management

Title III may strengthen budgeting, accounting, grants management, procurement, internal controls, risk management, student accounts, audit systems, data, and financial planning.

Institutions may acquire equipment and systems needed for stronger funds management.

Projects commonly address audit findings, delayed reporting, manual processes, incompatible systems, cybersecurity, and long-term fiscal forecasting.

Strong financial administration protects both federal awards and institutional accreditation.

Information Technology and Digital Infrastructure

Technology can support instruction, learning management, student information, finance, research, libraries, distance education, security, and institutional analytics.

Allowable projects may include equipment, telecommunications services, software, systems integration, network improvements, training, and technology-enabled instruction.

The institution must address procurement, accessibility, cybersecurity, licensing, maintenance, data governance, and sustainability.

The regulation prevents duplication of telecommunications resources available under specified Communications Act assistance.

Institutional Development and Fundraising Office

Funds may establish or improve a development office to increase contributions from alumni and the private sector.

Activities can include:

  • alumni databases;
  • annual giving systems;
  • major-gift capacity;
  • corporate and foundation relations;
  • grant prospecting;
  • planned giving;
  • donor stewardship;
  • campaign planning;
  • advancement technology; and
  • staff training.

The goal is to reduce dependence on short-term federal support by strengthening recurring private revenue.

Joint Use of Facilities

HBCUs may support joint use of laboratories, libraries, technology, and other educational facilities.

Shared arrangements can reduce duplication, expand access to specialised equipment, and support partnerships with nearby institutions, school systems, hospitals, research organisations, and community entities.

Agreements should define ownership, scheduling, maintenance, insurance, security, intellectual property, and continuing costs.

Teacher Education

Institutions may establish or enhance teacher-education programs designed to qualify students to teach in public elementary and secondary schools.

The program must include preparation for state teacher certification.

Funds can support curriculum, clinical partnerships, licensure preparation, faculty, assessment, recruitment, technology, and accreditation.

This authority reflects the historic role of HBCUs in preparing Black educators and serving public school systems.

Elementary and Secondary School Outreach

HBCUs may establish community outreach programs encouraging elementary and secondary students to develop academic skills and interest in postsecondary education.

Activities can include campus exposure, academic enrichment, college preparation, mentoring, summer programs, parent information, and school partnerships.

Outreach must advance the approved institutional-development purpose and should be distinguished from separate TRIO, GEAR UP, and K–12 grant authorities.

Financial and Economic Literacy

Institutions may improve financial and economic literacy for students and their families.

Programs can address budgeting, credit, borrowing, student loans, grants, repayment, savings, investing, consumer protection, and financial decision-making.

The statute gives particular attention to understanding Title IV student-aid programs.

Financial-literacy activity should supplement rather than replace required federal student-aid counselling.

Instructional Facilities and Physical Plant

Funds may support construction, maintenance, renovation, and improvement of classrooms, libraries, laboratories, and other instructional facilities.

Statutory authority also permits acquisition of real property connected to construction, renovation, or improvement of campus facilities.

Projects must comply with environmental, historic-preservation, accessibility, procurement, labor, safety, building, insurance, and federal interest requirements where applicable.

Title III grants are not equivalent to the much larger bond financing available under the HBCU Capital Financing Program.

Fifty-Percent Facilities Limitation

A grantee may not spend more than 50 percent of its award in a fiscal year on constructing or maintaining classrooms, libraries, laboratories, or other instructional facilities.

The limit preserves a substantial share of funding for academic, administrative, student-service, and institutional-development activity.

Institutions must classify facility-related personnel, equipment, technology, renovation, and maintenance costs carefully when applying the ceiling.

Endowment Development

An institution may use no more than 20 percent of its grant to establish or increase an endowment.

Each federal dollar must be matched immediately with one nonfederal dollar when deposited in the endowment.

Federal and matching funds become corpus subject to federal endowment restrictions.

Endowment income may support institutional purposes according to governing rules, while the protected corpus remains invested.

Institutions must maintain separate records, investment controls, valuations, governing approvals, and documentation of nonfederal matching funds.

Purpose of Endowment Assistance

HBCUs historically have held smaller endowments than many comparable institutions because of restricted access to accumulated wealth, alumni income disparities, discriminatory philanthropy, and unequal public support.

Endowment income can provide flexible recurring support for instruction, scholarships funded from earnings, maintenance, student services, faculty, and institutional resilience.

The 20-percent ceiling balances long-term capital growth with immediate institutional needs.

Federal endowment rules prevent an institution from treating matched corpus as ordinary operating cash.

Other Approved Activities

The statute and regulation permit other activities that contribute to the program purpose and receive explicit approval from the Secretary.

This authority gives institutions flexibility to respond to changing educational conditions.

A proposed activity is not allowable merely because it appears useful; it must be included in the application, connected to institutional strengthening, and approved.

Unallowable Activities

Program funds may not support:

  • activities omitted from the approved application;
  • other-purpose activities lacking Secretarial approval;
  • activities inconsistent with an applicable state higher-education plan;
  • activities inconsistent with an applicable state higher-education desegregation plan;
  • religious instruction;
  • religious worship;
  • proselytisation;
  • activities of a school or department devoted solely to preparation for religious vocations;
  • unallowable entertainment or personal expenses;
  • costs lacking adequate documentation;
  • unapproved construction or property activity; or
  • other costs prohibited by statute, Part 608, EDGAR, or the Uniform Guidance.

Religiously Affiliated HBCUs

A religiously affiliated HBCU can receive Title III assistance when it satisfies the institutional definition and uses the funds for secular, allowable institutional-development activities.

Federal funds cannot finance religious worship, instruction, proselytisation, or a divinity school or department whose sole purpose is preparing students for religious vocations.

Institutions must separate federal expenditures, personnel time, facilities, and activities from prohibited religious uses.

Supplement, Not Supplant

Part B funds must add to institutional resources and may not replace money the institution otherwise would have provided.

The applicant explains how federal funds will supplement and, to the extent practical, increase resources for the funded activity.

Supplanting concerns arise when an institution moves an existing recurring cost to Title III without preserving the previously budgeted institutional support.

Documentation may include prior budgets, governing-board decisions, funding history, and explanations of new or expanded activity.

Indirect-Cost Restriction

Part 608 prohibits use of an indirect-cost rate to determine allowable costs.

The institution must charge approved direct costs to the grant according to actual benefit and documentation.

Administrative functions can be allowable when they are part of an approved institutional-strengthening activity, but an unrestricted overhead percentage cannot be added.

Procurement, Equipment, and Real Property

Grantees must follow federal procurement standards, institutional policies, competition requirements, conflict-of-interest rules, cost and price analysis, and documentation requirements.

Equipment records must identify acquisition cost, location, use, condition, disposition, and federal interest.

Construction and real-property projects can create long-term federal obligations concerning title, use, insurance, disposition, and repayment.

Institutions should obtain required prior approvals before purchasing property, changing scope, or beginning major renovation.

Grant Personnel and Time Documentation

Salary and benefit charges must reflect work actually performed for approved activities.

Institutions need personnel records, job descriptions, payroll documentation, and after-the-fact support consistent with federal cost principles.

Title III funds should not support institutional positions unrelated to the approved development plan.

Sustainability planning is necessary when grant-funded positions are intended to continue after the project period.

Performance Measurement

Each activity should include measurable objectives, baselines, annual targets, evidence sources, and responsible personnel.

Measures can include:

  • retention and graduation;
  • course success;
  • licensure pass rates;
  • graduate-school enrollment;
  • faculty credentials;
  • research participation;
  • fundraising and alumni giving;
  • audit findings;
  • financial-system performance;
  • facility completion;
  • technology reliability;
  • student-service use;
  • teacher certification; and
  • institutionalisation of new practices.

Annual Performance Report

Grantees submit an Annual Performance Report for each project year.

The report describes expenditures, activities, objectives, outcomes, personnel, institutionalisation, endowment use, and other required data.

The Department’s public program page states that the reporting window ordinarily opens October 1 and closes ninety days later.

The online system generates a final performance report from annual submissions at the end of the grant.

Audits

Part 608 requires compliance and financial audits at least once every two years.

An institution subject to the federal Single Audit Act can satisfy the program requirement through its compliant Single Audit.

Auditors examine internal controls, allowable costs, cash management, procurement, equipment, endowment, reporting, and compliance with the grant award.

The Department of Education Inspector General must receive access to necessary records.

Recordkeeping

Institutions should retain:

  • approved applications and amendments;
  • grant award notifications;
  • formula data and source records;
  • comprehensive development plans;
  • budgets and ledgers;
  • payroll and personnel records;
  • procurement and contract files;
  • equipment inventories;
  • construction and real-property records;
  • endowment match and investment records;
  • student-service and academic outcome data;
  • performance reports;
  • audit and corrective-action records;
  • prior approvals; and
  • evidence of institutionalisation.

Carryover and Unexpended Funds

Institutions may carry unobligated balances into later budget periods under applicable federal grant rules and Department policy.

Carryover does not permit use outside the approved scope.

The Higher Education Act permits qualifying unexpended funds from the initial five-year period to be used during a succeeding five-year period when properly obligated for an authorised purpose.

Funds not properly expended or used within the statutory period must be repaid to the United States Treasury.

Budget and Program Changes

Some changes can be made under general grant flexibility, while others require prior Department approval.

Approval may be required for:

  • major changes in scope or objectives;
  • new legislatively allowable activities;
  • construction or real-property changes;
  • endowment use;
  • key-personnel changes;
  • significant budget revisions;
  • extensions beyond ordinary authority;
  • changes affecting matching funds; and
  • other actions identified in the award or Uniform Guidance.

Drawdowns and Cash Management

Institutions draw federal funds through the Department’s payment system for immediate allowable needs.

Advance payments must be limited to the minimum amount needed and timed closely to actual disbursement.

Drawing funds early to preserve availability or holding excessive federal cash can create interest liability and audit findings.

Endowment deposits follow separate immediate matching and investment requirements.

Termination and Loss of Eligibility

The Secretary terminates unexpended grant funds when an institution loses accreditation.

Termination also occurs when the institution loses legal authority to operate as an eligible two-year or bachelor’s-degree institution.

Federal enforcement can also include special conditions, suspension, withholding, recovery, debarment, or other remedies for serious noncompliance.

Closure or merger requires careful treatment of assets, records, endowment, federal interests, and remaining balances.

Relationship to Title III Part A

The general Strengthening Institutions Program under Part A serves eligible colleges with low educational expenditures and high enrollment of needy students.

Part B is specifically reserved for HBCUs meeting the historic federal definition.

Part A is generally competitive, while Part B distributes formula allocations among approved eligible HBCUs.

Institutions must follow statutory restrictions governing simultaneous participation in overlapping Title III and Title V programs.

Relationship to the HBCU Graduate Institutions Program

Section 326 establishes the Strengthening Historically Black Graduate Institutions Program.

HBGI awards support legislatively named graduate and professional institutions in law, medicine, dentistry, veterinary medicine, engineering, science, mathematics, and other fields.

HBGI funds can support graduate scholarships and fellowships, facilities, endowments, academic resources, student services, administration, and development.

The HBGI program has separate statutory institutions, allocation rules, minimums, and match requirements.

Relationship to HBCU Capital Financing

The HBCU Capital Financing Program provides federally guaranteed bond financing for construction, renovation, equipment, refinancing, and other capital projects.

Capital Financing produces repayable debt, often in amounts far larger than a Title III grant.

Part B facilities activity is grant-funded but subject to annual limits and federal grant restrictions.

Institutions may coordinate the programs but cannot charge the same cost to both sources.

Relationship to Pell Grants

Pell Grants provide need-based aid to eligible students.

Title III Part B provides institutional support to the college or university.

Pell recipient counts influence 50 percent of the HBCU formula, but the Part B grant does not become individual Pell aid.

A student can benefit indirectly through stronger instruction, facilities, advising, technology, and services while also receiving a Pell Grant separately.

Relationship to Predominantly Black Institutions

Predominantly Black Institutions are defined mainly through current enrollment, student-need, institutional-type, and financial criteria.

An HBCU is defined by historic establishment and mission.

A PBI need not have been founded before 1964 and may have developed its Black student enrollment more recently.

The Department ended fiscal year 2025 discretionary funding for the current-enrollment-based PBI program while continuing HBCU support.

Relationship to Pandemic Emergency Relief

CARES Act and later emergency laws provided separate Higher Education Emergency Relief Fund allocations to HBCUs and other colleges.

Emergency funds addressed pandemic disruption, student aid, technology, lost revenue, and institutional costs under special temporary authority.

Title III Part B remained a continuing institutional-development program and should not be conflated with HEERF.

Emergency laws temporarily protected formula calculations and permitted certain flexible uses of unexpended Title III funds.

Public HBCUs

Public HBCUs operate within state systems and depend substantially on state appropriations, tuition, federal grants, and public capital.

Historic state underfunding, program duplication, desegregation litigation, deferred maintenance, and system governance influence Title III priorities.

Federal grants supplement but do not replace state obligations to fund institutions lawfully and equitably.

Part 608 prohibits activities inconsistent with an applicable state higher-education desegregation plan.

Private HBCUs

Private HBCUs rely on tuition, philanthropy, churches, alumni, federal aid, grants, investment income, and auxiliary revenue.

Smaller enrollments and endowments can make private institutions especially sensitive to demographic change, accreditation costs, debt, deferred maintenance, and enrollment volatility.

Title III development offices, academic programs, fiscal systems, facilities, and endowment matching can support long-term sustainability.

Two-Year HBCUs

Eligible junior and community colleges form an important part of the HBCU sector.

They provide technical education, associate degrees, transfer pathways, workforce training, and access for students who may not begin at four-year institutions.

Congress has periodically reserved supplemental Part B funding for eligible two-year HBCUs.

In fiscal year 2024, the Department reported thirteen supplemental Part B awards totalling $3 million.

Effects on Students

Students benefit through improved courses, laboratories, libraries, tutoring, advising, financial systems, technology, teacher preparation, and campus facilities.

Institutional strengthening can improve retention, graduation, licensure, graduate-school preparation, and employment.

The effect is indirect and varies by project; receiving Title III funds does not guarantee a particular student service or financial award.

Students can participate in planning and evaluation through surveys, committees, service use, and institutional governance.

Effects on Faculty and Staff

Faculty can receive advanced-degree support, professional development, curriculum resources, research equipment, exchanges, and technology training.

Administrative staff can receive systems, training, policies, and capacity for finance, assessment, grants, information technology, advancement, and student services.

Grant-funded positions can create instability if the institution lacks a plan to continue essential work after the grant.

Institutional Capacity and Self-Sufficiency

The program’s long-term objective is stronger capacity rather than permanent federal substitution for institutional management.

Capacity includes sound finances, qualified faculty, effective governance, modern systems, maintained facilities, successful students, diversified revenue, and credible accreditation.

Self-sufficiency does not mean that HBCUs should receive no public support. It means the institution can sustain the improvements and manage resources effectively after a particular grant project ends.

Program Achievements

  • Created a dedicated federal institutional-aid program for HBCUs.
  • Reduced competition between HBCUs and unrelated developing institutions for core Title III funds.
  • Provided recurring resources for academic, administrative, fiscal, and facility improvement.
  • Linked funding with service to Pell Grant recipients.
  • Rewarded graduation and progression into underrepresented graduate and professional fields.
  • Supported laboratories, libraries, technology, and instructional facilities.
  • Strengthened tutoring, counselling, advising, and retention systems.
  • Supported faculty credentials and professional development.
  • Improved teacher-education programs and school outreach.
  • Built fundraising and alumni-development capacity.
  • Allowed matched federal support for endowment growth.
  • Provided a five-year planning horizon.
  • Made annual mandatory HBCU funding permanent through the FUTURE Act.
  • Supported public, private, four-year, and two-year HBCUs.
  • Created a continuing federal record of HBCU institutional needs and performance.

Limitations and Failures

  • Annual discretionary funding remains subject to appropriations.
  • Formula awards do not reflect every institutional need.
  • The Pell factor can favour larger institutions.
  • Graduate counts can favour institutions with larger graduating classes.
  • The graduate-school factor requires difficult longitudinal data.
  • Small institutions can receive limited formula shares despite high fixed operating costs.
  • Title III grants cannot replace adequate state funding.
  • The 50-percent facilities ceiling limits response to severe deferred maintenance.
  • Endowment use requires an immediate nonfederal match that financially weak institutions may struggle to raise.
  • Federal grant administration and reporting can burden small staffs.
  • Five-year projects can become fragmented across many activities.
  • Grant-funded positions and subscriptions may be difficult to sustain.
  • Performance measures may emphasise outputs rather than long-term institutional health.
  • Old regulatory lists and outdated guidance can create confusion.
  • Formula and regulatory text do not always reflect later statutory amendments consistently.
  • Federal funding cannot eliminate historic capital, wealth, and state-appropriation disparities by itself.

Major Controversies

Historical Status and Equal Protection

Supporters view the program as institutional remediation tied to the history and mission of HBCUs. Critics question the use of institution-specific classifications in federal aid.

Formula Equity

The 50–25–25 formula rewards need and outcomes but may not account sufficiently for fixed costs, deferred maintenance, state underfunding, or institutional scale.

State Underfunding

Federal Title III grants can be used politically to obscure state obligations to fund public HBCUs equitably.

Construction versus Academic Uses

Institutions with severe facility needs may prefer more construction flexibility, while federal policy reserves funds for academic and administrative development.

Endowment Match

Matching requirements leverage private support but can advantage institutions already able to raise money.

Institutional Sustainability

Some institutions remain financially vulnerable despite decades of Title III assistance, raising questions about funding levels, management, enrollment, state policy, and structural market pressures.

Documented Outcomes

Title III has financed academic programs, facilities, information systems, faculty development, student services, fiscal management, fundraising, and endowments at nearly every eligible HBCU.

HBCUs continue to make substantial contributions to Black degree attainment and preparation for graduate, professional, scientific, educational, and public-service careers.

Attribution is difficult because institutions combine Title III with tuition, state appropriations, Pell Grants, philanthropy, research grants, capital financing, and other federal programs.

A completed renovation or new information system is an output; stronger completion, fiscal stability, accreditation, and long-term student opportunity are outcomes.

Evaluation should examine whether federally supported practices remain in operation after the grant and whether the institution becomes more academically and financially resilient.

Historical Significance

The program represents federal recognition that Black higher education developed under unequal legal, fiscal, and social conditions.

It moved HBCU institutional aid from a general developing-institutions competition into a dedicated statutory framework.

The formula connects educational access, completion, and advanced study with institutional capacity.

Permanent Part F funding reduced recurring uncertainty over one major portion of HBCU support.

The program remains one of the central federal mechanisms for sustaining HBCUs as educational, cultural, scientific, professional, and community institutions.

Research Cautions

  • Part B and Part F are separate funding streams. Do not combine them without identifying each legal authority.
  • Discretionary does not mean conventionally competitive. Part B awards are formula based among approved eligible institutions.
  • HBCU status is historical. It does not depend on a current Black-enrollment percentage.
  • Eligibility is not an award. The institution must apply, submit data, remain accredited, and satisfy grant conditions.
  • Pell Grants and Title III serve different recipients. Pell goes to students; Title III strengthens institutions.
  • HBGI is a separate program. Graduate and professional institutions follow section 326.
  • Capital Financing is debt. It should not be described as a Title III Part B grant.
  • The current statute contains a two-tier minimum. The older eCFR language stating a universal $500,000 floor does not fully reflect the later amendment.
  • Fiscal-year total HBCU support is broader than Part B. Department-wide totals can include many grant, aid, and institutional programs.
  • Appropriation, obligation, and expenditure differ. A grant award may be spent across several years.
  • Old regulatory institution names may be obsolete. Use current accreditation and NCES records.
  • Program success cannot be inferred from award size alone. Institutionalisation and long-term outcomes require separate evidence.

Summary

The HBCU Title III Strengthening Program is the principal recurring federal institutional-development grant for historically Black colleges and universities.

The modern program was established as a dedicated Title III Part B structure in 1986, building on institutional assistance created by the Higher Education Act of 1965.

An eligible institution must have been established before 1964, maintain the principal historic mission of educating Black Americans, possess state legal authority, and remain accredited or preaccredited.

HBCU eligibility is based on historical status and mission, not a required current percentage of Black students.

Institutions submit a comprehensive development plan and formula data concerning Pell Grant recipients, graduates, and graduates attending advanced programs in underrepresented fields.

Fifty percent of formula funding is based on Pell recipients, 25 percent on graduates, and 25 percent on graduate or professional school participation in disciplines where Black Americans are underrepresented.

Grants can support academic programs, scientific equipment, laboratories, libraries, faculty development, student services, financial management, information technology, fundraising, teacher education, community outreach, financial literacy, instructional facilities, and approved endowment growth.

Construction and maintenance of instructional facilities are generally limited to 50 percent of an annual award.

Endowment use is limited to 20 percent and requires an immediate dollar-for-dollar nonfederal match.

Grants last up to five academic years and require annual performance reports, financial and compliance audits, complete records, supplement-not-supplant compliance, and continued accreditation.

Annual discretionary Part B funding operates alongside permanent mandatory Part F funding made permanent by the FUTURE Act of 2019.

In fiscal year 2024, the Department reported $400.966 million in Part B funding and $80.155 million through the parallel Part F stream.

As of August 2, 2026, the application status was closed, the most recent full cycle was fiscal year 2022, and eligible institutions remained within a five-year formula-grant period subject to annual appropriations, data, accreditation, and compliance.

The program has strengthened HBCU academics, administration, facilities, student support, fundraising, and endowments, but federal grants alone cannot eliminate historic state underfunding, small endowments, deferred maintenance, enrollment pressures, and long-term capital disparities.
