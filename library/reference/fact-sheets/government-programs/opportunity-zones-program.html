Opportunity Zones Program

The federal place-based tax incentive that permits investors to obtain preferential capital-gains treatment by investing eligible gains through Qualified Opportunity Funds that finance businesses and property in designated low-income census tracts.

Overview

The Opportunity Zones Program is the common name for a federal capital-gains tax incentive created by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. The law added Sections 1400Z-1 and 1400Z-2 to the Internal Revenue Code. It authorizes states and United States territories to nominate qualifying low-income census tracts for designation as Qualified Opportunity Zones and allows taxpayers to invest eligible gains in those areas through Qualified Opportunity Funds.

Opportunity Zones are not a conventional federal grant program. The federal government does not select individual development projects, allocate a fixed amount of credit authority, or require investors to compete for awards. Instead, qualifying taxpayers elect tax treatment on eligible investments, Qualified Opportunity Funds self-certify with the Internal Revenue Service, and investment can flow to any property or business satisfying the statutory and regulatory rules.

The original system operated through 8,764 Qualified Opportunity Zones designated in 2018 and 2019 across all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and five United States territories. State and territorial chief executive officers nominated most tracts, and the Treasury Department certified the nominations. Congress separately deemed all qualifying low-income census tracts in Puerto Rico designated under the original rules.

Under the original tax regime, an investor could defer an eligible capital gain or qualified Section 1231 gain by investing a corresponding amount in a Qualified Opportunity Fund within the applicable 180-day period. The deferred gain generally becomes taxable upon an earlier inclusion event or on December 31, 2026. Historical five- and seven-year holding periods could reduce the amount of deferred gain recognized by 10 or 15 percent.

The most significant original incentive is the treatment of post-investment appreciation. When an investor holds a qualifying Qualified Opportunity Fund interest for at least ten years and makes the required election, the investor can generally increase the investment’s basis to fair market value at sale or exchange, excluding qualifying appreciation from federal income.

A Qualified Opportunity Fund must be organized as a corporation or partnership for the purpose of investing in Qualified Opportunity Zone property and generally must hold at least 90 percent of its assets in qualifying property. The fund can invest directly in tangible business property or indirectly through qualifying stock or partnership interests in a Qualified Opportunity Zone business.

Opportunity Zone investments have financed apartments, mixed-use developments, hotels, offices, industrial buildings, warehouses, renewable-energy projects, operating businesses, healthcare facilities, community facilities, and other enterprises. Early federal tax data showed that real estate received the largest share of reported investment.

The program attracted bipartisan support as a method of directing private capital into distressed communities without annual appropriations. It also generated substantial criticism concerning weak original reporting requirements, investor-centered benefits, uneven geographic distribution, luxury or market-rate development, displacement, gentrification, limited resident participation, and uncertainty about whether subsidized investment would have occurred without the tax incentive.

Public Law 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025, made the Opportunity Zones incentive permanent and substantially revised it. Beginning in 2027, new census-tract designations will take effect for ten-year periods, with new designation rounds every ten years. The law tightened the definition of an eligible low-income community, eliminated the rule permitting certain merely contiguous tracts, ended Puerto Rico’s automatic blanket designation, created enhanced rural incentives, and established extensive new reporting requirements.

The permanent post-2026 investment regime replaces the fixed December 31, 2026 inclusion date with a rolling five-year deferral period. A five-year investment receives a basis increase equal to 10 percent of the deferred gain, or 30 percent when invested in a Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund. Qualifying appreciation can remain excluded after ten years, but the permanent regime limits the fair-market-value adjustment to appreciation through the investment’s thirtieth anniversary.

As of August 2, 2026, the program is in transition. Governors and other eligible state or territorial chief executives began nominating census tracts on July 1, 2026. The ordinary nomination period lasts ninety days, subject to one thirty-day extension. New designations will become effective January 1, 2027 and continue through December 31, 2036.

The original and new designation systems will temporarily overlap. Previously designated Puerto Rico zones remain designated through December 31, 2027, while other original zones remain designated through December 31, 2028. Transitional guidance preserves specified treatment for existing investments, planned development, ordinary business-property replacement, and continuing businesses after those dates.

Definitions

Opportunity Zone
The common term for a Qualified Opportunity Zone: a population census tract certified and designated under Internal Revenue Code Section 1400Z-1.
Qualified Opportunity Zone
A qualifying low-income census tract formally designated for Opportunity Zone tax treatment. The abbreviation QOZ is commonly used.
Qualified Opportunity Fund
A domestic corporation or partnership organized to invest in Qualified Opportunity Zone property and maintaining at least 90 percent of its assets in qualifying property. The abbreviation QOF is commonly used.
Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund
A post-2026 Qualified Opportunity Fund that holds at least 90 percent of its assets in qualifying property used in Qualified Opportunity Zones comprised entirely of rural areas. The abbreviation QROF is commonly used.
Eligible gain
A capital gain or qualifying Section 1231 gain eligible for deferral when timely invested in a Qualified Opportunity Fund and not arising from a transaction with a related person.
Qualifying investment
An equity interest in a Qualified Opportunity Fund received in exchange for an eligible investment and satisfying the requirements for Opportunity Zone tax treatment.
Nonqualifying investment
A fund interest not eligible for Opportunity Zone tax benefits, such as a debt interest or the portion of an investment exceeding the eligible deferred gain under applicable rules.
Inclusion event
A sale, exchange, transfer, distribution, gift, liquidation, or other transaction that reduces or terminates an investor’s qualifying interest and causes some or all deferred gain to become taxable under federal rules.
Deferred gain
Eligible gain whose federal income recognition is postponed because the taxpayer timely invested a corresponding amount in a Qualified Opportunity Fund and made the required election.
Basis
The tax measure used to determine gain or loss. A qualifying original-regime Opportunity Fund investment generally begins with zero basis and receives adjustments under statutory rules.
Basis step-up
An increase in tax basis that reduces gain recognized or eliminates qualifying appreciation. The program uses basis adjustments for five-year holdings and for investments held at least ten years.
Qualified Opportunity Zone property
Qualified Opportunity Zone stock, a Qualified Opportunity Zone partnership interest, or Qualified Opportunity Zone business property. The abbreviation QOZP is commonly used.
Qualified Opportunity Zone stock
Qualifying stock acquired at original issue from a domestic corporation that operates or becomes a Qualified Opportunity Zone business and satisfies statutory holding-period requirements.
Qualified Opportunity Zone partnership interest
A qualifying capital or profits interest acquired directly from a domestic partnership operating or becoming a Qualified Opportunity Zone business.
Qualified Opportunity Zone business property
Qualifying tangible property purchased after the applicable statutory date, whose original use begins in the zone or that is substantially improved, and whose use remains substantially within the zone. The abbreviation QOZBP is commonly used.
Qualified Opportunity Zone business
A trade or business satisfying tangible-property, gross-income, intangible-property, financial-property, and prohibited-business rules. The abbreviation QOZB is commonly used.
Original use
The commencement of depreciation or first qualifying use of property within a Qualified Opportunity Zone by the fund or business, subject to detailed regulatory rules for vacant property, leased property, used property, and improvements.
Substantial improvement
The required increase in adjusted basis of acquired used property during a thirty-month period. The ordinary threshold is generally 100 percent of the building or property basis being tested; qualifying entirely rural property uses a 50-percent threshold after July 4, 2025.
Thirty-month period
The period during which a fund or business must make the required additions to basis to satisfy the substantial-improvement test, subject to authorized extensions and aggregation rules.
Ninety-percent investment standard
The requirement that a Qualified Opportunity Fund hold at least 90 percent of its assets in Qualified Opportunity Zone property, measured on two annual testing dates.
Seventy-percent tangible-property standard
The regulatory threshold under which at least 70 percent of tangible property owned or leased by a Qualified Opportunity Zone business must satisfy the Qualified Opportunity Zone business-property rules.
Fifty-percent gross-income test
The requirement that at least 50 percent of a Qualified Opportunity Zone business’s gross income derive from the active conduct of a trade or business within a Qualified Opportunity Zone, using one or more regulatory tests.
Working-capital safe harbor
A regulatory rule permitting a Qualified Opportunity Zone business to hold cash, cash equivalents, or short-term debt under a written development plan, schedule, and expenditure framework, generally for up to thirty-one months.
Qualified Opportunity Zone business-property basis
The tax basis used in determining whether property meets acquisition, original-use, substantial-improvement, and asset-testing requirements.
Low-income community
A census tract satisfying statutory poverty or median-family-income thresholds. The original system generally used the New Markets Tax Credit definition; the permanent system adopts stricter criteria for post-2025 designations.
Contiguous tract
Under the original system, a limited number of non-low-income tracts adjacent to designated low-income tracts could qualify when their median family income did not exceed the statutory limit. Public Law 119-21 repealed this eligibility path for new designations.
Decennial determination date
July 1, 2026 and each July 1 occurring ten years after the preceding determination date, when the process for a new designation round begins.
Applicable start date
For a zone designated under the permanent system, the January 1 following federal certification and designation.
Designation period
The ten-year period beginning on a zone’s applicable start date and ending the day before the tenth anniversary of that date.
Rural area
For the enhanced Opportunity Zone rules, an area other than a city or town with more than 50,000 inhabitants and other than an urbanized area contiguous and adjacent to such a city or town.
Self-certification
The process by which an eligible corporation or partnership identifies itself as a Qualified Opportunity Fund by filing Form 8996 rather than applying for advance IRS approval.
Form 8996
The IRS form used by an entity to certify and report its status as a Qualified Opportunity Fund and calculate compliance with the 90-percent investment standard.
Form 8997
The investor’s annual statement reporting Qualified Opportunity Fund investments, deferred gains, acquisitions, dispositions, and year-end holdings.
Form 8949
The federal tax form used to report sales and exchanges of capital assets and make or report Opportunity Zone gain-deferral elections.

Historical Development

Place-Based Tax Policy

Opportunity Zones developed from a long history of federal and state policies directing tax benefits, grants, credit, or regulatory preferences toward distressed places. Empowerment Zones, Renewal Communities, Enterprise Communities, New Markets Tax Credits, and disaster-recovery zones provided important precedents.

Untapped Capital-Gain Theory

The Opportunity Zones proposal focused on unrealized capital gains held by investors. Its central theory was that taxpayers would redirect capital into distressed communities when offered deferral and potential exclusion of future appreciation.

Bipartisan Legislative Development

The Investing in Opportunity Act attracted bipartisan sponsorship. Senators Tim Scott and Cory Booker and Representatives Pat Tiberi and Ron Kind played central roles in advancing the proposal before its inclusion in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act.

Tax Cuts and Jobs Act

Congress enacted the final incentive as part of the 2017 tax legislation. The enacted version established broad investor and fund rules but omitted several reporting provisions contained in earlier proposals.

Rapid Designation

States had a short period in 2018 to identify and nominate census tracts. Governors used different procedures, including public requests, local-government submissions, economic analysis, agency recommendations, investor interest, and political judgment.

Regulatory Development

Because the statute left many operational questions unresolved, Treasury and IRS issued two proposed-regulation packages, final regulations, revenue rulings, notices, frequently asked questions, forms, and pandemic relief.

Permanent Renewal

Public Law 119-21 transformed the originally time-limited incentive into a recurring permanent system. The law addressed several longstanding criticisms by tightening tract eligibility and requiring federal reporting, while preserving the investor-centered tax structure.

Original 2018 Designation Process

Eligible Low-Income Communities

The original system generally used the New Markets Tax Credit definition of a low-income community. A census tract could qualify through a poverty-rate test or a median-family-income test, with special rules for high-migration rural areas, low-population tracts, targeted populations, and territories.

Contiguous Tracts

A limited number of tracts not independently qualifying as low-income communities could be nominated when they were contiguous with a nominated low-income tract and their median family income did not exceed 125 percent of the neighboring tract’s income.

State Nomination Limit

States generally could nominate up to 25 percent of their low-income communities. Jurisdictions with fewer than 100 qualifying tracts could nominate up to twenty-five tracts. Contiguous tracts could constitute no more than 5 percent of the state’s designated zones.

Governor Selection

Governors and other eligible chief executives selected tracts. Federal law did not require a uniform public-participation, scoring, anti-displacement, or project-selection process.

Treasury Certification

Treasury reviewed whether nominated tracts satisfied statutory requirements and certified qualifying nominations. Certification did not establish that every future project within the tract served residents or produced a public benefit.

Puerto Rico

Congress deemed all qualifying low-income communities in Puerto Rico designated, resulting in substantially broader territorial coverage than in states.

Final Count

The original process produced 8,764 designated tracts. Approximately one-tenth of the United States population lived within the designated geography.

Boundary Stability

Original zone boundaries follow the census-tract boundaries used at designation and were not altered by subsequent Census Bureau tract revisions.

Permanent Ten-Year Designation System

Recurring Rounds

Public Law 119-21 establishes a new designation process every ten years. The first decennial determination date is July 1, 2026.

Stricter Low-Income Definition

A tract qualifies for the 2027 round when it meets one of two principal standards:

  • Median family income does not exceed 70 percent of the applicable statewide or metropolitan median; or
  • The poverty rate is at least 20 percent and median family income does not exceed 125 percent of the applicable statewide or metropolitan median.

Repeal of Contiguous-Tract Eligibility

New zones must independently qualify as low-income communities. A higher-income tract cannot be designated merely because it borders a qualifying tract.

Puerto Rico

The original blanket designation rule ends after 2026. Puerto Rico’s governor participates in the same nomination framework applied to states and other territories for new designations.

Twenty-Five-Percent Limit

A state generally may nominate no more than 25 percent of its eligible low-income tracts for each ten-year designation period. If a jurisdiction contains fewer than 100 eligible tracts, it can nominate up to twenty-five or all eligible tracts when fewer than twenty-five exist.

Eligible 2027 Tracts

Revenue Procedure 2026-14 identifies 25,332 tracts eligible for nomination, including 8,334 tracts entirely within rural areas.

Nomination Window

The 2026 nomination period began July 1 and lasts ninety days. An eligible chief executive may request one thirty-day extension.

Effective Period

Tracts certified in 2026 become designated on January 1, 2027. Their ten-year period ends December 31, 2036.

Temporary Overlap

New zones can overlap geographically with original zones, but original Puerto Rico designations remain effective through 2027 and other original designations through 2028. The overlap allows transition but creates complex acquisition and qualification rules.

Original Investment Tax Benefits

Temporary Deferral

A taxpayer can defer eligible gain by investing a corresponding amount in a Qualified Opportunity Fund within the applicable 180-day period. The deferred amount is recognized upon the earlier of an inclusion event or December 31, 2026.

Five-Year Basis Increase

An original-regime investment held for at least five years before the mandatory inclusion date receives a basis increase equal to 10 percent of deferred gain. This reduces the amount recognized.

Seven-Year Basis Increase

An investment held for at least seven years receives an additional 5-percent basis increase, producing a total exclusion of 15 percent of deferred gain. Because mandatory inclusion occurs in 2026, the calendar windows for new investors to earn the historical five- and seven-year reductions have closed.

Ten-Year Appreciation Exclusion

An investor holding a qualifying interest for at least ten years can elect to increase basis to fair market value when the interest is sold or exchanged, generally eliminating federal income tax on qualifying post-investment appreciation.

Asset-Sale Election

Final regulations permit qualifying investors in certain partnerships and S corporations to exclude their share of eligible gain from a Qualified Opportunity Fund’s sale of qualifying assets after the investor’s ten-year holding period.

Outer Election Date

The original regulations generally permit the ten-year fair-market-value election for qualifying dispositions through December 31, 2047.

Investor Residence

An investor does not need to live, work, or operate an existing business in a Qualified Opportunity Zone. The investor benefit depends on the investment and fund, not personal residence.

Equity Requirement

The investor must receive an equity interest in the Qualified Opportunity Fund. A loan to the fund does not itself constitute a qualifying investment.

Permanent Post-2026 Tax Benefits

Rolling Five-Year Deferral

For amounts invested after December 31, 2026, deferred gain generally becomes taxable upon the earlier of sale or exchange of the investment or five years after the investment date. The permanent system therefore replaces a single national inclusion date with an investment-specific five-year period.

Ordinary Fund Basis Increase

At the five-year inclusion date, an investment in an ordinary Qualified Opportunity Fund receives a basis increase equal to 10 percent of deferred gain.

Rural Fund Basis Increase

An investment in a Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund receives a basis increase equal to 30 percent of deferred gain after five years. This produces a substantially larger exclusion of the initially deferred gain.

Ten-Year Appreciation Treatment

After a minimum ten-year holding period, an investor can elect fair-market-value basis treatment for qualifying appreciation.

Thirty-Year Limit

When an investment is sold before its thirtieth anniversary, basis can equal fair market value on the disposition date. If it is held longer, the statutory fair-market-value adjustment is limited to the investment’s value on the thirtieth anniversary, causing later appreciation to remain potentially taxable.

Continuing 180-Day Rule

Eligible gain generally must still be invested within the applicable 180-day period, subject to separate timing rules for partnerships, estates, trusts, regulated investment companies, and other pass-through entities.

Permanent Availability

Future investors can enter during recurring designation periods rather than facing the original 2026 sunset. Property-acquisition and business rules refer to each zone’s applicable start date.

Qualified Opportunity Funds

Entity Form

A Qualified Opportunity Fund must be an entity classified as a domestic corporation or partnership for federal tax purposes. A limited-liability company can qualify when taxed as a corporation or partnership.

Purpose

The organizing documents and operations must support investment in Qualified Opportunity Zone property. A fund cannot satisfy its purpose by investing in another Qualified Opportunity Fund.

Self-Certification

The fund elects Qualified Opportunity Fund status on Form 8996 with its timely filed federal tax return. IRS does not ordinarily issue advance project approval.

Ninety-Percent Test

The fund measures qualifying assets twice each taxable year, generally at the end of the first six-month period and at year-end. The average qualifying percentage must be at least 90 percent.

Valuation

Funds generally use applicable financial-statement values or cost basis under regulatory rules. Consistent valuation is necessary to calculate the asset percentage and any penalty.

Penalty

A fund failing the 90-percent standard can owe a monthly penalty based on the shortfall unless reasonable cause applies.

Direct and Indirect Investment

A fund can own Qualified Opportunity Zone business property directly. More commonly, it invests through a subsidiary partnership or corporation operating as a Qualified Opportunity Zone business.

Fund Governance

Federal tax law does not prescribe one investor-protection, community-benefit, fee, disclosure, or governance model. Private offering documents establish management authority, fees, distributions, capital calls, risk allocation, exit rights, and investor remedies.

Securities Law

Qualified Opportunity Fund interests can constitute securities and remain subject to federal and state securities law, antifraud rules, offering exemptions, investment-adviser regulation, and private contractual obligations.

Qualified Property and Businesses

Three Forms of Property

A Qualified Opportunity Fund can satisfy its asset test through:

  • Qualified Opportunity Zone stock
  • Qualified Opportunity Zone partnership interests
  • Qualified Opportunity Zone business property held directly

Qualified Opportunity Zone Business

A subsidiary business must satisfy the 70-percent tangible-property threshold, derive at least 50 percent of gross income from active business within the zone, use a substantial portion of its intangible property in the active business, and hold less than 5 percent of property in nonqualified financial assets outside permitted working capital.

Prohibited Businesses

Specified businesses cannot qualify, including golf courses, country clubs, massage parlors, hot-tub facilities, suntan facilities, racetracks, gambling facilities, and stores whose principal business is selling alcoholic beverages for off-premises consumption.

Purchase Requirement

Qualifying property generally must be purchased from an unrelated person after the applicable statutory date. Related-party thresholds restrict transactions designed merely to transfer existing assets into a fund structure.

Leased Property

Leased tangible property can qualify without the same original-use or substantial-improvement requirement applied to purchased property, but the lease must satisfy market-rate and anti-abuse standards.

Inventory

Inventory can receive special treatment when used in a zone business, including rules for property moving into and out of the zone in ordinary operations.

Working Capital

Qualified businesses can hold development cash under a written plan identifying intended use, a reasonable schedule, and expenditure consistent with that schedule. Multiple overlapping safe-harbor periods can apply to separate capital infusions when requirements are satisfied.

Real-Estate Investment

New Construction

New buildings and improvements can qualify when developed on property meeting acquisition and zone-use requirements. Land does not need to satisfy a separate substantial-improvement calculation when used in an active qualifying trade or business.

Existing Buildings

An acquired existing building generally must be substantially improved unless its original use within the zone begins with the fund or business or another regulatory exception applies.

Ordinary Substantial-Improvement Test

The owner generally must make additions to basis during thirty months exceeding the adjusted basis of the property being tested. For a building-and-land purchase, the improvement calculation ordinarily focuses on the building basis rather than land basis.

Rural Substantial-Improvement Test

For property in a zone comprised entirely of a rural area, the required addition to basis was reduced from 100 percent to 50 percent beginning July 4, 2025.

Vacant Property

Property vacant for the regulatory minimum period can qualify for original-use treatment. Shorter vacancy periods can apply in zones designated as disaster areas or where the property was vacant before designation.

Brownfields

Contaminated or previously developed land can qualify when the fund or business makes investments sufficient to improve safety and compliance under applicable law.

Triple-Net Leasing

Merely holding and leasing property under a passive triple-net arrangement can fail the active-trade-or-business requirement. Operational responsibility and services matter.

Housing

Market-rate, mixed-income, workforce, student, senior, affordable, and supportive housing can qualify when the investment structure satisfies tax rules. Opportunity Zone law itself generally does not impose income or rent restrictions.

Operating Businesses

Active Conduct

A Qualified Opportunity Zone business must actively conduct a trade or business. Passive investment holdings generally do not satisfy the operating-business rules.

Gross-Income Tests

Regulations provide several methods for satisfying the 50-percent gross-income requirement, including tests based on employee or contractor service hours, amounts paid for services, and facts demonstrating that management and operational functions occur within the zone.

Tangible Property

At least 70 percent of owned or leased tangible property generally must satisfy the Qualified Opportunity Zone business-property rules.

Intangible Property

A substantial portion of patents, licenses, software, brands, processes, and other intangible property must be used in the active conduct of the business within the zone.

Cash and Financial Assets

Less than 5 percent of the average unadjusted basis of property can consist of nonqualified financial property, excluding reasonable working capital and specified debt instruments.

Start-Up Businesses

Working-capital safe harbors allow a new business time to develop facilities, hire employees, acquire equipment, obtain permits, and begin operations.

Challenges

Operating businesses often require risk capital, management support, customer markets, workforce, intellectual property, and flexible exits. These needs can be more difficult to align with ten-year investor horizons than real-estate development.

How an Investor Participates

Recognize Eligible Gain

The taxpayer realizes capital gain or qualifying Section 1231 gain from a sale or exchange with an unrelated person.

Determine the Investment Period

The taxpayer identifies the applicable 180-day period. Pass-through gains can produce alternative starting dates for partners, shareholders, beneficiaries, estates, or trusts.

Select or Form a Fund

The taxpayer can invest in a third-party Qualified Opportunity Fund or establish an eligible fund. Investors should evaluate tax, securities, financial, legal, community, environmental, and development risks.

Receive Equity

The taxpayer contributes cash or qualifying property in exchange for a partnership interest or corporate stock. Only the eligible portion receives Opportunity Zone treatment.

Make the Election

The taxpayer reports the deferral election on Form 8949 and related federal tax-return schedules.

File Annual Form 8997

The taxpayer reports beginning and ending holdings, acquisitions, dispositions, and deferred gains each year while holding a qualifying investment.

Recognize Deferred Gain

Under the original system, remaining deferred gain is generally included for the taxable year containing December 31, 2026. Under the permanent system, inclusion generally occurs five years after investment or earlier disposition.

Hold for Ten Years

The investor must retain the qualifying interest for at least ten years to seek exclusion of qualifying appreciation.

Exit and Elect

At disposition, the investor reports the transaction and makes the applicable fair-market-value basis election when eligible.

Representative Investment Example

Original Regime

Assume an investor realized a $1,000,000 eligible capital gain in 2021 and timely invested the entire amount in a Qualified Opportunity Fund. The investment satisfied all program requirements and remained held through December 31, 2026.

Because the investor held the interest for at least five years, the original basis increase could reduce the deferred gain recognized in 2026 by 10 percent. The investor would generally recognize $900,000 of deferred gain, subject to tax rates, other adjustments, and individual circumstances.

If the fund interest later increased to $2,500,000 and the investor sold it after the ten-year holding period while making the required election, the $1,500,000 of qualifying post-investment appreciation could generally be excluded from federal income.

Permanent Regime

Assume an investor realizes and invests $1,000,000 of eligible gain in an ordinary Qualified Opportunity Fund on July 1, 2027. The gain is generally deferred until the earlier of an inclusion event or July 1, 2032.

At five years, the 10-percent basis increase generally reduces the includible deferred gain to $900,000. A comparable investment in a Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund could receive a 30-percent increase and reduce the includible deferred gain to $700,000.

If the investment remains held for at least ten years, qualifying appreciation can receive fair-market-value basis treatment. Appreciation after the thirtieth anniversary is not covered by the permanent regime’s full fair-market-value adjustment.

These examples are simplified. Actual results depend on gain type, timing, basis, inclusion events, fund compliance, partnership allocations, debt, distributions, state taxation, transaction costs, and future law.

Rural Opportunity Zones

Rural Definition

A rural area excludes cities and towns with populations above 50,000 and urbanized areas contiguous and adjacent to such cities or towns.

Existing Rural Zones

IRS Notice 2025-50 identified 3,309 original Qualified Opportunity Zones comprised entirely of rural areas.

Eligible 2027 Rural Tracts

Revenue Procedure 2026-14 identifies 8,334 entirely rural low-income tracts eligible for nomination in the 2027 round.

Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund

A QROF must hold at least 90 percent of assets in qualifying property used within zones comprised entirely of rural areas.

Thirty-Percent Basis Increase

Post-2026 investments held five years in a QROF receive a basis increase equal to 30 percent of deferred gain rather than the ordinary 10 percent.

Reduced Improvement Threshold

The substantial-improvement requirement for qualifying rural property is 50 percent of adjusted basis rather than 100 percent. This change took effect July 4, 2025 and can apply within existing entirely rural zones.

Rural Development Challenges

Rural projects can face small markets, limited workforce, infrastructure deficits, lower property values, scarce development expertise, weak broadband, distance from services, and limited conventional financing.

Potential Uses

Rural Opportunity Zone investment can support housing, manufacturing, food systems, agriculture-related processing, healthcare, energy, tourism, broadband, community facilities, Main Street redevelopment, and small-business expansion.

Reporting, Oversight, and Compliance

Original Reporting Gap

The original statute required fund and investor tax forms but did not establish a comprehensive public performance-reporting system. Treasury, GAO, states, and researchers therefore lacked timely information about project locations, industry, employment, housing, community outcomes, and investor characteristics.

Form 8996

Funds annually report certification, asset values, qualifying property, census-tract information, subsidiary businesses, and calculation of the 90-percent standard.

Form 8997

Investors annually report Qualified Opportunity Fund holdings and deferred gains.

New Fund Reporting

Public Law 119-21 requires annual electronic returns containing fund identity, total assets, qualifying assets, industry codes, census tracts, residential units, employment indicators, investment amounts, property types, and investor dispositions.

Business Reporting

Qualified Opportunity Zone businesses must furnish information needed by funds to complete the new federal returns.

Penalties

The permanent law establishes daily penalties for incomplete or incorrect fund reporting, with higher maximums for funds exceeding $10 million in gross assets and substantially larger penalties for intentional disregard.

Annual Public Reports

Treasury must publish annual information concerning the number of funds, assets, industries, geographic distribution, employment, real-estate share, residential units, and investments by census tract.

Impact Reports

Beginning with specified later reports, Treasury must assess outcomes including job creation, poverty reduction, business starts, unemployment, income, rent burden, housing, homeownership, property values, demographics, and comparisons with similar undesignated tracts.

IRS Compliance

IRS examines investor elections, fund certification, asset testing, property qualification, related-party transactions, working capital, substantial improvement, prohibited businesses, inclusion events, deferred-gain recognition, and long-term basis elections.

State Oversight

States select zones but do not generally approve individual federal tax investments. Some states created certification, reporting, community-benefit, or complementary tax-credit systems, while others did not.

Housing and Community Development

Housing Production

Opportunity Funds can finance construction or rehabilitation of rental housing, for-sale housing, mixed-use buildings, senior housing, student housing, workforce housing, and other residential property.

No Federal Affordability Requirement

The Opportunity Zone statute generally does not require residential units to be affordable to low-income households. Affordability arises only when another program, covenant, subsidy, zoning rule, public agreement, or voluntary commitment imposes it.

Layering with Housing Programs

Opportunity Zone equity can be combined with Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, New Markets Tax Credits in eligible structures, tax-exempt bonds, HOME funds, Community Development Block Grants, Project-Based Vouchers, state housing credits, local subsidies, and private debt.

Commercial Development

Funds can finance retail, offices, hotels, restaurants, industrial property, warehouses, entertainment, and mixed-use development, subject to prohibited-business and active-use rules.

Community Facilities

Healthcare, childcare, education, nonprofit, cultural, and community facilities can qualify when operated through eligible business structures.

Infrastructure

The incentive is generally best suited to property and businesses generating investor returns. Pure public infrastructure without a revenue-producing entity can be difficult to finance directly.

Community-Benefit Agreements

Local governments and community organizations can negotiate affordability, hiring, contracting, public-space, service, environmental, and anti-displacement commitments when projects require public land, zoning, subsidies, permits, or partnership support.

Land-Value Effects

Designation can increase expectations concerning development and land value. The incentive can support reuse of difficult property while also rewarding owners who held land before designation.

African American Communities and Racial Equity

Designated Geography

Many original zones contain predominantly African American or Latino communities because the eligibility system targeted places shaped by segregation, redlining, deindustrialization, urban renewal, highway construction, public and private disinvestment, and concentrated poverty.

Access to Capital

The program can bring equity into communities where conventional investment has been limited. New housing, businesses, industrial facilities, healthcare, and commercial development can expand local assets and tax bases.

Investor Versus Resident Eligibility

Unlike the Empowerment Zone employment credit, Opportunity Zone tax benefits do not require an investor, employee, owner, or customer to live in the zone. Tax benefits can therefore flow to outside investors without a direct statutory link to resident income or employment.

Minority-Owned Businesses

Operating businesses owned by African American entrepreneurs can receive Opportunity Fund capital. In practice, fund structures often favor scalable projects, experienced sponsors, collateral, high projected returns, and long-term exits that many small businesses cannot easily provide.

Real-Estate Concentration

Early tax data indicate that real estate dominated reported Qualified Opportunity Zone business investment. Real estate can produce visible neighborhood change but does not necessarily create large numbers of permanent jobs or resident ownership.

Gentrification and Displacement

New investment can raise property values, rents, taxes, and development pressure. Without affordable housing, tenant protection, homeowner assistance, community ownership, and small-business preservation, existing residents can bear the costs of revitalization without sharing its gains.

Community Participation

The original federal statute did not require a community strategic plan or resident approval of individual investments. Local participation depends on state nomination processes, municipal land-use authority, project financing, public subsidies, developer practice, and community organization.

Ownership

Racial-equity outcomes depend on who owns land, funds, businesses, housing, and appreciating assets. Investment located in a Black community is not equivalent to investment owned or controlled by Black residents.

Measurement

Evaluation should distinguish increased property values and capital flows from changes in resident income, employment, housing stability, business ownership, wealth, health, education, and political power.

Geographic Scope

The original Opportunity Zone system includes 8,764 census tracts in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, and the United States Virgin Islands.

Original zones include central-city neighborhoods, rural counties, Tribal lands, former industrial districts, downtowns, waterfronts, suburban commercial areas, university-adjacent neighborhoods, disaster-affected communities, and developing corridors.

Geographic conditions vary widely. Some zones have strong transit, appreciating real estate, anchor institutions, and active development markets. Others face population loss, weak infrastructure, environmental contamination, low rents, limited financing, and scarce development capacity.

Investment has not been evenly distributed. Early federal data showed that a large share of reported investment was concentrated in a relatively small subset of zones and tended to favor tracts with stronger economic characteristics within the designated universe.

The 2027 nomination system starts from 25,332 eligible low-income tracts. Each state or territory selects only a limited share, making gubernatorial choices central to the future map.

New-zone eligibility is narrower than under the original system. Contiguous higher-income tracts no longer qualify, and the median-income threshold is reduced for a major eligibility path.

Original and new zones can overlap during 2027 and 2028. Investors and businesses must identify which designation and acquisition rules apply to each tract and property.

Outcomes and Evidence

Capital Formation

Federal tax-return data demonstrate that Qualified Opportunity Funds attracted substantial capital during the program’s early years. Treasury’s Office of Tax Analysis estimated investment flows of approximately $4 billion in 2018, $26 billion in 2019, and $18 billion in 2020 using available forms.

Industry Distribution

Real estate accounted for approximately two-thirds of reported Qualified Opportunity Zone business investment in early tax data. Finance, insurance, construction, and professional services represented much smaller shares.

Geographic Reach

By the end of 2020, early Treasury analysis found reported qualifying investment in approximately 48 percent of zones, meaning more than half had no reported investment in the data then available.

Concentration

Approximately 84 percent of reported investment through 2020 was concentrated in the top 20 percent of zones that had received investment.

Zone Characteristics

Zones receiving investment tended to have higher educational attainment, household income, and property values and lower unemployment than zones receiving no reported investment. This pattern suggests that market readiness strongly influenced investment location.

Economic Causation

It remains difficult to determine how much development was newly caused by the incentive, accelerated by it, relocated from nearby areas, or subsidized despite already being financially viable.

Resident Outcomes

Early studies reached mixed findings concerning employment, business creation, property prices, housing construction, and investment. Increased activity within a tract does not establish that existing low-income residents received the benefits.

Reporting Reform

The 2025 law’s annual fund and business reporting requirements are intended to provide more complete evidence concerning location, industry, employment, housing, investment concentration, and community outcomes.

Long Time Horizon

The ten-year holding benefit and long development periods mean that definitive evaluation requires many years of data. Investor exits, resident change, business survival, housing affordability, and post-designation outcomes remain important.

Benefits and Limitations

Uncapped Private Capital

The program can attract investment without a fixed national credit allocation or annual project appropriation.

Flexible Uses

Funds can invest in numerous forms of property and business rather than one prescribed sector.

Patient Capital

The ten-year appreciation benefit encourages longer holding periods than many ordinary investment strategies.

Local Designation

Governors can identify communities based on state and local knowledge, subject to federal eligibility limits.

Rural Enhancements

The permanent system provides a larger deferred-gain exclusion and lower improvement threshold for qualifying rural investment.

No Direct Resident Benefit

Federal law does not generally require local hiring, affordable housing, resident ownership, community participation, wage levels, or services.

Taxpayer Concentration

The benefit primarily serves taxpayers possessing eligible gains and sufficient capital to make long-term investments.

Market Selection

Capital tends to flow toward places and projects offering attractive returns, which may not be the most distressed or socially important.

Complexity

Investors and funds must navigate tax, securities, partnership, real-estate, valuation, compliance, and development rules.

Federal Revenue Cost

Deferral, basis increases, and exclusion reduce federal revenue. The public benefit depends on whether new economic activity and community gains justify the tax expenditure.

Displacement

Investment can increase land and housing costs unless paired with preservation and anti-displacement measures.

Weak Original Transparency

The original reporting structure prevented timely public understanding of funds, projects, jobs, housing, and distribution. The permanent law improves reporting but implementation remains ongoing.

Major Policy Debates

New Investment or Windfall

Supporters argue that the incentive changes investment decisions and makes difficult projects viable. Critics argue that it can reward projects already planned in improving neighborhoods.

Real Estate or Operating Businesses

Real estate is easier to secure, value, and hold for ten years. Operating businesses may produce stronger job and innovation effects but carry greater risk and uncertain exit timing.

Investor Return or Community Benefit

The statute defines tax qualification, not community benefit. A profitable qualifying project can satisfy federal law without reducing poverty or materially benefiting existing residents.

Designation Accuracy

Some original tracts were criticized as insufficiently distressed or already gentrifying. The permanent system tightens eligibility and eliminates contiguous-tract designations.

Uncapped Incentive

Opportunity Zone tax benefits have no annual national allocation cap comparable to the New Markets Tax Credit. This permits scale but weakens federal control over total revenue cost and project selection.

Community Planning

Empowerment Zones required strategic plans and partnerships. Opportunity Zones rely primarily on investor decisions. Debate concerns whether tax benefits should require local plans, impact standards, or community approval.

Housing Supply and Affordability

Market-rate construction can increase housing supply, while deeply affordable housing generally requires additional subsidy. Opportunity Zone law does not determine which type receives investment.

Gentrification

Property appreciation can demonstrate market success while increasing displacement pressure. The tax benefit can reward appreciation even when existing residents do not share ownership.

Permanent Renewal

The 2025 law provides long-term certainty and recurring redesignation. Critics argue that Congress made the program permanent before comprehensive evidence established its effects.

The 2026 Transition

Original Gain Recognition

Taxpayers holding original qualifying investments through December 31, 2026 generally must include their remaining deferred gain on the federal return covering that date. The inclusion does not terminate eligibility for the ten-year appreciation election on the continuing investment.

New Investments

Amounts invested after December 31, 2026 use the permanent five-year deferral system. New property acquired after that date generally must relate to a zone designated under the permanent system, subject to transitional exceptions.

Previously Designated Zones

Original Puerto Rico zones remain designated through 2027 and other original zones through 2028. However, acquisition rules for post-2026 property depend on the new statutory applicable-start-date framework.

Working-Capital Plans

Notice 2026-40 permits specified post-2026 property acquisitions under a written working-capital plan adopted by December 31, 2026 when the business receives and expends required portions of planned capital by that date.

Ordinary Replacement Property

An existing business in an original zone can acquire qualifying post-2026 property necessary to replace or modernize property in the ordinary course of business. The exception does not generally cover expansion into a new product or business.

Continued Business Treatment

Qualifying property acquired while the original designation remained effective can continue receiving specified zone treatment through December 31, 2047. Existing businesses can also continue satisfying certain active-business tests through that date.

State Nominations

State and territorial leaders are presently selecting 2027 zones. Their choices will determine the program’s next ten-year geography and influence future investment distribution.

Pending Guidance

Treasury and IRS continue developing regulations, forms, reporting systems, nomination tools, and transition rules implementing Public Law 119-21.

Key Distinctions

Opportunity Zone and Qualified Opportunity Fund

An Opportunity Zone is a designated census tract. A Qualified Opportunity Fund is the corporation or partnership through which an investor makes a qualifying investment.

Opportunity Zone and Qualified Opportunity Zone Business

The zone is a geographic designation. The business is an operating entity satisfying income, property, financial-asset, and prohibited-business requirements.

Original and Permanent Regimes

The original regime uses a fixed December 31, 2026 gain-inclusion date and historical five- and seven-year basis increases. The permanent regime uses an investment-specific five-year deferral and recurring ten-year zones.

Ordinary QOF and Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund

An ordinary QOF can invest in qualifying urban or rural zones. A QROF must meet the 90-percent test through property used in entirely rural zones and provides a 30-percent five-year basis increase for post-2026 investors.

Opportunity Zones and Empowerment Zones

Empowerment Zones combined grants, employment credits, bonds, strategic plans, and interagency assistance. Opportunity Zones provide investor capital-gains benefits through Qualified Opportunity Funds and do not require comparable local plans.

Opportunity Zones and New Markets Tax Credits

The New Markets Tax Credit uses competitively allocated credit authority awarded to certified Community Development Entities. Opportunity Zones have no national allocation cap and rely on investor gain elections and self-certified funds.

Opportunity Zones and Low-Income Housing Tax Credits

LIHTC provides tax credits tied to income-restricted rental housing and state allocation. Opportunity Zones provide capital-gains benefits and do not independently impose housing affordability.

Opportunity Zones and HUBZones

HUBZones provide federal contracting preferences to qualifying small businesses. Opportunity Zones provide tax treatment for investors and funds.

Opportunity Zones and Enterprise Zones

Enterprise zone is a general state or local policy category. Qualified Opportunity Zone is a specific federal census-tract designation under the Internal Revenue Code.

Tax Deferral and Tax Exclusion

Deferral postpones taxation of the original eligible gain. Exclusion can reduce the original deferred gain through a basis increase or eliminate qualifying appreciation after a long holding period.

Project Qualification and Public Benefit

Tax qualification means a project satisfies federal tax rules. It does not establish that the project is affordable, equitable, locally supported, or socially beneficial.

Summary

The Opportunity Zones Program is a federal place-based capital-gains tax incentive created in 2017. Investors obtain preferential tax treatment by timely investing eligible gains in Qualified Opportunity Funds that hold qualifying property or business interests within designated low-income census tracts.

The original system established 8,764 Qualified Opportunity Zones. It provides temporary deferral of eligible gain until an earlier inclusion event or December 31, 2026, historical basis increases for five- and seven-year holdings, and potential exclusion of qualifying appreciation after a minimum ten-year holding period.

Qualified Opportunity Funds self-certify and generally must hold at least 90 percent of assets in Qualified Opportunity Zone property. Investments can be made directly in tangible business property or indirectly through qualifying corporations and partnerships. Property generally must begin its original use in the zone or be substantially improved.

The program has attracted substantial investment, especially into real estate. Early federal evidence also shows uneven distribution: many zones received no reported investment, while a large share of capital concentrated in a smaller group of relatively market-ready tracts.

The incentive can finance housing, businesses, industrial facilities, healthcare, commercial development, and other assets. It does not independently require affordable housing, resident hiring, community ownership, local planning, or direct benefits for existing residents.

Public Law 119-21 made Opportunity Zones permanent. Beginning in 2027, new designations last ten years and recur every decade. The law tightens tract eligibility, ends contiguous-tract selection, removes Puerto Rico’s automatic designation, creates Qualified Rural Opportunity Funds, reduces the rural substantial-improvement threshold, and requires extensive federal reporting.

The permanent investment regime uses a rolling five-year deferral. Five-year investors receive a 10-percent basis increase, while Qualified Rural Opportunity Fund investors receive 30 percent. The ten-year appreciation exclusion continues, subject to a thirtieth-anniversary limit.

As of August 2026, states and territories are nominating the first permanent-system zones. New designations become effective January 1, 2027, while original zones remain temporarily active through 2027 or 2028. The program’s next phase will depend on state selection, Treasury implementation, investor behavior, rural participation, reporting quality, community safeguards, and whether capital investment produces durable gains for existing residents.
