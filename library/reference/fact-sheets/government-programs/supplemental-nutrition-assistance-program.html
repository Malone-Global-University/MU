Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program

The federal nutrition-assistance entitlement formerly known as the Food Stamp Program, providing eligible households with electronic benefits to purchase food through authorized retailers while supporting food security, poverty reduction, and economic stabilization.

What Is the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program?

SNAP is a federal program that provides monthly food-purchasing benefits to eligible households with low income.

The program supplements a household’s own food budget rather than guaranteeing payment of all food costs. Federal law assumes that households with net income can contribute approximately thirty percent of that income toward food.

SNAP is federally financed and governed but administered primarily through state and territorial agencies. The federal government pays benefits, authorizes retailers, establishes national requirements, and monitors administration. State agencies process applications, determine eligibility, calculate allotments, issue EBT benefits, and conduct recertification.

SNAP is an entitlement under federal law. Eligible households may receive benefits without Congress establishing a fixed national number of participant slots, although appropriations and federal operations remain necessary to administer and issue benefits.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains SNAP’s historical development, legal framework, federal-state administration, household eligibility, benefit calculation, work rules, EBT system, retailer participation, program integrity, nutrition policy, outcomes, and current status.

It does not replace a state eligibility determination. State options, income limits, deductions, certification procedures, work-rule waivers, and benefit amounts change over time.

Disaster SNAP, SNAP Employment and Training, SNAP-Ed, the Restaurant Meals Program, territorial Nutrition Assistance Programs, and the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations are discussed as related components or alternatives but may require separate fact sheets.

Geographic Scope

SNAP operates in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, Guam, and the United States Virgin Islands.

Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, and the Virgin Islands receive separate maximum-allotment and income schedules reflecting statutory geographic adjustments.

Puerto Rico, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands receive federal Nutrition Assistance Program block grants instead of participating in SNAP.

Within participating jurisdictions, access varies according to state administration, rural geography, tribal jurisdiction, language, transportation, retailer availability, online purchasing, and local office capacity.

Historical Context

Federal food-assistance policy developed from the combination of agricultural surpluses, unemployment, poverty, nutrition concerns, and the need to support food purchasing.

Early programs attempted to connect households lacking purchasing power with farm commodities that could not be sold at adequate prices.

Over time, policy shifted from physical commodity distribution and paper coupons toward a nationwide entitlement using electronic benefits in ordinary retail markets.

SNAP’s history is therefore connected to agricultural policy, poverty policy, civil rights, welfare reform, retail regulation, public health, economic stabilization, and information technology.

The First Food Stamp Program, 1939–1943

The first federal Food Stamp Program began in Rochester, New York, in 1939 during the New Deal.

Participants purchased orange stamps usable for ordinary food and received additional blue stamps for designated surplus commodities.

The program linked relief for low-income households with agricultural-market support.

It ended in 1943 as wartime employment reduced unemployment and agricultural surpluses.

Food Stamp Pilot Programs, 1961–1964

President John F. Kennedy authorized modern food-stamp pilot projects beginning in 1961.

Pilot households purchased coupons equal to their normal food expenditures and received a larger coupon value reflecting the cost of an economical diet.

The pilots tested administration, retailer participation, benefit use, nutrition, and the relationship between food assistance and commodity distribution.

Their expansion helped establish the administrative basis for permanent legislation.

Food Stamp Act of 1964

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Food Stamp Act of 1964 on August 31, 1964.

The law established a permanent Food Stamp Program intended to improve nutrition among low-income households and strengthen the agricultural economy.

Participating households purchased food coupons and received bonus value based on income and household circumstances.

States administered eligibility under federal standards, while the Department of Agriculture authorized retailers and financed the bonus value.

National Expansion

Participation expanded during the late 1960s and early 1970s as additional counties and states adopted the program.

The Food Stamp Act amendments of 1970 established uniform national eligibility and benefit standards and required statewide operation.

Nationwide implementation was completed in 1974.

Expansion transformed food stamps from a collection of local programs into a national safety-net institution.

Food Stamp Act of 1977

The Food Stamp Act of 1977 made major changes to eligibility, administration, and benefit delivery.

Its most important reform eliminated the purchase requirement. Eligible households no longer had to pay cash to obtain a larger value of food stamps.

The purchase requirement had excluded households unable to assemble the required cash even when they were otherwise eligible.

The 1977 law also revised income standards, deductions, fraud controls, outreach, and state administration.

1980s Retrenchment and Reform

Legislation during the early 1980s reduced eligibility and benefits, strengthened verification, and changed deductions.

Later measures restored or revised selected provisions while continuing emphasis on program integrity.

The period established a recurring policy conflict between expanding nutrition access and limiting federal cost, eligibility, and administrative error.

Welfare Reform and the 1996 Changes

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 substantially restricted food-stamp eligibility.

It created the time limit for able-bodied adults without dependents, restricted eligibility for many lawfully present noncitizens, reduced benefits, and increased state administrative authority.

Later legislation restored eligibility to several groups, including many children, older adults, people with disabilities, refugees, and lawful permanent residents after a waiting period.

Public Law 119-21 revised noncitizen eligibility again in 2025.

Transition to Electronic Benefits Transfer

Paper food coupons were gradually replaced by Electronic Benefits Transfer systems.

EBT reduced printing, handling, and coupon redemption while allowing electronic authorization and transaction monitoring.

By June 2004, EBT was the sole method of SNAP issuance in all participating states and territories.

Electronic delivery improved convenience and reduced visible distinction at checkout, but it created cybersecurity, skimming, system-outage, and data-governance risks.

Renaming the Program as SNAP

The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 renamed the Food Stamp Program the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.

The underlying statute became the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008.

The new name emphasized nutrition and electronic benefits rather than paper stamps.

“Food stamps” remains common historical and public terminology, but SNAP is the official federal name.

American Recovery and Reinvestment Act

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 temporarily increased maximum SNAP benefits during the Great Recession.

Participation and spending increased as unemployment and income loss expanded eligibility.

The temporary increase supported household food spending and functioned as an economic stimulus.

Benefits later declined when the temporary increase expired.

Agricultural Act of 2014

The 2014 farm bill revised retailer standards, work-rule waivers, utility deductions, program integrity, employment and training, and pilot authority.

It authorized the Food Insecurity Nutrition Incentive program, later known through the Gus Schumacher Nutrition Incentive Program.

The law continued SNAP as a central component of the recurring farm-bill coalition joining agricultural and nutrition policy.

Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018

The 2018 farm bill reauthorized SNAP without adopting proposed broad statutory work-requirement expansions.

It strengthened employment and training evaluation, retailer integrity, data exchange, and nutrition incentives.

The law also required reevaluation of the Thrifty Food Plan by 2022 and every five years thereafter under the law then in force.

2021 Thrifty Food Plan Reevaluation

USDA reevaluated the Thrifty Food Plan in 2021 using current food prices, dietary guidance, consumption patterns, and nutritional requirements.

The reevaluation increased the cost basis used for maximum SNAP allotments beyond ordinary inflation adjustment.

Supporters argued that the prior plan did not reflect realistic food preparation, dietary guidance, or purchasing patterns. Critics questioned the methodology, statutory interpretation, and long-term cost.

Public Law 119-21 later limited future reevaluations to cost-neutral changes.

Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023

The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 temporarily expanded the age range subject to the able-bodied-adult time limit and added exemptions for veterans, people experiencing homelessness, and certain former foster youth.

The age expansion was scheduled through fiscal year 2030.

Public Law 119-21 replaced these exception rules in 2025, extended the relevant age range through age sixty-five, and added exemptions for specified Native populations.

Public Law 119-21 and the 2025 SNAP Changes

On July 4, 2025, the President signed Public Law 119-21, commonly called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025.

The law made major SNAP changes involving:

  • the Thrifty Food Plan and future cost-neutral reevaluation;
  • able-bodied-adult time-limit exceptions and waivers;
  • standard utility allowances and internet expenses;
  • state benefit cost sharing based on payment error rates;
  • reduction of the federal administrative-cost share beginning in fiscal year 2027;
  • termination of mandatory SNAP-Ed funding after fiscal year 2025; and
  • restriction of noncitizen eligibility.

Different provisions have different effective dates and implementation schedules.

Federal Administration

SNAP is administered federally by the Food and Nutrition Administration within USDA.

The agency was known as the Food and Nutrition Service until June 1, 2026.

Federal responsibilities include:

  • interpreting and enforcing the Food and Nutrition Act;
  • approving state plans and options;
  • paying federal benefit costs;
  • reimbursing authorized administrative costs;
  • authorizing and monitoring retailers;
  • setting annual allotments, deductions, and income standards;
  • administering EBT and online-purchasing requirements;
  • operating quality control;
  • reviewing waivers;
  • publishing data and research; and
  • investigating trafficking and retailer violations.

Federal-State Administrative Structure

SNAP combines federal law and financing with state eligibility administration.

State agencies:

  • accept applications;
  • conduct interviews;
  • verify information;
  • determine household composition;
  • calculate income and deductions;
  • issue EBT benefits;
  • manage recertification and reporting;
  • operate employment and training programs;
  • establish claims and hearings;
  • prevent duplicate participation; and
  • report quality-control and financial data.

State variation arises from federal options, waivers, administrative systems, staffing, outreach, categorical eligibility, reporting rules, and local service delivery.

Entitlement Status

SNAP is a federal entitlement. A household meeting the applicable statutory and administrative requirements is eligible for benefits even when participation increases unexpectedly.

Federal spending therefore expands when unemployment, poverty, disasters, or policy changes increase the eligible population.

Entitlement status distinguishes SNAP from block grants with fixed territorial or state funding ceilings.

Administrative failure can still delay or deny benefits despite statutory entitlement.

The SNAP Household

SNAP eligibility is determined for a “household,” which is not always identical to a tax household, lease, family, or mailing address.

A household generally consists of people who live together and customarily purchase and prepare food together.

Federal law requires certain people living together to be included in the same household, including spouses and most parents and children under age twenty-two.

Other residents at one address may qualify as separate households when they purchase and prepare food separately and are not subject to mandatory grouping.

General Eligibility

Eligibility depends on financial and nonfinancial rules.

Major factors include:

  • household composition;
  • gross income;
  • net income after allowable deductions;
  • countable resources where applicable;
  • citizenship or eligible immigration status;
  • state residence;
  • student status;
  • work registration and time-limit rules;
  • identity and required verification;
  • duplicate participation; and
  • compliance with reporting and certification requirements.

Gross and Net Income Tests

Under standard federal rules, most households must have gross monthly income at or below 130 percent of the federal poverty level and net monthly income at or below 100 percent.

Households containing a person age sixty or older or a person meeting the SNAP disability definition generally need to meet only the net-income test under ordinary federal rules.

Broad-based categorical eligibility can alter the gross-income and resource screening used in many states.

Income rules distinguish earned income, unearned income, excluded payments, self-employment, sponsored noncitizen income, and other sources.

Resource and Asset Rules

Federal SNAP rules limit countable liquid resources for households not protected by categorical eligibility.

Countable resources can include cash and money in bank accounts, while many assets are excluded.

Common exclusions include the household’s home and lot, personal belongings, most retirement accounts, and resources of people receiving certain categorical benefits.

Vehicle treatment and categorical eligibility vary by state. Many participating households are not subject to a separate federal asset test because of state policy.

Categorical Eligibility

Categorical eligibility allows a household to qualify for SNAP financial screening based partly on receipt of, or authorization for, benefits or services under another qualifying program.

Traditional categorical eligibility applies to households in which all members receive specified cash assistance.

Broad-based categorical eligibility uses state-funded Temporary Assistance for Needy Families benefits or services to modify SNAP gross-income or resource screening.

It does not eliminate all SNAP requirements. Households must still meet applicable net-income, household, citizenship, work, and certification rules.

Allowable Income Deductions

SNAP calculates net income by subtracting authorized deductions from countable income.

Major deductions include:

  • a standard household deduction;
  • twenty percent of earned income;
  • dependent-care expenses needed for work, training, or education;
  • legally owed and paid child support in states using the deduction;
  • medical expenses above the federal threshold for elderly or disabled household members;
  • homeless household shelter deduction where applicable; and
  • excess shelter costs after other deductions.

Documentation and state calculation methods can materially affect the final allotment.

Shelter and Utility Deductions

Shelter costs can include rent, mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and qualifying utilities.

Households may receive an excess-shelter deduction when allowable shelter costs exceed half of adjusted income.

States commonly use standard utility allowances instead of requiring exact monthly utility bills.

Public Law 119-21 limited automatic heating-and-cooling utility-allowance treatment based solely on qualifying energy assistance to households containing an elderly or disabled member and prohibited internet service fees from being included in the excess-shelter calculation.

Benefit Calculation

The monthly allotment is generally calculated by multiplying net monthly income by thirty percent, rounding according to federal rules, and subtracting that amount from the maximum allotment for the household size.

A household with no countable net income generally receives the maximum allotment.

A household with income receives less than the maximum because it is expected to contribute part of its own resources toward food.

Minimum benefits apply to certain one- and two-person households, particularly those with elderly or disabled members, under current law and geographic schedules.

Thrifty Food Plan

The Thrifty Food Plan is USDA’s lowest-cost food plan designed to meet current dietary guidance for a reference family.

It supplies the basis for the maximum four-person SNAP allotment. Federal ratios convert that amount for other household sizes.

Annual inflation adjustments occur each October using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers for the relevant twelve-month period.

Under Public Law 119-21, USDA may reevaluate the food baskets no earlier than October 1, 2027, but a reevaluation cannot increase the plan’s cost.

Application Process

A household applies through the SNAP agency in the jurisdiction where it lives.

Applications may be submitted online, in person, by mail, by fax, or through other state-approved methods.

The filing date can be established by submitting an application containing at least the applicant’s name, address where required, and signature.

State agencies conduct an interview and request verification concerning identity, residence, household members, income, deductions, immigration status where applicable, and work requirements.

Processing Time and Expedited Service

State agencies generally must process eligible applications within thirty days.

Households meeting expedited-service criteria must receive faster processing, generally within seven days.

Expedited eligibility can apply when income and liquid resources are extremely low relative to expenses or when a household contains certain destitute migrant or seasonal farmworkers.

Expedited service accelerates initial issuance but does not permanently waive verification or continuing eligibility requirements.

Certification and Recertification

Approved households receive benefits for a certification period rather than permanent uninterrupted eligibility.

Certification length varies by household circumstances, state policy, age, disability, income stability, and simplified-reporting rules.

Before expiration, the household must complete recertification, provide required information, and ordinarily participate in an interview unless waived.

Administrative delays during recertification can cause benefit interruption even when the household remains eligible.

Household Reporting Requirements

Households must report specified changes according to the reporting system assigned by the state.

States may use change reporting, simplified reporting, periodic reports, or combinations permitted by federal law.

Reportable changes can involve income exceeding a threshold, household composition, address, shelter costs, employment, or able-bodied-adult work hours.

Failure to report a required change can create an overpayment claim, while unnecessary reporting can increase administrative burden and error.

Notices and Fair Hearings

State agencies must provide written notice of approval, denial, benefit amount, reduction, termination, overpayment, and other adverse actions.

Households may request a fair hearing to challenge an eligibility or benefit decision.

Continued benefits may be available when a hearing is requested before the effective date of certain adverse actions, although the household may owe repayment if the agency is upheld.

Due process requires meaningful notice, language access, an opportunity to present evidence, and an impartial decision.

Electronic Benefits Transfer

EBT is the electronic system used to issue and redeem SNAP benefits.

A household receives a card and personal identification number. Eligible purchases debit the SNAP account and credit the authorized retailer.

Benefits generally remain available after issuance subject to inactivity and expungement rules.

EBT accounts are distinct from cash-assistance accounts even when one card accesses both.

Foods SNAP Can Purchase

SNAP can generally purchase:

  • fruits and vegetables;
  • meat, poultry, and fish;
  • dairy products;
  • breads and cereals;
  • snack foods;
  • nonalcoholic beverages;
  • seeds and plants producing food for household consumption;
  • cold prepared foods intended for off-premises consumption; and
  • other food products bearing a Nutrition Facts label.

Items SNAP Cannot Ordinarily Purchase

SNAP cannot ordinarily purchase:

  • alcoholic beverages;
  • tobacco and nicotine products;
  • vitamins, medicines, and dietary supplements bearing a Supplement Facts label;
  • live animals, subject to narrow statutory exceptions;
  • hot foods at the point of sale;
  • foods intended for immediate onsite consumption;
  • pet food;
  • soap, paper goods, cleaning products, and toiletries;
  • household supplies;
  • delivery fees, tips, and service fees; and
  • other nonfood merchandise.

Restaurant Meals Program

Federal law permits states to operate a Restaurant Meals Program for specified SNAP participants who may have difficulty preparing food.

Eligible groups generally include people who are elderly, disabled, experiencing homelessness, and their spouses.

Participating restaurants require state approval and federal retailer authorization.

The program is optional and is not available in every state or locality.

Online Purchasing

SNAP households can use EBT for eligible food purchases through approved online retailers and e-commerce systems.

SNAP benefits cannot pay delivery, service, membership, or tip charges.

Retailers must ensure that only eligible items are charged to the SNAP account and that transactions use approved secure payment processes.

As of 2026, online SNAP purchasing was widely available in participating states, while Guam and the Virgin Islands had not implemented the online system.

Retailer Authorization

USDA authorizes stores, farmers markets, direct-marketing farmers, meal services, and other eligible firms to accept SNAP.

A retail food store generally must meet staple-food stocking requirements or derive more than half of total gross retail sales from staple foods.

Staple categories include meat, poultry or fish; bread or cereals; vegetables or fruits; and dairy products.

Authorization is separate from a state business license, EBT equipment contract, or participation in WIC.

Farmers Markets and Direct-Marketing Farmers

Farmers markets and direct-marketing farmers can become authorized SNAP retailers.

Markets may use wireless terminals, centralized tokens, paper scrip, or approved electronic systems to process benefits for individual vendors.

Nutrition-incentive programs can provide additional purchasing value for fruits and vegetables using federal, state, local, or philanthropic funds.

Market authorization expands access to local food but requires careful accounting and vendor training.

State Food-Restriction Demonstrations and Waivers

Federal law defines SNAP-eligible foods nationally, but states may request federal approval for demonstrations or waivers affecting eligible purchases.

Proposals have included restrictions on sugar-sweetened beverages, candy, or other products and incentives for designated nutritious foods.

Such changes require federal legal authority, retailer-system modification, participant notice, evaluation, and safeguards against confusion or unequal access.

A state announcement does not itself change federal SNAP purchasing rules until the required approval and implementation occur.

General Work Requirements

SNAP has general work requirements separate from the able-bodied-adult time limit.

Unless exempt, individuals generally must register for work, accept suitable employment, avoid voluntarily quitting or reducing work without good cause, and comply with assigned employment and training requirements.

Common exemptions include age, disability or incapacity, responsibility for a young child, participation in certain treatment or education programs, and receipt of unemployment compensation, subject to federal rules.

Individuals age sixty or older remain exempt from the general work requirements even though some may be within the separate ABAWD time-limit age range after the 2025 law.

Able-Bodied Adult Without Dependents Time Limit

The ABAWD rule limits certain adults to three months of SNAP in a three-year period unless they work, participate in qualifying work activities, or meet an exception.

Qualifying activity generally requires at least eighty hours per month of work, workfare, approved training, or an allowed combination.

The rule is a benefit time limit rather than merely a work-registration requirement.

States must screen individuals for exceptions, explain requirements, track countable months, and restore eligibility when a person meets work or exemption conditions.

The 2025 Work-Rule Changes

Public Law 119-21 revised ABAWD exceptions effective July 4, 2025.

The time limit generally applies from age eighteen through age sixty-five unless another exception applies.

The parental exception is limited to a parent or household member responsible for a dependent child younger than fourteen.

The law retained exceptions for pregnancy and medical unfitness and added exceptions for Indians, Urban Indians, and specified California Indians.

It did not continue the temporary statutory exceptions for veterans, people experiencing homelessness, and certain former foster youth enacted in 2023.

ABAWD Waivers

States may request federal waiver of the ABAWD time limit for qualifying areas.

Public Law 119-21 removed the general “lack of sufficient jobs” criterion and generally requires an unemployment rate above ten percent for geographic waivers.

Special provisions allow Alaska and Hawaii to request broader exemptions based on good-faith implementation efforts and documented barriers.

Waivers affect the time limit, not every SNAP work requirement.

SNAP Employment and Training

Every SNAP state agency must operate an Employment and Training program.

Components can include job search, job-search training, basic education, vocational training, work experience, apprenticeships, case management, and supportive services.

States may operate mandatory, voluntary, or mixed systems within federal rules.

Participants should receive transportation, child care, tools, books, uniforms, or other reimbursements necessary for assigned activities when required by law.

College and Postsecondary Students

Students enrolled at least half time in an institution of higher education are generally ineligible unless they meet a federal student exemption.

Exemptions can involve work hours, work-study, disability, care of dependents, receipt of specified assistance, participation in approved training, or other statutory conditions.

Student eligibility differs from household income eligibility and is a frequent source of confusion.

Temporary pandemic student exemptions expired after the federal public-health emergency.

Special Rules for Elderly and Disabled Households

SNAP defines an elderly person as age sixty or older and uses a statutory disability definition tied to specified benefit or medical categories.

Households containing an elderly or disabled member may receive:

  • exemption from the gross-income test under ordinary rules;
  • a higher resource limit where the asset test applies;
  • deduction of qualifying medical expenses;
  • an uncapped excess-shelter deduction;
  • longer certification periods in some states;
  • simplified reporting or interview options; and
  • minimum-allotment protection for qualifying small households.

Citizenship and Noncitizen Eligibility

United States citizens and nationals may qualify if they meet all other SNAP requirements.

Undocumented immigrants are not eligible for SNAP, but they may apply on behalf of eligible household members such as United States citizen children.

Public Law 119-21 limited eligible noncitizen categories principally to lawful permanent residents, Cuban and Haitian entrants, and citizens of the freely associated states, subject to waiting-period and exemption rules.

Refugees, asylees, trafficking survivors, people with withholding of removal, and several other groups previously eligible based on humanitarian status lost direct eligibility under the 2025 amendment unless they qualify through another permitted status.

Ineligible household members can be excluded as recipients while their income and resources may still affect the eligible household.

Tribal Communities and FDPIR

Eligible households on or near Indian reservations may receive SNAP or participate in the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, subject to program rules.

FDPIR provides a monthly package of USDA Foods through tribal organizations or state agencies.

Households generally cannot receive SNAP and FDPIR in the same month.

Tribal communities face distinct issues involving distance to retailers, food prices, sovereignty, traditional foods, state administration, and access to federal systems.

Territorial Nutrition Assistance Programs

Puerto Rico, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands operate Nutrition Assistance Program block grants instead of SNAP.

Block-grant funding is capped and provides territorial governments greater administrative discretion than ordinary SNAP.

Because NAP is separate, its eligibility, benefit, work, retailer, and funding rules should not be described automatically as SNAP rules.

Guam and the United States Virgin Islands participate in SNAP.

Disaster SNAP

Disaster SNAP, commonly called D-SNAP, provides temporary food assistance after a presidential disaster declaration when USDA approves a state request.

It can serve households not ordinarily eligible for SNAP when disaster income loss and disaster-related expenses reduce available resources.

Approved households generally receive one month of benefits equal to the maximum allotment for household size.

Existing SNAP households may receive supplemental disaster benefits when authorized.

State Options and Administrative Variation

Federal law permits states to choose among approved policy options.

State variation can involve:

  • broad-based categorical eligibility;
  • vehicle-resource rules;
  • simplified reporting;
  • certification length;
  • standard medical deductions;
  • child-support treatment;
  • employment and training design;
  • Restaurant Meals Programs;
  • call centers and local offices;
  • online applications;
  • identity verification; and
  • benefit issuance schedules.

State flexibility does not authorize departure from federal civil-rights, due-process, or core eligibility requirements.

SNAP Quality Control

SNAP Quality Control measures the accuracy of state eligibility and benefit determinations using statistically selected case reviews.

Payment error rates include both overpayments and underpayments.

An error can result from household reporting, state calculation, verification, system programming, policy interpretation, or documentation.

Payment error is not equivalent to fraud. Most quality-control errors do not establish intentional deception.

Payment Error Rates

USDA publishes national and state payment error rates.

Rates measure the dollar value of benefits issued incorrectly relative to total reviewed issuance under quality-control methodology.

High rates can lead to corrective-action requirements, liability, and—under Public Law 119-21—future state responsibility for a portion of benefit costs.

Payment error rates should not be cited as the percentage of participants committing fraud.

State Benefit Cost Sharing Beginning in Fiscal Year 2028

Historically, the federal government paid the full cost of ordinary SNAP benefits.

Public Law 119-21 established state benefit shares based on payment error rates, generally beginning in fiscal year 2028.

Administrative Cost Sharing

Through fiscal year 2026, the federal government generally reimburses fifty percent of approved state SNAP administrative costs.

Public Law 119-21 reduces the ordinary federal share to twenty-five percent beginning in fiscal year 2027, leaving states responsible for seventy-five percent.

The reduction affects eligibility administration, information systems, outreach, and other costs authorized under the general administrative-cost provision.

Separate statutory financing continues for SNAP Employment and Training and for Indian Tribal Organizations administering SNAP as state agencies under applicable rules.

Program Integrity

Program integrity includes accurate eligibility, secure benefit issuance, lawful retailer transactions, prevention of duplicate participation, recovery of overpayments, and protection of participant rights.

Integrity is not limited to punitive enforcement. It also requires adequate staffing, understandable notices, functional computer systems, reliable identity matching, and timely correction of underpayments.

Excessively burdensome verification can deter eligible households and increase procedural denials.

Intentional Program Violations

An intentional program violation involves intentionally making a false or misleading statement, concealing facts, or committing another prohibited act to obtain or increase benefits.

State agencies may pursue administrative hearings, waivers, civil claims, disqualification, or criminal referral.

Disqualification periods depend on the violation and prior history.

The government must distinguish intentional conduct from misunderstanding, clerical error, language barriers, or agency mistake.

Retailer Trafficking

SNAP trafficking includes exchanging benefits for cash, ineligible items, controlled substances, weapons, or other prohibited consideration.

USDA investigates retailers using transaction data, undercover activity, complaints, records, and other evidence.

Penalties can include monetary sanctions, temporary disqualification, permanent disqualification, civil money penalties, and criminal prosecution.

Retailers are responsible for violations committed by owners, managers, and employees under program rules.

EBT Skimming and Benefit Theft

Electronic theft can occur through card skimming, cloning, phishing, stolen credentials, fraudulent websites, and unauthorized transactions.

States and EBT contractors use transaction monitoring, card replacement, PIN security, chip-card development, fraud alerts, and participant education.

Federal authority for replacing stolen benefits has changed over time and may be limited by appropriations and statutory expiration dates.

Theft from a household is distinct from household fraud.

Overpayments and Claims

State agencies establish claims when a household receives more benefits than legally entitled.

Claims can result from agency error, inadvertent household error, or intentional program violation.

Collection methods include benefit reduction, repayment agreements, tax-offset systems, and other authorized recovery.

Households may challenge the amount, cause, calculation, or collection through fair-hearing procedures.

Underpayments and Lost Benefits

Administrative errors can also cause households to receive too little or no benefit.

State agencies must restore benefits when federal rules require correction of an improper denial, termination, delay, or underissuance.

Quality discussions that examine only overpayments provide an incomplete picture of program accuracy.

SNAP Nutrition Education and Obesity Prevention

SNAP-Ed funded nutrition education, policy, systems, and environmental strategies for SNAP participants and other eligible low-income populations.

States worked through universities, extension services, health departments, nonprofit organizations, and other implementing agencies.

Public Law 119-21 ended the statutory requirement for federal SNAP-Ed funding after the fiscal year 2025 allocation.

Fiscal year 2026 guidance addressed grant closeout and use of remaining authorized carryover funds. Nutrition education may continue through other federal, state, local, or private resources, but not through an automatic continuing SNAP-Ed allocation under the amended provision.

Nutrition Incentives

Nutrition incentives increase the purchasing power of SNAP households for fruits, vegetables, or other designated foods.

Programs can operate at farmers markets, grocery stores, mobile markets, and health-care settings.

The Gus Schumacher Nutrition Incentive Program supports approved incentive and produce-prescription projects outside the ordinary SNAP allotment.

Incentives preserve participant choice while encouraging designated purchases, but geographic availability is uneven.

Effects on Food Security

USDA research generally finds that SNAP reduces food insecurity among participating households after accounting for the fact that households often enter SNAP when conditions are deteriorating.

Benefit increases have been associated with increased food spending and reduced food insecurity, while benefit reductions have been associated with declining food spending and greater hardship.

SNAP does not eliminate food insecurity because benefits are supplemental, food prices vary, and households face housing, health, transportation, and income pressures.

Effects on Poverty

SNAP benefits are not counted as cash income in the official poverty measure but are included in the Supplemental Poverty Measure.

Research finds that SNAP reduces the depth and severity of poverty, particularly among children.

The program frees household cash for rent, utilities, transportation, medicine, and other necessities even though benefits can be spent only on eligible food.

Diet Quality and Nutrition

SNAP increases resources available for food, but evidence concerning overall diet quality is mixed.

Participant diets are shaped by income, food prices, time, transportation, retail access, culture, cooking facilities, marketing, health conditions, and neighborhood food environments.

Restricting purchases, increasing benefits, providing incentives, and improving food access represent different policy strategies with distinct administrative consequences.

Economic Stabilization

SNAP is countercyclical: participation and benefits generally rise when unemployment and poverty rise and decline as economic conditions improve.

Because low-income households spend benefits quickly, SNAP supports retailers, wholesalers, transportation, food processing, agriculture, and local employment.

USDA Economic Research Service estimates have found that one billion dollars in additional SNAP benefits during a slowing economy can increase gross domestic product by approximately $1.54 billion.

Working Households

SNAP is not limited to households without employment.

Many participating households have earnings but remain eligible because wages are low, work hours fluctuate, household size is large, or shelter and dependent-care costs are high.

The twenty-percent earned-income deduction recognizes work-related costs and preserves a gradual relationship between earnings and benefit reduction.

Children and Families

Children constitute a large share of SNAP participants.

Household eligibility supports children indirectly through the family food budget rather than issuing a separate child benefit.

SNAP participation also creates categorical connections to school meals, Summer EBT, and other programs under applicable laws and state procedures.

Benefit adequacy, parental work schedules, child care, housing, and school access all affect family food security.

Older Adults

Older adults may qualify under special income, medical-expense, shelter, resource, interview, and certification rules.

Participation among eligible older adults has historically been lower than among some other groups because of stigma, limited mobility, small expected benefits, application burden, and misunderstanding of eligibility.

Simplified applications, telephone interviews, combined benefit projects, outreach, and longer certification periods can improve access.

People with Disabilities

SNAP disability status is defined by federal law and is not based solely on a self-reported medical condition.

Qualifying households may deduct medical expenses, avoid the gross-income test, receive uncapped shelter deductions, and qualify for special resource and certification rules.

Agencies must provide reasonable accommodation and accessible communication during application, interview, reporting, and hearing processes.

Racial and Geographic Equity

SNAP serves households across racial and ethnic groups, but participation reflects unequal exposure to poverty, unemployment, disability, low wages, segregation, and regional economic conditions.

Rural households may face long distances to offices and retailers. Urban households may face high housing costs, store access problems, and administrative congestion.

Tribal communities confront state jurisdiction, limited retailers, food prices, and the relationship between SNAP and FDPIR.

Civil-rights compliance requires nondiscrimination, language access, disability access, fair hearings, and equitable administration.

Stigma, Administrative Burden, and Churn

Eligible households may not participate because of stigma, misinformation, documentation demands, interviews, language barriers, office hours, technology, or fear concerning immigration consequences.

“Churn” occurs when eligible households leave the program and return within a short period, often because of procedural interruption rather than true loss of eligibility.

Churn increases household hardship and state workload.

Simplified reporting and longer certifications can reduce churn, while weak call centers or automated systems can create new barriers.

COVID-19 Pandemic

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Congress and USDA authorized emergency allotments, expanded online purchasing, adjusted interviews and reporting, and issued other flexibilities.

Emergency allotments generally raised participating households to the maximum benefit and later provided additional minimum supplements.

The national emergency allotments ended after February 2023.

Pandemic Electronic Benefit Transfer was a separate child-nutrition benefit and should not be confused with ordinary SNAP.

Fiscal Year 2024 Program Scale

USDA Economic Research Service reported that in fiscal year 2024:

  • SNAP served an average of 41.7 million participants per month;
  • federal spending totaled $99.8 billion;
  • average monthly benefits were $187.20 per participant;
  • approximately 12.3 percent of United States residents participated; and
  • SNAP accounted for about seventy percent of USDA nutrition-assistance spending, excluding Summer EBT.

Participation and spending should be dated because economic conditions, benefits, and eligibility law change.

Current Status as of August 2, 2026

SNAP remained operational under the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, as amended.

Fiscal year 2026 maximum allotments and income standards were in effect through September 30, 2026.

The Food and Nutrition Administration was implementing Public Law 119-21 provisions concerning work requirements, immigration eligibility, utility deductions, SNAP-Ed closeout, state administrative financing, and future benefit cost sharing.

The ordinary federal administrative share remained fifty percent through fiscal year 2026 and was scheduled to fall to twenty-five percent in fiscal year 2027.

State benefit cost sharing based on payment error rates was generally scheduled to begin in fiscal year 2028, subject to statutory delay provisions.

Why SNAP Matters

SNAP is the nation’s largest nutrition-assistance program and the principal federal mechanism for increasing the food-purchasing power of low-income households.

Unlike a fixed block grant, it responds automatically to household eligibility and economic conditions.

Its EBT structure allows participants to purchase food through ordinary retail markets while preserving federal controls on products and retailers.

The program reduces food insecurity and poverty but does not eliminate either condition because benefits are supplemental and households face competing expenses.

SNAP’s federal-state design creates national entitlement rules alongside substantial administrative variation. Its effectiveness therefore depends on benefit adequacy, accurate state systems, retailer access, civil rights, work-rule administration, and protection against fraud and theft.

Summary

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program is the federal food-purchasing benefit formerly known as the Food Stamp Program. Its permanent statutory foundation was enacted in 1964, nationwide operation was achieved in 1974, and the 1977 law eliminated the coupon purchase requirement.

SNAP is federally governed and financed but administered through state and territorial agencies. Eligible households receive monthly benefits through Electronic Benefits Transfer and use them at authorized retailers for food intended for household consumption.

Eligibility depends on household composition, income, deductions, resources where applicable, citizenship or eligible immigration status, student rules, and work requirements. Benefit amounts are based on the Thrifty Food Plan and reduced according to net household income.

The program served an average of 41.7 million people per month in fiscal year 2024 and functions as both a nutrition program and an automatic economic stabilizer.

Public Law 119-21 made major changes beginning in 2025, including broader ABAWD time-limit coverage, narrower noncitizen eligibility, reduced federal administrative reimbursement, future state benefit cost sharing, cost-neutral Thrifty Food Plan reevaluation, and the end of mandatory SNAP-Ed funding after fiscal year 2025.

As of August 2, 2026, SNAP remained operational while the Food and Nutrition Administration and state agencies implemented these changes.
