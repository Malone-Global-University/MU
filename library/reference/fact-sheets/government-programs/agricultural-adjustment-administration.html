Agricultural Adjustment Administration

The New Deal agricultural agency that administered production adjustment, benefit payments, commodity marketing, soil-conservation incentives, acreage allotments, price-support loans, and related farm programs from 1933 until its 1942 redesignation.

Terminology Note

AAA may refer either to the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, the federal agency, or to the Agricultural Adjustment Act, the statute authorising major parts of its work. This page concerns the agency and its operating programs.

The original Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 and the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 were separate statutes. The Supreme Court invalidated the original processing-tax and production-control plan in United States v. Butler in January 1936, but the Agricultural Adjustment Administration itself did not immediately disappear. It was reorganised under the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act and later administered the revised 1938 framework.

In February 1942, the Agricultural Adjustment Administration was redesignated the Agricultural Adjustment Agency and placed within the Agricultural Conservation and Adjustment Administration. This fact sheet principally covers the 1933–1942 administration.

Overview

The Agricultural Adjustment Administration was created during the first hundred days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency to address the collapse of the farm economy.

Agricultural prices had fallen more sharply than many of the costs farmers continued to pay. Heavy debt, mortgage foreclosure, weak export markets, accumulated surpluses, drought, and declining rural purchasing power threatened farm families, banks, merchants, processors, and local governments.

The AAA attempted to raise agricultural prices by reducing the quantity of selected commodities reaching the market. Participating producers signed contracts to remove land from production, reduce planted acreage, limit output, or comply with commodity-specific marketing plans.

Under the original 1933 system, taxes on processors financed rental and benefit payments to participating producers. The agency also negotiated marketing agreements, licensed handlers, supported commodity loans, purchased surpluses, and coordinated relief distribution.

The program represented a major expansion of federal authority over agriculture. Federal officials gathered production data, established national and local allotments, supervised contracts, audited compliance, calculated payments, and built a nationwide network of farmer committees.

Results varied sharply by commodity, region, land tenure, race, farm size, and access to local institutions. The system benefited many commercial farm operators but could impose serious costs on tenants, sharecroppers, agricultural labourers, consumers, and nonparticipating producers.

After the Supreme Court invalidated the original processing-tax and contract system in 1936, Congress preserved the policy objective through soil-conservation payments and later enacted a more comprehensive 1938 statute. The agency consequently evolved rather than ending in 1936.

Classification and Scope

This page treats the Agricultural Adjustment Administration as a government program because the primary subject is federal administration: producer contracts, processing taxes, commodity programs, payments, county committees, compliance, marketing agreements, price support, conservation, quotas, storage, tenant protection, and program outcomes.

The Agricultural Adjustment Acts of 1933 and 1938, Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act, Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act, Bankhead Cotton Control Act, Kerr–Smith Tobacco Control Act, Jones–Costigan Act, and related statutes belong primarily under Laws and Legislation.

The agency itself may also be cross-referenced under Institutions. The Great Depression, Dust Bowl, farm protest movements, Southern Tenant Farmers' Union, and United States v. Butler belong in their corresponding historical, organisational, legal, or social-movement collections.

This fact sheet distinguishes the AAA from the Farm Credit Administration, Commodity Credit Corporation, Resettlement Administration, Farm Security Administration, Soil Conservation Service, Federal Surplus Relief Corporation, and Federal Crop Insurance Corporation, although these institutions frequently worked with or succeeded parts of its program.

Historical Context

American agriculture entered crisis years before the general economic collapse of 1929. Wartime export demand and high prices had encouraged land purchases, borrowing, expansion, and production during World War I.

After the war, international demand weakened while production capacity remained high. Farm prices declined, debt burdens persisted, and rural income lagged behind urban and industrial income through much of the 1920s.

The Great Depression intensified the imbalance. USDA historical analysis found that realised net income of farm operators in 1932 was less than one-third of its 1929 level. Farm prices fell by more than half, while the prices of goods and services farmers purchased declined substantially less.

Farmers faced foreclosure, tax delinquency, forced sales, bank failure, falling land values, and inability to purchase machinery, clothing, medical care, household goods, or business supplies.

Farm organisations proposed export subsidies, price guarantees, production controls, cooperative marketing, cost-of-production formulas, and domestic allotments. Earlier federal efforts through the Federal Farm Board had failed to control output or sustain prices.

The Roosevelt administration concluded that recovery required coordinated federal action to reduce surpluses and restore the purchasing relationship between agriculture and industry.

Program Purpose

Raise Farm Prices

Reducing marketable surpluses was intended to prevent excessive supply from depressing commodity prices.

Restore Farm Purchasing Power

Higher prices and federal payments would enable farm households to purchase manufactured goods and services, supporting wider national recovery.

Stabilise Farm Income

Contracts, loans, storage, quotas, and payments were designed to reduce destructive price swings and provide more predictable income.

Coordinate National Production

The agency sought to align planted acreage and livestock output with domestic demand, exports, stocks, and expected consumption.

Regulate Commodity Marketing

Marketing agreements and orders addressed prices, classifications, handling practices, market shares, and unfair competition among processors and distributors.

Remove and Distribute Surpluses

Federal purchases and related institutions redirected selected surplus food toward relief, school, institutional, export, or other nonordinary markets.

Promote Soil-Conserving Land Use

After 1936, payments increasingly encouraged grasses, legumes, cover crops, contour practices, erosion control, and shifts away from soil-depleting crops.

Preserve an Agricultural Reserve

The 1938 framework sought to store commodities during abundant years and release them during scarcity through the “ever-normal granary” concept.

Parity and the Fair-Exchange Objective

The 1933 statute sought to restore the purchasing power of agricultural commodities to the relationship that had existed during the 1909–1914 base period.

Policymakers selected this period because they regarded it as a time of relative balance between farm prices and the prices farmers paid for household and production goods.

The term parity entered federal agricultural law formally in the 1938 act. A parity price was not simply the historical price of a commodity. It adjusted the base-period price according to changes in the prices farmers paid.

Parity became a central benchmark for price support, payments, political debate, and measurements of agricultural purchasing power.

The benchmark favoured a national economic relationship rather than guaranteeing that every farm, tenant, labourer, region, or commodity would receive equal income.

Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933

The Agricultural Adjustment Act authorised the secretary of agriculture to use multiple methods rather than one uniform commodity program.

Major powers included:

  • entering voluntary acreage- or production-reduction agreements with producers;
  • making rental and benefit payments;
  • levying and adjusting processing taxes;
  • negotiating marketing agreements with processors, handlers, and producer associations;
  • licensing processors and handlers to eliminate unfair practices or charges;
  • removing agricultural surpluses;
  • expanding markets;
  • collecting production, price, processing, and marketing information; and
  • terminating commodity programs when emergency conditions no longer required them.

The act also declared a consumer interest in avoiding an excessive increase in the share of retail food expenditures attributable to the agricultural program.

Agency Establishment and Leadership

The Agricultural Adjustment Administration was established in the Department of Agriculture when the statute became law on May 12, 1933.

Secretary Henry A. Wallace selected George N. Peek as the first administrator. Peek had long advocated federal action to restore farm purchasing power and was associated with proposals to manage agricultural surpluses and foreign trade.

Internal disagreements developed over exports, processing industries, the role of consumer interests, and the balance between farm organisations and departmental economists.

Chester C. Davis succeeded Peek and directed much of the agency's major 1934–1935 production-control and marketing work.

The agency combined agricultural economists, commodity specialists, attorneys, statisticians, accountants, field agents, extension personnel, information workers, and locally elected or selected producer committees.

Administrative Structure

Office of the Administrator

The administrator coordinated national policy, commodity programs, legal interpretations, payments, field operations, processor regulation, and relationships with the secretary of agriculture.

Commodity Divisions

Separate units designed and administered programs for cotton, wheat, corn and hogs, tobacco, dairy products, rice, sugar, peanuts, and other commodities.

Production Division

Production personnel calculated acreage bases, reduction goals, farm allotments, contract terms, compliance, and national supply estimates.

Marketing and Processing Divisions

These offices negotiated marketing agreements, licensed handlers, studied processing margins, regulated selected practices, and administered orders.

Legal Division

Attorneys drafted contracts and regulations, interpreted statutory authority, litigated disputes, reviewed marketing agreements, and advised on tenant and processor issues.

Comptroller and Finance Offices

Financial personnel administered processing-tax receipts, producer payments, audits, claims, refunds, and accounting.

Division of Information

The information service produced publications, films, radio programs, photographs, posters, press releases, instructions, and educational material.

Consumers' Counsel Division

The division represented consumer interests in commodity programs, processor regulation, retail prices, food marketing, and nutritional policy.

Field and Compliance Services

Federal and state personnel supervised contracts, measurements, reporting, committee operations, inspections, and payment eligibility.

State, County, and Community Administration

The AAA relied on a decentralised system because millions of farms required local acreage records, production histories, measurements, signatures, inspections, and payment calculations.

State agricultural extension services and land-grant colleges supplied county agents, office space, local knowledge, and existing relationships with farmers.

County and community committees helped determine farm bases, explain contracts, organise sign-up campaigns, verify compliance, allocate quotas, resolve disputes, and process payments.

The committee structure gave participating producers a role in administration and became an enduring feature of federal farm policy.

It also reinforced local power. Committee membership frequently favoured landowners and established commercial farmers. Tenants, sharecroppers, Black farmers, women, migrant workers, and poor farmers were underrepresented or excluded.

Local discretion could produce favouritism, inaccurate measurements, payment diversion, intimidation, racial discrimination, and inconsistent enforcement of tenant protections.

Processing Taxes

The original program financed many producer payments through excise taxes imposed when designated agricultural commodities first entered domestic processing.

A cotton processor, flour mill, meat packer, tobacco manufacturer, dairy handler, or other regulated enterprise could become responsible for the tax according to commodity-specific rules.

The secretary established tax rates with reference to the difference between current farm prices and the intended fair-exchange value, surplus conditions, and program requirements.

Congress appropriated the proceeds for adjustment payments, surplus removal, administration, market expansion, and related agricultural purposes.

Processors challenged the taxes in court, sought injunctions and refunds, and argued that the system transferred costs to consumers and exceeded federal constitutional power.

The processing tax became the central constitutional vulnerability of the original 1933 plan.

Producer Contracts and Benefit Payments

  1. Establish the farm base. Officials determined the farm's historical acreage, production, livestock, marketings, or another commodity measure.
  2. Set the reduction requirement. National officials established a percentage or quantity necessary to reduce supply.
  3. Offer a contract. The producer agreed to reduce acreage, output, or marketings for a stated period.
  4. Determine tenant interests. The contract could identify landlords, tenants, sharecroppers, and division of payments or production shares.
  5. Measure compliance. Local committees and field personnel inspected acreage, livestock, or marketings.
  6. Calculate the payment. Rental, benefit, parity, or other payments were based on the farm base, reduction, commodity formula, and compliance.
  7. Issue payment. Federal checks were distributed to eligible contract parties.
  8. Audit and resolve disputes. Officials addressed incorrect bases, overplanting, payment division, landlord–tenant complaints, processor issues, and contract violations.

Although the contracts were described as voluntary, the program increased economic pressure to participate because cooperating producers received payments and higher prices while noncooperators could benefit from reduced national output without accepting restrictions.

Cotton Adjustment Program

Cotton became the first large and highly visible AAA production-control program.

Cotton prices had fallen from twenty-nine cents per pound in 1923 to approximately 6.5 cents in 1932, while carryover stocks were exceptionally large.

On June 19, 1933, the administration announced a campaign to eliminate approximately ten million acres—about one-fourth of the growing cotton crop. Participating growers ploughed under between 25 and 50 percent of designated acreage in exchange for cash rental payments or cash combined with an option tied to government-held cotton.

The target was reached. Later contracts limited cotton acreage for the 1934 and 1935 crops and provided rental and parity payments based on historical acreage and production.

Commodity Credit Corporation loans allowed growers to hold cotton rather than sell immediately at depressed prices.

The Bankhead Cotton Control Act of 1934 supplemented voluntary contracts with taxes on cotton ginned beyond individual allotments after approval in a producer referendum.

Cotton adjustment raised prices and payments for many operators but reduced demand for tenant and wage labour and intensified disputes over who was entitled to federal benefits.

Wheat Adjustment Program

The wheat program used a domestic-allotment model rather than destroying the growing 1933 crop.

Contracting producers received adjustment payments on the portion of their historical production estimated to enter domestic consumption.

In return, they agreed to reduce wheat acreage for the 1934 and 1935 crops. USDA historical analysis identifies reductions of 15 percent for 1934 and 10 percent for 1935.

Drought reduced national wheat supplies and limited the acreage cuts that otherwise might have been required.

The program was supplemented by export assistance, international negotiations, surplus purchases for relief, and later Commodity Credit Corporation loans.

Corn–Hog Program

Corn and hog production were treated together because feed supplies strongly influenced hog breeding, marketings, and pork output.

In 1933, the agency and producer representatives developed an emergency program to remove young pigs and breeding sows from future market supply.

Federal purchases and slaughter generated public criticism because food animals were destroyed during widespread hunger. Some meat and processed products were directed to relief use, but processing and distribution capacity did not allow all products to be consumed.

Later corn–hog contracts paid participating farmers to reduce corn acreage and hog production according to historical bases.

Drought and feed shortages also reduced livestock supplies, making it difficult to isolate the program's effect from weather and broader market conditions.

Tobacco Adjustment Program

Tobacco programs combined acreage controls, quantity limits, producer contracts, marketing agreements, and commodity-specific base periods.

Several tobacco markets closed temporarily in 1933 while growers demanded higher prices and federal action.

Marketing agreements required selected processors and buyers to pay higher prices and purchase quantities comparable to previous market activity.

Contracts limited acreage or marketable quantity. Some planted tobacco was ploughed under during the first adjustment period.

The Kerr–Smith Tobacco Control Act of 1934 imposed taxes on tobacco marketed beyond quotas after producer approval, supplementing voluntary adjustment.

Milk and Dairy Programs

Milk posed a distinctive problem because it was perishable, produced continuously, sold through highly local markets, and divided among fluid and manufactured uses.

The agency used marketing agreements, licenses, classified pricing, pooling, handler regulation, and regional orders rather than relying principally on acreage reduction.

Dairy regulation sought to stabilise producer prices, distribute higher-value fluid-milk markets among producers, and regulate handlers who purchased, processed, or distributed milk.

The Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act of 1937 preserved and strengthened the legal basis for federal marketing orders after the original AAA decision.

Federal milk marketing orders became one of the most durable institutional legacies of the New Deal agricultural system.

Rice, Peanuts, Grain, and Other Commodities

Commodity programs differed according to crop biology, market structure, regional concentration, processing, storage, export dependence, and political organisation.

Rice programs used acreage adjustment, production data, marketing measures, and producer participation.

Peanuts, rye, flax, barley, grain sorghum, and cattle were added to the statutory list of basic commodities in 1934.

The Warren Potato Act briefly created a compulsory allotment-and-tax program for potatoes in 1935. Congress repealed it in February 1936 after the Supreme Court invalidated the broader AAA system.

Cattle policy shifted toward emergency purchase and drought response rather than an ordinary production-reduction contract when the 1934 drought caused severe distress.

Sugar Adjustment Program

The Jones–Costigan Act of 1934 added sugarcane and sugar beets to the adjustment system.

Sugar policy combined processing taxes, benefit payments, domestic and offshore quotas, acreage allotments, and market controls.

The program had to account for production in the continental United States, Hawai‘i, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, the Philippines, and imports from Cuba and other suppliers.

Sugar legislation contained labour standards not generally present in other original commodity programs. Participating growers were required to meet minimum-wage conditions, restrict child labour in sugar-beet fields, and avoid reducing the number of sharecroppers below the designated base.

These protections were important but depended on administration, recordkeeping, local enforcement, and the worker's ability to complain safely.

Surplus Removal and Relief Distribution

Production adjustment operated alongside federal efforts to purchase and redirect existing surpluses.

The Federal Surplus Relief Corporation and related agencies purchased commodities that could be distributed to unemployed and low-income people through relief institutions.

Purchases included wheat, flour, meat, dairy products, fruits, vegetables, and other goods depending on market conditions and program authority.

Surplus removal sought to support farm prices while improving nutrition. Transportation, refrigeration, processing, packaging, storage, and local distribution limited the volume that could be used effectively.

The combination of crop destruction and hunger became one of the most controversial images of the original AAA, even though the program also purchased food for relief.

Commodity Credit Corporation and Price-Support Loans

The Commodity Credit Corporation was established in October 1933 to support agricultural prices through loans and other commodity operations.

A nonrecourse loan allowed a producer to pledge an eligible commodity as collateral. If the market price rose, the producer could repay the loan and sell the commodity. If the price remained below the loan obligation, the producer could forfeit the collateral according to program rules.

Loans reduced pressure to sell immediately after harvest and helped establish a price floor.

The CCC initially supported cotton and later became central to wheat, corn, tobacco, and other commodity programs.

The AAA designed or administered many adjustment policies while the Commodity Credit Corporation supplied the financing institution for loans, purchases, and storage operations.

Consumers' Counsel Division

Frederick C. Howe was appointed to represent consumer interests within the Agricultural Adjustment Administration in June 1933.

The Consumers' Counsel Division studied processing, distribution, retail prices, marketing margins, nutrition, consumption, and the effects of agricultural regulation on households.

The division participated in hearings involving marketing agreements and licenses and challenged practices it regarded as excessive, monopolistic, or harmful to consumers.

Its existence reflected the conflict between raising farm prices and maintaining affordable food.

The division's influence was limited when producer, processor, and commodity-program priorities outweighed consumer concerns. It was transferred to the Surplus Marketing Administration in 1940 and later liquidated.

Tenant Farmers and Sharecroppers

The AAA's principal contractual relationship was often with the person recognised as the farm operator or landowner.

Cotton and other contracts contained provisions concerning the interests of tenants and sharecroppers, including payment division and continued occupancy. Administration depended heavily on information supplied by landowners and local committees.

Acreage reduction reduced the land and labour required to produce the crop. A landowner could respond by dismissing tenants, consolidating farms, converting cropland to other uses, adopting machinery, or retaining payments while reducing the tenant's share.

Tenants and sharecroppers frequently lacked written leases, legal representation, voting power, transportation, access to federal officials, or protection from eviction.

Complaints involved payment diversion, forged or coerced signatures, failure to identify tenants on contracts, eviction after contract approval, replacement of sharecroppers with wage labour, and denial of committee participation.

The 1938 act directed the secretary, “as far as practicable,” to protect tenant and sharecropper interests. The qualifying language and local enforcement structure limited the strength of this safeguard.

African American Farmers and Agricultural Workers

African American farmers were disproportionately concentrated among tenants, sharecroppers, farm labourers, and owners of small or heavily indebted farms.

Black owners could sign contracts and receive benefits, but unequal access to extension services, credit, committees, legal assistance, information, and political power limited participation.

Black tenants and labourers were especially vulnerable when landowners reduced cotton acreage. National Park Service historical material estimates that AAA acreage-reduction policy displaced approximately 200,000 African American owners, sharecroppers, and workers.

White-controlled county committees could exclude Black farmers from decision-making, establish unequal farm bases, delay applications, divert benefits, or fail to investigate landlord abuses.

Farm consolidation and mechanisation reinforced the displacement produced by acreage controls and contributed to rural-to-urban migration.

The program was not uniformly harmful. Higher commodity prices, direct payments, loans, conservation aid, and marketing support benefited some Black owners and operators. Outcomes depended strongly on land tenure and local administration.

Southern Tenant Farmers' Union

The Southern Tenant Farmers' Union formed in Arkansas in 1934 as an interracial organisation of Black and white tenants, sharecroppers, farm labourers, ministers, and supporters.

The union challenged eviction, payment diversion, low wages, plantation control, and the unequal administration of agricultural adjustment.

Organisers demanded that federal benefits reach the people who cultivated the land and that reduction programs not eliminate tenant livelihoods without protection or alternatives.

Members faced violence, arrest, intimidation, loss of housing, and economic retaliation.

The union's activism drew national attention to the difference between improving farm-owner income and protecting the full rural labour force.

Women and Farm Households

Farm women contributed labour to planting, harvesting, livestock, dairying, gardens, food preservation, poultry, household production, bookkeeping, and commodity marketing.

Federal contracts and payments generally recognised the male farm operator or titled landowner, obscuring women's economic contribution.

Women who owned or operated farms could participate, but local assumptions about household leadership and committee membership restricted representation.

Higher farm income could improve household food, clothing, education, healthcare, equipment, and housing. Reduced tenant employment or eviction could instead deepen insecurity for women and children.

Extension home-demonstration services helped households use gardens, food preservation, nutrition, sewing, and household management to survive the Depression, but these activities were distinct from the AAA's commodity-payment system.

Farm Size and Distribution of Benefits

Payment formulas based on historical acreage or production directed larger absolute payments to farms with larger established bases.

Large operators possessed greater administrative capacity, stronger relationships with county agents, better records, and more ability to shift acreage or wait for payments.

Small farmers benefited from higher prices and could receive payments, but modest acreage produced smaller checks.

Tenants and labourers who owned no commodity base could experience reduced work without receiving a proportional federal payment.

The program therefore addressed the commodity-price problem more effectively than the unequal distribution of land, credit, and bargaining power.

Drought, the Dust Bowl, and Program Interaction

Severe drought affected large parts of the United States during the first AAA years.

Weather reduced wheat, corn, forage, and livestock supplies, raising prices independently of planned production adjustment.

Emergency cattle purchases removed distressed animals, supplied payments to producers, and addressed the collapse of pasture and feed.

Drought complicated program evaluation because price increases reflected both federal policy and reduced natural output.

The Dust Bowl also strengthened support for tying farm payments to soil conservation, erosion control, cover crops, grass, and improved land use after 1936.

United States v. Butler

In United States v. Butler, decided January 6, 1936, the Supreme Court reviewed the processing tax and production-control system through litigation involving the Hoosac Mills Corporation.

The Court held that Congress possessed a broad power to tax and spend for the general welfare but concluded that the AAA used taxation and payments to regulate agricultural production, a subject the majority treated as reserved to the states.

The majority characterised the producer contract system as economic coercion and invalidated the interconnected tax, appropriation, and production-control plan.

The ruling disrupted processing-tax collections, payments, contracts, and related compulsory controls. Congress also repealed the potato program and certain commodity-control laws.

The decision did not prevent all federal agricultural spending or permanently end commodity regulation. Congress redesigned the legal foundation around conservation, federal spending, interstate commerce, marketing, loans, storage, quotas, and referenda.

Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Framework

Congress approved the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act on February 29, 1936, and Roosevelt signed it on March 1.

The law combined soil conservation, improved land use, and farm-income support.

Payments encouraged producers to shift acreage from soil-depleting crops to grasses, legumes, cover crops, pasture, and other conserving uses.

The AAA was reorganised under the new framework and worked through state programs and existing agricultural institutions.

The constitutional theory differed from the original system. Payments were connected to conservation and public expenditure rather than financed through a commodity-specific processing tax designed to compel production control.

The new system preserved acreage adjustment in practical effect while placing greater emphasis on conservation practices and income parity.

Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938

The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 created a more comprehensive and durable farm-policy framework.

Major features included:

  • continuation of soil-conservation and domestic-allotment payments;
  • national, state, county, and farm acreage allotments;
  • marketing quotas for selected commodities when supplies became excessive;
  • producer referenda before specified quotas became effective;
  • penalties for marketing beyond quotas;
  • price-support loans through the Commodity Credit Corporation;
  • storage and reserve policies associated with the ever-normal granary;
  • crop insurance for wheat through the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation;
  • local farmer committees for program administration;
  • provisions directing protection of tenants and sharecroppers as far as practicable; and
  • continued recognition of agricultural cooperatives and extension services.

The statute did not restore the original processing-tax contract system. It combined multiple constitutional and administrative tools developed after Butler.

Marketing Quotas and Producer Referenda

Marketing quotas limited the amount of a designated commodity that could be sold without penalty when national supplies exceeded statutory or administrative thresholds.

The secretary announced proposed quotas and producers voted in referenda. Commodity-specific approval requirements determined whether the quota became effective.

Farm quotas were connected to acreage allotments, production history, national requirements, and local allocation.

The referendum system supplied producer consent and helped answer objections that federal controls were imposed without agricultural participation.

Voting rules and local administration could favour established landowners and producers with recognised acreage or marketing bases.

Ever-Normal Granary

The ever-normal granary concept sought to prevent both ruinous surplus and dangerous scarcity.

During abundant years, price-support loans and storage would hold commodities away from immediate sale.

During short-crop years or emergency demand, stored reserves could return to the market.

The approach connected production adjustment with commodity credit, storage, crop insurance, and national food security.

Storage carried costs and risks, including spoilage, quality decline, administrative expense, market distortion, and government accumulation of large stocks.

Farm Income and Price Effects

USDA historical analysis reports that farm income in 1935 was more than 50 percent above the 1932 level, due in part to federal agricultural programs.

Adjustment payments directly increased participating producers' cash income.

Reduced output, drought, commodity loans, currency changes, export conditions, general recovery, and market expectations also affected prices.

The contribution of the AAA differed by commodity. Cotton, wheat, hogs, tobacco, dairy, sugar, and rice operated through different market structures and program tools.

Aggregate gains do not establish that all rural groups benefited. Owners, tenants, labourers, consumers, processors, regions, and commodities experienced different results.

Consumer Effects

Raising agricultural prices could increase food and clothing costs, especially for low-income households.

Processors could absorb, shift, or share processing-tax costs depending on market conditions.

Supporters argued that national recovery required viable farm income and that agricultural collapse harmed consumers through unemployment, bank failure, and declining demand.

Relief distribution, surplus purchases, school and institutional food, and consumer oversight attempted to offset some adverse effects.

The program's consumer impact cannot be reduced to one national price effect because commodity supplies, processing concentration, retail markets, imports, and household diets differed.

Program Achievements

  • It established a national administrative system for agricultural production and commodity adjustment.
  • It delivered direct payments to participating producers during severe economic crisis.
  • It contributed to recovery in farm prices and purchasing power.
  • It developed acreage bases, farm records, compliance systems, and local producer committees.
  • It created durable federal commodity marketing agreements and orders.
  • It supported development of Commodity Credit Corporation loans and price floors.
  • It connected production policy to soil conservation after 1936.
  • It established producer referenda and marketing quotas under the 1938 framework.
  • It supported surplus purchase and relief distribution.
  • It created the Consumers' Counsel Division to represent household and market interests.
  • It developed early labour protections in the sugar program.
  • It established the institutional foundation of later agricultural stabilisation, conservation, and farm-support programs.

Limitations and Failures

  • The original processing-tax and contract system was held unconstitutional.
  • Destroying crops and livestock during widespread hunger damaged public legitimacy.
  • Drought and general recovery make the agency's independent economic effect difficult to isolate.
  • Payments based on historical acreage favoured larger established producers in absolute terms.
  • Landowners frequently controlled contracts and local administration.
  • Tenant and sharecropper protections were weak, ambiguous, or poorly enforced.
  • Acreage reduction displaced tenants, sharecroppers, and agricultural labourers.
  • African American farmers encountered racially discriminatory committees, extension systems, credit, and legal institutions.
  • Local committees underrepresented poor farmers, women, tenants, and labourers.
  • Higher farm prices could raise costs for consumers.
  • Marketing and processor regulation generated complex legal disputes and administrative burdens.
  • Production controls could reward historical output and create barriers for new farmers.
  • Government loans and storage could accumulate expensive surpluses.
  • The program stabilised commodity agriculture without resolving unequal land ownership or rural poverty.

Major Controversies

Destruction during Hunger

Cotton plough-up and livestock reduction were criticised as morally indefensible while millions required food, clothing, and relief.

Constitutional Power

Opponents argued that agriculture was local production reserved to the states. Supporters argued that national prices, processing, interstate markets, and economic emergency required federal coordination.

Processor Taxes

Processors challenged the tax as unconstitutional, economically burdensome, and transferable to consumers.

Large Farms versus Small Farms

Payment formulas and committee systems appeared to favour large operators and established production bases.

Tenant Displacement

Landlords could obtain federal benefits while eliminating tenants or reducing their share of farm income.

Racial Administration

White-controlled Southern institutions administered programs affecting a large Black tenant and labour population with limited federal civil-rights enforcement.

Consumer Prices

The policy objective of raising farm prices conflicted with the need to make food and clothing affordable.

Voluntary versus Coercive Control

Contracts were formally voluntary, but payments, quotas, taxes, market access, and national coordination created strong pressure to participate.

Historical Significance

The AAA transformed agricultural policy from occasional emergency assistance into a permanent federal system concerned with prices, income, production, conservation, marketing, credit, storage, and risk.

The county-committee system created a durable partnership between federal agencies and participating producers.

The program established policy tools that continued through later agencies: acreage allotments, conservation payments, marketing quotas, commodity loans, marketing orders, surplus removal, crop insurance, and price-support benchmarks.

The agency also revealed the unequal effects of treating the farm operator as the principal policy unit in a rural economy built partly on tenancy, sharecropping, wage labour, and racial hierarchy.

United States v. Butler became a major constitutional decision involving federal spending, coercion, agricultural regulation, and the relationship between national and state power.

Later agricultural legislation used revised constitutional mechanisms and survived, allowing New Deal farm policy to become a long-term component of the federal government.

1942 Redesignation and Wartime Transition

Executive Order 9069 of February 23, 1942, redesignated the Agricultural Adjustment Administration as the Agricultural Adjustment Agency.

The order consolidated the agency with the Sugar Agency, Federal Crop Insurance Corporation, and Soil Conservation Service in the Agricultural Conservation and Adjustment Administration.

Later 1942 and 1943 executive orders moved the agency through wartime food-production and distribution structures.

Wartime policy shifted from reducing surpluses toward increasing strategic food and fibre production, managing prices, allocating supplies, and supporting military and civilian demand.

The agency's functions later passed through the War Food Administration, Production and Marketing Administration, Commodity Stabilization Service, Agricultural Stabilization and Conservation Service, and modern Farm Service Agency lineage.

Records and Research Value

Principal records are maintained by the National Archives in Record Group 145, Records of the Farm Service Agency and predecessor agricultural adjustment institutions.

Records include:

  • central and subject correspondence;
  • administrator and commodity-division files;
  • circular letters, regulations, instructions, and forms;
  • contract, docket, compliance, and payment records;
  • processing-tax, refund, and legal files;
  • marketing agreements, licenses, and orders;
  • producer committee and county administrative records;
  • tenant and landlord complaint files;
  • commodity production, acreage, price, and marketing reports;
  • Consumers' Counsel correspondence and studies;
  • Commodity Credit Corporation loan and financial records;
  • press releases, speeches, radio material, and publications;
  • photographs of farming, soil conservation, commodities, and publicity activity;
  • motion pictures and filmstrips;
  • records concerning government assistance to farmers;
  • state and county committee records retained in regional repositories; and
  • successor-agency files concerning conservation, stabilisation, quotas, loans, and marketing.

Related materials appear in Record Group 16, Records of the Office of the Secretary of Agriculture; Record Group 114, Records of the Soil Conservation Service; Record Group 83, Records of the Bureau of Agricultural Economics; Record Group 96, Records of the Farmers Home Administration; and records of the Commodity Credit Corporation, Farm Security Administration, land-grant colleges, extension services, and local agricultural committees.

Genealogical and Local-History Value

Farm and county records may identify landowners, operators, tenants, sharecroppers, acreage, crops, livestock, contract numbers, payments, signatures, committee members, disputes, and compliance findings.

Agricultural census schedules, county tax records, deeds, leases, crop-lien records, farm ledgers, extension reports, newspapers, and court files can help interpret AAA documentation.

Researchers should search under the landowner, farm operator, county, commodity, plantation or farm name, and local committee because tenants may not appear in national indexes.

Displacement, migration, and eviction may be documented indirectly through relief records, Farm Security Administration photographs, union records, school enrollment, city directories, and later oral histories.

Research Cautions

  • AAA may mean the agency or the act. Specify Agricultural Adjustment Administration, Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, or Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938.
  • The agency did not end automatically in January 1936. The original tax-and-contract plan was invalidated, but the administration was reorganised and continued.
  • Parity terminology changed. The 1933 program pursued the 1909–1914 purchasing-power relationship, while the 1938 law formally used the parity concept.
  • Commodity programs were not uniform. Cotton, wheat, hogs, tobacco, dairy, rice, sugar, and other commodities used different contracts, taxes, loans, quotas, and marketing rules.
  • Payments do not equal net benefit. Debt, rent, lost acreage, labour displacement, higher costs, and payment distribution affected the final household result.
  • Farm operator did not always mean cultivator. Records may centre the landlord or principal operator while omitting tenants, family workers, and wage labourers.
  • Local administration matters. County committee composition, race, tenure, landholding, and political relationships could determine actual access.
  • Price recovery had multiple causes. Drought, currency policy, broader recovery, exports, loans, and changing supply interacted with AAA policy.
  • Project and payment totals vary. Sources may count contracts, farms, producers, checks, acreage, commodities, program years, or successor programs differently.
  • Period records use discriminatory terminology. Archival classifications should be interpreted critically and not repeated without context.

Definitions

Acreage allotment
The amount of land assigned to a state, county, farm, or producer for planting a designated commodity under an adjustment program.
Adjustment payment
Federal compensation paid to a cooperating producer under acreage, production, conservation, parity, or commodity program rules.
Agricultural Adjustment Administration
The USDA agency established in 1933 to administer farm production, income, marketing, conservation, loan, and commodity-adjustment programs.
Basic commodity
A commodity designated by statute for specified adjustment, tax, loan, quota, marketing, or price-support provisions.
Benefit payment
A payment to a producer for participation in a production- or acreage-adjustment program.
Commodity loan
A loan secured by an eligible agricultural commodity and used to support prices and delay sale.
Compliance
Satisfaction of acreage, production, conservation, marketing, reporting, or other requirements necessary to receive program benefits.
Domestic allotment
A program that connected producer payments to the estimated portion of national production consumed within the domestic market.
Ever-normal granary
A reserve policy under which commodities are stored during abundant years and released during scarcity.
Marketing agreement
An agreement between the secretary of agriculture and commodity handlers or producers establishing market practices, prices, quantities, classifications, or other conditions.
Marketing order
A binding federal regulation governing handling of a commodity within a defined market after statutory procedures and producer or handler approval.
Marketing quota
A limit on the quantity of a commodity that may be marketed without penalty under an approved federal program.
Nonrecourse loan
A commodity-secured loan that may be satisfied through repayment or forfeiture of the pledged commodity under program rules.
Parity
A statutory measure intended to preserve the purchasing-power relationship between farm commodities and the goods and services farmers buy.
Processing tax
An excise imposed under the original 1933 act when a designated commodity entered its first domestic processing stage.
Producer referendum
A vote in which eligible commodity producers approve or reject specified quotas or program controls.
Rental payment
Compensation for removing designated acreage from production under an adjustment contract.
Sharecropper
A cultivator who farms another person's land in exchange for a share of the crop, often receiving housing, supplies, and credit from the landowner.
Tenant farmer
A farmer who rents land under cash, fixed-rent, share-rent, or other arrangements rather than owning it.

Summary

The Agricultural Adjustment Administration was the New Deal agency responsible for restoring farm income through production adjustment, benefit payments, commodity marketing, conservation, price-support loans, acreage allotments, quotas, storage, and related programs.

Under the 1933 Agricultural Adjustment Act, participating producers reduced acreage or output in exchange for payments financed largely through processing taxes. Commodity programs included cotton, wheat, corn and hogs, tobacco, dairy, rice, sugar, and other crops and livestock.

The agency built a nationwide administrative network of USDA commodity divisions, extension services, county agents, and producer committees. This system delivered payments and coordinated national production but also concentrated power among established landowners.

Many farm operators benefited from higher prices and federal payments. Tenants, sharecroppers, labourers, and small farmers could receive fewer benefits or lose work and housing when acreage was reduced. African American rural workers were disproportionately harmed by discriminatory local administration, weak tenant protections, farm consolidation, and displacement.

In January 1936, the Supreme Court invalidated the original processing-tax and production-control plan in United States v. Butler. Congress reorganised agricultural adjustment around soil conservation and enacted a revised 1938 system using conservation payments, acreage allotments, marketing quotas, producer referenda, commodity loans, storage, and crop insurance.

The agency continued until 1942, when it became the Agricultural Adjustment Agency within a wartime agricultural structure. Its policy tools and local committee system became enduring foundations of federal farm programs.
