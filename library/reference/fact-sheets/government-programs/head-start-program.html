Head Start Program

The federally funded early-childhood program established during the War on Poverty to promote school readiness by combining education, health, nutrition, disability, social, and family services with formal parent participation and locally administered community programs.

What Is the Head Start Program?

Head Start is a federal program that promotes the school readiness of young children from families with low income through comprehensive education, health, nutrition, disability, mental-health, social, and family-engagement services.

Head Start is not one federally operated school system. The federal government awards grants directly to eligible local organizations, which operate programs under the Head Start Act, federal grant conditions, monitoring requirements, and the Head Start Program Performance Standards.

Head Start Preschool principally serves children ages three to compulsory school age. Early Head Start serves pregnant women, infants, toddlers, and their families. Specialized branches serve tribal communities and migrant and seasonal farmworker families.

The program differs from a classroom-only preschool because education is one part of a broader child-and-family system. Programs screen and support health, development, hearing, vision, oral health, nutrition, disabilities, family goals, and transitions to school.

Scope of This Fact Sheet

This fact sheet explains Head Start’s origins, legal development, program branches, eligibility, comprehensive services, governance, funding, monitoring, research findings, controversies, and continuing institutional role.

It is not a substitute for current federal guidance concerning enrollment, grant operation, staffing, or compliance. Eligibility, income guidelines, funding, and performance requirements can change through law, regulation, appropriations, and administrative action.

Early Head Start, American Indian and Alaska Native Head Start, Migrant and Seasonal Head Start, and Early Head Start–Child Care Partnerships are treated as branches of the larger system but may require separate fact sheets.

Geographic Scope

Head Start operates in all fifty states, the District of Columbia, and United States territories through local grants and specialized national or regional structures.

Programs serve urban, suburban, rural, frontier, tribal, and agricultural communities. Local design reflects population density, transportation, family work schedules, housing, language, culture, public schools, health-provider availability, and child-care markets.

American Indian and Alaska Native programs are operated by tribal governments, tribal colleges, tribal agencies, or other eligible organizations. Migrant and Seasonal Head Start adapts to families whose agricultural work and residence may cross ordinary service boundaries.

Historical Context

Head Start emerged during the War on Poverty, when federal policymakers treated childhood poverty as a condition involving education, health, nutrition, housing, family resources, discrimination, and unequal access to institutions.

Many children entered elementary school after experiencing inadequate medical or dental care, food insecurity, limited access to preschool, developmental risks, segregated services, and schools with unequal resources.

Educators, physicians, psychologists, social workers, civil-rights advocates, community organizers, and parents argued that intervention before elementary school could improve development and reduce later inequality.

Head Start’s designers therefore adopted a comprehensive model involving education, health, nutrition, social services, and parent participation rather than a narrow classroom-only program.

Origins in the War on Poverty

The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 created OEO and Community Action Program authority. Although the original law did not name Head Start, its demonstration and community-action authority allowed OEO to develop the initiative.

OEO assembled an expert panel led by pediatrician Robert E. Cooke. The group included specialists in medicine, education, psychology, social work, child development, and public administration.

The Cooke committee recommended a comprehensive program addressing physical health, mental health, nutrition, education, family needs, and community participation.

President Lyndon B. Johnson announced Project Head Start in 1965 as a large summer demonstration. Public demand and local participation quickly turned it into a continuing program.

The 1965 Launch

Project Head Start was publicly launched on May 18, 1965. Local communities organized classrooms, health screenings, meals, transportation, parent activities, and social services under compressed timelines.

Schools, churches, Community Action Agencies, universities, nonprofit organizations, public agencies, and volunteers supplied facilities and staff.

Rapid implementation produced uneven quality, but the demonstration established broad demand and created a national constituency among parents, educators, health professionals, community organizations, and civil-rights groups.

Statutory Purpose

The modern Head Start Act defines the program’s purpose as promoting the school readiness of children with low income by enhancing cognitive, social, and emotional development.

The statute connects readiness with language, literacy, mathematics, science, social-emotional functioning, creative arts, physical skills, approaches to learning, comprehensive services, parent engagement, community partnerships, and coordination with later schooling.

Federal Administration

Head Start is administered by the Office of Head Start within the Administration for Children and Families, United States Department of Health and Human Services.

The Office of Head Start awards grants, issues regulations and guidance, conducts monitoring, operates the Designation Renewal System, supports training and technical assistance, collects data, coordinates research, and enforces program requirements.

Federal administration establishes national requirements, but local grant recipients remain responsible for daily operations.

Transfer from OEO

Head Start began within OEO because it was a War on Poverty demonstration frequently operated through Community Action Agencies.

In 1969, the Nixon administration transferred the program to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare and placed it within the Office of Child Development.

Supporters argued that Head Start required a permanent child-development home. Critics feared that transfer would weaken parent participation and its broader antipoverty mission.

Many Community Action Agencies nevertheless continued as local Head Start grant recipients.

Head Start Preschool

Head Start Preschool is the original and largest branch. It principally serves children ages three and four before elementary school.

Preschool services combine structured learning with health, nutrition, disability, mental-health, family, and transition supports.

Programs establish school-readiness goals, select curricula aligned with federal learning domains, and use ongoing observation to individualize instruction.

Early Head Start

Congress created Early Head Start in 1994 to serve pregnant women, infants, toddlers younger than age three, and their families.

Early Head Start emphasizes prenatal health, infant and toddler development, responsive caregiving, secure relationships, family well-being, early intervention, and continuity of services.

Services may be provided in centers, family child-care homes, family homes, or approved combinations of settings.

American Indian and Alaska Native Head Start

American Indian and Alaska Native Head Start programs serve tribal communities through tribal governments, tribal agencies, tribal colleges, and other eligible organizations.

Programs may incorporate tribal languages, cultural practices, community knowledge, and locally defined approaches to child development.

Administration must respect tribal sovereignty and address remote geography, transportation, housing, health access, workforce recruitment, and language preservation.

Migrant and Seasonal Head Start

Migrant and Seasonal Head Start serves children and families whose livelihoods depend on agricultural work.

Migrant programs may follow families across regions and operate schedules corresponding to planting and harvesting. Seasonal programs serve agricultural families who remain within a community.

Programs address continuity of education and health records, transportation, housing instability, language access, occupational risks, mobility, and irregular work hours.

Early Head Start–Child Care Partnerships

Early Head Start–Child Care Partnerships expand high-quality infant-and-toddler services through collaboration with existing child-care providers.

Grants can support staffing, professional development, facilities, supplies, family services, health services, and program quality.

Participating providers must meet applicable Head Start requirements while coordinating with licensing and child-care subsidy systems.

The Comprehensive-Service Model

Head Start’s central institutional feature is the integration of multiple services around the child and family.

The model recognizes that readiness can be affected by physical health, hearing and vision, oral health, nutrition, mental health, development, disability access, language, housing, transportation, parent employment, family stress, community safety, and teacher-child interaction.

Programs therefore coordinate services that ordinary preschool classrooms may not provide directly.

Early Learning and Development

Head Start provides developmentally appropriate learning experiences supporting approaches to learning, social-emotional development, language, literacy, mathematics, scientific reasoning, physical development, and cognition.

Teachers use play, conversation, routines, group activities, books, movement, creative arts, and exploration.

Federal standards do not prescribe one national curriculum. Local recipients select curricula and assessment systems satisfying federal requirements and local needs.

School Readiness

Modern policy gives explicit priority to school readiness while preserving comprehensive services.

Programs establish measurable goals aligned with the Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework and, where appropriate, state early-learning standards.

Readiness includes social regulation, communication, curiosity, physical development, relationships, approaches to learning, and participation in classroom routines—not only letter and number recognition.

Language and Dual-Language Learners

Head Start serves families speaking many languages. Programs must communicate accessibly and support children who are dual-language learners.

Effective practice may include home-language support, planned English acquisition, bilingual staff, interpreters, culturally responsive materials, appropriate assessment, and coordination with receiving schools.

Language difference should not be confused with disability or developmental delay.

Health Services

Programs work with families to determine whether children have accessible health care and are current on preventive and primary services.

Services include health-history review, screening, hearing and vision services, immunization review, medical and dental referrals, treatment follow-up, medication planning, health education, and coordination with Medicaid, CHIP, clinics, and private providers.

Head Start is not a substitute for a medical home. Its role is to identify needs, reduce barriers, coordinate care, and support families in using health systems.

Mental Health and Social-Emotional Support

Head Start treats mental health as part of child development, family well-being, and program quality.

Programs use consultation, staff support, classroom strategies, family partnerships, screening, referral, and community coordination.

Services may address trauma, stress, challenging behavior, family crisis, caregiver mental health, and staff well-being.

Nutrition Services

Programs provide meals and snacks designed to meet children’s nutritional needs and comply with applicable federal requirements.

Nutrition services include age-appropriate food, accommodation of medically necessary diets, family education, healthy feeding practices, growth review, and connection with food-assistance programs.

Shared meals also support language, social development, self-help skills, culture, and classroom routine.

Children with Disabilities

Head Start has a longstanding responsibility to include children with disabilities and developmental delays.

Programs coordinate with agencies responsible for Part C and Section 619 of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act.

Federal requirements generally expect at least ten percent of actual enrollment to consist of children determined eligible for IDEA services, subject to applicable procedures and waivers.

Programs support screening, referral, evaluation coordination, accessible environments, individualized services, family advocacy, and participation in ordinary activities.

Family Engagement

Head Start treats parents and caregivers as partners in child development rather than passive recipients.

Programs work with families concerning child-development goals, health, disability services, parent education and employment, housing, transportation, financial stability, mental health, leadership, and transitions to kindergarten.

Family partnerships should be individualized and voluntary rather than based on a uniform assumption that every family has the same needs.

Parent Governance

Parent governance is one of Head Start’s defining features.

Every Head Start agency must establish a Policy Council, and delegate agencies generally establish policy committees. Center-based programs also establish parent committees.

Parents of currently enrolled children constitute a majority of the Policy Council and are elected by enrolled parents.

Governance is shared with the grant recipient’s governing body or tribal council, which retains legal and fiscal responsibility.

Policy Council

The Policy Council is the formal parent-led policy body for a Head Start grant recipient.

Membership includes a majority of parents of currently enrolled children and community representatives. Each program option must be proportionately represented.

The council participates in decisions concerning program direction, selection priorities, funding applications, personnel policies, and other matters established by law and regulation.

Governing Body and Tribal Council

The governing body possesses legal and fiscal responsibility for the grant recipient. In tribal programs, an appropriate tribal governing authority performs this function.

Responsibilities include financial management, audit review, safeguarding funds, senior leadership, compliance, risk management, and shared decision-making with the Policy Council.

Head Start governance differs from the tripartite-board model of Community Action Agencies, even when a Community Action Agency is the grant recipient.

Eligibility

Major eligibility pathways include family income below federal poverty guidelines, receipt of specified public assistance, homelessness, foster-care status, and other statutory categories or allowances.

Pregnant women may qualify for Early Head Start. Children with disabilities may be enrolled through selection and disability-reservation requirements, including some children whose family income exceeds the ordinary threshold.

Eligibility does not guarantee placement. Local funded capacity is limited, and programs use approved selection criteria to prioritize applicants.

Enrollment Above the Poverty Guidelines

Federal law permits limited enrollment above the ordinary income threshold.

Programs may generally enroll up to ten percent of participants from families above the poverty guidelines.

Under specified conditions, programs may enroll an additional group of children from families with incomes between the poverty line and 130 percent of the poverty line after meeting statutory safeguards.

Recruitment, Selection, Enrollment, and Attendance

Programs must actively recruit eligible children and pregnant women in their service areas.

Selection criteria may consider income, public assistance, homelessness, foster care, age, disability, family circumstances, local need, previous access to early education, and approved community priorities.

Programs monitor vacancies and attendance. Chronic absence may indicate transportation, health, housing, family-employment, or program-quality barriers.

Transitions

Head Start supports transitions between home, Early Head Start, Head Start Preschool, child care, special education, and kindergarten.

Activities include record sharing with consent, school visits, parent information, disability-service coordination, alignment of learning goals, and support for enrollment, attendance, and transportation.

Transition planning matters because early gains can be weakened when receiving schools lack continuity or comparable support.

Community Assessment

Grant recipients assess eligible populations, poverty, disability prevalence, languages, homelessness, foster care, health resources, child-care supply, schools, transportation, employment schedules, community strengths, and service gaps.

Findings inform program options, locations, hours, recruitment, partnerships, staffing, and conversion of slots between Head Start and Early Head Start.

Community Partnerships

Programs coordinate with schools, early-intervention agencies, health and dental providers, mental-health professionals, Medicaid and CHIP, child-care providers, housing organizations, food programs, workforce agencies, libraries, tribal institutions, and Community Action Agencies.

Partnerships expand services but require clear responsibility, consent, record protection, referral follow-up, and accountability.

Grant Recipients and Delegate Agencies

The Office of Head Start awards most program funds directly to local grant recipients rather than operating centers itself.

Recipients may include Community Action Agencies, school districts, municipal or county agencies, nonprofit organizations, tribal governments, universities, faith-based organizations, and other eligible entities.

A grant recipient may operate services directly or use delegate agencies. The grant recipient remains accountable for delegate performance.

Funding

Congress appropriates Head Start funding annually. Federal awards support local operations, training and technical assistance, monitoring, research, and administration.

In fiscal year 2024, federal program facts reported approximately $12.27 billion in total resources, including about $11.86 billion for Head Start and Early Head Start operations.

Grant recipients generally provide a nonfederal share unless waived. Contributions may include cash, facilities, volunteer services, or donated goods.

Funding supports a defined number of slots and does not automatically serve every eligible child.

Program Workforce

Head Start depends on teachers, assistant teachers, home visitors, family-service workers, health staff, nutrition workers, mental-health consultants, disability coordinators, drivers, managers, and administrative personnel.

Programs face recurring challenges involving compensation, turnover, qualifications, bilingual recruitment, rural staffing, professional development, stress, and competition with schools and child-care systems.

Workforce quality is central because program effectiveness depends heavily on stable, skilled relationships with children and families.

Federal Monitoring

The Office of Head Start monitors grant recipients through Focus Area reviews, risk assessments, classroom observations, audits, enrollment data, incident reporting, Program Information Reports, and follow-up concerning deficiencies.

Monitoring can lead to corrective action, technical assistance, suspension, termination, or competition under the Designation Renewal System.

Federal review covers far more than classrooms: governance, fiscal management, health and safety, enrollment, staffing, comprehensive services, and organizational systems are also examined.

Designation Renewal System

The 2007 reauthorization required a system under which grants would not continue indefinitely without periodic quality review.

Grant recipients generally receive five-year project periods. Programs meeting applicable conditions may be renewed; programs triggering specified conditions must compete.

Determinations can consider deficiencies, audit findings, license status, disqualification, classroom-interaction measures, school-readiness requirements, and material noncompliance.

Research and Evaluation

Head Start has been studied through the Head Start Impact Study, Early Head Start Research and Evaluation Project, Head Start Family and Child Experiences Survey, Baby FACES, administrative data, demonstrations, and longitudinal research.

Findings vary by cohort, branch, service model, comparison condition, program quality, outcome, and follow-up period.

Administrative data describe participants and operations but do not alone establish causal impact.

Head Start Impact Study

The national Head Start Impact Study used random assignment to compare children offered Head Start access with children not offered a place through the study.

It found improved preschool experiences and initial positive effects in selected cognitive, health, and parent-reported outcomes.

By the end of third grade, few statistically detectable differences remained between the original groups.

Interpretation is complicated because many control-group children received other preschool services and some children offered Head Start did not participate. The study evaluated real-world access, not Head Start versus no early education.

Early Head Start Research Findings

Federal research found positive effects in selected areas of child cognitive, language, and social-emotional development, parenting, home environment, and service use.

Effects varied by implementation quality, service model, family characteristics, and participation.

Findings reinforced the importance of intensity, stable relationships, family support, and high-quality implementation.

The Fade-Out Debate

“Fade-out” refers to a reduction over time in measured differences between program and comparison groups.

Explanations include later receipt of similar services by comparison children, elementary schools failing to build upon gains, narrow assessment instruments, program-quality variation, family and neighborhood conditions, and benefits appearing outside test scores.

Fade-out of one academic measure does not prove that health, disability-identification, family, attendance, or other benefits never occurred. It does require caution in claiming permanent academic effects.

Major Achievements

  • Created a permanent national early-childhood program for families with low income.
  • Established comprehensive services as part of school-readiness policy.
  • Integrated education with health, nutrition, mental health, oral health, disability, and family support.
  • Created formal parent governance through Policy Councils and parent committees.
  • Expanded preschool access in communities with limited early-childhood infrastructure.
  • Created Early Head Start for pregnancy, infancy, and toddlerhood.
  • Supported tribal and culturally responsive early-childhood programs.
  • Developed specialized services for migrant and seasonal farmworker families.
  • Helped identify children needing early-intervention or special-education services.
  • Generated extensive national data and research.
  • Created a direct federal-local grant system surviving multiple administrative reorganizations.
  • Served more than forty million children and families by the middle of the 2020s, according to federal reporting.

Limitations and Continuing Challenges

  • Federal funding does not provide a place for every eligible child.
  • Availability and program quality vary geographically.
  • Staff compensation and turnover threaten continuity.
  • Facilities and transportation limit enrollment in some communities.
  • Part-day or school-year schedules may not meet every family’s work needs.
  • Programs must coordinate many systems with separate rules.
  • Specialists may be unavailable locally.
  • Monitoring and compliance create substantial administrative burdens.
  • Research findings vary across outcomes and follow-up periods.
  • Early gains may not be sustained by under-resourced elementary schools.
  • Housing instability, illness, transportation, and employment can disrupt attendance.
  • Annual appropriations and expired authorization periods create uncertainty.

Head Start cannot independently eliminate childhood poverty, unequal schools, housing instability, health inequity, or family income insecurity.

Civil Rights and Equal Access

Head Start was established during the Civil Rights Era and became important in communities excluded from segregated schools, medical systems, and local government.

Programs receiving federal assistance are subject to nondiscrimination law. Language access, disability accommodation, transportation, geographic distribution, and culturally responsive programming are practical components of equal access.

The program also expanded employment and leadership opportunities for African American, Latino, Native American, and low-income parents and professionals.

African American Communities

Head Start held particular importance in African American communities affected by segregated schools, discriminatory health systems, unequal employment, poverty, and exclusion from local political institutions.

Community Action Agencies, churches, civil-rights organizations, schools, and neighborhood groups helped establish local programs.

Head Start created positions for Black teachers, aides, health workers, administrators, parents, and community leaders while providing formal parent authority through governance.

Tribal Sovereignty and Cultural Continuity

American Indian and Alaska Native Head Start operates within the legal and political context of tribal sovereignty.

Tribal programs may use Head Start resources to strengthen language preservation, cultural identity, community governance, and locally defined educational practice.

Federal monitoring must be applied with consultation and attention to tribal law, culture, geography, and government-to-government relationships.

Homelessness, Foster Care, and Child Welfare

Children experiencing homelessness and children in foster care possess statutory eligibility pathways that do not depend on ordinary household-income calculation in the same manner as other applicants.

Programs coordinate with shelters, schools, child-welfare agencies, foster parents, kinship caregivers, and service organizations.

Head Start can provide developmental continuity during housing instability, placement changes, trauma, transportation problems, and missing records.

Relationship to Child Care and State Prekindergarten

Head Start is part of a larger early-care system including private child care, child-care subsidies, state prekindergarten, public schools, home visiting, and special education.

It differs from child-care subsidy programs because it directly funds grant recipients to provide a comprehensive federally regulated program.

State prekindergarten varies in eligibility, schedule, standards, funding, and service scope and does not necessarily include Head Start’s health, family, governance, and social-service components.

Relationship to Community Action

Head Start developed under OEO’s Community Action authority and originally functioned as a major community-action component.

Community Action Agencies frequently became Head Start grant recipients because they possessed local boards, neighborhood networks, grant capacity, and multiservice programs.

After transfer to Health, Education, and Welfare, Head Start acquired a distinct federal administration and legal framework. It is not merely a subdivision of the modern Community Services Block Grant.

Records and Research Sources

Records are distributed across OEO and HHS holdings, Office of Head Start grant files, Program Information Reports, monitoring reports, congressional materials, Community Action Agency archives, Policy Council minutes, community assessments, research datasets, oral histories, and tribal or school-district records.

Child and family records are protected by privacy requirements and are not generally public historical records.

How Head Start Should Be Evaluated

Evaluation should identify the branch, cohort, service model, locality, duration, comparison services, and outcome under review.

  • Was the study evaluating access, attendance, dosage, or completion?
  • What services did comparison children receive?
  • Was program quality measured?
  • Were outcomes cognitive, social, health, family, disability, or economic?
  • How long after participation were outcomes measured?
  • Did later schools sustain early gains?
  • Did attrition or selection affect findings?
  • Did the study distinguish Head Start Preschool from Early Head Start?

Why the Head Start Program Matters

Head Start transformed early-childhood policy by making comprehensive development, rather than custodial care or classroom instruction alone, the basis of a national program.

It established the principle that health, nutrition, disability services, mental health, family conditions, and parent leadership affect readiness for school.

Its governance structure gives enrolled parents formal decision-making authority alongside the organization legally responsible for federal funds.

The program survived OEO’s dissolution, changes in administration, debates over effectiveness, and the growth of state preschool and child-care systems.

Summary

Head Start is a federally funded comprehensive early-childhood program created in 1965 during the War on Poverty. It began as an eight-week OEO demonstration and developed into a permanent national system serving preschool children, infants, toddlers, pregnant women, and families.

The program combines early education with health, oral-health, nutrition, mental-health, disability, family, and social services. It operates through local public, nonprofit, tribal, school, community-action, and other eligible grant recipients rather than federally operated centers.

Parents possess formal authority through Policy Councils and parent committees while governing bodies retain legal and fiscal responsibility. The Office of Head Start monitors recipients through data, reviews, audits, classroom observations, and the Designation Renewal System.

Research has found positive effects on preschool experiences and selected early outcomes, while long-term findings vary and some measured academic differences diminish. Evaluation must account for comparison services, program quality, later schools, and comprehensive objectives.

Head Start remains one of the most durable institutions created by the War on Poverty.
