Police Abolition

A political and social framework seeking the long-term dismantling of conventional policing and the construction of alternative systems for safety, prevention, emergency response, conflict resolution, accountability, and community well-being.

Fact Sheet Overview

Subject type
Political ideology, social-movement objective, institutional critique, and long-term public-safety framework.
Core meaning
The replacement of conventional police institutions with different systems intended to prevent harm, respond to emergencies, resolve conflict, investigate serious wrongdoing, and provide accountability without relying on the present model of armed law enforcement.
Principal geographic focus
This fact sheet focuses on the United States, although abolitionist ideas and campaigns also exist internationally.
Principal historical roots
Black freedom struggles, opposition to racialized policing, prison abolition, Black feminist organizing, anti-carceral movements, community-control campaigns, transformative justice, and movements against state violence.
Principal period of national prominence
The movement developed over several decades and entered broad national debate after the 2020 murder of George Floyd and the protests that followed.
Central argument
Abolitionists argue that recurring police violence, racial inequality, criminalization, surveillance, and coercive control are structural features of policing rather than isolated failures that can be fully corrected through conventional reform.
Constructive component
Police abolition proposes building institutions that reduce violence and social instability through housing, healthcare, education, employment, youth development, mental-health care, violence interruption, survivor support, emergency medical response, and community-based accountability.
Transition strategy
Common strategies include reducing police budgets and duties, decriminalizing conduct, removing police from schools and social-service functions, ending militarization, creating civilian crisis-response systems, strengthening community institutions, and refusing reforms believed to expand police legitimacy or capacity.
Relationship to Defund the Police
Many abolitionists treat defunding as one transitional strategy. Not every supporter of police-budget reduction supports the complete abolition of police.
Relationship to prison abolition
Police abolition usually forms part of a broader prison-industrial-complex abolition framework that also challenges jails, prisons, detention, surveillance, and punitive social control.
Organizational structure
No single organization, leader, platform, or implementation plan governs the movement. Abolitionist organizations differ in emphasis, sequence, local strategy, and proposed alternatives.
Legal status
Police abolition is not a federal law or uniform government program. Police authority, duties, funding, and organization are governed primarily through state and local law.
Implementation status
No uniform abolitionist model has replaced conventional policing across a major United States jurisdiction. Governments have adopted selected nonpolice response programs, decriminalization measures, violence-prevention initiatives, and transfers of duties without abolishing policing as an institution.
Principal criticisms
Critics question how abolition would address violent emergencies, organized crime, investigation, deterrence, due process, domestic and sexual violence, unequal private security, institutional accountability, and the transition from existing systems.
Historical significance
Police abolition moved public debate beyond officer misconduct and reform toward foundational questions about why police exist, which responsibilities they should hold, what produces safety, and whether public institutions can be designed without conventional policing.

What Is Police Abolition?

Police abolition is the political objective of dismantling conventional police institutions and replacing their functions with different systems of prevention, response, investigation, conflict resolution, and accountability. It rejects the assumption that a permanent armed police force must remain the central institution responsible for public safety.

Abolition is both a negative and a constructive project. The negative component seeks to reduce and ultimately end policing, criminalization, surveillance, arrest, and coercive state control as presently organized. The constructive component seeks to create social conditions and institutions that prevent harm, address conflict, support victims, and respond to emergencies without reproducing the harms abolitionists associate with policing.

The term does not describe one legislative bill or administrative plan. Some abolitionists describe a gradual process of shifting resources and responsibilities over many years. Others emphasize immediate closure of particular units, removal of police from specified settings, or refusal of reforms believed to strengthen the institution. Local campaigns therefore require separate examination rather than assuming that every use of the term proposes the same sequence or endpoint.

Scope and Terminology

Police abolition concerns the institution of policing, not merely the employment status of individual officers. It asks which powers, duties, organizational cultures, technologies, budgets, and legal authorities should continue to exist and which institutions should replace them.

Abolitionists generally use “police” to mean state-authorized agencies with powers of surveillance, detention, arrest, coercion, and force. Their analysis may also include school police, transit police, campus police, immigration enforcement, probation, parole, private security, and other institutions when those bodies perform comparable carceral functions.

This fact sheet explains the United States movement, its historical development, main arguments, proposed mechanisms, internal differences, criticisms, and partial policy experiments. It does not assume that all crime, interpersonal violence, or emergency response would disappear under an abolitionist system. The dispute concerns which institutions should address those problems and by what authority and methods.

No Single Abolitionist Blueprint

Police abolition is decentralized. Organizations and writers share a general opposition to conventional policing but disagree about strategy, timing, institutional design, use of government, and the role of law.

Some abolitionists emphasize municipal budgets and the transfer of police duties. Others focus on decriminalization, labor organizing, mutual aid, survivor support, housing, or community accountability. Some seek to use legislation and constitutional rights to reduce the carceral state. Others are skeptical that legal systems created through slavery, segregation, and punishment can become reliable instruments of abolition.

These differences matter. A proposal to remove armed officers from mental-health calls is not the same as a proposal to eliminate homicide investigators. A campaign to end school policing is not a complete municipal safety plan. A citywide transition would require decisions concerning emergency communications, evidence collection, missing persons, traffic safety, domestic violence, public disorder, weapons, organized crime, and the use of force. Abolitionist literature offers frameworks and examples, but no single model has resolved every institutional question.

Relationship to Historical Abolitionism

Modern police and prison abolition deliberately invokes the moral and political tradition of movements that sought to abolish slavery. The comparison is not a claim that policing and chattel slavery are identical institutions. It is an argument that some institutions produce harms so fundamental that reform within their existing structure is insufficient.

Abolitionists also draw attention to the Thirteenth Amendment's exception permitting involuntary servitude as punishment for crime, the development of convict leasing after emancipation, racialized criminal law, segregation, and the later expansion of imprisonment and surveillance. These connections form part of the movement's broader claim that American punishment systems developed through histories of racial control.

Critics dispute the strength, completeness, or policy implications of these historical comparisons. A fact sheet should therefore distinguish the movement's intellectual genealogy from the separate historical question of how particular police departments originated and changed across regions and periods.

Black Freedom Struggle and Community Control

Police abolition emerged from longer struggles against racial violence and unequal law enforcement. During the civil-rights era, Black communities challenged police brutality, discriminatory arrest, surveillance, political repression, and the failure of police to protect Black residents from white violence.

Organizations and neighborhood campaigns advanced demands including civilian review, community control, independent investigation, removal of abusive officers, armed self-defense, legal observation, bail support, and alternative community institutions. These campaigns did not all demand abolition, but they created political traditions from which later abolitionist analysis developed.

The Black Panther Party's patrols of police activity, community service programs, and criticism of state violence became important reference points. Black feminist organizers later emphasized that public safety debates must also address sexual violence, domestic abuse, poverty, disability, gender identity, and the ways criminal systems can harm survivors while claiming to protect them.

Prison Abolition Foundations

Contemporary police abolition developed closely with prison abolition. Prison abolitionists argued that incarceration should not be treated as the inevitable response to social conflict, poverty, addiction, mental illness, or violence. They criticized the expansion of prisons, jails, probation, parole, and surveillance as an interconnected carceral system.

Writers and organizers including Angela Davis, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, and other Black feminist scholars and activists helped develop the idea that abolition requires creating social institutions capable of making prisons and police less necessary. This approach directs attention toward housing, healthcare, education, employment, family support, political power, and community capacity rather than treating punishment as an isolated policy sector.

The concept of the “prison industrial complex” described the network of government agencies, political incentives, private interests, labor systems, surveillance practices, and cultural assumptions supporting imprisonment and policing. Police abolition applies this broader analysis to the agencies that initiate many entries into the criminal legal system.

Critical Resistance and the Modern Movement

Critical Resistance became one of the most influential organizations in modern prison-industrial-complex abolition. Organizers formed the project in 1997 and held the first Critical Resistance conference at the University of California, Berkeley, in September 1998. The gathering brought together activists, scholars, formerly and currently incarcerated people, labor organizers, and community groups.

Critical Resistance defined abolition as a political vision and practical organizing strategy aimed at eliminating imprisonment, policing, and surveillance while creating lasting alternatives. Its campaigns, publications, and training materials helped establish abolition as an institutional program rather than solely a philosophical critique.

The organization also developed an influential distinction between reforms that reduce the power and reach of the carceral system and reforms that expand budgets, staffing, surveillance, legitimacy, or technological capacity. This distinction became central to later debates over body cameras, community policing, training, and civilian oversight.

Reforms and “Reformist Reforms”

Abolitionists do not necessarily oppose every immediate policy change. They often distinguish between measures that reduce harm and institutional power and measures that make policing appear more legitimate while leaving its authority intact or expanding it.

Measures commonly viewed as consistent with abolition include decriminalization, release from detention, reduced police contact, closure of facilities, transfer of noncriminal duties, removal of police from schools, restrictions on surveillance, and investment in nonpolice services. Measures often criticized as “reformist reforms” include additional training, larger diversity initiatives, new technology, expanded data systems, and community-policing programs when they increase funding or deepen police presence.

The classification is disputed even within the movement. A body-camera program may document misconduct but also expand surveillance. Civilian oversight may improve accountability but preserve the institution. A crisis-response team may reduce police encounters but remain dependent on police dispatch and backup. Abolitionists evaluate reforms partly by whether they reduce the future scale, legitimacy, and necessity of policing.

MPD150 and a Police-Free Future

In 2017, the Minneapolis-based collective MPD150 published Enough Is Enough: A 150-Year Performance Review of the Minneapolis Police Department. The project combined historical research, community interviews, political education, and proposals for a police-free future.

MPD150 presented abolition as a gradual process rather than the immediate disappearance of every police function. Its materials proposed shifting funding, responsibility, and public expectations toward community-based systems of safety, support, and prevention.

The project became nationally influential after Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd in 2020. Although MPD150 did not control the city government's response, its prior work gave local and national organizers an established abolitionist framework during the protests.

Movement for Black Lives and Invest-Divest

The Movement for Black Lives connected police and prison policy to a broader program concerning racial justice, political power, economic investment, reparations, and community control. Its Vision for Black Lives included an “Invest-Divest” framework calling for resources to be withdrawn from criminalization and redirected toward community needs.

The framework helped establish budget allocation as a civil-rights issue. Police funding was not treated only as an administrative question; it was compared with government investment in housing, schools, healthcare, employment, transportation, environmental protection, and youth development.

Invest-divest can be used by both abolitionists and non-abolitionists. For abolitionists, it is a transition mechanism intended to reduce the social conditions that produce violence while decreasing institutional reliance on police and punishment.

National Prominence After George Floyd's Murder

Police abolition entered broad national discussion after Derek Chauvin murdered George Floyd in Minneapolis on May 25, 2020. Protests spread across the United States and internationally, combining demands for prosecution, police accountability, racial justice, budget changes, and institutional transformation.

“Defund the Police” became the most visible slogan associated with structural change, but abolitionist organizers argued that budget reduction was only one part of a larger project. National debate expanded to include the removal of police from schools, nonpolice mental-health response, demilitarization, decriminalization, community violence intervention, and replacement of police departments.

The protests produced increased public attention but not a unified abolitionist program. Elected officials, organizations, protesters, and voters used the terms “defund,” “dismantle,” “disband,” “reimagine,” and “abolish” differently, creating substantial confusion over intended outcomes.

8 to Abolition

The 8 to Abolition platform appeared during the 2020 protests as an abolitionist response to the reform-oriented 8 Can't Wait campaign. Its authors argued that limits on police use of force were insufficient because similar reforms had already existed in some jurisdictions without ending police violence.

The platform organized its program around eight demands:

  1. Defund the police.
  2. Demilitarize communities.
  3. Remove police from schools.
  4. Free people from prisons and jails.
  5. Repeal laws that criminalize survival.
  6. Invest in community self-governance.
  7. Provide safe housing for everyone.
  8. Invest in care rather than policing.

The demands illustrate that abolition extends beyond police departments. It joins institutional reduction with housing, care, decriminalization, and community governance. The platform was influential within activist networks but did not become an official national government program.

Core Abolitionist Arguments

Structural Harm

Abolitionists argue that police violence and racial inequality are not merely the result of a few individual officers. They identify legal authority, organizational incentives, union protections, prosecutorial relationships, surveillance, militarization, and broad discretionary power as structural sources of harm.

Criminalization of Social Problems

Police are frequently dispatched to problems involving homelessness, mental illness, substance use, school discipline, family conflict, poverty, and public disorder. Abolitionists argue that coercive enforcement often manages the visible symptoms of these conditions without resolving their causes.

Unequal Protection and Enforcement

The movement emphasizes racial disparities in stops, searches, arrests, force, surveillance, and punishment, while also arguing that marginalized communities may receive inadequate protection when they report violence or victimization.

Limited Preventive Capacity

Police usually respond after an emergency has begun or a harmful act has occurred. Abolitionists therefore prioritize institutions intended to prevent violence through stable housing, healthcare, education, income, mediation, environmental design, youth opportunity, and community relationships.

Institutional Expansion Through Reform

Abolitionists argue that repeated reform cycles can produce larger budgets, more training, new technologies, and renewed legitimacy without eliminating abuse. They therefore judge policy changes by whether they reduce the institution's future reach rather than whether they improve its public image.

Building Alternatives

Abolitionist writing commonly emphasizes that abolition is not only the removal of police. It is the construction of institutions capable of performing necessary social functions without reproducing the same patterns of coercion and inequality.

Proposed alternatives include:

  • civilian behavioral-health and substance-use crisis teams;
  • emergency medical response independent of routine police deployment;
  • community violence-intervention and credible-messenger programs;
  • housing-first systems and homelessness outreach;
  • youth programs, schools, employment, recreation, and family support;
  • survivor-centered services for domestic and sexual violence;
  • restorative and transformative justice processes;
  • civilian traffic agencies and automated infrastructure designed with due-process safeguards;
  • unarmed mediation and neighborhood conflict-response teams;
  • public-health approaches to drugs, addiction, and behavioral crisis;
  • democratic community governance over safety resources; and
  • specialized investigative or emergency functions redesigned outside conventional police departments.

These proposals vary in evidence, scale, authority, and readiness. A successful pilot for low-risk behavioral-health calls does not by itself demonstrate how a complete abolitionist system would handle every form of violence. Abolitionists generally present such programs as components of a larger transition rather than complete substitutes operating alone.

Decriminalization and Reducing Police Contact

Decriminalization reduces the number of situations in which police can stop, search, cite, arrest, or detain people. Abolitionist campaigns commonly target laws concerning drug possession, sex work, homelessness, loitering, fare evasion, school discipline, immigration, minor traffic violations, and other conduct described as criminalizing poverty or survival.

Decriminalization can occur without abolishing police, but it serves an abolitionist function when it permanently removes categories of conduct from police jurisdiction. The strategy also seeks to prevent fines, warrants, court debt, probation, incarceration, and collateral consequences that can follow low-level enforcement.

Critics argue that removing legal sanctions can create disorder or leave vulnerable people without protection. The effect depends on whether governments replace enforcement with effective services, regulation, civil remedies, public-health systems, and accessible forms of dispute resolution.

Civilian Crisis Response

Civilian crisis-response programs send clinicians, paramedics, social workers, peer specialists, or other unarmed personnel to selected calls that do not present an identified violent threat. These programs seek to reduce unnecessary police encounters and match the responder to the underlying need.

Denver's Support Team Assisted Response program, commonly called STAR, is one example. Denver 9-1-1 dispatches behavioral-health clinicians and paramedics to eligible low-risk calls involving mental-health distress or substance use. STAR operates alongside, rather than in place of, the city's police department.

Such programs demonstrate that some calls can be reassigned from police. They do not by themselves establish police abolition. Important implementation questions include eligibility rules, operating hours, geographic coverage, dispatch accuracy, responder safety, backup policy, information sharing, follow-up care, funding, and whether police remain the default when civilian capacity is unavailable.

Violence and Serious Harm

The most persistent challenge to police abolition concerns active violence, armed threats, homicide, sexual assault, domestic violence, organized crime, and other serious harm. Critics argue that a society requires institutions with lawful authority, investigative capacity, and the ability to intervene rapidly when people face immediate danger.

Abolitionist responses vary. Some emphasize prevention, survivor services, community accountability, violence interruption, and reduction of weapons and economic instability. Some accept that specialized emergency responders may remain during a long transition but argue that they should be narrowly authorized and institutionally separated from routine policing. Others envision community defense or public-safety bodies that would not carry the history, scope, culture, or powers of present police departments.

The central unresolved issue is not whether serious violence exists. It is how a post-police system would authorize coercive intervention, investigate wrongdoing, protect due process, prevent retaliation, and remain accountable. Abolitionist theory provides principles and local practices, but no large jurisdiction has fully tested a comprehensive replacement system across all categories of serious harm.

Domestic Violence, Sexual Violence, and Survivor Safety

Domestic and sexual violence present a major test for both existing policing and abolitionist alternatives. Many survivors do not report abuse because they fear disbelief, retaliation, family separation, immigration consequences, economic loss, or additional violence. Police and courts may provide emergency intervention and legal protection, but they can also fail to prevent harm or expose survivors to systems they distrust.

Black feminist abolitionists developed transformative-justice and community-accountability practices partly to address violence without relying exclusively on police, prosecution, or incarceration. These approaches may include survivor-led safety planning, housing, financial support, removal from danger, behavioral intervention, community monitoring, accountability agreements, and long-term prevention.

Critics question whether voluntary or community processes can control determined violent offenders, protect confidential information, guarantee impartiality, or enforce outcomes. Abolitionist planning must therefore address survivor autonomy, emergency protection, evidence, repeat offending, children's safety, disability access, and the risk that informal authority can reproduce family or community power inequalities.

Investigation, Evidence, and Accountability

Conventional police departments investigate crimes, collect evidence, identify suspects, locate missing people, execute warrants, and refer cases for prosecution. Performance varies greatly, and many reported offenses are never cleared. Abolitionists argue that investigative functions can be separated from armed patrol and redesigned around specialized civilian institutions.

A replacement system would still require standards governing search, privacy, evidence preservation, forensic methods, witness protection, disclosure, error correction, and appeals. It would also need safeguards against false accusation, political retaliation, racial bias, corruption, and unequal access to investigative resources.

Transformative justice focuses on repairing harm, changing conditions, and preventing recurrence rather than relying primarily on punishment. It does not eliminate the need to determine what occurred, protect affected people, and impose meaningful boundaries. The design of legitimate and enforceable accountability remains one of the movement's most difficult institutional questions.

Governance and Public Authority

Police abolition raises questions about who may use force, under what legal authority, and subject to what review. Eliminating a public police department without creating legitimate alternatives could shift coercive power to private security, armed groups, employers, landlords, wealthy neighborhoods, or informal community leaders.

Many abolitionists reject privatization and vigilantism. They argue for democratic community governance, public investment, transparent institutions, and narrowly defined powers. Nevertheless, the word “community” does not automatically guarantee fairness. Communities contain unequal wealth, status, ideology, race, gender, age, disability, and political power.

Any durable alternative would need rules concerning jurisdiction, professional competence, public records, complaints, discipline, conflict of interest, financial oversight, civil rights, due process, and removal of abusive personnel. Abolition therefore requires institutional design as well as criticism of existing agencies.

Minneapolis and the 2021 Public-Safety Proposal

Minneapolis became the principal national test of abolition-related municipal politics after George Floyd's murder. In June 2020, a majority of City Council members pledged to dismantle the Minneapolis Police Department and create a different public-safety system.

The eventual 2021 ballot proposal would have removed the charter requirement for a police department and created a Department of Public Safety that could include licensed peace officers when necessary. The proposal did not itself provide a complete operational abolition plan, and supporters and opponents disagreed over its likely consequences.

Minneapolis voters rejected the amendment on November 2, 2021, by approximately 56 percent to 44 percent. The result demonstrated that national attention and activist support did not produce voter approval for replacing the police department. Minneapolis continued to pursue selected reforms and alternative public-safety initiatives within a system that retained conventional policing.

Disbanding or Reorganizing a Department Is Not Necessarily Abolition

Governments sometimes dissolve a police department and transfer law-enforcement duties to another agency. This may change jurisdiction, management, contracts, staffing, or governance while preserving armed patrol, arrest authority, criminal enforcement, and incarceration.

Abolitionists therefore distinguish institutional replacement from administrative reorganization. A city that closes its department and contracts with a county sheriff has not abolished policing. A department renamed as a public-safety agency remains a police institution if its powers and functions continue substantially unchanged.

This distinction is important when discussing Camden, New Jersey, where the city police department was dissolved and replaced by a county police force in 2013. The change constituted restructuring and expansion under a different agency, not police abolition.

Major Criticisms

Public-Safety Risk

Critics argue that reducing police capacity before alternatives are proven could increase violence, delay emergency response, weaken investigations, and place low-income communities at greater risk. Some empirical research finds that additional police staffing can reduce homicide, including homicide victimization among Black civilians, although policing also imposes measurable social costs through arrests and other enforcement.

Incomplete Institutional Design

Abolitionist principles often identify what should be reduced more clearly than how a complete replacement system would operate. Critics demand specific plans for command, dispatch, investigations, evidence, warrants, armed threats, cross-jurisdictional crime, corruption, and judicial review.

Unequal Private Protection

Wealthy institutions and neighborhoods may purchase private security, controlled access, insurance, legal services, and technology. If public policing disappears without equal public alternatives, poorer communities could face both inadequate protection and increased informal coercion.

Community Power and Due Process

Community-based systems can reproduce local hierarchies, retaliation, favoritism, and discrimination. Critics argue that formal law, professional standards, appeal rights, and external oversight remain necessary to protect unpopular individuals and minority groups within communities.

Political Feasibility

Police departments are embedded in state constitutions, municipal charters, civil-service systems, labor agreements, criminal codes, courts, insurance structures, and emergency management. Full abolition would require extensive legal and institutional reconstruction and sustained public support that has not yet existed nationally.

Evidence Limitations

Many proposed alternatives operate only for selected calls, limited hours, or small populations. Evidence that one program performs a narrow function effectively cannot automatically establish the safety of removing police from unrelated duties. Critics call for staged implementation, independent evaluation, and clear failure-response procedures.

Abolitionist Responses to Criticism

Abolitionists commonly answer that the current system must also be judged by its failures. Police do not prevent every violent act, solve every reported crime, protect every survivor, or distribute safety equally. The relevant comparison is therefore not between an imperfect alternative and a perfectly functioning police system.

Movement organizations also reject the idea that abolition requires an immediate institutional vacuum. They describe a long transition in which governments reduce police duties while building and evaluating replacement systems. Under this view, abolition is a direction governing successive decisions rather than a single date on which all police disappear.

Abolitionists further argue that public safety cannot be measured only through reported crime or arrests. Measures should also include police violence, displacement, incarceration, trauma, school exclusion, family separation, economic security, housing stability, health, and residents' ability to obtain help without fear of punishment.

How Abolitionist Policies Could Be Evaluated

An evidence-based evaluation would need multiple measures rather than a single ideological test. Relevant indicators could include:

  • homicide, assault, robbery, sexual violence, domestic violence, and other serious harm;
  • response times and emergency availability;
  • victim and survivor access to protection and services;
  • police contacts, arrests, citations, searches, force, injury, and death;
  • racial, geographic, disability, gender, and income disparities;
  • case clearance, evidence quality, wrongful accusation, and procedural fairness;
  • housing stability, mental-health outcomes, school participation, and employment;
  • costs transferred across police, health, housing, courts, emergency services, and community organizations;
  • public trust, willingness to seek help, and perceived safety;
  • private-security expansion, vigilantism, or displacement of coercion into less accountable institutions; and
  • long-term prevention rather than only short-term incident response.

Evaluation must distinguish the failure of an abolitionist principle from the failure to fund or implement a proposed alternative at sufficient scale. It must also avoid assuming that a renamed or transferred function has changed merely because it moved outside a police budget.

Current Status

Police abolition remains an influential but contested political framework. It has shaped organizing, scholarship, municipal debate, campus policy, school-police campaigns, protest movements, and the development of nonpolice response programs.

The movement has not produced a uniform abolition of conventional policing in a major United States jurisdiction. Many police budgets increased after the immediate 2020 protest period, and state governments in some jurisdictions restricted municipal authority to reduce police funding. Voters and elected officials often supported selected reforms or alternative-response programs while rejecting complete institutional replacement.

Abolitionist influence is therefore visible primarily through agenda setting, political education, partial transfers of responsibility, decriminalization, community-based safety work, and continuing pressure against police expansion. Whether these developments represent steps toward abolition, independent reforms, or a durable mixed public-safety system remains disputed.

Historical Significance

Police abolition changed the questions asked in modern criminal-justice debate. Instead of beginning with how to improve police conduct, abolition begins by asking which social problems should be assigned to police, why armed enforcement became the default response, and what institutions might produce safety with less coercion.

The movement also joined public safety to broader material conditions. Housing, healthcare, employment, education, disability services, transportation, environmental conditions, and political power became central components of a debate once confined largely to law enforcement and courts.

Its most important contribution may be the development of an institutional horizon against which reforms are judged. Its principal unresolved challenge is converting a wide-ranging moral and political critique into durable systems capable of protecting people from both state violence and interpersonal violence at full municipal or national scale.

Key Distinctions

Police abolition
The long-term objective of dismantling conventional policing and building alternative systems of safety, prevention, response, investigation, and accountability.
Defund the Police
A demand to reduce or redirect police money, duties, personnel, or authority. It may be a limited policy program or a transition strategy toward abolition.
Police reform
Changes intended to improve the conduct, accountability, training, supervision, or legitimacy of an existing police institution.
Disbanding
Formal dissolution of a particular department. Policing continues when another law-enforcement agency assumes the same duties.
Prison abolition
The broader objective of replacing prisons, jails, detention, and punitive incarceration with different systems of accountability and social provision.
Transformative justice
An approach seeking to address harm, support survivors, create accountability, and change the conditions that enabled violence without relying primarily on punishment or incarceration.
Restorative justice
A structured process focusing on harm, responsibility, repair, and participation by affected people. It can operate within or outside the criminal legal system and is not automatically abolitionist.
Community control
Democratic authority by residents over public-safety priorities, budgets, personnel, or institutions. It may seek control of an existing police department or support replacement of policing.
Civilian alternative response
Dispatch of nonpolice professionals to selected calls. Such programs can reduce police contact without abolishing the department.
Privatization
Transfer of security functions to private companies or property owners. Most abolitionist frameworks reject replacing public police with less accountable private coercion.
Anarchy or absence of rules
Police abolition does not necessarily mean the absence of law, emergency response, investigation, boundaries, or accountability. The movement disputes the institutional form through which those functions should operate.

Summary

Police abolition is a political ideology and movement objective seeking the long-term dismantling of conventional policing and the construction of alternative institutions for safety, prevention, emergency response, investigation, conflict resolution, and accountability. It developed through Black freedom struggles, prison abolition, Black feminist organizing, community-control campaigns, and opposition to the prison industrial complex.

Abolitionists argue that police violence, racial inequality, criminalization, and institutional expansion are structural problems that ordinary reform cannot fully resolve. Their proposed strategies include defunding, decriminalization, removal of police from schools and social-service functions, civilian crisis response, community violence intervention, housing, healthcare, education, survivor support, and transformative justice.

Critics question how abolition would handle violent emergencies, investigations, due process, domestic and sexual violence, organized crime, unequal private security, and the transition to replacement institutions. No major United States jurisdiction has implemented a comprehensive abolitionist system, although partial alternatives have reassigned selected duties. Police abolition remains historically significant because it changed public debate from how police should operate to whether conventional policing should remain the principal institution through which society defines and provides public safety.
