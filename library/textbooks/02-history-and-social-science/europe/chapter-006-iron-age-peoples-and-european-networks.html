Iron Age Peoples and European Networks

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

No single people called "the Celts" ever ruled a single Celtic state, no ancient nation called "Germany" existed to be invaded or defended, and no unified Iron Age civilization stretched from the Atlantic to the Black Sea. What existed instead was something more interesting and more difficult to summarize: thousands of distinct communities, speaking related but far from identical languages, connected by rivers, mountain passes, sea lanes, and long chains of exchange in iron, salt, amber, and prestige goods, and organized into ever shifting arrangements of chiefdoms, fortified settlements, and regional confederations that later observers, and later nations, would try to simplify into single names.

Picture an ironworker at a hillfort above the Danube, sometime in the seventh century BCE, feeding charcoal into a small clay furnace and hammering the bloom repeatedly into a usable blade, a laborious process whose results depended heavily on the smith's skill, and whose finished edge might, in these early centuries, be no better, and sometimes worse, than a well made bronze equivalent. Picture, a few centuries later and several hundred kilometers to the west, an artisan in what is now eastern France shaping a bronze flagon in swirling, asymmetrical patterns that art historians will eventually call La Tène style, patterns whose curling lines share more with the art of distant workshops than with any single political community. Picture a merchant on the Adriatic coast loading amber that has traveled overland from the Baltic shore, destined for a Greek or Etruscan buyer who has never seen the northern sea it came from and who has no idea, and no need to know, what language the people who gathered it actually spoke. None of these people thought of themselves as living in an "Iron Age." None of them belonged to a nation in any modern sense. Yet all of them were part of a genuinely European story, one this chapter must tell honestly, without collapsing its enormous diversity into a handful of convenient labels.

This chapter closes Part I of the textbook, the Part devoted to Europe before classical civilization. Chapters 3, 4, and 5 traced, in turn, the Paleolithic hunter gatherers who first settled the continent, the Neolithic transformation that brought farming and permanent settlement, and the Bronze Age societies whose megaliths, metals, and long distance trade networks knit growing populations together into increasingly hierarchical communities. This chapter carries that story forward into the Iron Age, roughly the centuries between about 800 BCE and the point, different in different regions, at which written Greek and Roman sources begin to describe particular Iron Age societies in some detail. It is, in one sense, the last chapter before the textbook's attention narrows onto the Greek world in Part II and the Roman world in Part III. In a more important sense, however, it is not a mere prelude to Greece and Rome at all. The societies examined here, Hallstatt and La Tène communities across central and western Europe, Jastorf and other northern groups, Italic peoples of the Italian peninsula, Illyrian and Thracian communities of the Balkans and the lower Danube, Iberian and Celtiberian and Lusitanian communities of the Iberian Peninsula, and Baltic communities of the northeast, had their own historical trajectories, their own internal complexity, and their own significance, independent of whatever the Greeks or Romans later made of them.

This chapter asks students to hold two ideas in mind at once, ideas that can feel contradictory but are not. First, Iron Age Europe was extraordinarily diverse. There was no single Iron Age people, no single Iron Age language, and no single Iron Age political system. Second, Iron Age Europe was also extraordinarily connected. Salt from Hallstatt's mines, iron from Noric ore deposits, amber from the Baltic shore, tin from the Atlantic west, wine and painted pottery from the Mediterranean world, all moved across enormous distances through networks of exchange that linked communities who might never meet face to face and who might not have understood one another's languages at all. Diversity and connection were not opposites. They were two faces of the same historical reality.

A further caution runs throughout this chapter, and indeed through the rest of this textbook wherever ancient peoples are discussed. The names historians and archaeologists use for Iron Age communities, Celtic, Germanic, Illyrian, Thracian, Iberian, and others, come from a tangled mixture of sources: Greek and Roman writers who often knew these peoples only at a distance or through the eyes of soldiers and merchants, nineteenth and twentieth century archaeologists who invented classificatory labels for patterns of material culture, and much later national movements that sometimes appropriated ancient names for modern political purposes. None of these labels map cleanly onto a fixed ethnic group, a fixed language, or a fixed ancestry, and none of them should be read backward into modern national boundaries. A modern French person is not simply a descendant of "the Gauls" in any straightforward sense, a modern German is not simply a descendant of "the Germanic tribes," and a modern person in the Balkans is not simply the direct heir of a single ancient Illyrian or Thracian nation. This chapter tries to use these necessary but imperfect labels carefully, explaining at each step what kind of evidence stands behind them and what kind of claim they can, and cannot, support.

Finally, this chapter must prepare students for an important transition in the textbook's overall architecture. Chapter 7, which opens Part II, does not continue directly from the end of this chapter's chronology. Instead, it turns back in time to examine the Aegean Bronze Age, the Minoan and Mycenaean world that flourished many centuries before the Iron Age societies described here. This is not an error or an inconsistency. Part I has surveyed the whole of prehistoric Europe broadly, region by region and period by period, ending with the Iron Age networks that stretched across the continent on the eve of extensive written documentation. Part II now narrows its focus dramatically, returning chronologically to trace one regional trajectory, the Greek world, in much greater depth from its own Bronze Age beginnings. Students should not imagine that the Iron Age societies of this chapter simply gave way to the Greek world described in Chapter 7. They existed, in many cases, at the same time as, before, and long after the Mycenaean palaces, and many of them, including La Tène Celtic societies and Illyrian and Thracian communities of the Balkans, remained historically significant for centuries after Chapter 7's Aegean narrative begins.

What follows, then, is not a countdown to Rome. It is an account, on its own terms, of the many dynamic, interconnected societies that occupied the European landmass in the centuries before extensive written history, an account that should leave students with a richer and more accurate mental map of ancient Europe than the simplified national categories, "ancient France," "ancient Germany," "ancient Spain," that popular imagination too often supplies.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the transition from bronze to iron technology occurred unevenly across Europe and why this transition should not be understood as a simple story of technological superiority.
  2. Distinguish among archaeological cultures, linguistic categories, and ethnonyms recorded by Greek and Roman writers, and explain why none of these map neatly onto modern ethnic or national identities.
  3. Describe the Hallstatt culture's elite centers, salt and metal wealth, burial practices, and connections to the Mediterranean world.
  4. Describe the La Tène culture's art, warfare, settlement patterns, and geographic range, while explaining why "Celtic" describes a linguistic and cultural continuum rather than a single unified people or state.
  5. Analyze the limits of applying later Roman terms such as "Germani" backward onto earlier northern European archaeological cultures such as Jastorf.
  6. Identify the linguistic and cultural diversity of Italic peoples of the Italian peninsula before Roman expansion, without conflating this survey with the origins of Rome itself.
  7. Explain the historical significance of Illyrian and Thracian communities of the Balkan and Danubian regions and their connections to the Aegean, the Adriatic, and the Black Sea.
  8. Describe the internal diversity of Iron Age Iberia and the Atlantic and Baltic zones of Europe.
  9. Trace the major exchange networks, iron, salt, amber, tin, and Mediterranean prestige goods, that connected Iron Age European societies across great distances.
  10. Evaluate the evidence, archaeological, linguistic, and textual, that historians use to reconstruct Iron Age societies, and assess the limitations of each type of evidence.
  11. Critique the practice of projecting later national identities backward onto Iron Age peoples.
  12. Contextualize Iron Age Europe's connections to the wider Mediterranean, Aegean, and Eurasian worlds without treating those connections as evidence of political unity.

Historical Context

Chapter 5 traced the Bronze Age transformation of Europe, a period, beginning at different times in different regions but generally underway by the third and second millennia BCE, during which monument building, metallurgy in copper and its alloy bronze, social hierarchy, and long distance trade networks reshaped communities across the continent. By the Bronze Age's later centuries, elite groups in many regions had accumulated wealth and status through control of metal production and exchange, warrior burials had become a widespread means of displaying status, and networks moved tin, copper, amber, and finished bronze goods across distances of hundreds or even thousands of kilometers. This chapter takes up the story where Chapter 5 left it, examining how these Bronze Age foundations were transformed, though never simply erased, by the adoption of iron technology and by continued, and in some respects intensified, patterns of exchange, hierarchy, and regional diversification.

The label "Iron Age" is, like many periodization terms discussed in the refinements governing this textbook, an analytical convention rather than a natural or universally experienced boundary. Archaeologists originally developed the three age system, Stone, Bronze, and Iron, in the nineteenth century as a way of organizing museum collections and excavated material by the dominant metal technology represented, and the system has proven durable and useful, but it should not be mistaken for a claim that Iron Age communities experienced a sudden, unified transformation. Iron working spread gradually, over centuries, and its adoption depended on local ore availability, existing metalworking knowledge, trade contacts, and the practical advantages iron offered over bronze in particular circumstances. Bronze, meanwhile, continued to be used for many purposes, especially ornamental and prestige goods, throughout the period this chapter examines and well beyond it. Students should therefore resist the temptation to imagine a single moment when "the Iron Age began," and should instead think in terms of a long, regionally variable process of technological, social, and economic change.

This chapter's geographic and thematic scope is unusually broad, spanning the Atlantic coast of Iberia to the Baltic shore, and from Scandinavia to the Balkans, roughly from the early first millennium BCE through the centuries in which Mediterranean written sources begin to describe particular Iron Age societies in useful, though far from unbiased, detail. This breadth reflects the chapter's purpose as the capstone of Part I: rather than narrowing prematurely onto any single region destined to become historically dominant, this chapter surveys the whole continent's Iron Age societies and their interconnections, resisting the pull toward a narrative that treats Celtic Gaul, or the Italian peninsula, or the Greek world, as the "main line" of European development with other regions as mere background.

Readers should also understand what this chapter deliberately does not cover. Detailed Greek colonization of the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts, along with sustained Greek interaction with non Greek Mediterranean societies, is assigned to Chapter 9, in Part II. The Etruscans, the origins of Rome, and early Roman monarchy and republican development are assigned to Chapter 14, in Part III. This chapter therefore introduces Italic linguistic and cultural diversity in broad terms, and it introduces Mediterranean contact with Iron Age European societies where that contact is genuinely part of the Iron Age story itself, but it does not narrate Greek colonization in detail, and it does not tell the story of early Rome. Readers who want those narratives should look ahead to the chapters assigned to carry them.

Section 6.1

From Bronze to Iron: A Regionally Uneven Transition

The transition from bronze to iron as the dominant metal for tools and weapons is one of the most frequently oversimplified episodes in popular understanding of ancient technology. A common shorthand holds that iron simply replaced bronze because iron was stronger, and that this replacement occurred at more or less the same time across the ancient world. Neither claim survives close examination. Iron ore is, in fact, far more widely distributed across the earth's crust than the tin required, along with copper, to make bronze, and this widespread availability, rather than any inherent superiority of iron over well made bronze, is probably the more important reason iron technology eventually displaced bronze for most everyday tools and weapons. Early iron, moreover, was not necessarily superior to good quality bronze. Iron smelted in a simple bloomery furnace, the basic technology available to Iron Age European smiths, produces a spongy mass called a bloom, mixed with slag, that must be repeatedly heated and hammered, a process called forging, to drive out impurities and consolidate the metal into usable iron or, with additional processing, steel. Early iron tools and weapons were often no better, and sometimes worse, than well made bronze equivalents, and bronze continued to be prized, and produced, for many purposes, particularly ornamental and prestige goods, throughout the period this chapter examines.

What iron did offer, once smiths developed sufficient skill, was cost. Because iron ore deposits are common across much of Europe, whereas tin sources adequate for large scale bronze production are comparatively rare and concentrated in a few regions, discussed in Chapter 5, communities without easy access to long distance tin trade could, once ironworking knowledge spread to them, produce their own metal tools and weapons locally rather than depending on distant exchange networks. This mattered enormously for regions that had previously been peripheral to Bronze Age tin and copper trade routes, since local iron production could support a broader distribution of metal tools among ordinary farming households, not merely among elites who could command access to imported bronze.

Ironworking knowledge did not spread across Europe as a single wave, nor did it move along one clean, one directional path. Current archaeological evidence indicates that iron became increasingly established in southeastern Europe during the late second and early first millennia BCE through interaction with Anatolian and eastern Mediterranean metallurgical traditions, but regional adoption and innovation followed multiple, overlapping trajectories rather than a single relay running neatly from east to west. From this broadly eastern connection, ironworking knowledge and practice reached central, western, and eventually northern Europe over subsequent centuries, at differing paces in different places, with local communities in each region experimenting, adapting, and combining the new technology with their own existing metalworking traditions rather than adopting a uniform imported package of practices.

This unevenness has an important consequence for how this chapter, and the wider Master Instructions governing this textbook, treat the Iron Age as a period label. "Iron Age" does not describe a synchronized continental event, comparable to a single date on a calendar, but rather an archaeological convention describing a broad and regionally staggered pattern of technological and, closely associated, social change. Communities in southeastern Europe were working iron regularly by roughly the tenth or ninth century BCE, while some communities in the far north of Scandinavia continued to rely more heavily on bronze for a longer period, adopting ironworking more fully only in subsequent centuries. Even within regions generally described as "Iron Age," bronze objects, particularly ornaments, vessels, and other prestige goods, remained common, prized, and often more elaborately worked than contemporary iron objects, since iron's practical advantages lay chiefly in tools and weapons rather than in the kind of decorative display for which bronze's casting properties remained well suited.

Students should therefore resist any narrative that treats the adoption of iron as a straightforward story of progress in which iron using societies were simply more advanced than their bronze using predecessors or neighbors. Iron's spread reflected practical considerations of ore availability, existing skill, and trade contact, layered onto already sophisticated Bronze Age societies with their own complex social hierarchies, religious practices, and exchange networks, discussed in Chapter 5. The Iron Age societies examined in the remainder of this chapter inherited, adapted, and transformed Bronze Age foundations rather than representing an abrupt break from them.

Section 6.2

Naming the Peoples of Iron Age Europe

Before this chapter can describe particular Iron Age societies, it must address a methodological problem that runs through the entire chapter and, indeed, through much of ancient European history: how do historians and archaeologists know what to call the people who lived in a given place at a given time, and what kind of claim does a given name actually support?

Historians and archaeologists draw on several distinct, and not always compatible, kinds of evidence. The first is material culture, the physical remains, pottery styles, metalwork, burial customs, settlement patterns, and other artifacts that archaeologists excavate and classify. When archaeologists observe that a broad geographic zone shares recurring patterns in these material remains, they often group that evidence together and give it a name, usually derived from the site where the pattern was first identified or most fully documented. Hallstatt and La Tène, discussed at length in Sections 6.3 and 6.4, are exactly this kind of label: an archaeological culture defined by recurring material patterns, not a self declared ethnic or political identity. Crucially, sharing an archaeological culture does not necessarily mean sharing a single language, a single ethnic identity, or a single political system. Trade, fashion, and technological diffusion can spread pottery styles or metalworking techniques among communities that spoke different languages and had no shared political organization whatsoever, just as, in the modern world, widely shared consumer goods or building styles do not indicate that everyone who uses them shares a single nationality.

The second kind of evidence is linguistic. Historical linguists reconstruct relationships among ancient languages by comparing surviving inscriptions, place names, personal names recorded by outside observers, and, for languages with descendants still spoken today, systematic comparison of vocabulary and grammar across related modern and ancient tongues. This work has established that many Iron Age European populations spoke languages belonging to related but distinct branches of the Indo European language family, including Celtic languages, Germanic languages, Italic languages, and others whose precise classification remains debated, such as the languages associated with Illyrian and Thracian speaking communities, for which the surviving evidence is too fragmentary to reconstruct in full. Linguistic classification is a genuinely different kind of evidence from archaeological classification, and the two do not always align. A community's language does not automatically determine its pottery style or burial customs, and neighboring communities speaking closely related languages might display quite different material cultures, while communities speaking unrelated languages might share very similar material cultures through trade and cultural exchange.

The third kind of evidence is textual, chiefly the writings of Greek and, somewhat later, Roman authors who described neighboring peoples they encountered through trade, diplomacy, warfare, or travel. Writers such as Herodotus, discussed in Chapter 1's Primary Source Window, Hecataeus, Poseidonios, whose now largely lost ethnographic writings survive chiefly through later authors who quoted or summarized them, Julius Caesar, and Tacitus, among others, provide invaluable but far from unbiased accounts of peoples they generally regarded as foreign, and often as less civilized than themselves. These accounts are shaped by the cultural assumptions, political purposes, and limited direct knowledge of their authors, who frequently generalized about entire regions on the basis of contact with a small number of communities, applied a single name to groups who did not necessarily use that name for themselves, and sometimes uncritically repeated earlier authors' claims rather than offering independent observation. Ethnonyms, the names by which outside observers, or occasionally the communities themselves, identified a people, must therefore be used with considerable caution. A name such as "Celtae," used by Greek and Roman writers, or "Germani," a name Julius Caesar and later writers applied to peoples east of the Rhine, tells historians how outside observers categorized a population, which is valuable information in its own right, but it does not necessarily tell historians how the people so labeled understood their own identity, whether they used the same name for themselves, or whether the label corresponded to a single coherent political or ethnic community rather than a loose grouping of distinct communities that outside observers found convenient to lump together.

The fourth and final kind of evidence, and the one requiring the greatest caution, is retrospective national identity, the practice, common from the eighteenth through the twentieth centuries and still influential in popular culture today, of treating ancient peoples as the direct ancestors of a modern nation and projecting modern national boundaries, characteristics, and even political aspirations backward onto them. Nineteenth century French nationalist writers embraced "the Gauls" as ancestral Frenchmen, German nationalist writers embraced Tacitus's Germani as ancestral Germans, and various twentieth century nationalist movements across the Balkans and Iberia have laid claim to Illyrian, Thracian, or Iberian ancestry as evidence of a continuous national lineage stretching back to antiquity. The refinements governing this textbook explicitly warn against precisely this kind of reasoning, and this chapter follows that warning closely: medieval France was not simply modern France at an earlier stage, and by the same logic, Iron Age Gaul was not simply ancient France, Iron Age Jastorf communities were not simply ancient Germans, and Iron Age Illyrian or Iberian communities were not simply the ancestors of a single modern nation in any straightforward, exclusive sense. Genetic ancestry, linguistic continuity, cultural memory, and modern national identity are four different things, related in complex and much debated ways, and conflating them produces bad history in service of modern political purposes.

With these cautions established, this chapter uses the conventional labels, Celtic, Germanic, Italic, Illyrian, Thracian, Iberian, Baltic, and others, because they remain the standard scholarly vocabulary and because avoiding them entirely would make the chapter unreadable. But each time such a label appears, students should understand it as shorthand for a complex, debated, and internally diverse historical reality, not as the name of a single ancient nation waiting to be described.

Historical Vocabulary
Archaeological Culture

Archaeological culture refers to a recurring, geographically bounded pattern of material remains, pottery styles, tools, burial customs, settlement forms, and other artifacts, that archaeologists identify through excavation and comparison and to which they typically give a name derived from the site where the pattern was first recognized or is most fully documented. Hallstatt, named for a site in Austria, and La Tène, named for a site in Switzerland, are two of the most important archaeological cultures discussed in this chapter.

It is essential to understand what an archaeological culture is not. It is not automatically evidence of a single ethnic group, a single language, or a single political community. Communities that shared broadly similar pottery, metalwork, or burial customs might have spoken quite different languages and belonged to entirely separate, even rival, political communities, connected chiefly by trade, fashion, and technological exchange rather than by shared identity. Conversely, communities sharing a single language or ethnic self identity might display considerable regional variation in material culture. Archaeologists therefore treat archaeological cultures as useful descriptive tools for organizing evidence, not as direct windows into ancient peoples' self understanding.

Section 6.3

Hallstatt Europe and the Early Iron Age

The Hallstatt culture takes its name from a site in the Salzkammergut region of the Austrian Alps, where extensive prehistoric salt mining, combined with unusually well preserved organic remains, textiles, wood, and leather, produced by the salt itself, allowed archaeologists to reconstruct an unusually detailed picture of an early Iron Age mining community. From this well documented type site, archaeologists have extended the name Hallstatt to a broader cultural horizon. The full Hallstatt sequence, as originally defined, spans phases labeled Hallstatt A through D, of which the earlier A and B phases are generally classified as Late Bronze Age, or Urnfield, rather than Iron Age. This chapter, like the wider scholarly convention it follows, uses "Hallstatt culture" to refer specifically to the Iron Age Hallstatt C and D phases, generally dated from roughly 800 to 450 BCE, that encompassed much of central Europe, stretching in various regional forms from eastern France across southern Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Czechia, and into parts of Hungary, Slovenia, and neighboring regions, with related material appearing further afield through exchange contacts.

Hallstatt communities were organized around a network of elite centers, many fortified on prominent hilltops, from which local rulers or leading families appear to have controlled access to valuable resources, particularly salt and iron ore, and to the long distance trade routes carrying these resources, along with finished goods, across considerable distances. The salt mines at Hallstatt itself, along with related deposits at Hallein and elsewhere in the eastern Alps, produced a commodity essential for food preservation and highly valuable in regional and long distance trade, discussed further in Section 6.9. Iron ore, more widely available than the tin required for bronze, supported growing local production of tools and weapons, even as bronze continued to be prized for ornamental purposes.

The clearest evidence for Hallstatt social hierarchy comes from elite burials, several of which have yielded spectacular grave goods indicating substantial wealth and status differentiation. Wagon burials, in which the deceased was interred along with a four wheeled wagon or cart, sometimes accompanied by horse harness fittings, bronze vessels, imported Mediterranean goods such as Greek or Etruscan pottery and metalwork, and locally produced prestige items, appear at several sites across the Hallstatt zone and demonstrate both considerable local wealth concentration and meaningful contact with Mediterranean civilizations to the south. The famous burial mound at Hochdorf in southwestern Germany, for instance, contained an elaborately furnished burial chamber with a bronze couch, a large bronze cauldron of Mediterranean manufacture that had originally held mead or wine, and gold ornaments, evidence that a single individual, presumably a local ruler or leading aristocrat, commanded resources and long distance connections sufficient to acquire such goods.

Hallstatt fortified centers, sometimes called princely seats by archaeologists, combined defensive fortification with evidence of craft production, storage, and, in several cases, imported Mediterranean luxury goods, suggesting that these sites functioned as political and economic centers controlling their surrounding territory and its trade connections. The Heuneburg, on the upper Danube in southern Germany, is among the best studied of these centers, having yielded evidence of substantial fortification, including, in one building phase, mudbrick construction techniques that appear to reflect Mediterranean architectural influence transmitted through trade contact, along with imported Greek pottery, indicating that Hallstatt elites were directly or indirectly connected to Mediterranean commercial networks reaching as far as the Greek colonial world of the western Mediterranean, examined further in Chapter 9.

Hallstatt society was not uniform across its considerable geographic range. Regional variation in burial customs, settlement organization, and material culture was substantial, and archaeologists generally distinguish, for organizational convenience, an eastern Hallstatt zone, roughly corresponding to Austria, Slovenia, and adjacent areas, from a western Hallstatt zone extending across southern Germany, Switzerland, and eastern France, each with somewhat different characteristic burial practices and material traditions. This regional diversity within a single named archaeological culture illustrates the caution discussed in Section 6.2: the label Hallstatt describes a broad cultural horizon within which considerable local and regional variation persisted.

Section 6.4

La Tène and the Diverse Celtic Worlds

Beginning around the fifth century BCE, a new archaeological culture, named La Tène for a site on the shore of Lake Neuchâtel in Switzerland, developed out of Hallstatt antecedents and spread, over the following centuries, across a remarkably wide swath of temperate and western Europe, including much of modern France, Belgium, southern Germany, Switzerland, Austria, Czechia, and parts of the British Isles, northern Italy, Iberia, and, through later migration discussed below, the Balkans and Anatolia. La Tène material culture is most immediately recognizable through its distinctive art style, characterized by flowing, asymmetrical curvilinear designs, stylized plant and animal motifs, and elaborate metalwork applied to weapons, jewelry, vessels, and horse harness fittings, an artistic tradition of considerable sophistication that developed its own regional variations across the wide territory in which it appeared.

La Tène communities are conventionally, and largely correctly, associated with speakers of Celtic languages, a branch of the Indo European language family whose reconstructed relationships link, among surviving and historically attested tongues, Gaulish, once spoken across much of what is now France, Celtiberian, spoken in parts of Iberia and discussed further in Section 6.8, and the Brittonic and Goidelic languages ancestral to the modern Celtic languages of the British Isles. The Celtic language later established in Brittany, Breton, does not represent straightforward Iron Age continuity in that region; it descends instead from Brittonic speech carried across the Channel by migrants from Britain during Late Antiquity and the early medieval centuries, a much later development outside this chapter's scope. This linguistic connection is genuinely significant and well supported by inscriptional and place name evidence. It is essential, however, that students not translate this linguistic connection into an image of a single unified Celtic people or Celtic state. Celtic speaking communities across the La Tène world were politically fragmented into numerous distinct tribal groupings, later recorded by Roman writers under names such as the Aedui, the Arverni, the Helvetii, and many others in Gaul alone, each with its own leadership, territory, and, frequently, rivalries and conflicts with neighboring Celtic speaking groups every bit as intense as with non Celtic neighbors. There was no Celtic king, no Celtic capital, and no Celtic army in any unified sense; there were, instead, dozens or hundreds of distinct communities who happened to share related languages, broadly similar material culture, and, in many though not all cases, comparable social and religious institutions.

La Tène society displayed considerable social hierarchy, with archaeological and later textual evidence pointing to warrior elites, sometimes described by Roman writers such as Julius Caesar as forming a distinct aristocratic class, alongside a broader population of farmers, craftspeople, and dependents. Caesar and other classical sources also describe a learned class of druids, combining religious, legal, and educational functions, chiefly among some Gaulish and, according to classical sources, British communities; this evidence should not be generalized into a claim that druidism was a uniform institution across all Celtic speaking societies, since testimony is concentrated on a limited number of regions and mediated entirely through Greco-Roman observers. Warfare, raiding, and the display of martial prowess appear to have been significant sources of status, reflected in elaborate weapon burials and the practice, attested archaeologically and confirmed in later Roman accounts, of displaying the severed heads of defeated enemies. Fortified hilltop and lowland settlements of varying scale existed across the La Tène world from an early date, but the classic large fortified towns that Roman writers, including Caesar, called oppida, plural of oppidum, a Latin term for a fortified town, belong principally to the Late Iron Age, developing across much of Gaul and central Europe from roughly the second century BCE onward. These oppida functioned as centers of craft production, trade, and political authority, some housing populations of several thousand people and displaying evidence of planned street layouts, specialized craft workshops, minted coinage, and substantial defensive earthworks, indicating a level of urban and political sophistication considerably beyond simple tribal villages.

La Tène associated populations were also notably mobile across considerable distances during the centuries this chapter examines, a mobility that should be understood as migration, raiding, and settlement by distinct groups rather than the movement of a single unified nation. Around 400 BCE and in subsequent decades, Celtic speaking groups moved into the Po Valley of northern Italy, coming into direct and sometimes violent contact with Etruscan and, eventually, Roman communities; a Celtic force, traditionally remembered as having come from the Senones tribe, is recorded by later Roman tradition as having sacked the city of Rome itself, an event traditionally dated to 387 or 390 BCE, though the precise date and many details rest on later Roman historical writing rather than contemporary documentation and should be treated with appropriate caution, a matter examined further when Chapter 16 addresses Rome's conquest of Italy. In the following century, other Celtic speaking groups moved southeastward into the Balkans, raided Greek sanctuaries including Delphi in 279 BCE according to later Greek accounts, and eventually crossed into Anatolia, where they settled in a region that came to be called Galatia, a name preserving the Greek term for Celtic peoples, Galatai, and where Celtic speaking communities persisted for centuries, eventually becoming the recipients of the New Testament epistle to the Galatians many centuries later, discussed in a very different context in Part IV. These migrations demonstrate the geographic reach and independent historical agency of Celtic speaking communities, operating according to their own political and military logic rather than functioning merely as a backdrop to Mediterranean history.

Historical Debate

Was There a "Celtic" People?

The question: Does the term "Celtic," as commonly used to describe La Tène associated populations across much of Iron Age Europe, describe a genuine ancient people with a shared identity, or is it primarily a modern linguistic and archaeological convenience that risks implying a unity that never existed among the many distinct communities it covers?

Interpretation A — "Celtic" describes a genuine, meaningful ancient linguistic and cultural grouping: Many historians and linguists argue that Celtic speaking communities shared enough in language, religious practice, social structure, and material culture, and were recognized as related by ancient Greek and Roman observers who used related terms such as Keltoi and Celtae, that "Celtic" describes a real and historically meaningful category, even though it does not imply political unity, comparable to how "Slavic" or "Germanic" describe real linguistic groupings today without implying that all Slavic or Germanic speaking peoples ever formed a single nation.
Interpretation B — "Celtic" as commonly used is substantially a modern and often nationalist construction: Other scholars, particularly in recent decades, have argued that the modern concept of a unified "Celtic" identity, especially as applied to the British Isles, owes more to eighteenth and nineteenth century antiquarian and nationalist scholarship than to ancient self identification, since many Iron Age communities labeled Celtic by modern scholars left no evidence that they used any shared name for themselves or recognized a common identity beyond their own immediate tribal or regional community, and some archaeologists caution that applying a single label across such a vast geographic and chronological range risks flattening enormous real diversity.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying linguistic, archaeological, and textual evidence described in this chapter, but differ in how much weight to give ancient Greek and Roman testimony about shared identity, how confidently to extend the term to regions such as the British Isles where the ancient use of a "Celtic" self identity is less directly attested, and how much unity a shared language family genuinely implies.

Why it matters: This debate directly affects how confidently later chapters, and popular historical writing more broadly, can speak of "the Celts" as opposed to more cautious language describing Celtic speaking or La Tène associated communities.

Student task: Using the evidence and cautions presented in Section 6.2 and this section, construct a brief argument for how a careful historian should use the term "Celtic" in a way that acknowledges genuine linguistic and cultural connections without implying political or ethnic unity that the evidence does not support.

Section 6.5

Northern Europe and the Problem of "Germanic" Identity

Northern continental Europe, encompassing much of modern Germany north of the Hallstatt and La Tène zones, along with southern Scandinavia, developed its own distinctive Iron Age archaeological traditions, of which the Jastorf culture, named for a site in Lower Saxony and generally dated from roughly the sixth century BCE onward, is the most extensively studied. Jastorf communities practiced mixed farming, cremation burial with grave goods generally more modest than the elaborate Hallstatt and La Tène elite burials discussed above, and metalworking that, while displaying some contact and exchange with La Tène communities to the south, followed its own distinctive regional trajectory.

The relationship between the Jastorf archaeological culture and the peoples whom later Roman writers, particularly Julius Caesar and, in far greater ethnographic detail, Tacitus in his treatise conventionally known as the Germania, called "Germani" is a matter requiring exceptional caution, and this section follows the Master Instructions' explicit warning against treating archaeological horizons as though they mapped directly onto later Roman ethnonyms. Caesar, writing in the first century BCE primarily to describe and justify his own military campaigns in Gaul, drew a broad distinction between Gauls west of the Rhine and Germani east of it, a distinction that appears to have served Caesar's own political and rhetorical purposes, emphasizing a clear frontier along a convenient river, at least as much as it reflected any clean ethnic or linguistic boundary actually observed by the peoples living in the region. Tacitus, writing more than a century later and never having visited the regions he described, compiled a wide ranging ethnographic account of peoples he called Germani that mixed genuine information, drawn from earlier writers, travelers, and traders, with literary and moralizing purposes of his own, since Tacitus's Germania was written partly to hold up what he portrayed as vigorous Germanic simplicity as an implicit criticism of what he regarded as Roman moral decline.

Modern archaeologists and linguists therefore urge considerable caution before treating the Jastorf culture simply as "the ancient Germans." The label "Germanic" is most defensibly applied today as a linguistic classification, describing a branch of the Indo European language family whose reconstructed common ancestor, proto Germanic, is estimated by historical linguists to have developed somewhere in northern Europe sometime in the first millennium BCE, and from which the later attested Germanic languages, including the language ancestral to English, descend. Whether, and to what extent, Jastorf material culture correlates with the geographic distribution of proto Germanic speakers remains a matter of scholarly inference rather than direct proof, since the Jastorf people left no writing of their own describing their language or self identity, and the correlation between a given archaeological culture and a given language family can never be established with certainty on material evidence alone.

This chapter therefore introduces Jastorf and related northern European Iron Age communities as a genuine and historically significant archaeological horizon, connected through exchange, discussed in Section 6.9, to La Tène communities to the south and to Baltic communities to the east, while explicitly declining to narrate, at this point, the later Roman frontier along the Rhine and Danube, the Roman military campaigns against various peoples Roman writers called Germani, or the migration era movements of the fourth and fifth centuries CE. Those subjects belong to later chapters, particularly Chapter 22's treatment of the Roman Empire's frontiers and Chapter 27's treatment of migration era transformation, examined in Part III and Part IV respectively. What this chapter establishes is simply this: northern European Iron Age societies existed, were materially and economically connected to their southern and eastern neighbors, and should not be read backward through the lens of Roman era ethnonyms or, still less, through the lens of modern national identity.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnonym and Retrospective Identity

An ethnonym is a name applied to a people, either by outside observers or by the community itself, to identify it as a distinct group. Historians must always ask who used a given ethnonym, when, for what purpose, and whether the community so named would have recognized or accepted it. Roman ethnonyms such as "Germani," "Celtae," and "Galli" were frequently applied by outside observers for administrative, military, or rhetorical convenience, and they should not be assumed to correspond precisely to a self conscious ethnic community with fixed boundaries.

Retrospective identity describes the historically common but methodologically dangerous practice of treating a modern national, ethnic, or political identity as though it existed, essentially unchanged, in antiquity, and then reading ancient peoples as the direct and exclusive ancestors of that modern identity. This chapter, consistent with the wider textbook's National Master Narrative Rule, resists this practice throughout, treating ancient linguistic and archaeological categories as historically specific and non-identical with any later national community.

Section 6.6

Italic Peoples before Roman Expansion

The Italian peninsula in the centuries before Roman expansion, examined in detail beginning with Chapter 14, was home to a linguistically and culturally diverse array of communities, most of whom spoke languages belonging to the Italic branch of the Indo European family, though the non Italic Etruscans, whose language remains only partially understood and appears unrelated to Indo European, occupied a central and influential position in Etruria, the region roughly corresponding to modern Tuscany, and are examined in their own right in Chapter 14 alongside the origins of Rome. This chapter introduces the peninsula's broader Italic diversity without narrating Etruscan civilization or early Roman history in detail, since both are assigned specifically to that later chapter.

Among the Italic speaking populations, Latin speaking communities occupied Latium, the region of west central Italy that included, among numerous other settlements, the small community that would eventually become Rome, a development examined in Chapter 14 rather than here. To the east and south, Sabine, Umbrian, and Oscan speaking peoples occupied substantial portions of central and southern Italy, including the Samnites, an Oscan speaking confederation of mountain communities in the central Apennines who would later fight a series of major wars against expanding Roman power, examined in Chapter 16. Further south, Greek colonial cities, established from the eighth century BCE onward and discussed in Chapter 9, occupied much of the coastal south of the peninsula and Sicily in a region later known in Latin as Magna Graecia ("Great Greece"), bringing sustained direct contact between Greek colonists and neighboring Italic and other communities.

These Italic communities were not politically unified, nor did they share a single material culture, even though linguistic evidence indicates that many of their languages were closely related, descending from a common Italic linguistic ancestor. Archaeological evidence indicates considerable regional variation in settlement pattern, burial custom, and material culture, reflecting the peninsula's own considerable internal geographic diversity, discussed in Chapter 2, including its mountainous Apennine spine, its fertile coastal plains, and its position astride major Mediterranean trade routes. Iron Age Italic communities practiced mixed agriculture, engaged in trade with Etruscan cities and, from the eighth century BCE onward, with Greek colonial settlements, and developed increasingly complex settlement hierarchies, with some larger sites showing evidence of defensive fortification, craft specialization, and emerging elite status differentiation broadly comparable, though not identical, to patterns observed in Hallstatt and La Tène Europe.

This chapter's treatment of Italic peoples is deliberately restrained, since a full account of Etruscan civilization, the specific circumstances of Rome's foundation and early monarchy, and the political development of the early Roman Republic belongs to Chapter 14 and subsequent chapters in Part III. What matters for this chapter's purposes is establishing that pre Roman Italy, like Gaul, the Balkans, and Iberia, was a mosaic of distinct peoples and communities, not a landscape already destined to become Roman, and that Rome's eventual rise, examined at length in later chapters, involved the conquest, incorporation, and, over centuries, gradual cultural transformation of these many distinct Italic communities, a process that was neither inevitable nor rapid.

Section 6.7

Illyrian, Thracian, and Balkan Societies

The Balkan Peninsula and the lower Danube basin, regions whose mountainous and fragmented geography was introduced in Chapter 2, were home during the Iron Age to numerous communities that Greek and, later, Roman writers grouped under the broad labels Illyrian, describing peoples of the western Balkans and Adriatic hinterland, and Thracian, describing peoples of the eastern Balkans and lower Danube region. As with the Celtic and Germanic labels discussed above, these terms describe broad and probably internally diverse linguistic and cultural groupings rather than single unified peoples, and the languages associated with both groupings are known today chiefly through place names, personal names recorded by outside observers, and a small number of inscriptions, since neither Illyrian nor Thracian speaking communities left substantial texts of their own, making these languages considerably less well understood than Celtic or Germanic languages.

Illyrian associated communities occupied territory along the eastern Adriatic coast and its mountainous hinterland, in regions corresponding to parts of modern Albania, Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Croatia, where Iron Age hillfort settlements, often strategically positioned to control mountain passes, coastal access, or river valleys, indicate a pattern of regional political organization broadly comparable to, though independently developed from, Hallstatt central Europe. Some Illyrian associated communities developed close and increasingly extensive contact with Greek colonial settlements established along the Adriatic coast from the sixth century BCE onward, a contact examined further in Chapter 9's treatment of Greek colonization, while by the fourth and third centuries BCE, textual evidence indicates the emergence of larger Illyrian associated political confederations capable of contesting power with neighboring Macedonian and, eventually, Roman forces, developments examined further in later chapters covering Rome's eastern Mediterranean expansion.

Thracian associated communities occupied a broad zone extending across the eastern Balkans and the lower Danube basin, in regions corresponding to parts of modern Bulgaria, Romania, northern Greece, and European Turkey, where proximity to the Aegean world, the Black Sea, and the steppe corridor discussed in Chapter 2 placed Thracian associated communities at a genuine crossroads of exchange and cultural contact. Thracian elite burials, including a number of richly furnished tumulus, or mound, burials excavated in modern Bulgaria, have yielded gold and silver vessels, weapons, and horse trappings indicating substantial elite wealth and, in several cases, direct or indirect Persian, Greek, and steppe nomadic artistic and technological influence, reflecting Thracian associated communities' position amid several major cultural and political spheres. By the fifth century BCE, Greek sources describe the emergence of the Odrysian kingdom, a Thracian associated political confederation of sufficient scale and organization to engage directly in Aegean and Black Sea regional politics, including diplomatic and military interaction with Athens and other Greek powers during the era of the Peloponnesian War, examined in Chapter 12, and, in the following century, complex relations with the expanding Macedonian kingdom under Philip II and Alexander the Great.

This chapter's treatment of Illyrian and Thracian societies deliberately avoids reducing them to a peripheral backdrop for Greek or, later, Roman history. The Master Instructions governing this textbook require that the Balkans remain visible as an internally significant historical region rather than merely a neighbor of more thoroughly documented Mediterranean civilizations, and this section's attention to Illyrian and Thracian political development, elite wealth, and cross regional contact reflects that commitment. At the same time, the relative scarcity of surviving textual evidence produced by Illyrian and Thracian speakers themselves, as opposed to evidence produced about them by Greek and Roman outsiders, means that much of what historians can say about these societies rests on archaeological inference and cautious reading of external testimony, a limitation students should keep firmly in view.

Section 6.8

Iberia, the Atlantic West, and the Baltic

The Iberian Peninsula during the Iron Age, like Italy and the Balkans, was home to considerable internal diversity rather than a single unified people. Archaeologists and historians conventionally distinguish several broad regional and linguistic groupings, while cautioning, consistent with this chapter's overall methodology, that these labels describe patterns in the evidence rather than fixed, bounded ethnic nations. Along the southern Atlantic and lower Guadalquivir river region, a distinctive Iron Age society, associated by many scholars with a semi legendary polity that later Greek and Roman writers called Tartessos, flourished chiefly during the eighth through sixth centuries BCE, developing considerable wealth evidently connected to control of regional metal resources, including silver, and to increasingly direct contact with Phoenician traders and colonists who established coastal settlements in the region from perhaps the ninth or eighth century BCE onward, a contact examined further in Chapter 9's broader treatment of Mediterranean colonization. This Tartessian associated florescence underwent major transformation around the middle of the sixth century BCE, though regional continuities persisted afterward in modified form. The archaeological and textual evidence for Tartessos remains genuinely limited and much debated, and this chapter presents it as a documented but incompletely understood regional Iron Age tradition rather than a fully reconstructed kingdom.

Along the eastern and southeastern Mediterranean coast of Iberia, communities that Greek and Roman writers called Iberians, a label that, in a further complication of ancient ethnonym usage, gave its name to the entire peninsula despite describing only a portion of its population, developed distinctive urban settlements, script systems adapted from Phoenician and Greek models, and increasingly close commercial contact with Greek and Phoenician, and later Carthaginian, traders and colonists. In the interior and north central plateau of the peninsula, communities conventionally called Celtiberians spoke an attested Celtic language, securely documented through surviving inscriptions, while developing distinctive regional material cultures, evident in hillfort settlements, weapon burials, and metalwork displaying both continental and Mediterranean influences. The name Celtiberian should not be read as evidence of a simple population mixture of "Celts" arriving from elsewhere and blending with existing "Iberians." Linguistic evidence establishes that a Celtic language was spoken in this region; it does not, by itself, establish any particular story of migration, conquest, or biological mixture, a distinction consistent with this chapter's broader caution, in Section 6.2, against conflating language, material culture, and ancestry. In the western portion of the peninsula, in territory corresponding to much of modern Portugal and western Spain, communities that Roman writers called Lusitanians occupied a region whose linguistic classification remains genuinely disputed among specialists, with the surviving evidence, chiefly a small number of inscriptions, insufficient to establish with confidence whether Lusitanian belonged to the Celtic branch of Indo European or represented a more distinct, if related, Indo European language.

Beyond Iberia, the Atlantic seaboard of western Europe, including the British Isles and the Atlantic coast of Iberia and France, participated in its own long distance maritime exchange networks, evident archaeologically in shared metalworking traditions and recurring patterns of object exchange, connecting communities along the Atlantic facade in patterns partly independent of the overland exchange networks linking Hallstatt and La Tène central Europe, a pattern this chapter treats as a genuine and historically significant Atlantic dimension of Iron Age Europe rather than a marginal extension of continental developments. This maritime and material evidence should be treated separately from linguistic evidence: some of these Atlantic regions, including much of the British Isles, were also home to Celtic speaking populations, discussed above, but a shared or related language is a distinct kind of evidence from shared exchange networks and does not, by itself, establish that Atlantic communities understood themselves as a single connected people.

To the northeast, the Baltic region, comprising the eastern Baltic coast and its hinterland in territory corresponding to parts of modern Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, and Kaliningrad, developed its own regional Iron Age societies, generally organized around smaller scale agricultural settlements than the elite centered hierarchies of Hallstatt or the fortified oppida of La Tène Europe, but connected to the wider European exchange system chiefly through the amber trade, examined further in Section 6.9. Linguistically, the southern and central portions of this region were home to communities speaking Baltic languages, a distinct branch of Indo European related to, but separate from, the Slavic languages that would become dominant across much of eastern Europe many centuries later, a later development examined in Part VI rather than here. The northern eastern Baltic, in territory corresponding to much of modern Estonia and neighboring areas, was instead home to communities speaking Finnic languages, part of the Uralic family discussed in Chapter 1 rather than the Indo European family, with substantial and prolonged contact, but uncertain and likely shifting prehistoric boundaries, between Baltic speaking and Finnic speaking populations. This chapter's attention to the Baltic region, following the explicit direction that Chapter 6 should not become an exclusively Celtic, Germanic, and Mediterranean narrative, ensures that northeastern Europe remains visible as a genuine participant in, rather than a remote periphery of, continental Iron Age history.

Section 6.9

Networks across Iron Age Europe

Having surveyed the major regional societies of Iron Age Europe in the preceding sections, this chapter now turns to the theme that connects them all: the extensive networks of exchange that linked communities across enormous distances, moving raw materials, finished goods, technologies, and, inevitably, ideas and cultural influences among peoples who might never meet directly and who, in many cases, spoke mutually unintelligible languages. These networks are, in an important sense, as historically significant as any single regional society examined above, because they demonstrate that Iron Age Europe, for all its political fragmentation and linguistic diversity, functioned as a genuinely interconnected historical space rather than a patchwork of isolated communities.

Iron itself, once smelting knowledge had spread, could increasingly be produced locally in regions with accessible ore, reducing, though never eliminating, the older Bronze Age dependence on long distance tin trade discussed in Chapter 5. Even so, particular iron producing regions, including the Noric region of the eastern Alps, whose high quality iron and steel became renowned enough to be specifically praised by later Roman writers, developed reputations and trade relationships that carried their products well beyond their immediate locality, demonstrating that iron production, like bronze production before it, could still generate specialized regional economies feeding into wider exchange networks rather than reducing every community to pure self sufficiency.

Salt, essential for food preservation and human and animal nutrition, as discussed in Chapter 2's treatment of European resources, remained one of the most consistently valuable commodities moved through Iron Age exchange networks, and control of major salt sources, as at Hallstatt itself and at Hallein in the eastern Alps, appears to have been directly connected to the elite wealth and fortified centers discussed in Section 6.3. Salt produced in the Alps moved outward through river and overland routes to communities across central Europe, exchanged for other goods, agricultural surplus, or the loyalty and labor of dependent populations that underpinned Hallstatt elite power.

Amber, fossilized tree resin gathered chiefly along the Baltic coast, particularly in areas corresponding to modern Poland's Baltic shore and the Kaliningrad region, traveled along a set of overland routes that scholars conventionally call the amber routes, connecting Baltic gathering sites to central European exchange hubs and, ultimately, to Mediterranean markets in Italy, Greece, and beyond, where amber's warm color and unusual electrostatic properties made it a highly prized material for jewelry and ornamental objects among Mediterranean elites who had no direct access to the Baltic coast themselves. Archaeologists have traced amber objects found in Mycenaean Greek burials, discussed in Chapter 7, and in Etruscan and other Italian contexts, back to Baltic sources through chemical analysis, demonstrating that this exchange network operated across many centuries and connected communities separated by well over a thousand kilometers of overland travel, crossing the Alps and other significant geographic barriers discussed in Chapter 2.

Mediterranean goods moved in the opposite direction along many of these same routes and others besides. Greek and, somewhat later, Etruscan wine, transported in distinctive amphorae, along with fine painted pottery, bronze vessels such as the large cauldron found at Hochdorf discussed in Section 6.3, and other prestige manufactured goods, traveled northward into Hallstatt and, later, La Tène central Europe, into Iberia, and into the Balkans, exchanged, in many cases, for raw materials, including metals, furs, amber passed onward from the Baltic, and, controversially and much debated among historians, enslaved people, since ancient Mediterranean societies, examined in Parts II and III, relied substantially on slavery, and evidence, both archaeological and textual, suggests that captives taken in warfare or raiding among Iron Age European communities entered Mediterranean slave markets through exactly these exchange networks.

These networks depended on the geographic features introduced in Chapter 2: river corridors, particularly the Rhône, Rhine, Danube, and Elbe, that connected the Mediterranean world to central and northern Europe; Alpine passes that, despite the difficulties discussed in Chapter 2, permitted the movement of goods, and occasionally armies, across a formidable mountain barrier; and coastal sea routes along the Atlantic, the Adriatic, and the Baltic that complemented overland exchange. No single Iron Age community controlled this entire system, and no single political authority coordinated it. It functioned instead through countless local and regional exchanges, often mediated by successive intermediary communities who each took a share of value as goods passed through their territory, a pattern of relay trade that helps explain why finished Mediterranean goods, and raw Baltic amber, could travel such enormous distances despite the political fragmentation this chapter has emphasized throughout.

Section 6.10

Review Checkpoint: Closing Part I

This chapter has completed Part I of the textbook, the Part devoted to Europe before classical civilization. Chapter 3 introduced the Paleolithic hunter gatherers who first inhabited the continent, adapting across tens of thousands of years to glacial and post glacial environments described in Chapter 2. Chapter 4 traced the Neolithic transformation, the spread of agriculture, animal domestication, and permanent settlement that reshaped population, diet, social organization, and the landscape itself. Chapter 5 examined the Bronze Age, monument building, metallurgy, and long distance exchange networks that produced increasingly hierarchical societies across much of the continent. This chapter has carried that story into the Iron Age, examining how ironworking technology spread unevenly, how Hallstatt and La Tène communities developed increasingly complex central European societies, how northern, Italic, Balkan, Iberian, Atlantic, and Baltic communities each followed their own regional trajectories, and how extensive exchange networks in iron, salt, amber, and Mediterranean goods connected these diverse societies without ever producing political unification.

The most important methodological lesson of this chapter, and one that should carry forward through the rest of this textbook, is this: the labels historians use for ancient peoples, Celtic, Germanic, Italic, Illyrian, Thracian, Iberian, and others, describe complex, internally diverse, and historically specific groupings identified through a combination of archaeological, linguistic, and textual evidence, not single unified nations comparable to modern states. There was no ancient France populated by a unified nation of Gauls waiting to become French, no ancient Germany populated by a fixed German nation, no single Celtic state, and no neat, one to one relationship between archaeological culture, language, ancestry, and later political identity. Students who carry this lesson forward will be far better equipped to read later chapters on Roman expansion, migration era transformation, and the formation of medieval kingdoms without falling into the trap of imagining ancient peoples as the direct, singular ancestors of modern nations.

Part II now begins with Chapter 7, which turns to the Greek world. It is essential that students understand that Chapter 7 does not continue directly from the chronological endpoint of this chapter. Rather than proceeding forward in time from the Iron Age societies just examined, Chapter 7 turns back in time to the Aegean Bronze Age, examining the Minoan and Mycenaean world that flourished many centuries before, during, and, in its collapse, well before the Hallstatt and La Tène societies described here reached their fullest development. Part I has surveyed the whole of prehistoric and early historic Europe broadly; Part II now narrows its lens to trace one regional trajectory, the Greek world, in much greater depth, beginning from its own Bronze Age origins. Many of the Iron Age societies examined in this chapter, particularly Celtic speaking La Tène communities and Illyrian and Thracian communities of the Balkans, remained historically active and significant for centuries after Chapter 7's Aegean narrative begins, and several of them, as this chapter has shown, came into direct contact and conflict with the Greek world explored in the chapters that follow.

Primary Source Window

Greek and Roman Observers on Iron Age Peoples: Poseidonios, Caesar, and Tacitus

Creators: Poseidonios of Apamea, Greek Stoic philosopher, writing in the second and first centuries BCE, largely surviving only through later authors who quoted or summarized him; Julius Caesar, Roman general and statesman, writing his Commentarii de Bello Gallico in the 50s BCE; Tacitus, Roman senator and historian, writing his Germania around 98 CE · Type: Ethnographic and military-political description of non Mediterranean European peoples by outside observers

Context: Poseidonios traveled in the western Mediterranean and wrote on Celtic peoples; the extent and firsthand character of his observations is difficult to reconstruct, since his work survives chiefly through later writers, including the geographer Strabo, who drew heavily on it and adapted it for their own purposes. Caesar composed his account of the Gallic Wars partly as a firsthand military record and partly as political self promotion directed at a Roman audience. Tacitus, writing more than a century after Caesar and without personal travel to the regions he described, compiled an ethnographic survey of peoples he called Germani, drawing on earlier writers and travelers' reports; his work has often been interpreted, though this remains a matter of scholarly debate rather than settled fact, as intended partly as a moral contrast with what he regarded as troubling decline in Rome itself.

Paraphrase of themes across Poseidonios, Caesar, and Tacitus Across these three authors, common themes recur: descriptions of feasting, hospitality, and status display among warrior elites; observations of a learned class, called druids in some Gaulish and, according to classical testimony, British communities, who combined legal, educational, and ritual functions; accounts of tribal political organization involving assemblies, councils, and elected or hereditary leaders; and descriptions of warfare, including the display of enemies' heads as trophies among some Celtic speaking groups, presented by these Greco-Roman authors as evidence of a society both admirable in certain respects, such as courage and hospitality, and troublingly foreign or "barbarian" in others.

Analysis: These accounts remain indispensable to historians because they provide detail about political organization, religious practice, and social custom that archaeology alone cannot recover. At the same time, each author wrote with specific purposes and limitations. Caesar sought to justify and glorify his own conquests. Tacitus's moralizing purpose, contrasting an idealized Germanic simplicity with Roman decline, is a widely held scholarly interpretation rather than a certainty the text itself announces. Even Poseidonios, a careful observer by ancient standards, filtered his reporting through Greek philosophical categories about civilization and its opposite, and his original observations reach us only through later authors' selective quotation and adaptation. In no case can historians establish how fluently these authors, or the informants and intermediaries on whom they relied, knew the local languages of the peoples described; much of their information was necessarily mediated through interpreters, traders, and earlier written sources rather than direct, unmediated fluency in local speech.

  2. Why might Tacitus's rhetorical purpose in the Germania, contrasting Germanic virtue with Roman decline, distort his ethnographic description?
  3. What are the limitations of relying on outside observers, none of whom were native speakers of the languages of the peoples they described, for understanding those peoples' own self identity?

Multiple Perspectives

Living within Iron Age Networks: Perspectives on Connection and Difference

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists and historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Hallstatt elite controlling a salt producing center: Wealth and status flow from controlling the routes along which salt, iron, and imported Mediterranean goods move, and a fortified hilltop residence, filled with bronze vessels acquired from distant Greek and Etruscan traders, displays a family's standing to rivals and dependents alike, even though most of one's daily political concerns involve nearby neighboring communities rather than distant peoples one will never meet.
From the perspective of a La Tène warrior in a Gaulish tribal community: Loyalty belongs first to one's own tribe and its leading families, and rival Celtic speaking peoples across the river or over the next hill are as likely to be enemies as any non Celtic speaking neighbor; a shared artistic style or similar language does not by itself create common cause, and honor won in raiding or warfare against a neighboring Celtic speaking tribe matters as much as any conflict with outsiders.
From the perspective of a Baltic amber gatherer: The resin gathered along this shore will travel, through many hands and many intermediary trades, to distant Mediterranean buyers who know nothing of the community that gathered it, exchanged along the way for goods, and perhaps metal, that never would have reached this coast otherwise, connecting a small northern community to a much larger world without that community ever needing to travel far from home.
From the perspective of a Greek merchant trading along the Adriatic coast: The Illyrian associated communities inland are, in Greek eyes, foreign and often described using a language of civilization and its absence that flattens their genuine political and social complexity, yet their goods, their willingness to trade, and their control of interior routes make them essential trading partners rather than merely subjects for ethnographic curiosity.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Bronze Age Networks to Iron Age Societies

What Changed

  • Iron, more widely available than the tin required for bronze, gradually reduced, though never eliminated, dependence on long distance tin trade for basic tools and weapons
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward larger fortified centers, culminating, by the later La Tène period, in substantial oppida with planned layouts and specialized craft production
  • Political organization in several regions, including La Tène Gaul and the Thracian Odrysian kingdom, developed toward larger, more complex confederations than typical Bronze Age chiefdoms
  • Contact with the Mediterranean world intensified considerably as Greek and Phoenician colonization brought sustained commercial relationships to Iberian, Italic, Illyrian, and other Iron Age communities

What Stayed the Same

  • Elite status continued to be displayed through wealth in prestige goods, elaborate burial, and control of valuable resources and trade routes, a pattern established already in the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5
  • Long distance exchange networks continued to move raw materials and finished goods across great distances through relay trade among many intermediary communities
  • Political fragmentation into numerous distinct, often rival, local and regional communities remained the norm across nearly every region examined in this chapter
  • Bronze remained valued for ornamental and prestige purposes even as iron became dominant for everyday tools and weapons

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents treating the Iron Age as a wholly new beginning severed from Bronze Age foundations, while still allowing students to appreciate genuine transformations in technology, settlement, and political scale that distinguish the societies examined in this chapter from their Bronze Age predecessors.

Cause and Effect

Why Iron Age Exchange Networks Expanded

Long-term causes

The wide geographic distribution of iron ore, compared to the rarer tin needed for bronze, allowed more communities to produce metal goods locally, while established Bronze Age trade routes and river and coastal geography, discussed in Chapter 2, provided ready made corridors for expanded exchange.

Short-term causes

Greek and Phoenician colonization from the eighth century BCE onward created new, closer Mediterranean trading partners for Iberian, Italic, Illyrian, and other Iron Age communities, while growing elite demand for prestige goods encouraged local rulers to invest in controlling and expanding trade routes.

Effect

Goods such as amber, salt, iron, wine, and fine pottery moved across distances of hundreds or thousands of kilometers, connecting communities from the Baltic to the Aegean and from Iberia to the Black Sea in an interlocking system of relay trade.

This chapter's caution

Extensive exchange did not produce political unification; connected communities remained politically fragmented and often rivalrous, a reminder that economic interdependence and political unity are separate historical phenomena.

Historical complexity: The same exchange networks that carried prestige goods and raw materials also, evidence suggests, carried captives taken in warfare and raiding into Mediterranean slave markets, a reminder that long distance trade in this period cannot be separated from the violence of warfare and raiding among Iron Age communities themselves.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life beyond the Elite Burial

Much of the evidence discussed in this chapter, elaborate wagon burials, fortified elite centers, and imported Mediterranean luxury goods, illuminates chiefly the lives of Iron Age elites, since wealthy burials and imposing settlements survive archaeologically far more visibly than the modest farmsteads and simple burials of ordinary families. Most people across Iron Age Europe lived not in fortified hilltop centers or oppida but in small agricultural settlements, farming cereals and legumes, raising cattle, sheep, and pigs, and supplementing their diet through hunting, gathering, and fishing where local conditions permitted, continuing patterns of mixed farming established since the Neolithic transformation examined in Chapter 4.

Archaeologists reconstruct the lives of these ordinary populations chiefly through settlement excavation, examining house forms, storage pits, agricultural tools, and animal bone assemblages, rather than through the richly furnished elite burials that dominate popular imagination of the Iron Age. This evidence suggests that most households produced much of what they needed themselves, while also participating, whether directly or through obligations to local elites, in the wider exchange networks discussed in Section 6.9, since even modest farming communities required, at minimum, iron tools, and often participated in local exchange of surplus agricultural goods, textiles, or craft products for items, such as salt, that they could not produce locally.

Women's roles in Iron Age European societies are known chiefly through burial evidence, which in several La Tène and Hallstatt contexts indicates that some women held considerable status, evident in occasional richly furnished female burials comparable in wealth, though not always in specific grave goods, to male elite burials, suggesting that at least some women in these societies could inherit or hold independent status and property, though the precise nature of women's political authority remains debated and almost certainly varied by region and period. Textile production, evident archaeologically through spindle whorls, loom weights, and, in exceptionally well preserved contexts such as Hallstatt's salt mines, actual fragments of woven cloth, appears to have been a significant and widespread household activity, likely performed chiefly, though probably not exclusively, by women, producing clothing and, in some cases, valuable woven goods that entered exchange networks alongside the metal and mineral commodities more often emphasized in archaeological discussion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has completed Part I of the textbook by surveying the diverse and interconnected societies of Iron Age Europe, from the introduction of ironworking technology through the eve of extensive Mediterranean written documentation. It has emphasized throughout that the transition from bronze to iron was a regionally uneven process spanning many centuries, driven chiefly by the wider distribution of iron ore rather than any simple technological superiority, and that bronze remained valued for many purposes long after iron became dominant for tools and weapons.

The chapter established a methodological foundation, distinguishing archaeological cultures, linguistic categories, and ethnonyms recorded by outside observers, and explaining why none of these forms of evidence should be treated as though they described single, unified, bounded ethnic nations comparable to modern states. This methodological caution ran through the chapter's survey of particular regional societies: the Hallstatt culture's fortified elite centers and salt and iron wealth in central Europe; the La Tène culture's distinctive art, oppida, and wide ranging Celtic speaking communities, whose considerable geographic mobility carried them into Italy, the Balkans, and Anatolia without ever producing a unified Celtic state; the Jastorf culture of northern Europe, whose relationship to later Roman ethnonyms such as "Germani" requires particular caution; the linguistically diverse Italic peoples of the Italian peninsula, introduced without anticipating the separate history of early Rome assigned to Chapter 14; the Illyrian and Thracian associated communities of the Balkans and lower Danube, including the historically significant Odrysian kingdom; and the internally diverse societies of Iberia, the Atlantic west, and the Baltic, including Tartessian, Iberian, Celtiberian, and Lusitanian associated communities and the linguistically diverse populations of northeastern Europe, including Baltic-speaking communities farther south and Finnic-speaking communities in the northern eastern Baltic.

A central theme of the chapter has been the extensive networks of exchange, in iron, salt, amber, and Mediterranean prestige goods, that connected these diverse societies across enormous distances without producing political unification. These networks depended on the river corridors, mountain passes, and coastal routes introduced in Chapter 2, and they demonstrate that Iron Age Europe, for all its political fragmentation and linguistic diversity, functioned as a genuinely interconnected historical space.

The chapter's most important lesson, reinforced throughout, is methodological: the labels historians use for Iron Age peoples describe complex, evidence based, and historically specific groupings, not single unified ancient nations, and modern national identities should never be projected backward onto them. Students who carry this lesson forward will be better prepared to understand later chapters covering Roman expansion, migration era transformation, and the formation of medieval kingdoms without falling into the trap of treating ancient peoples as the direct, exclusive ancestors of modern nations.

Part II now turns to the Greek world, beginning in Chapter 7 with the Aegean Bronze Age. This transition involves a chronological turn backward, not a continuation forward, and many of the Iron Age societies examined in this chapter remained historically significant for centuries after Chapter 7's narrative begins.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Iron Age
An archaeological period convention describing the era, beginning at different times in different regions, during which iron became the dominant metal for tools and weapons, following the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5.
Bloomery
A simple type of furnace used to smelt iron ore into a spongy mass called a bloom, which required repeated heating and hammering, or forging, to produce usable iron or steel.
Archaeological culture
A recurring, geographically bounded pattern of material remains identified by archaeologists, not necessarily corresponding to a single ethnic group, language, or political community.
Ethnonym
A name applied to a people, by outside observers or by the community itself, that requires careful evaluation of who used it, when, and for what purpose.
Retrospective identity
The historically common but methodologically dangerous practice of treating a modern national or ethnic identity as though it existed unchanged in antiquity.
Hallstatt culture
An early Iron Age archaeological culture, named for a salt mining site in Austria, associated with elite burials, fortified centers, and salt and iron wealth across central Europe, roughly 800 to 450 BCE.
La Tène culture
A later Iron Age archaeological culture, named for a site in Switzerland, associated with a distinctive curvilinear art style, oppida, and widespread Celtic speaking communities from roughly 500 BCE onward.
Celtic languages
A branch of the Indo European language family, including Gaulish, Celtiberian, and the Brittonic and Goidelic languages of the British Isles, spoken across a wide but politically fragmented range of Iron Age communities; Breton in Brittany descends from a later, early medieval migration rather than direct Iron Age continental continuity.
Oppidum (plural: oppida)
A Latin term, used by writers such as Julius Caesar, for large fortified towns developed by La Tène associated Celtic speaking communities from roughly the second century BCE onward.
Druids
A learned class, described chiefly by Roman writers such as Julius Caesar, combining religious, legal, and educational functions among some Gaulish and, according to classical testimony, British communities; not securely attested as a uniform institution across all Celtic speaking societies.
Jastorf culture
An Iron Age archaeological culture of northern Germany and southern Scandinavia, cautiously and incompletely associated by some scholars with early Germanic speaking populations.
Germani
A Roman ethnonym, used by Julius Caesar and later by Tacitus, for peoples generally located east of the Rhine, whose relationship to a single coherent ethnic or linguistic community remains historically uncertain.
Italic peoples
Linguistically related but politically distinct communities of the Italian peninsula before Roman expansion, including Latin, Sabine, Oscan, and Umbrian speaking populations.
Magna Graecia
The Latin name, meaning "Great Greece," for the Greek-colonized regions of southern Italy and Sicily settled by Greek colonists from the eighth century BCE onward.
Illyrian associated communities
Iron Age societies of the western Balkans and Adriatic hinterland, known chiefly through archaeology and later Greek and Roman description.
Thracian associated communities
Iron Age societies of the eastern Balkans and lower Danube, including the historically documented Odrysian kingdom.
Odrysian kingdom
A Thracian associated political confederation, prominent from the fifth century BCE, that engaged directly in Aegean and Black Sea regional politics.
Tartessos
A semi legendary Iron Age polity or cultural tradition of southwestern Iberia, associated with considerable wealth from regional metal resources and early Phoenician contact, known through limited and much debated evidence.
Celtiberians
Communities of the Iberian interior and north-central plateau who spoke an attested Celtic language and developed distinctive regional material cultures; the name does not establish a simple biological or migratory mixture of "Celts" and "Iberians."
Lusitanians
Communities of western Iberia, in territory corresponding to much of modern Portugal and western Spain, whose precise linguistic classification remains disputed among specialists.
Amber routes
Long distance overland exchange routes carrying amber from the Baltic coast to central Europe and, ultimately, to Mediterranean markets.
Noric iron
High quality iron and steel produced in the Noric region of the eastern Alps, renowned enough to be specifically praised by later Roman writers.
Relay trade
A pattern of exchange in which goods pass through many intermediary communities on their way to a distant destination, each taking a share of value along the route.
Baltic languages
A distinct branch of the Indo European language family, spoken by communities of the southern and central eastern Baltic coast and its hinterland, related to but separate from the Slavic languages; the northern eastern Baltic, including much of modern Estonia, was instead home to Finnic speaking, Uralic language communities, with substantial contact between the two zones.
Wagon burial
A form of elite burial, found at several Hallstatt sites, in which the deceased was interred with a four wheeled wagon or cart, indicating substantial wealth and status.
Linguistic reconstruction
The scholarly method of comparing surviving inscriptions, place names, and related languages to reconstruct the relationships and features of poorly attested or unattested ancient languages.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has closed Part I of the textbook by presenting Iron Age Europe not as a single civilization, and not as a mere waiting room before Greece and Rome, but as a continent of genuinely diverse, dynamic, and interconnected societies, each with its own history worth understanding on its own terms. Hallstatt elites controlling salt and iron, La Tène warriors and craftspeople whose art and mobility carried them from Iberia to Anatolia, northern communities whose relationship to later Roman ethnonyms remains genuinely uncertain, Italic peoples whose linguistic diversity preceded Rome's rise, Illyrian and Thracian communities engaging directly with Aegean and Black Sea politics, and Iberian, Atlantic, and Baltic societies at Europe's western and northeastern edges, all deserve to be understood as historical actors in their own right, not merely as background figures in someone else's story.

The methodological lessons of this chapter, particularly the caution against treating archaeological cultures, linguistic categories, and Greek and Roman ethnonyms as though they described single unified ancient nations, will matter throughout the rest of this textbook. Students will encounter these same interpretive challenges again when later chapters examine Roman provincial identity, migration era peoples, and the formation of medieval kingdoms whose rulers and chroniclers often claimed, for their own political purposes, a continuous descent from ancient peoples that the evidence itself cannot fully support.

This chapter has also modeled a way of thinking about connection without unity, a theme that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of trade, religion, migration, and culture across many centuries. Iron Age Europe's amber routes, salt trade, and Mediterranean exchange demonstrate that extensive interconnection and enduring political fragmentation are not contradictions but two aspects of the same historical reality, a pattern students will see again in medieval commercial networks, early modern colonial systems, and even the modern European Union examined in Chapter 134.

Part II now turns to the Greek world, beginning with the Aegean Bronze Age in Chapter 7. This is a chronological turn backward in time, not a continuation forward from this chapter, and many of the societies examined here, particularly Celtic speaking communities and Illyrian and Thracian peoples, remained historically active for centuries after the narrative Chapter 7 begins. Understanding the diverse, interconnected Iron Age Europe this chapter has described, will help students recognize, throughout the following chapters on Greece and Rome, that classical Mediterranean civilization developed alongside, in contact with, and sometimes in conflict with, an equally significant, equally dynamic wider European world.
