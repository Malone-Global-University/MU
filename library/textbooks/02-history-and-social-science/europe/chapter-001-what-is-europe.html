What Is Europe?

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

"Europe" is not a landmass with a natural border, not a single people, and not simply another name for Christendom or "the West." It is a historically formed geographic, cultural, and political idea, whose boundaries, meaning, and membership have been argued over for roughly twenty five centuries and remain contested today.

Open almost any atlas, news broadcast, or general encyclopedia and you will encounter the word "Europe" used as though its meaning were self evident: a continent stretching from Portugal's Atlantic coast to the Ural Mountains, from Norway's Arctic fjords to Crete's Mediterranean shores, home today to the twenty seven member states of the European Union among many others. And yet, when examined closely, almost every assumption bound up in the word "Europe" turns out to require careful definition. Where, precisely, does Europe end and Asia begin? Is Russia European, Asian, or both, depending on the period and the question being asked? Does "Europe" mean the same thing as "Christendom," "the West," or "the European Union," and if not, where exactly do these overlapping but distinct categories diverge?

Consider a single illustrative complication. A Muslim Bosnian villager in the mountains of the Balkans, an Orthodox Christian farmer on the Russian steppe west of the Urals, a Sephardic Jewish merchant in sixteenth century Thessaloniki, a Sami reindeer herder from Sápmi, the traditional Sami homeland spanning northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula, and a Roman Catholic bureaucrat in eighteenth century Vienna might each have a claim to belonging within "Europe," or might not, depending on how the term is defined: through geography, through religion, through political citizenship, or through some blend of all three. None of these individuals is a hypothetical curiosity invented to complicate an otherwise simple picture. Each represents a real, historically documented category of person whose presence within the space this textbook studies is central, not peripheral, to understanding its history.

This chapter does not begin with ancient Greece, the Roman Empire, or the European Union. It begins with something more basic, a set of definitions, and a set of warnings about definitions that go wrong. Loose or careless usage of terms such as "Europe," "European," "Christendom," "the West," and "the European Union" is not merely an academic inconvenience. It has shaped, and distorted, how outsiders and insiders alike have understood this region's history, sometimes erasing entire peoples, such as the Sami, the Roma, various Uralic-speaking and Turkic-speaking communities, Jews, Muslims, and others, from a story imagined as uniformly and eternally "European," and sometimes conflating a landmass and a culture with a single religion or a single modern institution in ways that misrepresent both.

This textbook studies a region defined chiefly by an evolving geographic space, by the political and cultural communities that have inhabited it, and by the shifting historical meanings that "Europe" has carried across time, while remaining attentive throughout to the many peoples within and alongside that space who cannot simply be folded into an undifferentiated "European" identity. Before any word resembling "Europe" existed as a meaningful geographic or cultural category, ancient societies inhabited these lands, such as early hunter gatherer bands, Neolithic farming communities, and Bronze and Iron Age peoples across the continent, and this textbook describes those societies as what they were, not as an anachronistic "ancient Europe" understood in the modern sense. Only later, through a long historical process beginning with ancient Greek geographic writing and continuing through Roman, Christian, medieval, and modern reinterpretation, did "Europe" come to carry the range of meanings it now holds.

Understanding how "Europe" came to mean what it now means, and understanding what it still does not mean, requires the same historical toolkit historians use to study any large, diverse, and long lived civilizational region: careful attention to chronology, geography, language, religion, evidence, and the political purposes behind competing national and religious narratives. It also requires humility. Europeans themselves have long debated, and continue to debate, where the boundaries of "Europe" lie, who belongs within it, and what that belonging should mean politically, culturally, and religiously.

This chapter establishes the vocabulary, geographic scope, chronological boundaries, and historical methodological commitments that will govern the following one hundred and forty three chapters. It explains why the book is titled a history of "Europe," organized across six Volumes and twenty one Parts, rather than a history of "Christendom," "the West," or the European Union alone, and it explains why those distinctions, often blurred in casual usage, matter enormously to writing this history honestly.

Finally, this chapter asks students to consider why any of this matters beyond the classroom. Confusing "Europe" with "Christendom," or "the West" with "Europe," or the European Union with the entire continent's history, has had real consequences: in politics, in popular understanding of migration and identity, and in the way millions of people's belonging is represented or misrepresented by outsiders and by Europeans themselves. Learning to define this region carefully is not merely a scholarly exercise in semantics. It is the necessary first step toward understanding, accurately and without anachronism, one of the most historically significant and internally diverse regions on earth.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Distinguish "Europe" from "Christendom," "the West," and "the European Union" as overlapping but non identical categories.
  3. Explain why the boundary between Europe and Asia is a historically constructed convention rather than a natural physical division.
  4. Identify major peoples and regions historically present within or alongside European history, including Byzantine and Orthodox societies, Jewish and Muslim communities, the Sami, the Roma, and various Uralic-speaking and Turkic-speaking populations.
  5. Describe the major geographic regions this textbook studies, including the Mediterranean world, western Europe, central Europe, northern Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia.
  6. Explain the chronological scope of this textbook, from prehistoric Europe through the contemporary European order.
  7. Analyze why applying the label "European" uncritically to ancient or medieval societies, or projecting the modern European Union backward in time, would be anachronistic.
  8. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of geographic, religious, cultural, and political definitions of European identity.
  9. Assess how modern national, religious, colonial, and integrationist narratives have shaped popular understanding of "Europe."
  10. Apply careful historical and geographic terminology when discussing regions, peoples, and periods introduced in this chapter.
  11. Contextualize the study of Europe within wider Mediterranean, African, and Asian historical processes.
  12. Identify the kinds of historical evidence, archaeological, textual, cartographic, and administrative, that allow historians to trace the emergence and transformation of the idea of Europe over time.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook, the prehistoric and ancient foundations of Volume I, the medieval Christendoms of Volume II, the Renaissance and dynastic states of Volume III, the Enlightenment and revolutionary transformations of Volume IV, the catastrophic wars of Volume V, and the Cold War and integration of Volume VI, they need a shared, carefully defined vocabulary.

The lands this textbook eventually calls "Europe" span an extraordinary range of environments and historical experiences: the Mediterranean coastlines of Iberia, Italy, Greece, and the Balkans; the great river plains of the Rhine, the Danube, the Vistula, the Dnieper, and the Volga; the Atlantic seaboard of France, the British Isles, and the Iberian Peninsula; the North and Baltic Sea littoral of the Low Countries, Germany, and Scandinavia; the mountain systems of the Alps, the Pyrenees, the Carpathians, and the Caucasus foothills; and the vast plains of European Russia stretching toward the Ural Mountains. No single ancient civilization, language, or political authority unified all of these regions before, or indeed after, the classical period. Long before any of them could meaningfully be called part of a single "Europe," they were home to varied prehistoric societies: Paleolithic hunter gatherers, Neolithic farming communities, and Bronze and Iron Age peoples whose material remains are examined on their own terms in the chapters that follow.

Historians studying this region must therefore resist two opposite errors. The first error treats "Europe" as a timeless, unchanging civilizational category stretching back to remote antiquity, projecting a modern or medieval European identity onto Paleolithic hunters, Bronze Age metalworkers, or classical Greeks who would not have recognized it in anything like its current sense. The second error, a reaction against the first, sometimes minimizes the genuine historical significance of the idea of Europe once it did emerge, treating it as merely a modern political invention with no roots in earlier centuries. This textbook adopts neither error. It describes the pre European societies of the continent honestly and on their own terms, and it treats the emergence, spread, and transformation of the idea of Europe from antiquity onward as one of the most significant developments in world history, without claiming that a unified European identity was always present or that it ever fully erased the continent's other peoples, languages, and religious communities.

A further complication concerns religion. Christianity spread across much of the continent between late antiquity and the early medieval centuries, and for many centuries "Christendom" functioned, in the minds of many of its own inhabitants, as a more meaningful category of belonging than any geographic "Europe." This has led, in much popular usage, to a conflation of "European" and "Christian." But the two categories have never been identical. Substantial Jewish communities have lived across the continent for roughly two thousand years. Muslim political and cultural presence shaped Iberia, Sicily, and, from the fourteenth century onward, large parts of southeastern Europe under Ottoman rule. Pre Christian religious traditions persisted in some regions for centuries after Christianization began elsewhere. Conversely, Christianity itself has never been religiously uniform across the continent: Latin Christianity in the west, Orthodox Christianity in the east and southeast, and, from the sixteenth century, multiple competing Protestant traditions, have all coexisted, often uneasily, within the space this textbook studies. Section 1.4 of this chapter examines the relationship between "Europe" and "Christendom" in more depth.

Section 1.1

A Question Before a Narrative

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France, Japan, or Brazil, the geographic and political referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a single, currently existing state. A history of "Europe" has no such anchor. It is a history of a geographic, cultural, and political space encompassing dozens of modern states, hundreds of millions of people, multiple major religious traditions, and several distinct sub regions, the Mediterranean, western Europe, central Europe, northern Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia, each with its own historical trajectory before, during, and after the periods when they became linked by shared institutions, religions, or political systems.

This opening section establishes the central interpretive commitment of the entire textbook: Europe is the organizing subject, not a synonym for Christendom, "the West," or the European Union. This commitment has several consequences that will recur throughout the following chapters. First, this textbook does not begin its narrative with Christianity, because doing so would make the fourth century Christianization of the Roman world an arbitrary starting point for a continent whose human history extends back tens of thousands of years and whose ancient civilizations, Aegean, Greek, and Roman, developed many centuries before Christianity existed at all. Second, this textbook does not simply substitute "the West" for "Europe," because "the West" as commonly used often includes non European regions, such as North America and Australia, while sometimes excluding parts of Europe, such as Orthodox and Ottoman controlled southeastern Europe, that this textbook treats as fully European. Third, this textbook does not treat "Europe" as a religious category, because European identity, however it is defined, has never mapped perfectly onto Christian religious identity in either direction, given the continent's substantial Jewish and Muslim populations across many centuries.

None of these clarifications is intended to diminish the historical importance of Christianity, of "the West" as a category of study in other contexts, or of the deep connections between European history and Christian institutional history. Christianity is, and will remain throughout this textbook, one of the central subjects of European history from late antiquity onward, and many chapters, particularly in Parts V through X, necessarily give Christian religious, institutional, and intellectual history sustained and serious attention. But treating "Europe" and "Christendom" as interchangeable terms, or treating European history as simply a story that culminates in the European Union, would misrepresent the actual historical record this textbook sets out to explain.

Historical Vocabulary
Europe

Europe is a term whose meaning has changed substantially over roughly twenty five centuries of recorded use. The origin of the name itself is uncertain. Ancient Greek tradition associated it with Europa, a mythological Phoenician princess abducted by Zeus in the form of a bull, but this connection was not necessarily the actual linguistic origin of the geographic term, and other etymologies have been proposed by modern scholars. The earliest surviving Greek geographic use of a name for "Europe" as a landmass appears in the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, composed sometime in the seventh or sixth century BCE, and the name was applied, with considerable inconsistency, to a landmass that Greek writers distinguished from Asia and Libya, their name for the African continent. In these earliest attestations, the boundary of "Europe" was uncertain even to the writers who used the term, and it designated a broad and shifting geographic zone rather than a coherent civilization, ethnicity, or political community.

Roman writers inherited and refined the Greek geographic scheme, generally treating Europe as one of three known continents alongside Asia and Africa, though Roman political and cultural life itself spanned all three without regarding the continental divisions as culturally decisive; the Roman Empire's core Mediterranean territories straddled Europe, Asia, and Africa without any sense that Roman identity was defined by residence on one continent rather than another.

In the medieval and early modern eras, "Christendom" often functioned as the more meaningful category of belonging for contemporaries, while "Europe" persisted mainly as a geographic term used by cartographers, geographers, and some political writers. Only from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries onward did "Europe" begin to acquire the fuller cultural and political significance familiar today, as a shorthand for a civilization understood, often with considerable ethnocentrism, as distinct from and frequently superior to other world regions, a usage that accompanied and helped justify European overseas expansion. In the modern era, "Europe" is generally understood as encompassing a geographic space, a set of overlapping cultural traditions, and, for a substantial though historically recent subset of European states, formal membership in political and economic institutions such as the European Union, a definition that includes Christian, Jewish, Muslim, and secular populations of many different ancestral and ethnic backgrounds, not only those descended from the ancient Greek or Roman worlds.

Section 1.2

Europe, Europa, and the Origins of a Name

Students encountering this textbook for the first time should understand where the name "Europe" came from and how uncertain its original geographic reference actually was.

The origin of the name is uncertain. Ancient Greek tradition connected it with Europa, a Phoenician princess whom, according to myth, Zeus abducted in the form of a bull and carried across the sea to Crete, but this traditional association is not established as the actual linguistic origin of the geographic term, and modern scholars have proposed other, competing etymologies. What is better established is that Greek writers, with the Homeric Hymn to Apollo among the earliest surviving attestations, began applying a related name to a landmass lying to the north and west of the Aegean during an early stage in the development of Greek continental geography, distinguishing it from Asia, the term then used for Anatolia and lands further east, and from Libya, the term then used for the African continent. From the beginning, however, Greek geographers disagreed about where the boundary between Europe and Asia actually lay. Some placed it at the Bosporus and the Black Sea, others extended it along various rivers thought to flow from a great northern landmass, and none of these ancient boundary conventions corresponded to any obvious natural feature capable of dividing a single Eurasian landmass into two continents with any real physical justification.

This uncertainty is not a minor antiquarian curiosity. It reflects an enduring truth that runs throughout this textbook: Europe and Asia together form a single continuous landmass, conventionally called Eurasia, and the line dividing them has always been a human convention rather than a geological or ecological necessity. One widely used modern convention places the boundary along the Ural Mountains, the Ural River, the Caspian Sea, the Caucasus Mountains, and the Turkish Straits, and this convention developed gradually between the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, associated particularly with Russian and western European geographers seeking a defensible eastern limit for a "European Russia" that could be distinguished from Russian territories further east. Even this convention is not universally applied, particularly in the stretch between the Caspian and Black Seas, where some geographers draw the line along the Greater Caucasus watershed and others prefer the more northerly Kuma-Manych Depression, a choice that determines whether the Caucasus states are classified as European, Asian, or transcontinental. This convention, while widely used today and adopted by this textbook for organizational purposes, does not reflect any uncontested ancient or natural division, and this textbook treats it as exactly what it is: a useful, historically contingent convention rather than a timeless geographic fact.

Roman writers inherited the Greek three continent scheme and applied it broadly, but Roman political practice paid it little attention. The Roman Empire at its height governed provinces on three continents by this scheme, Britain and Gaul in Europe, Syria and Anatolia in Asia, Egypt and the Maghreb in Africa, as a single integrated Mediterranean political system, examined further in Part III of this textbook. Roman administrators, soldiers, and merchants moved across these supposed continental boundaries without regarding them as culturally significant divisions. This matters for how this textbook approaches its subject: the ancient Mediterranean world that produced Greece and Rome, foundational subjects of Volume I, was never a purely "European" world in isolation from North Africa and the Middle East, and this textbook does not pretend otherwise.

Historical Vocabulary
Christendom, Latin Christendom, and Orthodox Christendom

Christendom describes the community of Christian peoples and states considered collectively, a category that for many centuries carried more weight in the self understanding of medieval Europeans than the geographic term "Europe" itself. Medieval writers more often described their world in terms of Christendom, or of more specific regional and dynastic loyalties, than in terms of a continental "Europe."

Christendom itself was never a single, unified thing. The mutual excommunications of 1054 between Rome and Constantinople, examined in Chapter 40, became a major landmark in a much longer and gradual estrangement between the Latin and Greek churches, a division that hardened further in subsequent centuries, particularly after the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204. It is useful to distinguish Latin Christendom, the western and much of central European world generally in communion with Rome and using Latin as its liturgical and scholarly language, from Orthodox Christendom, the Byzantine, Balkan, and Rus world using Greek, Church Slavonic, and other liturgical languages and in communion with Constantinople and other eastern patriarchates, and later with a growing number of autocephalous churches. These two Christendoms developed distinct institutions, theological emphases, artistic traditions, and political relationships with secular rulers, even as both remained recognizably Christian and both are treated in this textbook as fully part of European history.

This linguistic and institutional structure matters historically because it means that "Christian Europe" never meant one single, uniform religious world in daily practice. A resident of medieval Paris and a resident of medieval Kyiv shared a common Christian faith but belonged to institutionally separate, and at times mutually hostile, Christian traditions. Historians of Europe must therefore be attentive to which Christian tradition a given historical source or society represents, since assuming a single undifferentiated "Christian Europe" flattens centuries of genuine religious, institutional, and cultural difference.

Section 1.3

Geographic Europe and Its Uncertain Eastern Edge

No error is more common in popular geographic thinking about this continent than the assumption that Europe possesses a clear, natural eastern boundary. It does not, and the uncertainty matters historically in several directions.

In one direction: the conventional Europe-Asia boundary along the Ural Mountains places a substantial portion of the Russian state's population and territory within "Europe" while placing the much larger remaining Russian territory in "Asia," even though the Russian state has, since at least the reign of Peter the Great examined in later chapters, understood itself as a single political entity with deep and continuous engagement in European diplomacy, warfare, culture, and religion. This textbook, consistent with the Master Instructions governing the wider MGU History Series, addresses Russian history where it directly intersects with European political, economic, cultural, diplomatic, and military systems, without reproducing a complete independent history of Russia, which receives its own dedicated treatment elsewhere in the series.

In another direction: the Caucasus region, encompassing Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan, sits at a boundary zone where European, Middle Eastern, and Central Asian historical influences overlap, and different scholarly traditions have drawn the Europe-Asia line through this region in different places at different times. Similarly, the Turkish Straits at Constantinople have long served as both a boundary and a bridge, a reality made vivid by the fact that the historic Ottoman capital, and the modern city of Istanbul, sit astride the conventional continental divide, with a European quarter and an Asian quarter connected by bridges and ferries across the Bosporus.

Historians accordingly treat geographic Europe not as a fixed physical fact but as a working convention adopted for organizational clarity, while remaining alert to the ways this convention has been drawn differently across different periods and by different political and scholarly traditions. This textbook adopts one widely used conventional boundary, the Ural Mountains, the Ural River, the Caspian Sea, the Caucasus, the Black Sea, and the Turkish Straits, as an organizational tool for deciding which territories receive sustained regional treatment, while explicitly noting, as this section has done, that the convention is historically recent, geographically contested in its details, and not a timeless truth about the natural world.

Section 1.4

Christendom, "the West," and the Limits of a Religious Label

Popular writing about European history frequently assumes that "Europe" and "Christian" mean the same thing, or that "Europe" and "the West" are interchangeable. Neither assumption withstands close historical examination.

In one direction: substantial non Christian populations have lived within the geographic space this textbook calls Europe for most of its documented history. Jewish communities inhabited Mediterranean and later central and eastern European cities for roughly two thousand years, long before and throughout the Christian centuries, a history examined at multiple points in this textbook rather than treated as a footnote to a Christian majority story. Muslim rule shaped Iberia for centuries after 711 CE, examined in later medieval chapters including Chapter 47, and Muslim ruled Sicily, after flourishing under Arab governance, was conquered by the Normans, under whose Christian rule substantial Muslim communities and Arabic Islamic cultural traditions initially continued before gradually receding. From the fourteenth century, the Ottoman Empire brought substantial Muslim populations and governance to large portions of the Balkans, a Muslim political and demographic presence that persisted for centuries and that this textbook treats as fully part of European history rather than as an external intrusion, discussed further in the treatment of Ottoman southeastern Europe throughout Parts VI through XII.

In the other direction: European Christianity itself has never been religiously uniform. The mutual excommunications of 1054 became a major landmark in the centuries-long estrangement between the Latin and Greek churches, a division that hardened afterward, particularly following the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204, and the Reformation of the sixteenth century, examined in Part X, fractured Latin Christendom further into Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and various radical Protestant traditions, each with distinct institutions, theologies, and political alliances. Treating "Christian Europe" as a single coherent religious bloc obscures centuries of genuine religious conflict, difference, and coexistence that this textbook examines directly rather than smoothing over.

"The West" presents a related but distinct problem. As commonly used in the twentieth and twenty first centuries, "the West" often includes societies outside the European continent altogether, such as the United States, Canada, and Australia, while implicitly excluding or marginalizing Orthodox and formerly Ottoman controlled parts of Europe that this textbook treats as fully European. The modern sociopolitical concept of "the West" developed substantially during the nineteenth century, often in opposition to conceptions of Russia, "the East," and "the Orient," and it was then powerfully redefined during the Cold War, examined in Part XIX, as a transatlantic political and military bloc distinguishing capitalist, largely NATO aligned states from the communist states of the Soviet bloc, many of which are unambiguously European by any geographic or historical definition. This textbook therefore uses "the West" sparingly and with explicit definition when it appears, reserving "Europe" for the continent wide geographic and historical subject that is this book's actual concern.

Section 1.5

Cultural Europe and Political Europe as Separate Processes

One of the most important analytical distinctions in the study of Europe's historical formation is the distinction between cultural Europe, the shared, though never uniform, artistic, intellectual, religious, and linguistic traditions that developed across the continent over many centuries, and political Europe, the comparatively recent set of formal institutions, alliances, and unions, culminating in but not limited to the European Union, that have bound some European states together politically. These two processes became closely associated in some periods, but they proceeded at different speeds, through different mechanisms, and with very different regional outcomes, and conflating them obscures much of the actual historical record examined in this textbook.

Cultural exchange across the continent proceeded for millennia through trade, religious institutions, dynastic marriage, warfare, migration, and intellectual correspondence, producing overlapping but never identical artistic, architectural, legal, and philosophical traditions from Iberia to the Baltic to the Balkans. This cultural interconnection did not require, and long preceded, any formal political union. The Renaissance, examined in Part IX, and the Republic of Letters of the Enlightenment, examined in Part XIII, both illustrate cultural and intellectual networks that spanned much of the continent centuries before any political institution attempted to unify it.

Political Europe, by contrast, is a substantially more recent phenomenon. Various historical actors, including Charlemagne in the ninth century, the Habsburg dynasty in the sixteenth century, and Napoleon in the early nineteenth century, briefly united large portions of the continent under a single political authority, but none of these unifications was permanent, none encompassed the entire continent, and none was pursued or understood by contemporaries primarily as an act of "European" unification in the modern sense. The distinctly modern political project of formal European integration, beginning with the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951 and developing through the European Economic Community and eventually the European Union, examined in Chapters 133 and 134, represents a genuinely novel historical development responding to twentieth century circumstances, particularly the desire to prevent renewed Franco German warfare after two catastrophic world wars, rather than the inevitable culmination of a cultural unity that had supposedly existed all along.

Distinguishing these two processes allows historians to explain outcomes that a conflated "Europe has always been one civilization moving toward political unity" narrative cannot account for: why European states fought repeated and devastating wars against one another for centuries despite sharing substantial cultural and religious traditions; why the European Union includes some geographically European states while historically important European powers, such as the United Kingdom after 2020, have withdrawn from it; and why cultural affinities across the continent coexisted for centuries with fierce dynastic, religious, and national rivalries that this textbook examines without minimizing their intensity or consequences.

Section 1.6

Peoples Within and Beside European History

A responsible history of Europe must keep visible the many peoples who live within its states, share in its history, and yet have often been marginalized, excluded, or rendered invisible in conventional national narratives centered on dominant linguistic or religious majorities. This textbook commits explicitly to this practice throughout, rather than allowing these populations to disappear into an undifferentiated "European" mass.

Jewish communities have lived across the European continent for roughly two thousand years, contributing to its commercial, intellectual, religious, and cultural life while also experiencing recurring periods of restriction, expulsion, and, in the twentieth century, genocide during the Holocaust, examined in Chapter 121. This textbook treats Jewish history as integral to European history throughout, rather than confining it to chapters on persecution alone.

Muslim communities shaped Iberian, Sicilian, and Balkan history for many centuries, from the Arab Islamic conquest of Iberia in 711 CE through the long Ottoman presence in southeastern Europe that persisted into the twentieth century and whose descendant communities remain present in the Balkans today, examined in later medieval chapters, including Chapter 47, and in Chapter 41.

The Sami, indigenous to Sápmi, the traditional homeland spanning the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula, maintain distinct languages and cultural traditions that predate and persist alongside the Norwegian, Swedish, Finnish, and Russian states that came to claim their traditional territories, a history of internal colonization within Europe itself that this textbook treats seriously.

The Roma, present across the continent since at least the late medieval period, have experienced centuries of discrimination, forced settlement, expulsion, and, during the Second World War, genocide alongside European Jews, examined in Chapter 121, while also sustaining distinct cultural and linguistic traditions across many European states into the present.

Uralic-speaking and Turkic-speaking populations, including Finns, Estonians, and Hungarians, who speak languages of the broader Uralic family, and various Turkic populations of the Volga region and the Balkans, speak languages unrelated to the Indo European languages of most of the continent's population, a linguistic diversity that this textbook treats as integral rather than anomalous to European history.

Holding these populations in view throughout the textbook, rather than treating them as footnotes to a single dominant national or religious narrative, is not merely an exercise in inclusivity for its own sake. It reflects the actual historical composition of every major state, empire, and society this book examines, from the religiously and ethnically diverse subject populations of medieval and early modern composite monarchies to the complex demographic makeups of modern European nation states.

Section 1.7

The Geographic Scope of This Textbook

This textbook studies eight principal sub regions, each receiving sustained attention across its one hundred and forty four chapters rather than being subsumed into a single dominant narrative centered on any one country.

The Mediterranean world, comprising Iberia, Italy, Greece, and the Mediterranean islands, is the historical setting in which Greek and Roman civilization emerged, examined in Parts II and III, and remains central to the textbook's account of trade, religious exchange, and cultural transmission across many subsequent centuries.

Western Europe, comprising France, the Low Countries, and the British Isles, developed distinctive medieval and early modern political institutions, including durable monarchies and, in England, an evolving constitutional tradition, examined throughout Parts V, VII, and XII.

Central Europe, comprising the German lands, the Alpine region, Bohemia, Moravia, and Poland, was long organized through the decentralized political structures of the Holy Roman Empire and later through the competing powers of Austria and Prussia, examined in Part XII, and its history should not be read backward as an incomplete version of a unified modern Germany.

Northern Europe and Scandinavia developed distinct Viking Age, medieval, and early modern trajectories connected to but not subordinate to developments further south, examined at multiple points including Part V and Part XII.

The Baltic region occupies a hinge position between Scandinavia, the German lands, Poland Lithuania, and Russia, and receives attention to its own distinct trajectory of Christianization, commercial development through the Hanseatic League, and contested sovereignty, examined in Part VIII.

Eastern Europe and the Balkans, comprising Poland Lithuania, Hungary, the Danubian lands, and southeastern Europe, are examined with attention to Byzantine, Orthodox, and Ottoman influences that distinguish this region's historical trajectory from that of western Europe, examined throughout Part VI and later chapters on Ottoman southeastern Europe.

European Russia enters the narrative where Russian history intersects with the European political, economic, cultural, diplomatic, and military systems, without reproducing the separate Russian History textbook produced elsewhere in the MGU History Series, consistent with the Master Instructions governing this textbook.

The Ottoman European lands are treated as a major European as well as Middle Eastern and Mediterranean power wherever Ottoman history directly intersects Europe, examined especially in Chapter 41 and throughout Parts XII, XVI, and XVII.

Regions adjacent to, but not part of, Europe by this textbook's geographic definition, such as North Africa, the Middle East, and Central Asia, enter the narrative at the points where their histories directly intersect with European societies, as the Master Instructions governing this textbook specify, rather than receiving independent regional treatment of their own.

Section 1.9

Evidence, Historiography, and National Narratives

The history of Europe rests on an unusually rich and varied evidentiary base: prehistoric archaeological remains; Greek and Roman literary, epigraphic, and administrative sources; the Bible and early Christian religious literature; medieval chronicles, charters, and monastic records; legal codes and court records; parliamentary and administrative archives; poetry, chronicles, and literature across many vernacular languages; coins, seals, and inscriptions; architectural and archaeological evidence; maps and geographic treatises; and, for the modern era, an increasing volume of government records, newspapers, memoirs, and photographic and film evidence.

As with any richly documented civilization, this evidence has been interpreted differently by different historians pursuing different purposes. Medieval chroniclers, like their counterparts in other historiographical traditions examined elsewhere in the MGU History Series, often wrote to serve the political and religious interests of ruling dynasties and churches, and later scholars must read their accounts of conquests, successions, and miracles with the same critical attention any historian brings to court sponsored or monastery sponsored chronicles. Nineteenth and twentieth century nationalist historiography, emerging alongside the political movements discussed in Part XV, tended to emphasize a continuous narrative of national unity, cultural greatness, and shared destiny for particular European peoples, a narrative with real evidentiary grounding in language, territory, and shared historical experience, but one that this textbook treats as itself a historical phenomenon requiring analysis rather than as an unquestioned framework for organizing the entire book. Colonial era European scholarship, meanwhile, often approached non European societies through frameworks emphasizing supposed European superiority, frameworks that later generations of scholars, both within Europe and internationally, have substantially revised through more careful attention to the agency, dynamism, and historical complexity of colonized and non European societies.

This textbook draws on the substantial body of modern international and regional scholarship that has sought to move past both nationalist and colonial era frameworks, emphasizing instead regional diversity, the agency of non elite populations, the significant roles of non Christian and non majority communities, and rigorous attention to primary evidence, the same approach the Master Instructions governing this textbook require of every chapter that follows.

Section 1.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced the definitions, geographic scope, chronological boundaries, and historical methodological commitments that will govern the study of Europe throughout this textbook. Before moving into the specific content of subsequent chapters, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: "Europe" is a geographic, cultural, and political idea whose meaning has changed across roughly twenty five centuries, not a fixed or self evident fact of nature. "Europe," "Christendom," and "the West" are related but distinct terms describing geography, religious community, and a modern political and cultural bloc respectively. The boundary between Europe and Asia is a historically constructed convention, not a natural physical division. Jewish communities, Muslim communities, the Sami, the Roma, and various Uralic-speaking and Turkic-speaking peoples remain integral, visible parts of this history rather than disappearing into an undifferentiated European population. This textbook studies the Mediterranean world, western Europe, central Europe, northern Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia as distinct regions, each receiving sustained treatment. Before an identifiable concept of "Europe" existed, this textbook describes the ancient and prehistoric societies of the continent on their own terms rather than anachronistically calling them "European" in the modern sense. Historiography, including both traditional chronicle writing and modern nationalist and colonial era narratives, must be read critically rather than accepted uncritically.

Primary Source Window

Herodotus, Histories, Book 4, on the Division of the Continents, c. 430s–420s BCE

Creator: Herodotus of Halicarnassus, Greek historian · Date: composed in the fifth century BCE, likely completed in the 430s or 420s BCE · Type: Historical and ethnographic narrative examining the Greco Persian Wars and the peoples and lands surrounding the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds

Context: Herodotus, often called the "father of history" by later Western tradition, composed a wide ranging account of the conflicts between the Greek city states and the Persian Empire, incorporating extensive geographic and ethnographic digressions about the peoples of Europe, Asia, and Egypt. In discussing the customary Greek division of the world into three continents, he expressed open skepticism about the logic and consistency of that division.

Herodotus observed that Greek geographers could not agree on where the boundaries between the continents actually lay, particularly the boundary between Europe and Asia, and he questioned why a single landmass should be divided into three unequally named parts by convention rather than by any clear natural feature, noting the inconsistency of naming a supposed continent after a woman, Europa, who according to the Greeks he consulted never actually set foot upon it.

Analysis: This passage is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates that sophisticated skepticism about the coherence of "Europe" as a geographic category existed at the very moment the term first entered widespread Greek use, not merely as a modern academic critique imposed retrospectively. At the same time, Herodotus wrote from a particular fifth century Greek vantage point, shaped by the immediate experience of war with Persia, and his geographic and ethnographic observations, however perceptive, reflect the limits of Greek knowledge of distant regions in his own time.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a fifth century BCE Greek historian's skepticism about continental boundaries still be relevant to historians of Europe today?
  2. What does Herodotus's observation about the inconsistency of naming a continent after a mythological figure suggest about how ancient geographic categories were constructed?
  3. What are the limitations of using one ancient writer's geographic observations to draw conclusions about the "real" boundaries of Europe?
  4. How does the existence of ancient skepticism about continental divisions complicate any assumption that critical questioning of "Europe" as a category is a purely modern development?
Historical Debate

When Does "European" History Properly Begin?

The question: Should a history of "Europe" begin with ancient Greece, treated as the intellectual and political origin of a distinctly European civilization, or should it begin earlier, with the prehistoric human societies of the continent whose material remains long predate any concept of "Europe" at all, and even predate the Greek world by tens of thousands of years? Historians differ on how to weight these two possible starting points, and on how much continuity, if any, should be assumed between prehistoric European societies and the later civilizations that arose on the same landmass.

Interpretation A — European history begins meaningfully with ancient Greece: Some historians and popular writers argue that "Europe" as a civilizational and intellectual phenomenon effectively begins with Greek political thought, philosophy, and historical writing, treating prehistoric Europe as important background but not yet properly "European" in any meaningful cultural sense.
Interpretation B — European history must include its much longer prehistoric foundation: Other historians emphasize that the continent's Paleolithic, Neolithic, and Bronze and Iron Age societies developed sophisticated technologies, art, trade networks, and social structures over tens of thousands of years, and that beginning the story with Greece risks reducing this vast span of documented human history to a mere prelude, even though doing so requires care not to project any unified "European" identity onto these much earlier and highly diverse populations.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, archaeological remains, ancient Greek and Roman literary sources, and modern scholarly reconstruction, but differ in how much historical weight and independent significance to give the prehistoric material relative to the political and intellectual innovations that began in the Greek world.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically rather than doctrinally: it devotes Chapters 2 through 6 to the geography, environment, and prehistoric societies of Europe before treating the Greek world in Part II, treating prehistoric European history as historically significant in its own right while still organizing its overall Volume I structure around the classical Mediterranean world as a foundational, though not exclusive, point of departure.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which starting point you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by each choice of starting point.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gets to Define "Europe"? Perspectives on Naming a Continent

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a nineteenth century European nationalist intellectual: "Europe" names a civilization of shared classical, Christian, and rational inheritance, distinguished from and often superior to other world regions, and national self determination for European peoples is both historically justified and a legitimate political goal, a view examined further in Part XV.
From the perspective of a Sami, Roma, or Jewish community member within a European state: Membership within a European state's borders does not guarantee full inclusion in how that state or its neighbors imagine "European" identity, and a regional history organized entirely around a dominant national or Christian narrative risks marginalizing the distinct languages, cultures, and historical experiences of minority peoples who have lived within European societies for centuries or millennia.
From the perspective of a medieval Byzantine or Balkan Orthodox Christian: Rome, and later western European powers, did not always recognize Constantinople and the Orthodox world as fully part of a shared "Christendom," despite a common Christian faith, a tension rooted in the long estrangement marked by the mutual excommunications of 1054 and later reinforced by the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204, examined in Chapter 41.
From the perspective of a twentieth century architect of European integration: "Europe" names a political project of reconciliation and cooperation among states whose rivalries had twice produced catastrophic continental war, and formal institutions of economic and political integration represent a deliberate, contingent choice rather than an inevitable culmination of European history, a view examined further in Chapters 133 and 134.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Idea of "Europe" from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • The term "Europe" expanded from a narrow, inconsistently applied geographic designation to a broad cultural and, eventually, political category
  • The relative importance of "Christendom" versus "Europe" as a category of belonging shifted substantially between the medieval and modern periods
  • The geographic extent of territory considered "European" expanded and contracted as conventions about the Europe-Asia boundary changed
  • Modern political institutions, including the European Coal and Steel Community and the European Union, reinterpreted older cultural and geographic ideas of Europe as a basis for formal political and economic integration

What Stayed the Same

  • The boundary between Europe and Asia has remained a human convention rather than a fixed natural fact throughout its documented history
  • Significant non Christian and minority communities have persisted within the continent throughout its documented history
  • Regional diversity among the Mediterranean, western Europe, central Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia has remained a durable feature rather than dissolving into uniformity
  • Debates over who belongs within "Europe" and what that membership should mean have recurred across many centuries

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "Europe" as an ancient, unchanging essence, or treating it as nothing more than a modern political invention with no historical roots.

Cause and Effect

Why Definitional Confusion about Europe Persists

Long-term causes

The historical overlap between the spread of Christianity and the geographic space later called Europe, and the fact that much popular understanding of the continent's history has been shaped by dominant national or western European narratives rather than a continent wide, comparative view.

Short-term causes

Twentieth and twenty first century media, political, and diplomatic usage frequently treats "Europe," "the West," and "the European Union" as interchangeable for narrative convenience, reinforcing imprecise public usage.

Effect

Orthodox, Ottoman era, and eastern European populations, along with Jewish, Muslim, Sami, and Roma communities within Europe, are frequently misrepresented, marginalized, or rendered invisible in popular understanding of the continent.

This textbook's response

Consistent, careful use of "Europe," "Christendom," "the West," and "the European Union" as distinct terms throughout all one hundred and forty four chapters, with explicit attention to non dominant regions and communities wherever they are historically significant.

Historical complexity: Imprecise terminology is not merely a modern journalistic problem; it has roots in centuries of European writers themselves using geographic, religious, and political categories interchangeably for their own purposes, including to justify exclusion or overseas expansion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Ordinary Identity Belongs in This Chapter

Definitions of "Europe," "Christendom," and "the West" are not only matters for scholars and diplomats; they shape how ordinary people have experienced daily life, legal status, and belonging across the centuries this textbook studies. A Catholic peasant in medieval Poland, an Orthodox merchant in Ottoman controlled Thessaloniki, a Jewish artisan in early modern Amsterdam, and a Sami herder in northern Norway each navigated daily social, legal, and economic life shaped by their language, religion, local community, and regional identity, categories that often mattered more in practice than any single overarching label such as "European" or "Christian."

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society and everyday life examine how ordinary populations across the continent experienced marketplaces, villages, religious festivals, family and community obligation, taxation, and, in the modern era, citizenship and national identity. Because most surviving pre modern written sources were produced by a literate religious or administrative elite, historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own, women, rural farmers, pastoral communities, artisans, enslaved and unfree people, and religious and ethnic minorities, while also drawing on archaeology, material culture, and administrative records to reconstruct their experiences more fully.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced "Europe" as this textbook's organizing subject and has carefully distinguished it from several related but distinct categories with which it is often, and mistakenly, conflated: Christendom, the West, and the European Union. Europe, as this textbook defines and studies it, is a geographic, cultural, and political space whose boundaries and meaning have changed substantially since ancient Greek writers first applied the name "Europa" to a landmass they distinguished, inconsistently, from Asia and Africa.

The chapter has emphasized that geographic, cultural, and political definitions of Europe are related but distinct, and that careful use of these categories is essential to accurate historical understanding. It has explained why the conventional Europe-Asia boundary is a historically constructed convention rather than a natural fact, and why cultural Europe and political Europe, though historically connected, proceeded on very different timelines, producing regionally distinct outcomes still visible today in the continent's religious, linguistic, and political diversity.

Students have been introduced to the many peoples who live within, and alongside, mainstream European historical narratives without always being fully recognized as belonging to them: Jewish communities present for roughly two thousand years, Muslim communities that shaped Iberian, Sicilian, and Balkan history for centuries, the Sami of the far north, the Roma present across the continent since the late medieval period, and Uralic-speaking and Turkic-speaking populations whose languages and histories differ from the continent's Indo European majority.

The chapter established the textbook's geographic scope, the Mediterranean world, western Europe, central Europe, northern Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia, and explained why each region receives sustained, independent treatment rather than being absorbed into a single dominant narrative. It also established the textbook's chronological scope, from prehistoric Europe through the contemporary European order of the twenty first century, organized into six broad Volumes.

Finally, the chapter addressed historiography directly, explaining how traditional chronicle writing, modern nationalist narratives, and colonial era Western scholarship have each shaped, and in some cases distorted, popular understanding of European history, and committing this textbook to the more historically grounded, evidence based approach that contemporary international and regional scholarship has developed. The Historical Debate feature on when "European" history properly begins illustrated this challenge concretely: how much independent weight should be given to prehistoric European societies relative to the classical Mediterranean world that later observers have often treated as European civilization's true origin?

These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following one hundred and forty three chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of ancient civilizations, medieval Christendom, the Renaissance and Reformation, dynastic states and overseas empire, industrialization and nationalism, the world wars, the Cold War, European integration, and contemporary Europe.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Europe
A geographic, cultural, and political term whose scope has expanded and changed greatly since its narrow ancient usage as a name for an ill defined landmass distinguished from Asia and Africa.
Europa
A name appearing in ancient Greek myth and geography. Ancient tradition associated the geographic name with Europa, the mythological Phoenician princess, but the actual linguistic origin of the geographic term remains uncertain.
Eurasia
The single continuous landmass comprising Europe and Asia, whose division into two continents is a human geographic convention rather than a natural physical fact.
Christendom
The community of Christian peoples and states considered collectively; for many centuries a more meaningful category of belonging for contemporaries than geographic "Europe."
Latin Christendom
The western and much of central European Christian world generally in communion with Rome, using Latin as its liturgical and scholarly language.
Orthodox Christendom
The Byzantine, Balkan, and Rus Christian world using Greek, Church Slavonic, and other liturgical languages, in communion with Constantinople and other eastern patriarchates and, later, a growing number of autocephalous churches.
The West
A modern, often imprecisely defined political and cultural category that sometimes includes non European societies and sometimes excludes parts of Europe; used sparingly in this textbook.
The European Union
A modern political and economic union of European states, established when the Maastricht Treaty, signed in 1992, entered into force in 1993, representing one influential but historically recent institutional definition of "Europe."
Great Schism
The mutual excommunications of 1054 between Rome and Constantinople, a major landmark in the centuries-long estrangement between Latin and Orthodox Christianity that hardened further after 1204.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Al Andalus
The historical Arab Islamic and Amazigh ruled territory of the Iberian Peninsula, beginning in 711 CE, demonstrating that Muslim rule was, for centuries, a European reality.
The Sami
Indigenous peoples of Sápmi, spanning the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula, with distinct languages and cultural traditions predating the states that later claimed their territories.
The Roma
A transnational ethnic minority present across Europe since at least the late medieval period, with a documented history of migration, cultural contribution, and persecution.
Uralic languages
A language family including Finnish, Estonian, and Hungarian, unrelated to the Indo European languages spoken by most of the continent's population; "Finno-Ugric" is a related but narrower linguistic classification sometimes used within this family.
Herodotus
Fifth century BCE Greek historian whose Histories include an early skeptical discussion of the conventional division of the world into continents.
Nation state
A modern form of political organization claiming to represent a single national community; not applicable to most of European history before the nineteenth century.
Composite monarchy
An early modern form of political organization in which a single ruler governed multiple distinct territories, each retaining its own laws, institutions, and identity.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of European history have changed over time and why.
Primary source
Evidence originating from the historical period being studied or produced by participants, witnesses, institutions, or material processes directly connected to the subject, including documents, objects, later memoirs, oral testimony, and archaeological remains.
Secondary source
A later interpretation of the past, typically by a historian, drawing on primary sources.
Christian tradition or confession
A distinct branch or institutional body within Christianity, such as Catholicism, Orthodoxy, Lutheranism, the Reformed churches, or Anglicanism, each with its own institutions, theology, and history.
Minority community
A religious or ethnic group that is not part of the majority population of a given state or region, such as Jews, Muslims, the Sami, or the Roma within European states.
Historical memory
The way communities and states collectively remember and use the past to construct identity and meaning in the present.
Periodization
The historical practice of dividing the past into named eras, such as Antiquity, the Middle Ages, or the Renaissance, understood as analytical conventions rather than natural divisions of time.
Regional diversity
The recognition that Europe varies significantly by sub region, the Mediterranean, western Europe, central Europe, the Baltic, eastern Europe, the Balkans, and European Russia, rather than forming a single uniform society.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces, distinguishing Europe from Christendom from the West, separating cultural Europe from political Europe, keeping non dominant peoples and religious minorities visible, and reading historiography critically, are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

Europe's history is one of the most geographically, religiously, and regionally diverse historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included prehistoric societies that came long before any concept of "Europe"; classical Mediterranean civilizations whose influence and limits this textbook examines without exaggeration; centuries of medieval Christendom fractured between Latin and Orthodox traditions; a transformative early modern era of overseas expansion, religious conflict, and state formation; a modern era shaped by revolution, industrialization, nationalism, and catastrophic warfare; and a contemporary moment of renewed debate over integration, identity, and belonging. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to Europe's historical significance, it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that "Europe" is a geographic, cultural, and political idea rather than a synonym for Christendom or a predetermined path toward the European Union, that cultural and political integration were related but distinct processes, and that the continent's minority peoples are integral rather than peripheral to its history, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook, from the prehistoric foundations of Part I through the ancient Mediterranean world of Parts II and III, the medieval Christendoms of Volume II, the Renaissance, Reformation, and dynastic states of Volume III, the revolutionary and industrial transformations of Volume IV, the catastrophic wars of Volume V, and the Cold War, integration, and contemporary Europe of Volume VI, with the same careful, evidence based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the Paleolithic human settlement of Chapter 3 through the unresolved questions of contemporary Europe examined in Chapter 144. It is a story of many regions, many peoples, and many religious communities, joined by overlapping but never identical languages, faiths, and political systems, not a single unbroken thread and not a predetermined outcome. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
