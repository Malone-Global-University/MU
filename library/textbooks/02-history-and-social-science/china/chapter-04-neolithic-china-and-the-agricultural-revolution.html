Part I — China, Geography, and the Deep Past

Neolithic China and the Agricultural Revolution

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Somewhere along the middle Yellow River, roughly nine thousand years ago, a household scattered a handful of small, hardy seeds across a patch of cleared loess soil, returned weeks later to pull weeds and watch the plants grow, and then, at the end of the season, harvested a quantity of grain that could be stored and consumed long after the growing season ended. No one involved could have known that this small, repeated, unremarkable act — performed independently by many households across many generations in more than one region of East Asia — would eventually reshape nearly every dimension of human life in the lands this textbook studies.

Chapter 3 ended with the long, slow warming that followed the Last Glacial Maximum, a climatic transformation that, by roughly eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, had ushered in the more stable conditions of the Holocene epoch. This chapter picks up that thread and asks how the descendants of the Paleolithic hunter-gatherers examined in the previous chapter began, in different regions and at somewhat different times, to shift from a life organized around hunting, gathering, and high mobility toward a life increasingly organized around the deliberate cultivation of plants, the management of domesticated animals, and, eventually, permanent or semi-permanent village settlement. Historians and archaeologists commonly call this transformation the Neolithic Revolution, or the agricultural revolution, though, as this chapter will show repeatedly, "revolution" can be a misleading word for a process that unfolded gradually, unevenly, and over many centuries rather than as a single sudden event.

The story told in this chapter is, from the outset, a story of more than one beginning. In the middle and lower Yellow River region of northern China, communities began cultivating varieties of millet, a hardy, drought-tolerant grain well suited to the region's temperate, relatively dry climate. At roughly the same broad era, in the middle and lower Yangtze River region far to the south, communities in a warmer, wetter environment began cultivating rice, developing the labor-intensive but highly productive system of wet-rice paddy agriculture that would eventually support some of the densest populations in the pre-modern world. These two agricultural traditions did not develop because one region taught the other; the archaeological evidence indicates that millet cultivation in the north and rice cultivation in the south followed distinct domestication trajectories rooted in local plant ecology, local human experimentation, and local environmental pressures. This distinctness should not be mistaken for isolation, however: transitional zones such as the upper Huai and Han River drainages saw early crop and population interaction between millet- and rice-associated communities, and growing archaeobotanical and genomic evidence indicates that the two traditions were connected by increasingly substantial exchange of crops, technologies, and people from a relatively early period onward, even as each tradition developed largely on its own ecological and technological terms. This chapter therefore reinforces one of the central lessons of Chapters 1 and 2: Chinese civilization did not radiate outward from one single ancient center. It developed through the interaction of multiple regional societies, each adapting agriculture to its own landscape.

Understanding why communities gradually took up farming, and what farming did to the societies that adopted it, requires students to resist two tempting but misleading stories. The first misleading story imagines agriculture as an obvious improvement that any sensible community would quickly adopt once it discovered the technique — as though foraging were simply a less advanced stage on the way to farming. The archaeological and ethnographic evidence tells a more complicated story: foraging societies were often well nourished, physically capable, and knowledgeable about their environments, and early farming frequently required more, not less, daily labor than foraging, while offering a less varied diet and, in some cases, new health risks associated with sedentary village life and close contact with domesticated animals. The second misleading story imagines the shift to agriculture as a single decisive event, a moment when someone "invented" farming. In reality, the transition unfolded across many centuries, through a long, uneven process in which foraging and cultivation coexisted, plants were tended and encouraged long before they were fully domesticated, and communities moved back and forth between more mobile and more settled ways of life depending on local conditions, climate fluctuations, and social choices.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about what changed, and what did not, as farming took hold. Villages grew larger and more permanent, but many early farming communities also continued to hunt, fish, and gather wild foods well into the period when cultivated grains had become an important part of the diet. Pottery, which this chapter will show developed among still-mobile hunter-gatherers in southern China even before agriculture became dominant, expanded dramatically as farming communities needed containers for storing, cooking, and fermenting grain. Social organization, labor patterns, and family life all changed as communities invested more time and resources into specific plots of land, built more permanent housing, and began accumulating stored surpluses of grain that could support larger populations — but also, as later chapters will show, that could be seized, hoarded, or fought over, laying some of the groundwork for the social hierarchies and early states examined in Chapters 5 and 6.

Geography, introduced in Chapter 2, shaped this story directly. The fertile but easily eroded loess soils of the North China Plain and the Loess Plateau supported dry-land cultivation of millet using comparatively simple tools, while the warmer, wetter environment of the Yangtze basin, with its extensive wetlands and abundant water, supported the more labor-intensive management of flooded rice paddies. This chapter's account of agricultural origins should therefore be read alongside Chapter 2's account of environmental regions: the north-south agricultural distinction introduced there as a lasting feature of Chinese history had its roots in the very first millennia of farming discussed in this chapter.

Finally, this chapter asks students to appreciate both the achievement and the cost of the Neolithic transition. Agriculture could support substantially larger and denser populations than foraging in many environments and became one important foundation for particular forms of social and material complexity, laying groundwork relevant to the villages, regional cultures, and early states examined in Chapters 5 and 6. But it also introduced new burdens: more repetitive and physically demanding daily labor for many people, greater vulnerability to crop failure and famine, new patterns of disease associated with sedentary life and close animal contact, and, over time, new forms of social inequality as some households and communities accumulated more stored surplus, more fertile land, or more influence than others. Understanding the Neolithic Revolution as a story of genuine gains bound up with genuine costs — rather than a simple story of straightforward progress — is one of the central historical thinking skills this chapter aims to develop.

By the end of this chapter, students should understand how and why agriculture developed independently in northern and southern China, how farming reshaped diet, technology, settlement, family life, and social organization, and how the regional agricultural traditions established during the Neolithic period became one important foundation for the forms of increasing social complexity examined in Chapter 5, and for the early states examined in Chapter 6.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what archaeologists mean by the Neolithic period and why the shift to food production is often called a "revolution" despite unfolding gradually.
  2. Trace the independent development of millet cultivation in northern China and rice cultivation in the Yangtze River region.
  3. Compare the environmental conditions, crops, and agricultural techniques associated with northern and southern early farming traditions.
  4. Describe the process of plant and animal domestication and distinguish domestication from simple plant or animal use.
  5. Analyze how the adoption of agriculture affected settlement patterns, encouraging the growth of permanent or semi-permanent villages.
  6. Explain the development and significance of pottery, ground stone tools, and other household technologies associated with the Neolithic period.
  7. Evaluate how agriculture changed family organization, labor, and the daily lives of men, women, and children in early farming communities.
  8. Assess the relationship between agricultural surplus, population growth, and early social and environmental change.
  9. Identify the ways in which Neolithic agricultural developments laid groundwork for the regional cultures and early social complexity examined in Chapter 5.
  10. Evaluate competing historical and archaeological interpretations of why and how agriculture began in different regions of East Asia.
  11. Distinguish the genuine benefits of agricultural life from its costs, avoiding a simple narrative of agriculture as straightforward progress.
  12. Use archaeological, botanical, and zoological evidence appropriately, recognizing the strengths and limitations of each for reconstructing the Neolithic past.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did communities in different regions of East Asia begin cultivating different staple crops along largely distinct domestication trajectories, even as the regions remained connected through exchange?
  2. What does it mean to say that a plant or animal has been "domesticated," and how do archaeologists identify domestication in the material record?
  3. Why might early farming have required more labor than foraging, and why would communities adopt it anyway?
  4. How did the different environmental conditions of northern and southern China shape two distinct agricultural traditions?
  5. What changed, and what stayed the same, in daily life as communities shifted from mobile foraging toward settled village agriculture?
  6. How did the development of pottery relate to the development of agriculture, and why does the evidence complicate a simple assumption that the two arose together?
  7. What role did surplus grain play in reshaping family life, labor, and, eventually, social organization?
  8. Why should historians resist describing the Neolithic transition as an inevitable or straightforwardly beneficial development?
  9. How does the archaeological record allow historians to reconstruct diet, technology, and daily life for a period with no writing?
  10. How does the Neolithic period set the stage for the regional cultures and early states examined in the following two chapters?

Historical Context

Chapter 3 traced the long span of Paleolithic human presence in East Asia, emphasizing that hunter-gatherer communities had already developed deep, regionally specific knowledge of the East Asian landscape — its plants, animals, seasons, and resources — over hundreds of thousands of years before anyone began deliberately cultivating crops. This chapter picks up that thread at the close of the last Ice Age, when the long-term warming trend described in Section 3.7 began transforming the environmental conditions across the region in ways that, over several thousand years, made systematic cultivation of certain plants increasingly viable and, in some regions, increasingly advantageous.

The term "Neolithic," meaning "New Stone Age," was coined by nineteenth-century European archaeologists to describe a period characterized by polished and ground stone tools, pottery, and, eventually, settled agricultural life, in contrast to the unpolished, flaked stone tools of the preceding Paleolithic. Students should understand, as with the Paleolithic periodization discussed in Chapter 3, that this scheme originated in European archaeology and does not map perfectly onto every region of the world, including East Asia, where, as this chapter will show, some Neolithic technologies such as pottery developed among still-mobile hunter-gatherer communities well before agriculture became the dominant subsistence strategy, complicating any assumption that "Neolithic" traits arrived together as a single cultural package.

Two river systems dominate this chapter's geography, echoing the discussion in Chapter 2. The middle and lower Yellow River basin, with its temperate climate and fertile if erodible loess soils, became the center of early millet cultivation. The middle and lower Yangtze River basin, with its warmer, wetter, subtropical conditions and extensive wetlands, became the center of early rice cultivation. Archaeological evidence increasingly suggests that other regions of China, including parts of the northeast and southwest, developed their own local patterns of plant management and, in some cases, independent or semi-independent paths toward cultivation, though the northern millet and southern rice traditions remain the two most thoroughly documented and historically consequential agricultural origins within the geographic space this textbook studies.

Students should also bear in mind the chronological uncertainty that characterizes this period, much as Chapter 3 emphasized for the Paleolithic. Plant and animal domestication is now understood by archaeologists not as a single moment of invention but as a long, gradual process, often lasting many centuries or even millennia, during which human management of certain plant and animal populations intensified gradually, producing incremental genetic and morphological changes that archaeologists and botanists can sometimes, though not always, detect in the archaeological and archaeobotanical record. Dates given in this chapter, as in Chapter 3, should be understood as current best estimates based on the available evidence, subject to revision as new sites are excavated and new scientific techniques, including archaeobotany, zooarchaeology, and ancient DNA analysis, are applied to existing collections.

Finally, this chapter connects directly to the discussion of regional diversity introduced in Chapters 1 and 2. Just as this textbook resists treating "China" as a single unchanging political or cultural space, it resists treating "Chinese agriculture" as though it had one single point of origin. The two agricultural traditions introduced in this chapter — northern dry-land millet farming and southern wet-rice farming — became the foundation for two historically distinct regional societies whose different environments, crops, and labor systems would continue to shape Chinese history for thousands of years afterward, as later chapters on the Shang and Zhou states, the north-south division of the Age of Division, and the Tang and Song economic transformation will repeatedly show.

Section 4.1

The Transition to Food Production

The shift from foraging to farming, sometimes called the Neolithic Revolution, ranks among the most consequential transformations in human history anywhere in the world, and East Asia was one of a small number of world regions — alongside parts of the Fertile Crescent in Southwest Asia, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and parts of Africa and New Guinea — where plant cultivation and animal domestication developed independently rather than spreading from a single point of origin. Understanding why and how this transition occurred requires setting aside the assumption that farming was an obvious improvement waiting to be discovered. Paleolithic hunter-gatherer communities, as Chapter 3 explained, possessed deep, sophisticated knowledge of their environments, exploited a wide range of plant and animal resources, and were not, in any general sense, straining against the limits of what foraging could support. The transition to farming instead reflects a complex, still-debated combination of environmental change, population pressure, changing resource availability, accumulated ecological knowledge, and deliberate human experimentation, unfolding gradually across many centuries rather than through a single moment of discovery.

Archaeologists studying the origins of agriculture in China distinguish several overlapping stages that, together, constitute the Neolithic transition, though these stages did not always occur in a fixed order and often overlapped considerably. In an early stage, mobile foraging communities began deliberately managing wild plant populations — clearing competing vegetation, encouraging the growth of useful plants, and perhaps selectively harvesting or replanting seeds — without yet fully domesticating those plants in a genetic or morphological sense. In a subsequent stage, sustained human selection, whether deliberate or an unintended consequence of repeated harvesting and replanting, began to produce measurable changes in plant populations: larger seeds, non-shattering seed heads that stayed on the plant rather than dispersing naturally (making harvesting more efficient), and other traits that distinguish domesticated crops from their wild ancestors. In a further stage, cultivated plants became a dominant, rather than a supplementary, part of a community's diet, and settlement patterns, technology, and social organization increasingly reorganized around the demands of planting, tending, and harvesting specific crops.

This gradual, staged process helps explain why archaeologists increasingly avoid describing agricultural origins as a single dramatic "invention" and instead describe a long process of interaction between human communities and the plants and animals around them, sometimes described using the concept of niche construction: the idea that human activity actively reshapes the surrounding environment in ways that, in turn, reshape human behavior and, eventually, human biology and culture. Early foragers who cleared brush, dug up edible roots, or repeatedly returned to gather wild grains in the same locations were, often without any conscious long-term plan, altering local plant populations and local landscapes in ways that made those locations increasingly productive for specific useful plants — a feedback process that, sustained across many generations, gradually produced the domesticated crops on which later Chinese agricultural civilization would depend.

Two broad regional patterns dominate the archaeological evidence for early Chinese agriculture, corresponding to the environmental regions introduced in Chapter 2: the cultivation of millet in the comparatively dry, temperate environment of the middle and lower Yellow River basin, discussed in Section 4.2, and the cultivation of rice in the warmer, wetter environment of the middle and lower Yangtze River basin, discussed in Section 4.3. These two agricultural traditions developed largely independently of one another over a broadly similar span of centuries, generally placed by current archaeobotanical evidence within a range of roughly ten thousand to seven thousand years ago for the earliest stages of plant management, though the process of full domestication, in both regions, continued for a much longer span of time afterward. Students should treat any specific date offered in this chapter as a current estimate subject to revision, since archaeobotanical research continues to refine the chronology of early cultivation through improved recovery techniques, such as the systematic flotation of excavated soil to recover tiny charred plant remains, and through increasingly precise radiocarbon dating of the plant remains themselves rather than of associated charcoal or other material that might not be contemporary with the plant remains in question.

Section 4.2

Millet Agriculture in Northern China

In the middle and lower Yellow River basin, early farming communities gradually domesticated two related but distinct species of millet: broomcorn millet (Panicum miliaceum) and foxtail millet (Setaria italica). Both are small-seeded grasses, native to East Asia, that produce nutritious grain and tolerate the region's relatively dry, temperate climate far better than many other cereal crops, making them well suited to the loess soils and seasonal rainfall patterns of the North China Plain and the Loess Plateau discussed in Chapter 2.

Archaeobotanical evidence, including charred grain remains and phytoliths — microscopic silica structures that form within plant cells and survive in soil long after the organic plant material itself has decayed — indicates that millet exploitation in northern China unfolded across a long and geographically uneven sequence rather than a single, clean starting point. Early, low-intensity use of wild millet is documented at northern sites such as Nanzhuangtou and Donghulin by roughly eleven thousand five hundred to nine thousand five hundred years ago, well before clear evidence of morphological domestication. More securely documented cultivation of broomcorn millet appears at the site of Cishan by roughly ten thousand three hundred to eight thousand seven hundred years ago, with clearer evidence for foxtail millet emerging somewhat later still. Current syntheses treat millet domestication in China as a long, geographically mosaic process, unfolding at different rates in different communities, rather than a single event with a uniform start date.

The site of Jiahu, located not in the Yellow River valley proper but in the upper Huai River drainage — a transitional zone on the southwestern edge of the Huang-Huai-Hai Plain lying between the Yellow and Yangtze river systems — has provided especially rich evidence for early Neolithic life in this broader region, dated to roughly nine thousand to seven thousand eight hundred years ago. Excavations there have recovered early rice remains, evidence of pig management, sophisticated bone tools, some of the world's earliest known playable musical instruments carved from crane wing bones, and chemical residues suggesting the fermentation of rice, honey, and fruit into an early alcoholic beverage. Stable-isotope studies of human bone from Jiahu have suggested that millet may have contributed modestly to diet at the site, but direct archaeobotanical confirmation of cultivated millet there is lacking, and some plant remains once identified as millet-related have more recently been reassessed as *Echinochloa*, a wild weed common in paddy environments, rather than a cultivated grain. Jiahu is therefore best understood as an early rice-associated community in a transitional zone between the two great river systems, combining incipient cultivation with extensive continued hunting, fishing, and gathering, rather than as a site demonstrating combined millet-and-rice farming.

The domestication of millet was a gradual process involving genetic and morphological changes that unfolded over many centuries of repeated human selection, whether through deliberate choice of seed for replanting or through the unconscious selective pressure created by human harvesting methods. Wild millet seed heads shatter naturally, dispersing seeds before humans can harvest them efficiently; domesticated millet increasingly developed non-shattering seed heads that retained their seeds until harvested, a trait that would have been strongly favored, whether consciously or not, by human harvesters who could more easily collect seed from plants that retained their grain. Domesticated millet also generally developed larger seeds than wild varieties, a trait that likely reflects both deliberate selection for more productive plants and the effects of cultivation in disturbed, nutrient-rich soil near human settlements.

By the era of the well-documented Yangshao culture, examined further in Chapter 5 and generally dated from roughly seven thousand to five thousand years ago, millet farming had become a well-established, dominant subsistence strategy across much of the middle Yellow River region, supported by ground stone tools for processing grain, storage pits for preserving harvested millet, and settled or semi-settled village life. This mature millet-farming tradition would remain the agricultural foundation of northern Chinese society for thousands of years afterward, eventually supplemented, from later prehistoric and early historical periods onward, by wheat, which arrived in China from western Eurasia through overland exchange networks and became an increasingly important northern crop especially from the Han dynasty onward, discussed in Chapter 15, without ever fully displacing millet's historical and culinary importance in the region.

Section 4.3

Rice Agriculture in the Yangtze Region

While communities in the north were gradually domesticating millet, communities in the middle and lower Yangtze River basin were independently engaged in a broadly parallel process involving the management and eventual domestication of wild rice (the wild ancestors of Oryza sativa). Wild rice grew naturally in the wetlands, marshes, and seasonally flooded areas of the Yangtze basin, and archaeobotanical evidence, including rice phytoliths and charred grain, indicates that communities in this region were harvesting, and increasingly managing, wild rice populations by at least ten thousand to eight thousand years ago, with fuller domestication developing over the following several thousand years.

Rice domestication followed a broadly similar genetic and morphological pattern to millet domestication, involving the gradual selection, whether deliberate or incidental, of non-shattering seed heads, larger grains, and other traits associated with more efficient harvesting and higher yields. Because rice thrives in wet, often flooded conditions, its cultivation eventually came to depend on specific water-management techniques distinct from the dry-land methods used for millet in the north: the construction and maintenance of small bunded plots capable of holding standing water, drainage and the control of water levels through simple channels, and, for much of the Neolithic period, direct seeding into managed wetland or bunded plots rather than the transplanting of separately raised seedlings. Evidence of bunding and water control associated with early rice cultivation appears at the lower Yangtze site of Kuahuqiao by roughly seven thousand seven hundred years ago, and constructed paddy fields are documented at several later Neolithic sites. The now-familiar technique of transplanting rice seedlings from nursery beds into flooded paddies, by contrast, is a considerably later development, becoming a widespread technique only around the late Han dynasty and afterward, discussed in Chapter 15; students should not assume that Neolithic rice farmers already practiced transplanting.

Recent archaeobotanical research also cautions against describing rice domestication in the Yangtze basin as a single, uniform process. Comparative study of different lower Yangtze subregions, including the Jinqu Basin, the Ningshao Plain, and the Taihu Plain, has identified different rates and sequences of selection for domestication traits and different paces of agricultural intensification from one subregion to the next, even within the broader lower Yangtze rice tradition. Rice domestication, in other words, was protracted and regionally variable even within southern China, not a single, uniform "southern" process unfolding identically everywhere.

The site complex associated with the Hemudu culture, in the lower Yangtze region and generally dated from roughly seven thousand to five thousand three hundred years ago, has provided some of the richest evidence for early, increasingly intensive rice farming in the region, including large quantities of preserved rice remains, wooden agricultural implements, evidence of pile-supported wooden architecture suited to the region's wet, low-lying terrain, and lacquered wooden objects reflecting considerable craft sophistication. Detailed archaeobotanical and faunal analysis at the related site of Tianluoshan, however, indicates that rice cultivation, while increasingly important, had not yet displaced hunting, fishing, and the gathering of wild plant foods such as acorns as the dominant basis of the economy; wild resources continued to supply a very substantial share of the community's food well into the period when rice was already being actively cultivated. Hemudu-culture communities are therefore best understood as increasingly sedentary societies with an intensifying, economically significant rice-based agriculture layered onto a still-substantial foundation of hunting, fishing, and gathering, rather than as a society in which mature, intensive paddy agriculture had already replaced wild-resource subsistence.

Over the following millennia, rice cultivation techniques in the Yangtze region and beyond became increasingly sophisticated, eventually supporting extraordinarily dense agricultural populations by the historical periods examined in later chapters, particularly the Tang and Song periods discussed in Chapters 26 and 32. The deep roots of this agricultural tradition, reaching back to the earliest rice management in the Neolithic Yangtze basin, help explain why the region's economic and demographic importance grew so dramatically over the long span of Chinese history, a transformation Chapter 2 introduced and that recurs throughout this textbook.

Section 4.4

Animal Domestication

Alongside the domestication of millet and rice, Neolithic communities in China gradually domesticated several important animal species, integrating animal management into farming economies in ways that provided meat, labor, and other resources while also, in some cases, requiring communities to adjust settlement patterns and labor organization to accommodate animal care.

Pigs were among the earliest and most historically important domesticated animals in China, with zooarchaeological evidence — including changes in tooth size, bone structure, and age-at-death patterns that distinguish domesticated pig populations from wild boar — indicating pig management and domestication in multiple regions by roughly eight thousand to seven thousand years ago, including at Jiahu. Pigs proved well suited to Chinese farming villages because they could be fed on agricultural waste, foraged food, and household scraps, required comparatively little specialized pasture land unlike grazing animals, and provided a reliable, efficiently reproducing source of meat and fat, a pattern of pig husbandry that would remain central to Chinese agricultural life for thousands of years afterward.

Dogs were domesticated even earlier than pigs, likely during the Paleolithic period discussed in Chapter 3 rather than originating within the Neolithic transition itself, and continued to play important roles in Neolithic communities as companions, hunting aids, and, in some contexts, a food source, with the precise balance of these roles varying by region and period. Pigs and dogs remain the two domesticated animal species most securely documented in Neolithic China, and students should treat other proposed early domesticates with more caution. Two animals once commonly listed alongside pigs and dogs in accounts of Neolithic Chinese agriculture — chickens and water buffalo — require significant revision in light of more recent research. A comprehensive reassessment of several hundred archaeological sites across Asia has found that the earliest securely identified domestic chickens come from central Thailand, dated to roughly 1650–1250 BCE, with chickens not securely documented in central China until the late second millennium BCE; several bones from Neolithic Chinese sites once identified as domestic chicken have, on closer examination, turned out to be pheasant. Chickens should therefore not be presented as part of the Neolithic Chinese domesticated-animal economy. Similarly, zooarchaeologists now distinguish the extinct wild Chinese water buffalo, Bubalus mephistopheles, whose remains are common at Neolithic and Bronze Age sites, from the domestic water buffalo, Bubalus bubalis; ancient DNA evidence indicates the wild species was not the ancestor of the modern domestic buffalo, and there is no secure evidence for managed domestic water buffalo in Neolithic China. Domestic water buffalo appear to have arrived or developed considerably later, and their history in China remains an active subject of research. One more tentative recent finding deserves cautious mention: researchers studying the Tianluoshan site near Hemudu have proposed possible evidence for early goose management, dated to roughly seven thousand years ago, based on bone morphology and site context, though this remains a single, debated case rather than an established pattern.

Unlike some other regions of the ancient world, such as parts of western Eurasia where cattle, sheep, and goats became central to both agriculture and long-distance pastoral economies from an early period, the Neolithic economies of the Yellow River and Yangtze basins developed around a somewhat different combination of domesticated species — millet or rice as the staple plant foods, with pigs and dogs as the securely documented primary domesticated animals — reflecting the specific environmental conditions and available wild species of East Asia rather than following any single universal pattern of agricultural development. Sheep, goats, cattle, and, later, chickens and domestic water buffalo would become important in Chinese agriculture only in subsequent periods, particularly from the Bronze Age onward and, for sheep, goats, and cattle, often introduced or adapted through contact with Central Asian and steppe pastoral traditions, a pattern examined further in Chapters 7 and 8.

Section 4.5

Permanent Villages and Settlement

As communities increasingly relied on cultivated crops, settlement patterns across both northern and southern China shifted gradually toward greater sedentism — the practice of remaining in one location for extended periods, in some cases permanently, rather than moving seasonally across the landscape in the manner typical of the mobile Paleolithic communities discussed in Chapter 3. This shift was neither immediate nor universal; some Neolithic communities continued to combine cultivation with significant mobility, seasonal movement, or continued reliance on hunting, fishing, and gathering well into the period when farming had become economically important, and the transition to fully sedentary village life occurred at different rates in different regions.

In the middle Yellow River region, early to middle Neolithic villages, well documented at sites associated with the Yangshao culture examined further in Chapter 5, typically featured semi-subterranean houses — dwellings partly dug into the ground, with walls and roofs constructed from wood, thatch, and packed earth above the excavated pit — a construction technique that provided insulation against the region's seasonal temperature extremes using comparatively simple materials and labor. Villages were often organized around a central open area, with houses arranged in clusters, and many settlements were surrounded by ditches, which may have served purposes including drainage, boundary marking, and defense, though the specific function of any given ditch system requires careful site-by-site interpretation rather than a single universal explanation.

In the Yangtze basin, early to middle Neolithic villages associated with cultures such as Hemudu developed different architectural solutions suited to the region's wetter, often waterlogged terrain, including wooden structures raised on piles above the ground or water level, a technique that protected dwellings from flooding and dampness while making efficient use of the region's abundant timber resources. These differing architectural traditions reflect, once again, the theme introduced in Chapter 2: geography and environment shaped, without rigidly determining, the specific forms that human settlement and technology took in different regions of Neolithic China.

The growth of more permanent villages had significant social and economic consequences. Investment in fixed agricultural plots, storage facilities, and semi-permanent or permanent housing encouraged longer-term commitment to specific locations, in turn encouraging further investment in land improvement, irrigation or drainage infrastructure, and community structures such as shared storage or ritual spaces. Permanent settlement also allowed for the accumulation of possessions and stored food surpluses to a degree not practical for highly mobile foraging communities, since mobile groups generally limit their possessions to what can be readily carried. This accumulation of stored resources, examined further in Section 4.8, would eventually contribute to new forms of social differentiation as some households, lineages, or communities accumulated more stored wealth, more fertile land, or more social influence than others — a development examined in greater depth in Chapter 5's discussion of early social complexity.

Section 4.6

Pottery, Tools, and Household Production

Pottery is one of the technologies most closely associated with the Neolithic period, and China's archaeological record contains some of the earliest known pottery anywhere in the world. Excavations at Xianrendong Cave in Jiangxi province have recovered pottery fragments dated to approximately twenty thousand to nineteen thousand years ago, produced by communities that were still highly mobile hunter-gatherers, well before the emergence of agriculture in the region. This evidence significantly complicates any assumption that pottery and farming necessarily developed together as a single technological package; instead, it demonstrates that mobile foraging communities in southern China had already developed the technical knowledge required to fire clay into durable, useful vessels, likely for purposes such as processing and storing wild plant foods, well before agricultural life began.

As agricultural communities became more established and more sedentary from roughly seven thousand years ago onward, pottery production expanded significantly and became increasingly specialized, reflecting the growing importance of vessels for cooking grain, storing harvested crops, fermenting beverages, and serving food within increasingly settled village communities. The Yangshao culture of the middle Yellow River is particularly well known for painted pottery, decorated with geometric, animal, and abstract designs in red, black, and other pigments, while the later Longshan culture, examined in Chapter 5, is associated with distinctive black pottery produced using more advanced firing techniques, including the use of controlled, oxygen-reduced kiln environments that produced the characteristic dark color and, in the finest examples, remarkably thin, precisely formed vessel walls.

Alongside pottery, Neolithic communities developed ground and polished stone tool technologies that differed significantly from the flaked stone tools that had dominated the preceding Paleolithic period discussed in Chapter 3. Ground stone axes, adzes, and hoes, produced by grinding and polishing stone against an abrasive surface rather than by flaking, provided durable, effective tools for clearing land, working wood, and tilling soil — tasks of increasing importance as communities invested more heavily in agriculture. Bone, antler, and shell tools also remained important throughout the Neolithic period, used for tasks ranging from harvesting grain to producing textiles, fishing, and craft production.

Historical Vocabulary
Neolithic

The Neolithic, or "New Stone Age," is the archaeological term for the period generally characterized by polished and ground stone tools, pottery, and, in most regions of the world, the eventual adoption of agriculture and more settled village life, following the long preceding Paleolithic period discussed in Chapter 3. The term was coined by nineteenth-century European archaeologists and, like "Paleolithic," should be understood as a useful organizing category rather than a rigid, universally applicable timeline.

In China specifically, the Neolithic "package" of traits — pottery, ground stone tools, sedentary village life, and agriculture — did not arrive together as a single bundle. As this chapter has shown, pottery developed among mobile hunter-gatherers in southern China thousands of years before agriculture became economically dominant, and different regions adopted sedentary village life, intensive cultivation, and other Neolithic traits at different rates and in different combinations. Students should use the term "Neolithic" as a broad chronological and technological label rather than assuming that every Neolithic society everywhere in China looked or developed identically.

Historical Vocabulary
Domestication

Domestication refers to heritable, population-level genetic and morphological changes that occur in plant or animal populations as a result of sustained human management and selection, producing organisms that differ measurably from their wild ancestors and that become increasingly integrated into human-controlled systems of reproduction, feeding, and habitat. Dependence on humans is not, by itself, the defining test of domestication — feral populations of otherwise domesticated species can survive and reproduce without human care — so historians and archaeologists focus instead on measurable, heritable changes in the population as a whole. Domestication is a gradual, cumulative process rather than a single event: it can take many centuries or even millennia of repeated human interaction with a plant or animal population before the population as a whole displays clearly domesticated traits.

Archaeologists identify domestication in the material record through several kinds of evidence: changes in seed size and shape, the development of non-shattering seed heads in domesticated grains, changes in animal bone structure, tooth size, and age-at-death patterns that differ from wild populations, and, increasingly, ancient DNA evidence that can directly trace genetic changes over time. Domestication should be distinguished from simple use or management of wild plants and animals, which does not necessarily produce measurable genetic or morphological change, though such management, as this chapter has shown, often represents an important early stage on the longer path toward full domestication.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultigen and Landrace

A cultigen is a plant species or population that exists in its present form primarily because of human cultivation and selection, having been substantially altered from any wild ancestor by sustained human management; broomcorn millet, foxtail millet, and domesticated rice are all cultigens in this sense, even though each descends from an identifiable wild progenitor still found in the region today or in the recent past.

A landrace is a locally adapted, traditionally cultivated variety of a domesticated crop, shaped by generations of farmers selecting and replanting seed suited to local soil, climate, and taste, but not produced through the formal, systematic plant-breeding programs of the modern era. Neolithic and early historical Chinese millet and rice populations were landraces in this sense: regionally varied, farmer-managed populations rather than uniform, centrally standardized crop varieties. Understanding the distinction between a cultigen and a landrace helps students appreciate that domestication produced not a single fixed "domesticated rice" or "domesticated millet," but many locally adapted populations whose characteristics varied by region and continued to change over time.

Section 4.7

Family, Labor, and Community

The adoption of agriculture reorganized daily labor, family life, and community structure in ways that historians and archaeologists continue to study through a combination of archaeological, skeletal, and comparative ethnographic evidence. Farming required different, and in many respects more demanding, patterns of labor than foraging: clearing and preparing land, planting, weeding, guarding crops against animals and pests, harvesting, threshing, and processing grain, and, in the case of rice cultivation, constructing and maintaining water-management infrastructure, all required sustained, often seasonally concentrated effort from households and communities.

Archaeologists and bioarchaeologists studying skeletal remains from Neolithic sites have identified evidence suggesting that the shift to agriculture was accompanied, in some populations, by changes in patterns of physical stress and activity consistent with the demands of farming labor, as well as, in some cases, evidence of nutritional stress associated with a diet more heavily dependent on a smaller number of staple crops compared to the more varied diet often available to foraging communities. This evidence supports the broader point introduced at the start of this chapter: the transition to agriculture, while eventually enabling much larger and more complex societies, was not a simple or uncomplicated improvement in individual quality of life, and its costs were not necessarily distributed evenly within Neolithic communities.

Family and household organization also likely changed as communities became more sedentary and more dependent on specific plots of cultivated land. Permanent or semi-permanent housing, examined in Section 4.5, and the accumulation of stored grain, tools, and other possessions within a fixed dwelling may have increased the practical importance of household claims to particular parcels of land and particular stores of food, themes that would become increasingly visible in the more socially stratified societies examined in Chapter 5. Archaeology does not, however, allow historians to conclude with confidence that Paleolithic kin groups were less stable than Neolithic households, nor to specify what particular kinship or inheritance rules, if any, accompanied this shift; such claims should be treated as reasonable hypotheses suggested by the material evidence rather than as established fact.

The precise division of agricultural, household, and craft labor by gender and age in specific Neolithic communities cannot be determined with full certainty from archaeological evidence alone, and historians should be cautious about projecting later, more thoroughly documented patterns of gendered labor division backward onto Neolithic society without direct evidence. Some archaeological evidence, including burial goods, tool associations, and, at some sites, skeletal indicators of specific activities, has been used by researchers to propose hypotheses about gendered and age-based labor division in specific Neolithic communities, but such reconstructions remain interpretive and should be presented to students as informed hypotheses rather than settled fact.

Section 4.8

Population Growth and Environmental Change

Agriculture, by producing more calories per unit of land than foraging typically could in the same environment, supported the growth of significantly larger and denser human populations than had been possible during the Paleolithic period. Archaeological evidence indicates that Neolithic villages in both the Yellow River and Yangtze regions grew progressively larger and more numerous over the course of the Neolithic period, and that the total human population across the geographic space this textbook studies increased substantially between the early stages of plant cultivation and the emergence of the more socially complex Longshan- and Liangzhu-era societies examined in Chapter 5.

This population growth was neither immediate nor without limits. Agricultural communities remained vulnerable to crop failure caused by drought, flooding, pests, or other environmental disruptions, and the archaeological and paleoclimatic record indicates that Neolithic communities in China, like agricultural societies in many other parts of the world, experienced periods of hardship, settlement abandonment, and, in some cases, significant population decline in response to environmental or other pressures, even as the longer-term overall trend across the Neolithic period moved toward larger, more numerous, and more permanent settlements.

The expansion of agriculture also began to reshape the physical environment of the regions in which it developed, a pattern of human environmental impact that would continue and intensify throughout the rest of Chinese history. Clearing land for cultivation reduced forest cover in some areas; repeated cultivation of the same plots could affect soil fertility and structure over time; and the increasing concentration of human and domesticated animal populations in fixed settlements likely affected local ecosystems, water sources, and disease patterns in ways that are still being reconstructed by environmental archaeologists. This early evidence of human environmental modification anticipates a recurring theme in later chapters of this textbook: the long, complex, and sometimes contradictory relationship between agricultural development, population growth, and environmental change in Chinese history, from the flood-control challenges of the historical Yellow River discussed in Chapter 2 to the environmental pressures of the late imperial and modern periods discussed in Chapters 49 and 75.

Cause and Effect

Why Agriculture Began: Weighing the Causes

Environmental causes

Early Holocene warming and changing hydrological regimes, discussed in Chapter 3, altered plant habitats, wetland extent, and resource availability across the Yellow River and Yangtze basins — not through simple, uniform "stabilization," since Yangtze basin communities in particular continued to contend with substantial hydrological and sea-level change, but by creating new local opportunities and constraints within which sustained plant management could intensify.

Demographic and ecological causes

Some researchers propose that growing human populations, or increasing pressure on especially favorable foraging locations, encouraged communities to intensify their exploitation of specific, reliable plant resources through active management, gradually increasing dependence on those plants over generations.

Social and behavioral causes

Accumulated ecological knowledge, experimentation, and the social or ritual value of stored surplus food — for feasting, exchange, or status display — may have encouraged communities to invest increasing effort in cultivation even before it offered a clear net gain in efficiency over foraging.

Effects

Whatever the precise balance of causes in any specific community, the cumulative result across many centuries was a broad regional shift toward greater sedentism, larger populations, new technologies, new social arrangements, and, eventually, the foundations for the socially stratified societies examined in Chapter 5.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains why agriculture began in China or anywhere else in the world. Most current researchers favor multi-causal explanations that combine environmental opportunity, ecological knowledge, demographic pressure, and social motivation, rather than searching for one decisive trigger.

Section 4.9

Agriculture and the Foundations of Complex Society

This chapter closes by looking forward to Chapter 5's examination of regional Neolithic cultures and early social complexity, explaining how the agricultural developments traced in this chapter could support, without automatically producing, the more hierarchical, more materially elaborate societies that emerged in parts of the Yellow River and Yangtze regions during the later Neolithic period. Agricultural surplus — food produced beyond what a household needed for its own immediate consumption — provided one important material basis for several developments that would prove historically significant, without being the sole or sufficient cause of any of them. Surplus could facilitate specialization in tasks other than food production, including craft production, ritual leadership, and, eventually, administrative or military roles. It created goods, particularly stored grain, that could be accumulated, exchanged, given as gifts, or, in less benign circumstances, seized or controlled by some households or individuals at the expense of others, providing one material basis, among others, for the social and economic inequality documented at sites associated with the Longshan and Liangzhu cultures, examined in depth in Chapter 5. And it could support larger, denser populations that, in turn, created new organizational challenges and new possibilities for coordinated labor, exchange, and, eventually, political authority. Comparative evidence from complex hunter-gatherer societies elsewhere in the world, which developed significant social hierarchy without agriculture, shows that surplus of this kind is neither universally necessary nor sufficient for social complexity to emerge; it is best understood as one contributing factor among several, whose effects depended heavily on local social and historical circumstances.

It is important for students to understand that this trajectory — from early cultivation, through established village agriculture, toward increasing social complexity — was not an automatic or inevitable outcome of agriculture itself. Many agricultural societies throughout world history have remained organized around relatively egalitarian village communities for extended periods without developing the pronounced social hierarchies, monumental construction, and specialized elite craft production documented at sites such as Liangzhu. The specific combination of factors that produced increasing social complexity in later Neolithic China — including, but not limited to, agricultural surplus, population density, environmental and labor demands such as those associated with large-scale water management in the Yangtze delta, and social and ideological developments that are only partially visible in the archaeological record — will be examined in detail in Chapter 5, which builds directly on the agricultural foundations established in this chapter.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a clear understanding that the two major agricultural traditions introduced here — northern millet farming and southern rice farming — were not simply technical developments confined to the Neolithic period. They established enduring regional patterns of crop, diet, labor, and environmental relationship that would continue to shape Chinese history for thousands of years afterward, echoing through the discussion of Shang and Zhou agriculture in Chapters 7 and 8, the north-south agricultural contrast maintained across the Age of Division in Chapters 20 through 23, and the dramatic southern agricultural and commercial expansion of the Tang and Song periods examined in Chapters 26 and 32. The Neolithic Revolution, in other words, was not merely a prehistoric prelude to "real" Chinese history; it established structural patterns of regional difference that remained visible throughout the entire span of history this textbook covers.

Section 4.10

Chapter Review and Agricultural Transition Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the gradual, regionally distinct emergence of agriculture in the lands later associated with Chinese history, examining how communities in the middle and lower Yellow River basin domesticated millet and communities in the middle and lower Yangtze basin domesticated rice through independent, centuries-long processes rooted in local environmental conditions and human experimentation rather than through the spread of a single agricultural innovation from one point of origin.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. The transition from foraging to farming was a gradual, uneven process unfolding over many centuries rather than a single decisive event, and it should not be understood as an obvious or inevitable improvement in human welfare, since it often required more labor and offered a less varied diet than foraging while introducing new risks associated with sedentary life. Millet cultivation developed in the north and rice cultivation developed in the south as largely independent processes, reflecting the very different environmental conditions of the two regions introduced in Chapter 2, and this north-south agricultural distinction would continue to shape Chinese history for millennia. Pottery developed among mobile hunter-gatherer communities in southern China well before agriculture became economically dominant, complicating any assumption that Neolithic technological traits arrived together as a single package. The domestication of plants and animals was a gradual genetic and morphological process, identifiable in the archaeological record through changes such as non-shattering seed heads, larger grain size, and shifts in animal bone structure. Agriculture encouraged the growth of more permanent villages, more demanding and reorganized patterns of household labor, and, over time, the accumulation of stored surplus that would eventually support the increasing social complexity examined in Chapter 5. And throughout, historians and archaeologists must rely on archaeobotanical, zooarchaeological, and other scientific evidence, using cautious, evidence-based language appropriate to a period with no surviving writing.

Primary Source Window

The Jiahu Excavation Reports

Creator: Chinese archaeological teams excavating the Jiahu site in Henan province from the 1980s onward · Date represented: Approximately 9,000–7,800 years ago · Type: Archaeological excavation reports and scientific publications documenting a major early Neolithic settlement

Context: Excavations at Jiahu have recovered an unusually rich body of evidence for early Neolithic life in the upper Huai River drainage, a transitional zone between the Yellow and Yangtze river systems, including plant remains, animal bones, tools, pottery, and rare organic materials preserved under favorable conditions, allowing archaeologists to reconstruct a remarkably detailed picture of a community in the midst of a long, gradual transition involving early rice use alongside extensive continued foraging.

Paraphrase of the excavation findings (not a translated quotation):

The excavation reports describe the recovery of rice remains and other plant remains, managed pig populations, sophisticated bone tools including carved flutes capable of producing a musical scale, and chemical residues on pottery consistent with a fermented beverage combining rice, honey, and fruit, together suggesting a community engaged in early rice-associated cultivation alongside continued hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Analysis: The Jiahu evidence is valuable precisely because it resists a simple story of sudden agricultural "arrival." It shows a community engaged in a mixed, flexible subsistence strategy, combining early rice use with pig management, continued foraging, and a materially rich craft and musical culture. Notably, while isotopic evidence has raised the possibility of a modest millet component in diet, direct archaeobotanical confirmation of cultivated millet at the site is lacking, and material once proposed as evidence of millet has, on closer examination, more plausibly been identified as a wild paddy weed. This complexity supports the chapter's broader argument that the Neolithic transition was gradual, multifaceted, geographically uneven, and did not require communities to abandon foraging abruptly in favor of farming.

Historical Debate

One Origin or Many? Explaining the Rise of Agriculture in China

The question: Why did communities in the Yellow River and Yangtze basins begin cultivating plants at broadly similar times, and what combination of factors best explains this transformation? Archaeologists and historians have proposed several overlapping, sometimes competing, explanatory models.

Interpretation A — Environmental opportunity as the primary driver: Some researchers emphasize the importance of early Holocene environmental change, arguing that the retreat of Ice Age conditions and the resulting shifts in habitat and hydrology expanded the range and reliability of wild millet and wild rice populations to the point where sustained cultivation became newly viable in various localities, and that human communities responded relatively directly to these changed environmental conditions.
Interpretation B — Population pressure and resource stress as the primary driver: Other researchers emphasize demographic and ecological pressure, proposing that growing populations or increasing competition for the most productive foraging locations pushed communities toward more intensive, labor-demanding management of specific plant resources as a way of increasing food reliability, even at the cost of additional labor.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping archaeobotanical, climatic, and settlement-pattern evidence; they differ in how much causal weight to assign to environmental opportunity versus social and demographic pressure, and most current specialists favor multi-causal explanations that combine elements of both interpretations rather than treating either as a complete explanation on its own.

Why it matters: How historians and archaeologists explain the origins of agriculture shapes how they interpret the pace and inevitability of the transition, and it cautions against assuming that agriculture was either a simple, automatic response to improved climate or an unavoidable outcome of population growth.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, explain which interpretation you find more persuasive for explaining the roughly parallel timing of millet cultivation in the north and rice cultivation in the south, or explain why a combined explanation may be more accurate than either interpretation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in an Early Farming Village

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many sites, not quotations from specific named individuals.

From the perspective of an elder in a middle Yellow River millet-farming village: Knowledge of when to plant, how to prepare loess soil, and how to store harvested grain through the winter represented an accumulated, hard-won body of practical expertise, passed down through direct instruction and closely tied to the community's survival, even as hunting and gathering remained a valued supplement to the household's cultivated food.
From the perspective of a household in a lower Yangtze rice-farming community: Managing flooded rice paddies required sustained, cooperative labor with neighboring households to maintain shared water channels, and the reliability of the harvest depended as much on effective community cooperation as on any single household's individual effort.
From the perspective of a still-mobile forager living near an increasingly settled farming community: Not every community in Neolithic China adopted intensive agriculture at the same pace, and some groups likely continued to rely more heavily on hunting, fishing, and gathering while exchanging goods, ideas, or marriage partners with increasingly settled farming neighbors, illustrating that the Neolithic transition was uneven and did not eliminate other ways of life immediately.
From the perspective of a modern archaeobotanist studying charred grain remains: Distinguishing early cultivation from full domestication requires painstaking analysis of tiny plant remains recovered through soil flotation, and each new excavation has the potential to push back, refine, or complicate the currently accepted chronology of agricultural origins in China.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Diet and Daily Labor From Foraging to Farming

What Changed

  • Diet shifted toward greater reliance on a smaller number of staple cultivated grains, supplemented by, rather than dominated by, wild foods
  • Daily and seasonal labor became increasingly organized around the specific demands of planting, tending, and harvesting particular crops
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward greater permanence, larger village populations, and more substantial architecture
  • Technology diversified to include ground stone tools, expanded pottery production, and, in the south, wooden water-management structures

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting, fishing, and gathering continued to supplement cultivated food throughout most of the Neolithic period, even in well-established farming communities
  • Regional environmental adaptation, already visible in the Paleolithic period discussed in Chapter 3, continued to shape distinct northern and southern subsistence strategies
  • Communities remained organized primarily at the household and village level, without the larger political institutions examined in later chapters

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real transformations and the underlying continuities of the Neolithic transition helps students understand agriculture as a gradual reorganization of existing practices and knowledge rather than a sudden break with the past.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Neolithic Household

Archaeological evidence from village sites across the Yellow River and Yangtze regions allows historians to offer a cautious reconstruction of daily life within a Neolithic farming household. A household likely centered on a semi-subterranean or pile-supported dwelling, shared among several related individuals across generations, with daily activity organized around the agricultural calendar: preparing fields, planting in the appropriate season, tending crops against weeds and pests, and undertaking the labor-intensive work of harvesting, threshing, and storing grain for the months ahead.

Food preparation combined cultivated grains, processed using ground stone tools and cooked in pottery vessels, with continued hunting, fishing, and gathering of wild foods, reflecting the mixed subsistence strategy that characterized most of the Neolithic period. Craft production, including pottery making, tool production, and, at sites such as Hemudu, woodworking and lacquer production, likely occupied significant household and community time, particularly during agricultural off-seasons.

Community life increasingly involved cooperation beyond the individual household, particularly in regions such as the Yangtze basin where shared water-management infrastructure required coordinated maintenance, and, at some sites, in the construction of ditches, communal storage facilities, or early ritual spaces whose precise social and religious significance remains an active area of archaeological interpretation, discussed further in Chapter 5's examination of ritual authority and social hierarchy in the later Neolithic period.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined one of the most consequential transformations in the history of the lands later associated with China: the gradual shift, over many centuries, from mobile foraging toward settled agricultural life. Building on Chapter 3's account of the deep human past, this chapter traced how communities in the middle and lower Yellow River basin gradually domesticated millet, while communities in the middle and lower Yangtze basin, working independently, gradually domesticated rice, establishing two distinct regional agricultural traditions whose consequences would echo throughout the rest of Chinese history.

Students have learned that this transition was neither sudden nor simply beneficial. Early farming often demanded more, not less, daily labor than foraging, and it introduced new dietary, health, and environmental risks even as it eventually could support substantially larger and denser populations in many environments than foraging alone typically could. The chapter emphasized that pottery, often assumed to be a hallmark of Neolithic farming societies, actually developed among mobile hunter-gatherers in southern China well before agriculture became economically dominant, a finding that complicates simple narratives of Neolithic technological "packages" arriving together.

The chapter examined the process of domestication as a gradual genetic and morphological transformation, identifiable through archaeobotanical and zooarchaeological evidence such as non-shattering seed heads, larger grain size, and changes in animal bone structure, rather than as a single moment of discovery. It traced the secure domestication of pigs and dogs alongside the two major plant domestication traditions of millet and rice, while explaining why chickens and domestic water buffalo, once commonly listed among Neolithic Chinese domesticates, are now understood by specialists to belong to considerably later periods.

Students also examined how agriculture reshaped settlement patterns, encouraging the growth of increasingly permanent villages with architectural styles adapted to regional environments — semi-subterranean houses in the drier north and pile-supported wooden architecture in the wetter south — and explored how this increasing sedentism, in turn, reshaped family organization, labor patterns, and the accumulation of stored surplus and material possessions.

The chapter's discussion of population growth and environmental change demonstrated that agricultural expansion began reshaping the physical landscape of the regions in which it developed, anticipating a theme — the complex, evolving relationship between human societies and their environment — that recurs throughout the remainder of this textbook, from the historical Yellow River flood-control challenges discussed in Chapter 2 to the environmental pressures of the contemporary period discussed in Chapter 75.

Finally, this chapter looked forward to Chapter 5, explaining how agricultural surplus could support — without making inevitable — the increasing social complexity, craft specialization, and ritual authority documented at sites associated with the Yangshao, Longshan, Hemudu, and Liangzhu cultures. The two regional agricultural traditions established during the Neolithic period, this chapter has argued, were not merely a prehistoric backdrop to "real" Chinese history; they established enduring patterns of regional difference in crop, diet, labor, and environmental relationship that would continue to shape Chinese history across the entire span of chapters that follow.

Armed with this understanding of agricultural origins, students are now prepared to move into Chapter 5's examination of the diverse regional Neolithic cultures that built upon these agricultural foundations, developing increasingly complex forms of craft specialization, exchange, and social hierarchy that would, by Chapter 6, give rise to the earliest states discussed in this textbook.

A microscopic silica structure formed within plant cells that survives in soil long after organic plant material decays, useful for identifying ancient plant use.
Wild progenitor
The wild ancestral species from which a domesticated plant or animal descends.
Broomcorn millet
One of two principal millet species domesticated in northern China, a hardy, drought-tolerant grain.
Foxtail millet
The second principal millet species domesticated in northern China, widely cultivated alongside broomcorn millet.
Wet-rice cultivation
An intensive agricultural system involving flooded paddy fields, developed in the Yangtze basin and requiring significant water-management labor.
Dry-land farming
Agriculture relying on natural rainfall rather than irrigation or flooding, associated with millet cultivation in the north.
Sedentism
The practice of remaining in one location for extended or permanent periods, in contrast to the mobility typical of foraging societies.
Semi-subterranean house
A dwelling partly dug into the ground, common in early to middle Neolithic villages of the Yellow River region.
Ground stone tool
A stone tool shaped by grinding and polishing rather than flaking, associated with the Neolithic period.
Painted pottery
Pottery decorated with pigment designs, especially associated with the Yangshao culture of the middle Yellow River.
Kiln
A structure used to fire pottery or other materials at controlled, elevated temperatures.
Surplus
Food or other resources produced beyond a household's immediate consumption needs, which could facilitate craft specialization and social differentiation, though neither outcome followed automatically from surplus alone.
Storage pit
An excavated pit used to preserve harvested grain, common at Neolithic village sites.
Broad-spectrum foraging
A subsistence strategy relying on a wide range of wild plant and animal resources rather than a small number of staple foods.
Niche construction
The concept that human activity actively reshapes the surrounding environment in ways that, in turn, reshape human behavior and biology over time.
Agricultural intensification
The process of increasing the labor, technology, or resources invested in a given area of land to increase food production.
Social stratification
The organization of a society into levels with unequal access to wealth, status, or power, a development examined further in Chapter 5.
Jiahu
An early Neolithic settlement in the upper Huai River drainage, a transitional zone between the Yellow and Yangtze systems, showing early rice use, pig management, extensive continued foraging, and early musical instruments; secure evidence for cultivated millet is lacking.
Yangshao culture
A widespread middle Neolithic culture of the middle Yellow River, known for millet farming and painted pottery.
Hemudu culture
A Neolithic culture of the lower Yangtze region, known for sophisticated wet-rice cultivation and pile-supported architecture.
Longshan culture
A late Neolithic culture of the middle and lower Yellow River, associated with walled settlements and increasing social stratification.
Liangzhu culture
A late Neolithic culture of the lower Yangtze delta, associated with large-scale water management and elite jade production.
Xianrendong Cave
A cave site in Jiangxi province yielding some of the earliest known pottery in the world, produced by mobile hunter-gatherers.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago; its generally warmer but regionally variable climatic and hydrological conditions altered plant habitats and created changing opportunities and constraints for cultivation.
Bioarchaeology
The study of human skeletal remains to reconstruct ancient health, diet, and activity patterns.
Non-shattering seed head
A trait selected for in domesticated grains, in which seeds remain attached to the plant rather than dispersing naturally, making harvesting more efficient.
Flotation (archaeological technique)
A method of recovering tiny charred plant remains from excavated soil by separating light organic material in water.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined one of the deepest and most consequential foundations of Chinese history: the gradual, independent emergence of agriculture in the Yellow River and Yangtze basins. Understanding this transformation matters not because it produced an immediate "civilization," but because it established the material, environmental, and social preconditions from which the regional cultures, early states, and eventually the great dynastic civilizations examined throughout the rest of this textbook would emerge.

The two agricultural traditions introduced in this chapter — northern millet farming and southern rice farming — did not remain confined to the Neolithic period. They became enduring structural features of Chinese history, shaping regional diet, labor, settlement, and economic development across more than seven thousand years, from the early farming villages examined here through the Bronze Age states of Chapters 7 and 8, the north-south political division of the Age of Division in Chapters 20 through 23, and the dramatic southern economic ascendance of the Tang and Song periods in Chapters 26 and 32.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill: resisting the temptation to describe major transformations, even ones that eventually enabled remarkable human achievements, as simple, uncomplicated progress. The shift to agriculture brought real costs alongside its real, eventual benefits, and appreciating this complexity prepares students to think critically about later historical transformations examined throughout this textbook, from state formation to industrialization to modern economic development, none of which should be understood as a straightforward story of improvement without cost.

As students move forward into Chapter 5's examination of regional Neolithic cultures and early social complexity, they should carry with them a clear understanding of the agricultural foundations established in this chapter: two independently developed farming traditions, rooted in distinct regional environments, whose accumulation of surplus, population growth, and craft specialization could facilitate, without automatically producing, the more hierarchical, more materially elaborate societies examined in the chapters that follow — societies that would, in turn, lay the groundwork for the early states examined in Chapter 6 and the long dynastic history that occupies the great majority of this textbook.
