Part I — China, Geography, and the Deep Past
5

Regional Cultures, Villages, and Early Social Complexity

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

At Liangzhu, near the shore of the lower Yangtze delta, roughly five thousand years ago, laborers moved earth by the basketload to raise a system of dams, canals, and reservoirs across dozens of square kilometers of wetland, while other members of the same society carved and polished disks and tubes of hard, difficult jade with a precision that modern craftspeople still find remarkable to reproduce without metal tools. No single household could have organized this labor. No single family could have commanded the jade, the specialized carvers, or the authority needed to mobilize thousands of workers over a period of years to build this system, and then to sustain and maintain it across many generations afterward. Something new had emerged in the world of Neolithic villages described in Chapter 4 — something that later chapters will call, in various forms, social hierarchy, ritual authority, and, eventually, the early state.

Chapter 4 traced the long, gradual development of agriculture in northern and southern China, showing how communities in the Yellow River basin domesticated millet and communities in the Yangtze basin domesticated rice through largely independent processes rooted in local environment and experimentation. That chapter closed by noting that agricultural surplus could support, without automatically producing, more complex and more hierarchical forms of society. This chapter picks up that thread directly, asking how, why, and where some Neolithic communities in China moved from the relatively small, relatively egalitarian farming villages examined in Chapter 4 toward larger, more socially differentiated, more materially elaborate regional cultures — and why this process unfolded differently, and to different degrees, in different parts of the geographic space this textbook studies.

This chapter is fundamentally a chapter about regional diversity. Rather than describing a single "Chinese Neolithic culture" that gradually became more complex everywhere at the same pace, this chapter introduces several distinct archaeological cultures — Yangshao and Longshan in the middle and lower Yellow River region, Hemudu and Liangzhu in the lower Yangtze region, Hongshan in the northeast, and others — each with its own trajectory, its own material culture, its own forms of settlement, craft production, and, in some cases, social hierarchy. Students should resist the temptation to see these cultures as sequential stages on a single ladder leading inevitably toward "civilization." They were contemporaneous or overlapping, regionally distinct societies, interacting with one another across considerable distances through exchange networks that moved goods, materials, technologies, and probably people and ideas as well, but each developing according to its own local social, environmental, and historical logic.

The chapter also asks students to think carefully about a historical problem that will recur throughout this textbook: how and why social inequality emerges. The archaeological record examined in this chapter shows increasingly dramatic evidence of social differentiation over the course of the fifth, fourth, and early third millennia BCE — larger and more elaborate burials for some individuals than others, walled settlements suggesting organized defense and labor mobilization, specialized craft production of prestige goods such as jade and fine ceramics that required skill, time, and access to scarce materials, and, at some sites, evidence for large-scale coordinated labor projects that only some form of authority could have organized. Explaining why this differentiation occurred — and why it occurred more dramatically in some regions, such as the lower Yangtze delta, than in others — remains a subject of active archaeological research and scholarly debate, one this chapter introduces without pretending to resolve.

Geography, again, matters throughout this chapter. The dry, loess-based agricultural systems of the middle Yellow River region supported the walled, defensively organized Longshan-culture towns examined in Section 5.3, while the wetland environment of the Yangtze delta, where water management was especially important, is associated with the particularly dramatic social hierarchy documented at Liangzhu, examined in Section 5.5 — though, as that section discusses, scholars debate whether hydraulic works fostered political centralization there, resulted from already-centralized authority, or developed through a reciprocal interaction of both. The Hongshan culture of the northeast, examined in Section 5.6, developed yet another distinctive regional pattern, centered on ritual complexes and jade production in a colder, more marginal agricultural environment than either the Yellow River or Yangtze heartlands. These regional differences should reinforce, rather than complicate, the central lesson of this textbook's opening chapters: Chinese civilization did not emerge from a single point and radiate outward. It emerged from the interaction of multiple, materially and socially distinct regional societies, each contributing, in different ways and to different degrees, to the political and cultural world that the early states examined in Chapter 6 would eventually inherit.

By the end of this chapter, students should understand the major regional Neolithic cultures of the fifth through third millennia BCE, the evidence for increasing craft specialization, exchange, and social hierarchy across this period, and how these developments — while not directly or automatically producing the early states examined in Chapter 6 — created the social, material, and organizational preconditions from which those early states would eventually emerge.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the major regional Neolithic cultures of China discussed in this chapter and their approximate locations and dates.
  2. Explain why Neolithic China should be understood as a landscape of interacting regional cultures rather than a single unfolding "Chinese Neolithic."
  3. Compare Yangshao and Longshan cultures of the Yellow River region, tracing how settlement, craft production, and social organization changed over time.
  4. Compare Hemudu and Liangzhu cultures of the Yangtze delta, and assess why Liangzhu shows dramatically greater social hierarchy than earlier Yangtze-region cultures.
  5. Describe the Hongshan culture and its distinctive northern ritual and jade traditions.
  6. Analyze the archaeological evidence for craft specialization, exchange networks, and long-distance contact among Neolithic regional cultures.
  7. Evaluate the evidence for social hierarchy and ritual authority in the late Neolithic period, including burial goods, monumental construction, and specialized production.
  8. Assess competing scholarly explanations for the emergence of social complexity and inequality in late Neolithic China.
  9. Distinguish the specific, regionally variable pathways toward complexity from any single, uniform model of social evolution.
  10. Connect the developments examined in this chapter to the early states examined in Chapter 6, without assuming an inevitable or linear progression.
  11. Use archaeological evidence, including burial data, settlement patterns, and craft remains, to support historical claims about a period without writing.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did some late Neolithic societies in China develop pronounced social hierarchy while others remained comparatively egalitarian?
  2. What kinds of archaeological evidence allow historians to detect social inequality in a period without writing?
  3. How did environmental and economic differences between the Yellow River and Yangtze regions shape different regional trajectories toward complexity?
  4. What does the exchange of goods such as jade across long distances suggest about contact between distant Neolithic communities?
  5. How should historians interpret monumental construction, such as the hydraulic works at Liangzhu, and why do scholars disagree about whether Liangzhu society is best classified as an early state, a complex chiefdom, or a state-like society?
  6. Why do archaeologists caution against treating Neolithic regional cultures as sequential "stages" leading directly and inevitably to the early states of Chapter 6?
  7. What role did ritual, religion, and symbolic authority appear to play in the emergence of social hierarchy?
  8. How does the concept of craft specialization help historians understand changing forms of labor and status in late Neolithic society?
  9. What are the limits of using burial evidence to reconstruct the lives, beliefs, and social status of people who left no writing?
  10. How does this chapter's account of regional diversity prepare students to understand the "Xia question" examined in Chapter 6?

Historical Context

Chapter 4 established that agriculture developed independently in the middle and lower Yellow River basin, where communities domesticated millet, and in the middle and lower Yangtze basin, where communities domesticated rice, over a long span beginning roughly ten thousand years ago and continuing, in a gradual and geographically uneven process, for several thousand years afterward. By approximately seven thousand to five thousand years ago, agriculture had become the dominant, though rarely exclusive, subsistence strategy across much of both regions, supporting the growth of larger, increasingly permanent village communities such as those associated with the Yangshao and Hemudu cultures introduced briefly in Chapter 4.

This chapter begins where Chapter 4 left off chronologically, focusing primarily on the period from roughly five thousand to four thousand years ago — the later Neolithic — while also reaching back somewhat earlier to establish the fuller Yangshao and Hemudu context, and forward to the threshold of the early states examined in Chapter 6. During this span, archaeological evidence across multiple regions of China shows a marked intensification of several related trends: growing settlement size and, at some sites, the construction of substantial earthen walls or ditches around settlements; increasing craft specialization, particularly in fine pottery, jade, and other materials requiring skilled, time-intensive labor; increasingly unequal burial practices, in which some individuals were interred with dramatically more numerous, more elaborate, or more materially valuable grave goods than others; and, at a small number of exceptionally well-documented sites, evidence for large-scale, coordinated construction projects that indicate some form of authority capable of mobilizing labor beyond the immediate household or extended family.

Students should approach this evidence with the same interpretive caution urged throughout Chapter 4. The absence of writing in this period means that historians and archaeologists cannot access the names, titles, beliefs, or self-understanding of the people who built these societies; conclusions must be built carefully from material evidence — burial goods, settlement plans, craft remains, faunal and botanical evidence, and, increasingly, isotopic and genetic evidence — rather than from later written traditions that describe or claim to describe this period, most of which were composed many centuries afterward and reflect the concerns of much later societies. Chapter 6 will examine this problem directly in connection with the traditional accounts of the Xia dynasty; this chapter lays essential groundwork by showing what the archaeological record itself, considered on its own terms, actually documents about the several centuries before the earliest states.

Geographically, this chapter ranges more widely than Chapter 4. In addition to the middle Yellow River region (associated with Yangshao and, later, Longshan communities) and the lower Yangtze region (associated with Hemudu and, later, Liangzhu communities), this chapter introduces the Hongshan culture of the northeast, in the region spanning parts of modern Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, and Hebei — a reminder that significant Neolithic social development was not confined to the two river basins most emphasized in Chapter 4. Other regional Neolithic cultures existed elsewhere across the geographic space this textbook studies, including in the southwest, the southeast coast, and Sichuan; this chapter concentrates on Yangshao, Longshan, Hemudu, Liangzhu, and Hongshan both because they are especially well documented archaeologically and because their developments connect most directly to the early states examined in the chapters that follow, while acknowledging that this focus should not be mistaken for a claim that other regions lacked significant Neolithic development of their own.

Finally, students should carry forward from Chapter 4 the understanding that surplus, sedentism, and population growth could support, without automatically producing, increasing social complexity. This chapter explores what additional factors — environmental demands, craft specialization, exchange networks, ritual practice, and social competition among others — appear, on the basis of current archaeological evidence, to have contributed to the emergence of pronounced hierarchy in some regions and not others, while resisting any simple, universal formula that would explain the process the same way in every case.

Major Sites, Cultures, and Concepts

Reference — Sites, Cultures & Concepts

Yangshao culture
A widespread middle Neolithic culture of the middle Yellow River, roughly 7,000–5,000 years ago, known for millet agriculture, painted pottery, and semi-subterranean housing arranged around communal village spaces.
Longshan horizon
A group of related, regionally distinct late Neolithic cultures across the middle and lower Yellow River, roughly 5,000–4,000 years ago, sharing walled settlements, distinctive thin-walled black pottery, and increasing evidence of social stratification and interregional conflict, rather than forming one single unified culture.
Hemudu culture
A Neolithic culture of the lower Yangtze region, roughly 7,000–5,300 years ago, known for intensifying wet-rice cultivation, wooden pile-supported architecture, and lacquerware, alongside continued hunting, fishing, and gathering.
Liangzhu culture
A late Neolithic culture of the lower Yangtze delta, roughly 5,300–4,300 years ago, associated with large-scale hydraulic engineering, walled precincts, elite jade production, and the clearest Neolithic Chinese evidence for pronounced social hierarchy.
Hongshan culture
A Neolithic culture of the northeast, spanning parts of modern Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, and Hebei, traditionally dated to roughly 6,500–5,000 years ago, with its most elaborate ceremonial expression at Niuheliang dated more specifically to roughly 5,500–5,000 years ago, known for ritual complexes, altar-like platform structures, and distinctive jade carving traditions, including jade forms associated with dragon- and animal-like imagery.
Prestige goods
Objects, often made from rare or difficult-to-work materials such as jade, whose value lay significantly in their role as markers of status, authority, or ritual significance rather than in ordinary practical use.
Elite burial
A grave containing a markedly greater quantity or quality of goods than typical burials at the same site, used by archaeologists as one form of evidence for social differentiation.
Rammed earth (hangtu)
A construction technique in which earth is compacted in layers within a wooden frame to form durable walls, platforms, or foundations, used in Longshan-era settlement walls and, later, in Bronze Age architecture examined in Chapter 6.
Erlitou
A major early Bronze Age site examined in detail in Chapter 6, representing the threshold between the regional Neolithic cultures examined in this chapter and the early states of the following chapter.

Section 5.1

Neolithic Regional Diversity

Any account of the late Neolithic period in China must begin by rejecting a common and misleading simplification: the idea that Chinese civilization developed from a single ancient cultural "core" that gradually expanded outward to absorb or civilize its neighbors. The archaeological evidence tells a different story. Across the fifth, fourth, and early third millennia BCE, multiple regional societies across a broad swath of eastern Asia — from the middle Yellow River to the Yangtze delta to the northeastern woodlands — developed increasingly complex, increasingly hierarchical, and increasingly materially elaborate forms of social organization, largely independently of one another, even as they remained connected through overlapping and expanding networks of exchange, contact, and mutual influence.

Chinese archaeologists have sometimes described this pattern using the phrase "a sky full of stars" (mantian xingdou, 满天星斗), a metaphor associated with the archaeologist Su Bingqi, contrasting it with older models that imagined a single Yellow River "core" culture from which all later Chinese civilization descended. This chapter adopts that regional, multi-centered framework. Rather than presenting Yangshao, Longshan, Hemudu, Liangzhu, and Hongshan as sequential stages of a single developmental ladder, this chapter presents them as distinct, geographically situated regional cultures, each shaped by its own local environment, agricultural system, social history, and pattern of contact with neighboring societies, several of which developed pronounced social hierarchy through different specific pathways and at somewhat different times.

At the same time, students should not mistake this regional framework for a claim of total isolation. Archaeological evidence increasingly documents long-distance movement of goods, materials, and, in all likelihood, people, ideas, and technologies across considerable distances during this period — jade, for instance, moved across hundreds of kilometers between production centers and distant sites where finished or partially worked jade objects have been recovered, and certain ceramic forms, decorative motifs, and ritual practices appear, with local variation, across multiple regions, suggesting some degree of shared elite culture or at least mutual awareness among increasingly complex societies. Archaeologists sometimes describe this pattern of interaction among independent, competing, but connected societies using the concept of "peer polity interaction" — a useful reminder that increasing complexity in one region could be influenced by, and could in turn influence, developments in neighboring regions, without any one region simply dominating or originating the process for all the others.

This chapter also asks students to keep two related but distinct questions in view throughout: first, what changed materially and socially within particular regional cultures over time — larger settlements, more elaborate craft production, more unequal burials — and second, why these changes occurred, a question this chapter addresses directly in the Cause and Effect and Historical Debate features later in this chapter. Holding these two questions apart helps students avoid the common error of assuming that documenting change is the same as explaining it.

Section 5.2

Yangshao Communities

The Yangshao culture, first identified through excavations near the village of Yangshao in modern Henan province in the 1920s, refers to a broad and long-lived cultural tradition spread across much of the middle Yellow River region, generally dated from roughly seven thousand to five thousand years ago. Yangshao communities practiced established millet agriculture, supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering, and lived in villages of semi-subterranean houses, as introduced in Chapter 4, often arranged around a central open area that may have served communal, ritual, or administrative functions, though its precise use remains a subject of interpretation rather than certainty.

Yangshao communities are especially well known for their painted pottery, decorated with geometric patterns, stylized animal and human forms, and other motifs rendered in red, black, and, less commonly, white pigment. The site of Banpo, near modern Xi'an, provides one of the most thoroughly excavated Yangshao village plans, showing a residential area of houses surrounding a central plaza, a separate pottery-production area, and a cemetery area, suggesting a settlement with some degree of internal spatial organization, though not, on current evidence, dramatic social hierarchy of the kind documented at later Longshan and Liangzhu sites. Burial goods at most Yangshao cemeteries show relatively modest and fairly consistent variation between graves, which most archaeologists interpret as evidence for a society without pronounced hereditary status distinctions, though some Yangshao-era sites and later Yangshao-related phases show the beginnings of more marked differentiation, complicating any simple characterization of "Yangshao equality" as uniform across the entire tradition and its long duration.

Yangshao material culture also included ground stone agricultural tools, storage pits for grain, and evidence of craft production, including textile production suggested by spindle whorls and, at some sites, possible evidence of silk production, an early stage in a textile tradition that would become economically and culturally significant in later Chinese history, discussed further in Chapters 15 and 17. Yangshao communities buried their dead in cemeteries generally located near, but distinct from, residential areas, with burial practices that varied somewhat by region and sub-phase but that generally indicate ancestor-related or communal ritual practices whose specific content cannot be reconstructed with confidence from material evidence alone.

Toward the later part of the Yangshao sequence, and into the transitional centuries leading toward the Longshan culture examined in Section 5.3, archaeologists observe growing settlement size at some sites, increasing evidence of regional exchange, and, at a small number of sites, early evidence of larger, more centrally organized communities that some researchers interpret as showing the beginnings of settlement hierarchy — that is, a pattern in which some settlements grew notably larger than surrounding villages and may have exercised some degree of regional influence. This evidence remains less dramatic and less securely established than the clearer Longshan-era and Liangzhu-era evidence examined later in this chapter, and should be treated as a gradual, still-developing trend rather than a fully formed hierarchy.

Section 5.3

Longshan Societies

The Longshan culture, named for the site of Chengziyai in Shandong where distinctive black pottery was first identified in the late 1920s, refers to a set of related late Neolithic archaeological cultures spread across the middle and lower Yellow River region, generally dated from roughly five thousand to four thousand years ago. "Longshan" is best understood not as a single unified society but as a broad horizon of related regional cultures sharing certain technological and social features, including distinctive thin-walled black pottery produced through a reduction-firing technique that limited oxygen in the kiln, walled settlements constructed using rammed-earth (hangtu) techniques, and increasing evidence of social stratification and interregional competition or conflict.

Longshan-era settlements show a marked increase, compared to the Yangshao period, in the construction of substantial earthen walls surrounding settlements, some enclosing areas of tens of hectares and requiring the coordinated labor of many people over an extended period. The purpose of these walls likely included defense, given the roughly contemporaneous archaeological evidence for increasing weapon production, occasional evidence of violent trauma on skeletal remains, and settlement destruction at some sites, though walls could also have served to mark settlement boundaries, control access, or display the organizational capacity of a settlement's leadership, and in practice these functions likely overlapped rather than existing as mutually exclusive explanations.

Burial evidence from Longshan-era sites shows considerably more pronounced variation than most Yangshao-era cemeteries: some graves contain elaborate assemblages of fine pottery, jade ornaments, and other valuable goods, while the majority of graves at the same sites contain few or no such items, a pattern that most archaeologists interpret as evidence for the emergence of hereditary or at least markedly unequal social status. The distinctive Longshan black pottery itself required skilled, specialized production — fast-wheel throwing, careful control of firing atmosphere, and, in the finest examples, walls thinned to a fraction of a millimeter — suggesting the emergence of specialized craft producers working, in at least some cases, for elite patrons rather than solely for general household use.

Some Longshan-era sites also show early evidence of what may be interpreted as proto-writing or symbolic notation, including incised marks on pottery at several sites, though scholars remain divided on whether these marks constitute a genuine writing system, a system of clan or ownership marks, or simply decorative or accidental incisions; this evidence should be treated cautiously and distinguished clearly from the securely attested writing system examined in connection with Shang oracle bones in Chapter 7. Taken together, the walled settlements, unequal burials, specialized craft production, and evidence of interregional conflict associated with the Longshan horizon represent, on current archaeological evidence, one of the clearest indications within the Yellow River region of a late Neolithic society approaching, without yet fully reaching, the political and social complexity of the early states examined in Chapter 6.

Section 5.4

Hemudu and the Lower Yangtze

As introduced briefly in Chapter 4, the Hemudu culture of the lower Yangtze region, generally dated from roughly seven thousand to five thousand three hundred years ago, provides some of the richest evidence anywhere in Neolithic China for the material culture of an early rice-farming society. Waterlogged conditions at Hemudu and related sites have preserved an unusually large quantity of organic material, including wooden agricultural implements, wooden architectural components, and even textile fragments, that rarely survive at drier sites, giving archaeologists an unusually detailed picture of daily technology and craft in this particular regional tradition.

Hemudu communities built houses raised on wooden piles above the wet, low-lying terrain of the delta, a technique well suited to the region's flooding and dampness and requiring considerable woodworking skill, evidenced by mortise-and-tenon joinery found at the site. Alongside preserved rice remains documenting intensifying, though not yet fully dominant, rice cultivation, discussed in Chapter 4, Hemudu has yielded lacquered wooden objects — among the earliest known lacquerware in the world — carved bone and ivory artifacts, including finely worked bird-shaped objects that may have held ritual significance, and pottery decorated with incised and, at some vessels, painted designs distinct from the painted pottery traditions of the contemporaneous Yangshao culture in the north.

Detailed faunal and archaeobotanical analysis at the related site of Tianluoshan, as noted in Chapter 4, indicates that Hemudu-culture communities, despite their clear investment in rice cultivation, continued to rely heavily on hunting, fishing, and the gathering of wild plant foods such as acorns well into the period of active rice management, and burial evidence from Hemudu-related sites shows comparatively modest variation between graves, suggesting a society without pronounced hereditary hierarchy comparable to that documented later at Liangzhu. Hemudu should therefore be understood as a materially sophisticated but, on current evidence, still relatively egalitarian society — a useful point of contrast with the dramatically more stratified Liangzhu culture examined in the next section, which developed in a broadly similar region only a few centuries to roughly a millennium and a half later, depending on the specific dating estimates used and the local sub-region in question.

Lower Yangtze development in this period was not a single linear sequence. Hemudu and the roughly contemporaneous Majiabang culture developed as related but distinct regional traditions on either side of Hangzhou Bay: Hemudu communities were centered on the Ningshao Plain to the south of the bay, while Majiabang communities developed on the Taihu Plain to the north. On the Taihu Plain, Majiabang was followed by the Songze culture and then by Liangzhu, examined in Section 5.5, while Hemudu-related communities on the Ningshao Plain followed a partly distinct local trajectory before Liangzhu's influence and, eventually, its political reach expanded southward. This regional patchwork illustrates a broader theme of this chapter: social complexity did not emerge instantly, automatically, or along a single line of descent once rice agriculture became well established, but developed gradually and unevenly, through multiple overlapping and locally distinct regional cultures, each shaped by its own local circumstances rather than simply replacing its neighbors in a fixed sequence.

Section 5.5

Liangzhu and Regional Complexity

The Liangzhu culture, centered in the Taihu Lake region of the lower Yangtze delta and generally dated from roughly five thousand three hundred to four thousand three hundred years ago, provides the single clearest body of archaeological evidence in Neolithic China for a society organized around pronounced, apparently hereditary social hierarchy, large-scale coordinated labor, and sophisticated elite craft production, all developed without any securely attested system of writing.

At the core of Liangzhu society lay a large walled precinct, generally identified with the site now known as Liangzhu City, enclosing an area of roughly three square kilometers and surrounded by a still larger system of outer earthworks. Beyond the settlement itself, archaeologists have documented an extensive and remarkably sophisticated hydraulic system, including dams, dikes, and water-storage reservoirs constructed across a wide area to the north and west of the city, apparently designed to manage seasonal flooding and to support both agriculture and transportation. Estimates of the labor required for the initial construction of this system, based on the volume of earth moved and the scale of the associated infrastructure, suggest that thousands of workers were mobilized over a period of years, and the system itself appears to have remained in use and under maintenance for several centuries afterward — a scale of coordinated labor mobilization and sustained organizational capacity well beyond what a single village or a loose confederation of equal villages could typically achieve without some form of durable, centralized authority. Scholars debate the direction of causation between this organizational capacity and the hydraulic works themselves: some emphasize that the practical demands of large-scale water management likely helped drive political centralization, while others, drawing on recent quantitative analyses of the Liangzhu evidence, argue that traditions of concentrated political, military, and ritual power may have developed first and made the hydraulic system possible, rather than the reverse. A cautious synthesis is that the hydraulic works and Liangzhu's political centralization may have reinforced one another over time, rather than assuming that either simply caused the other.

Liangzhu burial evidence shows dramatic and consistent differentiation between elite and non-elite graves. A small number of elite burials, generally interpreted by archaeologists as belonging to a ruling or priestly stratum, contain dozens or even hundreds of finely worked jade objects, including the distinctive cong (a tube-shaped object, square in outer cross-section with a circular bore, often carved with stylized zoomorphic or human-like face motifs) and bi (a flat, circular disk with a central hole), alongside fine black pottery and other valuable goods, while the great majority of contemporary burials at Liangzhu-culture sites contain few or no comparable items. The consistency of this pattern across multiple sites, combined with the specialized skill evidently required to produce the finest Liangzhu jades — worked in the absence of metal tools through sawing, drilling, and abrasive grinding and polishing techniques that, while archaeologists debate the precise tools involved, clearly required enormous investments of time and specialized expertise — has led most archaeologists to interpret Liangzhu society as organized around a hereditary or at least strongly institutionalized elite, closely associated with ritual and, quite possibly, religious or cosmological authority, whose precise beliefs cannot be reconstructed from the material evidence alone but whose social and economic power is clearly documented.

Liangzhu's political classification remains a subject of active scholarly debate. UNESCO's World Heritage designation and a number of recent archaeological studies describe Liangzhu as an early regional state, pointing to its centralized labor mobilization, urban-scale planning, monumental architecture, and pronounced, apparently hereditary hierarchy as evidence of institutionalized political authority. Other researchers, working within different analytical traditions, prefer to describe Liangzhu as a complex chiefdom or a "state-like" society, reserving the term "state" for societies with more securely documented administrative institutions. This textbook treats the question as a live scholarly debate rather than a settled matter: the archaeological evidence securely demonstrates centralized labor mobilization, pronounced social stratification, urban-scale organization, and ritual authority at Liangzhu, but the absence of any securely attested writing system limits historians' ability to reconstruct its specific governing institutions, and different scholars, using different definitions of "state," reach different classifications on the basis of largely the same evidence.

The decline of Liangzhu society, beginning around forty-three hundred years ago, remains a subject of active research and some debate. Sediment and geological evidence from the region has increasingly pointed toward major flooding events, possibly associated with a period of unusually intense monsoon activity, as a significant contributing factor, overwhelming or destroying the hydraulic infrastructure on which Liangzhu's agricultural and settlement system depended. Other researchers emphasize that environmental stress of this kind likely interacted with social, political, and economic strains within a society that had, by this point, become heavily dependent on centralized coordination and specialized elite production; a system vulnerable to environmental disruption may also have been vulnerable to political fragmentation once that disruption began. Students should treat the Liangzhu decline as a case study in the complex, multi-causal nature of societal transformation and collapse, a theme this textbook will revisit in later chapters concerning the decline of dynasties and states, while being careful not to treat environmental explanation and political or social explanation as mutually exclusive alternatives.

Section 5.6

Hongshan and Northern Cultural Traditions

Outside the two great river basins that dominate much of this chapter, the Hongshan culture of the northeast — spanning parts of modern Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, and Hebei and traditionally dated to roughly six thousand five hundred to five thousand years ago — provides important evidence that significant Neolithic ritual and social complexity was not confined to the Yellow River and Yangtze heartlands emphasized elsewhere in this chapter and in standard, older narratives of Chinese civilizational origins.

Hongshan communities practiced a mixed subsistence economy combining millet cultivation with hunting and gathering, in an environment cooler and less reliably productive for intensive agriculture than the core Yellow River and Yangtze regions. Hongshan society is best known archaeologically for a remarkable tradition of jade carving, including distinctive forms such as coiled, dragon-like or embryo-like pendants (sometimes called "pig-dragons" in earlier scholarship, though this identification remains debated) and other zoomorphic and abstract jade forms whose specific ritual or cosmological meaning cannot be reconstructed with confidence but whose careful, skilled production indicates specialized craft labor and, quite likely, ritual or elite significance comparable in some respects, though not identical in form, to the elite jade production documented at Liangzhu.

The most striking Hongshan archaeological discoveries come from ritual complexes such as the site known as Niuheliang, dated more specifically within the broader Hongshan tradition to roughly five thousand five hundred to five thousand years ago, where archaeologists have excavated a series of platform-like earthen structures, interpreted by many researchers as altars, and an underground structure, sometimes described as a "goddess temple," containing fragments of clay figures, including a partially preserved life-sized female head with inlaid jade eyes, alongside evidence of large stone cairns covering elite burials furnished with jade objects but comparatively little pottery. This combination of monumental ritual architecture, elite burial, and specialized jade production, located away from ordinary residential settlement, has led many archaeologists to interpret Niuheliang as a specialized ceremonial center serving a wider regional population rather than an ordinary village, suggesting a society capable of organizing significant ritual and construction activity independent of, though presumably connected to, everyday agricultural settlement.

The Hongshan case illustrates an important methodological lesson for this chapter: significant social and ritual complexity in Neolithic China took multiple forms and emerged through multiple regional pathways, not always accompanied by the large-scale walled settlements or hydraulic engineering documented at Longshan and Liangzhu sites. Students should resist treating Hongshan as either a precursor to, or a lesser version of, developments in the Yellow River or Yangtze regions; it represents a distinct regional tradition whose relationship to those better-known traditions — whether through direct contact, parallel independent development, or some combination of both — remains an active subject of archaeological investigation.

Section 5.7

Craft Specialization and Exchange

A recurring theme across the regional cultures examined in this chapter is the increasing evidence for craft specialization — the production of particular goods, especially fine pottery and worked jade, by individuals or workshops with specialized skill and, quite possibly, dedicated time not spent directly on food production, rather than by ordinary households producing goods solely for their own use. Archaeologists infer specialization from several kinds of evidence: the technical skill and time investment required to produce certain objects, such as thin-walled Longshan black pottery or finely carved Liangzhu jade cong; evidence of standardized production techniques or forms suggesting workshop-based rather than purely individual household production; and the concentration of production debris, unfinished pieces, and tools associated with a particular craft at certain locations within a settlement, suggesting dedicated production areas.

Jade deserves particular attention as a material whose working required not only specialized skill but also access to a scarce and, in most regions of China, non-local raw material. Nephrite jade sources used by Neolithic Chinese craftspeople have been identified in several regions, and archaeological evidence indicates that finished or partially finished jade objects moved across considerable distances from their sources, suggesting organized systems of exchange capable of moving valuable, non-perishable prestige goods across regional and, in some cases, interregional networks. The concentration of the finest and most abundant jade objects in a small number of elite burials at sites such as Liangzhu and Niuheliang, discussed in Sections 5.5 and 5.6, indicates that access to jade, and to the specialized craftspeople capable of working it, was itself a marker of, and quite possibly a mechanism for reinforcing, social status.

Exchange networks documented in the late Neolithic period were not limited to jade. Archaeologists have identified evidence for the movement of distinctive pottery styles, decorative motifs, and, in some cases, likely finished ceramic vessels themselves across considerable distances, suggesting overlapping regional interaction spheres in which neighboring, and in some cases quite distant, communities were aware of, and to some degree influenced by, one another's material culture. This evidence supports the "peer polity interaction" framework introduced in Section 5.1: increasingly complex late Neolithic societies developed in dialogue and competition with one another, exchanging both goods and, quite plausibly, ideas about status, ritual, and authority, rather than developing in total isolation from one another.

Craft specialization and exchange also carry significant implications for understanding the social and economic organization of late Neolithic communities more broadly. The existence of specialized producers implies some mechanism, whether direct elite patronage, communal organization, or market-like exchange of goods for food and other necessities, by which specialized craftspeople obtained the food and other resources they needed despite not producing all of it themselves. Historians and archaeologists cannot determine with confidence which of these mechanisms, or what combination of them, operated in any specific late Neolithic community, but the mere existence of specialized production represents an important step away from the largely self-sufficient household economy of earlier Neolithic villages examined in Chapter 4 and toward the more differentiated, more economically interdependent societies that would characterize the early states examined in Chapter 6 and the Bronze Age civilizations examined in Chapters 7 and 8.

Section 5.8

Social Hierarchy and Ritual Authority

The evidence surveyed throughout this chapter — unequal burials, walled settlements, monumental hydraulic engineering, specialized elite craft production, and ritual complexes distinct from ordinary settlement — converges on a central conclusion: by the later Neolithic period, several regional societies in China had developed forms of social hierarchy considerably more pronounced than those documented in the earlier Yangshao and Hemudu communities examined in Sections 5.2 and 5.4. This section examines what archaeologists can, and cannot, confidently say about the nature of this hierarchy.

Burial evidence provides the single most widely used form of evidence for social hierarchy in a period without writing. Archaeologists generally interpret dramatic and consistent differences in the quantity, quality, and rarity of grave goods across contemporary burials at the same site as evidence for differences in the social status of the deceased, on the reasoning that mortuary practice, while shaped by many factors including religious belief, kinship obligation, and individual circumstance, generally reflects, at least in broad terms, differences in the social position, wealth, or authority a person held, or that their family or community wished to display, during life or through the burial itself. The consistency of unequal burial patterns across multiple sites within the Liangzhu and Longshan cultural horizons, discussed in Sections 5.3 and 5.5, and the concentration of extraordinarily labor-intensive and materially valuable goods, particularly jade, in a small number of graves, supports the inference that these societies had developed some form of institutionalized, and quite possibly hereditary, social ranking, though the precise mechanisms of that ranking — whether based primarily on kinship, ritual role, control of labor or resources, military authority, or some combination of these — cannot be determined with confidence from burial evidence alone.

Ritual and religious authority appear, on current evidence, to have been closely bound up with social hierarchy in several late Neolithic contexts. The association of the most elaborate elite burials with distinctive ritual objects, particularly jade forms such as the cong and bi at Liangzhu, and with dedicated ritual architecture separate from ordinary settlement, as at Hongshan's Niuheliang complex, suggests that whatever authority late Neolithic elites held was expressed, and quite possibly legitimated, at least in part through ritual, cosmological, or religious means rather than through purely secular or military power alone, though the two forms of authority were very likely intertwined rather than sharply separated in the way that a modern distinction between "religious" and "political" authority might suggest.

Archaeologists commonly use the term "chiefdom" to describe societies with hereditary or semi-hereditary leadership, some capacity to mobilize labor and resources beyond the individual household, a degree of settlement hierarchy in which some settlements are notably larger or more elaborate than others, and evidence of ranked social status, but without the more extensive administrative institutions and territorial reach typically associated with a state. Archaeologists distinguish a state from a chiefdom primarily by looking for institutionalized political authority that persists independently of any single leader, more formal territorial organization and administrative hierarchy, greater capacity for sustained resource mobilization and labor control across a wider territory, and more clearly bureaucratic or specialized administrative roles. Students should note that systematic writing is not, by itself, a required criterion for statehood in current archaeological practice; writing, where it survives, can provide unusually strong and direct evidence of administration, but many archaeologists recognize early states that developed and functioned without any securely attested writing system, and definitions of "state" vary considerably among scholars. This is one reason why Liangzhu's political classification, discussed above, remains genuinely debated rather than settled by the absence of texts alone.

Students should use "chiefdom" and "state" as useful analytical categories among several, rather than as rigid or universally applicable labels separated by a bright line. Not every late Neolithic society examined in this chapter fits either category equally well, and some archaeologists prefer alternative frameworks that emphasize the specific, locally variable features of each society rather than fitting it into a general cross-cultural typology.

Section 5.9

From Regional Cultures to Early States

This chapter closes by looking forward to Chapter 6's examination of Erlitou and the traditional Xia narrative, explaining how the regional developments traced across Sections 5.2 through 5.8 relate to, without directly or automatically producing, the early state societies examined in the next chapter. Students should resist two tempting but misleading conclusions. The first misleading conclusion would be to assume that all late Neolithic societies examined in this chapter fit the same political category. As Section 5.5 discussed, Liangzhu is classified by UNESCO and a number of archaeologists as an early regional state, while other scholars prefer terms such as complex chiefdom or state-like society; Longshan-horizon and Hongshan societies likewise varied substantially in their scale of political organization and should be assessed separately rather than assumed to have reached, or fallen short of, the same threshold as Liangzhu. The second misleading conclusion would treat the regional cultures examined in this chapter as a single unbroken line of development leading directly and inevitably to the specific early states of the following chapters, when in fact several of the societies examined in this chapter, including Liangzhu, appear to have declined, fragmented, or transformed substantially rather than developing in an unbroken line into later Bronze Age states.

What can be said with more confidence is that the developments traced in this chapter — increasing craft specialization, expanding exchange networks, pronounced and apparently hereditary social hierarchy in some regions, ritual authority closely bound up with social status, large-scale coordinated labor mobilization, and, in the case of Longshan-era sites, walled settlements and evidence of interregional conflict — collectively establish social, organizational, and technological precedents on which the early states examined in Chapter 6 would draw, whether through direct regional continuity, the diffusion of specific technologies and practices, or simply the establishment of a broader social and political environment in which further concentration of power and authority became increasingly possible. The transition examined in Chapter 6, centered on the site of Erlitou in the middle Yellow River region and on the debated relationship between archaeological evidence and the traditional textual accounts of a Xia dynasty, should be understood as building on, rather than simply replacing, the Longshan-era social patterns examined in Section 5.3, even as the specific relationship between particular late Longshan-era communities and the subsequent Erlitou-period state remains an active subject of archaeological research.

Students moving into Chapter 6 should carry forward a clear sense of the diversity, complexity, and unevenness of the developments examined in this chapter: multiple regional societies, each following its own trajectory, some reaching considerable heights of social hierarchy and organizational capacity before declining or transforming, others developing more gradually, and none developing in isolation from the others. This diversity, rather than a single, simple story of steady progress toward the state, is the essential foundation for understanding how, where, and why the early states examined in the next chapter emerged when and where they did.

Section 5.10

Chapter Review and Early Cultures Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the major regional cultures of late Neolithic China, tracing how communities in the middle Yellow River, the lower Yangtze delta, and the northeast developed increasingly complex, increasingly differentiated, and, in some cases, increasingly hierarchical forms of social organization between roughly seven thousand and four thousand years ago, building on the agricultural foundations established in Chapter 4.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. Neolithic China should be understood as a landscape of multiple, regionally distinct cultures — Yangshao and Longshan in the Yellow River region, Hemudu and Liangzhu in the Yangtze delta, and Hongshan in the northeast, among others — rather than a single unfolding "Chinese Neolithic" radiating outward from one center. These regional cultures developed largely independently but remained connected through expanding networks of exchange, particularly in jade and other prestige goods, that moved materials, objects, and quite possibly ideas and practices across considerable distances. Social hierarchy, evidenced through increasingly unequal burial practices, specialized craft production, walled settlements, and, in the case of Liangzhu, large-scale coordinated hydraulic engineering, became markedly more pronounced in several regions during the later Neolithic period, though to different degrees and through different specific pathways in different regions, with Liangzhu providing the single clearest archaeological case for pronounced, apparently hereditary social ranking. Ritual and religious authority appear to have been closely, though not simply, bound up with the emergence of social hierarchy in multiple regional contexts. And these developments, while establishing important social, organizational, and technological precedents, did not automatically or directly produce the early states examined in Chapter 6; several late Neolithic societies, including Liangzhu, declined or transformed substantially, illustrating that increasing complexity was neither permanent nor a guaranteed, one-way process.

The Liangzhu Excavation Record

Creator: Chinese archaeological teams excavating the Liangzhu site complex in Zhejiang province from the 1930s onward, with major systematic work particularly since the 1980s · Date represented: Approximately 5,300–4,300 years ago · Type: Archaeological excavation reports and scientific publications documenting a major late Neolithic walled city and hydraulic system

Context: Sustained excavation at Liangzhu and surrounding sites has documented a walled precinct, an extensive outer hydraulic system of dams and reservoirs, elite cemeteries, and workshop areas, together allowing archaeologists to reconstruct a detailed picture of a highly organized regional society without any securely attested system of writing.

Paraphrase of the excavation findings (not a translated quotation):

The excavation reports describe a walled precinct of roughly three square kilometers, an associated outer hydraulic system of dams and water-storage reservoirs extending across a wide surrounding area, and a small number of exceptionally furnished burials containing large numbers of finely carved jade cong, bi, and other objects, in marked contrast to the great majority of contemporary graves in the region, which contain comparatively few goods.

Analysis: The Liangzhu evidence is significant because it documents, through material remains alone, a scale of labor organization, craft specialization, and social inequality that scholars variously classify as an early regional state, a complex chiefdom, or a state-like society, despite the absence of any writing system that could confirm titles, political structures, or specific historical events. This makes Liangzhu an important test case for how far historians can reconstruct social and political organization purely from material evidence, and for how much classification itself depends on the specific definition of "state" a given scholar adopts.

Historical Debate

How and Why Did Inequality Emerge?

The question: Why did some late Neolithic societies in China, such as Liangzhu and the Longshan-culture communities, develop pronounced and apparently hereditary social hierarchy, while other broadly contemporary societies, and many earlier Neolithic communities, remained comparatively egalitarian?

Interpretation A — Environmental and organizational demands as the primary driver: Some researchers emphasize that specific environmental challenges, particularly the large-scale water management required by intensive rice agriculture and flood control in the Yangtze delta, created organizational demands that could most effectively be met through centralized coordination, and that the individuals or groups who organized this labor were able to convert their organizational role into lasting social and ritual authority.
Interpretation B — Competitive accumulation and prestige-good exchange as the primary driver: Other researchers emphasize social and political competition among ambitious individuals or lineages, who used control over scarce prestige goods such as jade, feasting, ritual knowledge, and exchange relationships with distant communities to accumulate followers, status, and influence, gradually converting temporary personal prestige into more lasting, and eventually hereditary, social rank.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping burial, settlement, and craft-production evidence; they differ in how much causal weight to assign to practical organizational necessity versus social competition and prestige accumulation, and most current specialists favor explanations that combine elements of both, recognizing that organizational demands and social competition likely reinforced one another rather than operating as fully separate processes.

Why it matters: How historians explain the origins of social inequality shapes broader understanding of whether hierarchy is best understood as a practical response to shared problems, a product of competitive individual or lineage ambition, or some combination of both — a question with implications well beyond Neolithic China.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, particularly the contrast between Liangzhu and Hemudu or between Longshan and Yangshao, evaluate which interpretation better explains the emergence of pronounced hierarchy, or explain why a combined explanation may be more accurate than either interpretation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Stratified Village

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many sites, not quotations from specific named individuals.

From the perspective of an elite lineage member at Liangzhu: Access to skilled jade carvers, control over ritual knowledge, and the ability to organize labor for the shared hydraulic system likely reinforced one another, allowing a small number of families to accumulate and, quite possibly, pass on status, resources, and ritual authority across generations.
From the perspective of a specialized jade carver: Years of training were likely required to master the demanding techniques needed to work hard nephrite jade — sawing, drilling, grinding, and polishing with mineral abrasives — though the precise materials and forms of the tools involved remain partly debated, and a carver's livelihood probably depended on some form of support, whether through direct elite patronage or exchange of finished jade for food and other necessities, that freed the carver from full-time agricultural labor.
From the perspective of an ordinary farming household at a Longshan-era walled town: Contributing labor to the construction and maintenance of settlement walls, alongside ordinary agricultural work, may have offered a degree of protection from raiding or conflict with neighboring communities, even as the same household likely had far less access to fine pottery, jade, or other prestige goods than the small number of elaborately furnished burials documented at some sites.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist studying burial inequality: Careful, systematic comparison of grave goods across many burials at the same site, rather than focusing only on the most spectacular finds, is essential for distinguishing a genuine pattern of social hierarchy from the natural variation that exists in any human community, and each new excavation has the potential to refine or complicate the current understanding of how, when, and why hierarchy emerged.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Ritual and Political Authority From Yangshao to Longshan and Liangzhu

What Changed

  • Burial goods shifted from relatively modest, fairly consistent assemblages in earlier Yangshao and Hemudu communities toward dramatically unequal assemblages at Longshan and, especially, Liangzhu sites
  • Settlements grew larger, and some, particularly in the Longshan-era Yellow River region, were enclosed by substantial rammed-earth walls
  • Craft production became increasingly specialized, particularly in fine pottery and worked jade, requiring skill and time investment well beyond ordinary household production
  • Evidence for large-scale coordinated labor becomes especially visible and reaches an exceptional scale at Liangzhu, building on organizational capacities already evident in earlier ceremonial construction such as the Hongshan complex at Niuheliang

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture, established in Chapter 4, remained the basic economic foundation of all the societies examined in this chapter
  • Household organization remained a fundamental unit of community life throughout this period; kinship and lineage structures probably shaped many communities as well, but they are considerably harder to reconstruct uniformly from material evidence alone
  • Ritual practice, whatever its specific content, continued to play an important role in community life, as it had in earlier Neolithic communities
  • No society examined in this chapter had yet developed a securely attested, systematic form of writing

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real transformations and the underlying continuities of the late Neolithic period helps students understand the emergence of social hierarchy as a gradual reorganization and intensification of existing social patterns rather than a sudden, unprecedented break with the past.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Social Complexity

Economic causes

Continued agricultural intensification, discussed in Chapter 4, produced growing and, in some regions, increasingly concentrated surplus, while craft specialization in jade and fine pottery created new forms of valuable, storable, and exchangeable wealth distinct from ordinary agricultural goods.

Environmental and organizational causes

In the Yangtze delta especially, water management was especially important for flood control and agriculture, and coordinated labor beyond the household or single village was clearly required to build and maintain systems such as Liangzhu's. Scholars debate whether hydraulic works fostered political centralization, resulted from already-centralized authority, or developed through a reciprocal interaction of both.

Social and ideological causes

Competitive feasting, exchange of prestige goods, and the development of shared or competing ritual practices and cosmological beliefs, whose specific content cannot be reconstructed with confidence, likely provided mechanisms through which individuals or lineages could accumulate, display, and eventually transmit social status.

Effects

The cumulative result, in several but not all regions, was the emergence of walled settlements, pronounced and apparently hereditary social hierarchy, specialized elite craft production, and, at Liangzhu, large-scale coordinated infrastructure — developments that established important precedents for, without directly guaranteeing, the early states examined in Chapter 6.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains the emergence of social hierarchy in late Neolithic China, and the process unfolded differently, and to different degrees, in different regions. Most current researchers favor multi-causal explanations that combine economic, environmental, organizational, social, and ideological factors, rather than searching for one decisive trigger.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Late Neolithic Community

Archaeological evidence from Longshan- and Liangzhu-era sites allows historians to offer a cautious reconstruction of daily life within a late Neolithic community shaped by increasing social differentiation. Most households likely continued to live much as earlier Neolithic farming families had, working agricultural fields, tending stored grain, and producing much of their own pottery and tools, but now within a community that included, for the first time, a small number of households or lineages distinguished by access to fine craft goods, ritual objects, and, quite possibly, specific ritual or organizational roles within the community.

Specialized craftspeople, particularly jade carvers and skilled potters, likely occupied a distinct social position, supported through some combination of elite patronage and exchange, their labor woven into the community's ritual and status economy rather than devoted primarily to ordinary subsistence production. At walled Longshan-era towns, some members of the community likely participated in the construction and maintenance of settlement walls and other communal infrastructure, labor that, while demanding, may also have provided a degree of protection and communal identity distinct from that of unwalled villages.

Community ritual life, whatever its specific beliefs and practices, appears to have become increasingly elaborate and increasingly tied to social status during this period, evidenced by the growing investment of labor, material, and skill in funerary practice and, at sites such as Niuheliang, dedicated ceremonial architecture. Students should recognize that most people in these societies, despite the dramatic evidence documented at elite burials and monumental sites, likely continued to live agricultural lives not radically different in daily texture from those described in Chapter 4, even as the overall social and political structure of their communities was changing in significant ways around them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined a crucial transitional period in the history of the lands later associated with China: the several centuries during which multiple regional Neolithic societies, building on the agricultural foundations traced in Chapter 4, developed increasingly complex, increasingly differentiated, and, in several important cases, increasingly hierarchical forms of social organization. Rather than presenting this development as a single, unified process, this chapter has emphasized the regional diversity of late Neolithic China, tracing distinct trajectories in the middle Yellow River region (Yangshao giving way to the regional Longshan-horizon cultures), the lower Yangtze delta (where the broadly contemporaneous Hemudu and Majiabang traditions gave way, on the Taihu Plain, to Songze and then Liangzhu, while Hemudu-related communities on the Ningshao Plain followed a partly distinct path), and the northeast (the Hongshan ritual and jade tradition centered on sites such as Niuheliang).

Students have learned that these regional cultures, while developing largely independently, remained connected through expanding networks of exchange, particularly in jade and other prestige goods, that moved materials and, quite plausibly, ideas and practices across considerable distances, a pattern archaeologists describe using concepts such as "peer polity interaction." The chapter emphasized that evidence for social hierarchy — unequal burial goods, walled settlements, specialized craft production, and large-scale coordinated labor — became markedly more pronounced during the later Neolithic period in several regions, most dramatically at Liangzhu, where an extensive hydraulic system and consistently unequal elite burials furnished with elaborate jade objects provide the clearest Neolithic Chinese evidence for pronounced, apparently hereditary social ranking.

The chapter examined the concept of the "chiefdom" as one useful, though not universally applicable, analytical category for understanding societies of this kind, occupying an intermediate position between the relatively egalitarian villages of earlier Neolithic periods and the fully developed early states examined in Chapter 6. It explored competing scholarly explanations for the emergence of social hierarchy, weighing environmental and organizational demands, particularly the practical requirements of large-scale water management, against social and ideological explanations centered on competitive prestige-good exchange, feasting, and ritual authority, while noting that most current researchers favor multi-causal explanations combining elements of both.

Students also examined the striking case of Liangzhu's decline, generally associated with major flooding but likely involving an interaction of environmental and social or political factors, as an early illustration of a theme that will recur throughout this textbook: complex societies, once established, are not permanent, and their transformation or collapse typically involves multiple interacting causes rather than any single explanation.

Finally, this chapter looked forward to Chapter 6, cautioning students against two misleading conclusions: first, that all late Neolithic societies examined in this chapter fit the same political category, when in fact scholars classify Liangzhu variously as an early regional state, a complex chiefdom, or a state-like society, while Longshan-horizon and Hongshan societies show their own distinct and generally less centralized patterns of organization that should be assessed on their own terms; and second, that the developments examined in this chapter led in a single, unbroken line directly to the specific early states of the following chapters. Instead, the chapter has argued that the regional developments of the late Neolithic period — craft specialization, exchange networks, social hierarchy, ritual authority, and large-scale labor organization — established important social, organizational, and technological precedents from which the early states examined in Chapter 6 would draw, in a complex, regionally variable, and not fully linear process of historical development.

Armed with this understanding of regional Neolithic social complexity, students are now prepared to move into Chapter 6's examination of Erlitou and the traditional Xia narrative, where they will confront directly the central methodological problem introduced throughout this chapter and Chapter 4: how historians distinguish archaeological evidence from later textual tradition when reconstructing a period at the very edge of, and eventually crossing into, the earliest stages of a more fully documented Chinese historical record.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a crucial and often underappreciated stage in the deep history of the lands later associated with China: the period during which multiple regional societies, each developing largely on its own terms, began to construct the social hierarchies, specialized economies, and organizational capacities that would eventually make the early states of Chapter 6 possible. Understanding this period matters because it demonstrates, with unusual clarity, that Chinese civilization did not spring from a single source but was built, gradually and unevenly, from the interaction of multiple distinct regional traditions.

The regional cultures introduced in this chapter — Yangshao and Longshan, Hemudu and Liangzhu, Hongshan and its neighbors — did not remain confined to the Neolithic period in their historical significance. The patterns of craft specialization, ritual authority, and social hierarchy documented here anticipate themes that will recur throughout this textbook, from the bronze-working elites of the Shang examined in Chapter 7 through the scholar-official hierarchies of the imperial period and the regional economic and cultural diversity that remains a defining feature of Chinese history into the contemporary period discussed in the book's final chapters.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill: resisting the temptation to read a single, linear story of progress into a body of evidence that in fact shows multiple, regionally distinct pathways, some of which, like Liangzhu, rose to remarkable heights of organizational and artistic achievement before declining or transforming. Appreciating this complexity prepares students to think critically about later historical transformations examined throughout this textbook, none of which should be understood as a simple, inevitable march toward a predetermined outcome.

As students move forward into Chapter 6's examination of Erlitou and the Xia question, they should carry with them a clear understanding of the social and organizational foundations established in this chapter: multiple regional societies, each developing increasing complexity through its own combination of environmental pressure, craft specialization, exchange, ritual authority, and social competition, together creating the broader historical environment from which the earliest state societies examined in the following chapter would emerge.
