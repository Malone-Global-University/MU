Part I — China, Geography, and the Deep Past
1

Studying Chinese History

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

"China" has never been one fixed place, one unbroken government, or one unchanging people. It is a name that has covered many different territories, many different states, and many different ideas of belonging across more than three thousand years of recorded history — and a much longer span of human settlement before that.

When readers open a book titled China: A History of a Civilization, many expect a single continuous story: a line of dynasties running from ancient emperors to the present day, each rising, ruling, and falling in an orderly sequence, all governing more or less the same territory under more or less the same culture. This expectation is understandable. It is also, in important ways, misleading. The history of the lands, peoples, and states associated with "China" is far more varied, far more regionally diverse, and far more historically contingent than the popular image of an ancient, unchanging civilization suggests.

Consider a single fact that unsettles the simple story: the character or word we translate as "China" — most often rendered in English from the Chinese Zhongguo (中国), commonly glossed as "the central states" or "the central lands" — did not always refer to the same territory, the same government, or even the same idea. In the Bronze Age, it could refer to a small cluster of states along the middle Yellow River. Centuries later, it might mean a large multi-ethnic empire stretching from Central Asia to the South China Sea, ruled by a dynasty whose royal family was not ethnically Han Chinese at all. In yet other periods, several rival states each claimed to represent the true center of civilization while dismissing their neighbors as peripheral or barbarian. The geographic and political meaning of "China" has been argued over, redrawn, and reinterpreted for as long as the concept has existed.

This chapter does not begin with an emperor, a battle, or a famous date. It begins with a question that must be asked before any of those things can be understood: what, exactly, are we studying when we study "Chinese history"? Are we studying a place — and if so, which borders, in which century? Are we studying a people — and if so, which language, which ancestry, which identity, understanding that these have all changed over time? Are we studying a government — and if so, which dynasty, which state, which claim to legitimate rule? Or are we studying something larger and looser: a civilization, meaning a long tradition of shared institutions, writing systems, philosophical and religious traditions, and cultural practices that has been carried, contested, and transformed by many different peoples across a vast and varied landscape?

The honest answer is that Chinese history is the study of all of these things at once, and that a serious student must learn to hold them together without collapsing them into a single, simplified narrative. This textbook treats "China" as a historically changing idea and a historically changing space — one that has included the agricultural heartlands of the Yellow and Yangtze river valleys, the grasslands and deserts of Inner Asia, the mountains of Tibet, the oases of the Tarim Basin, the forests of Manchuria, the subtropical south, and the maritime world of the southeast coast, each with its own peoples, languages, environments, and histories, sometimes governed together, sometimes governed apart, and always in dynamic relationship with one another.

To study this history well, students need tools: an understanding of chronology and how historians organize time into periods; an appreciation for archaeology and what physical remains can and cannot tell us about societies that left no writing, or whose writing has not survived; a critical eye for written sources, especially official histories composed by later dynasties with their own political purposes; and an awareness of how national narratives — both traditional Chinese ones and modern nationalist ones, Chinese and non-Chinese alike — have shaped, and sometimes distorted, popular understanding of the past.

This chapter also introduces a distinction that will recur throughout the textbook: the difference between what later tradition claims about the past and what contemporary evidence can actually support. Chinese historical writing possesses one of the longest continuous traditions of historical record-keeping in the world, and this is a remarkable resource for historians. But it is not a flawless one. Traditions recorded centuries after the events they describe — such as the stories of the earliest legendary rulers, or the traditional account of the Xia dynasty discussed later in this textbook — must be treated with particular care, distinguished clearly from what archaeology and contemporary inscriptions can independently confirm.

Finally, this chapter asks students to think about why any of this matters. Chinese history is not simply the history of a distant or foreign place. It is the history of one of the largest and most continuously documented human societies on earth, a civilization that has shaped world trade, technology, philosophy, art, and politics for millennia and that continues to shape global affairs today. Understanding how Chinese history has been studied, told, and sometimes misused equips students to think critically not only about China's past, but about how any nation's history — including their own — is constructed, contested, and put to use in the present.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the study of Chinese history encompasses, beyond a simple sequence of dynasties and emperors.
  2. Trace how the geographic and political meaning of "China" has changed across different historical periods.
  3. Distinguish between civilization, dynasty, state, empire, region, ethnicity, and modern nation-state as historical categories.
  4. Define chronology and periodization, and evaluate different systems for organizing Chinese historical time.
  5. Describe the major geographic regions associated with Chinese history and explain why geography shaped settlement, agriculture, and political power.
  6. Analyze the role of archaeology in reconstructing periods with little or no surviving writing.
  7. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of oracle-bone inscriptions, bronze inscriptions, excavated manuscripts, and official dynastic histories as historical evidence.
  8. Explain why later official histories must be read critically rather than accepted as neutral, contemporary records.
  9. Assess how modern nationalist narratives — in China and elsewhere — have shaped popular understandings of Chinese history.
  10. Apply the concepts of cause, effect, continuity, and change to the study of Chinese historical development.
  11. Identify the difference between traditional dynastic narratives and what contemporary evidence can independently confirm, particularly for early periods such as the Xia.
  12. Contextualize the study of Chinese history within wider Asian and global historical processes.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook — the Paleolithic and Neolithic societies of Chapters 3 through 5, the Bronze Age states of Chapters 6 through 8, the classical philosophical traditions of Chapters 11 and 12, the great empires of Chapters 13 through 50, and the modern crises, revolutions, and contemporary transformations of Chapters 51 through 76 — they need a shared vocabulary and a shared set of habits of mind for approaching the past critically.

The land historians now generally call "East Asia" contains an extraordinary range of environments: the fertile loess soils of the North China Plain, watered by the Yellow River (Huang He); the vast subtropical drainage basin of the Yangtze River (Chang Jiang), the longest river in Asia; the mountainous interior of Sichuan; the semi-arid grasslands and deserts of the Mongolian Plateau and the Ordos region; the high plateau of Tibet; the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin in what is now Xinjiang; the forested river valleys of Manchuria in the northeast; the subtropical hills and river deltas of the south and southeast coast; and the maritime approaches of the South China Sea. No single political center controlled all of these regions for most of recorded history. When a dynasty did succeed in governing many of them together — as the Han, Tang, Yuan, and Qing dynasties each did in different ways — the resulting empire was necessarily a multiethnic, multilingual, and administratively complex polity, not a single uniform nation in the modern sense.

Historians studying this space also need to understand that the idea of a continuous "Chinese" civilization is itself a historical construction, built up over centuries by court historians and, from the late nineteenth century onward, modern nationalist thinkers. This does not make the idea false or without value: real continuities run through the region's history, including a shared tradition of written characters (Hanzi) used and adapted across many spoken languages, recurring institutions of centralized bureaucracy, and enduring philosophical and religious traditions such as Confucian ethics, Daoist thought, and, from the first centuries CE, Buddhism. Section 1.2 examines how the geographic and political meaning of "China" itself changed even as these deeper continuities persisted.

At the same time, historians must resist projecting a single "Chinese" identity backward onto periods and peoples who would not have recognized such a category. The rulers of the Yuan dynasty were Mongols who also ruled much of Eurasia; the rulers of the Qing dynasty were Manchus who governed a multiethnic empire that included Han Chinese, Mongols, Tibetans, Uyghurs, and many other peoples as distinct legal and administrative categories. Many periods of division — the Three Kingdoms, the Northern and Southern Dynasties, the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, the era of Song, Liao, Western Xia, and Jin — saw multiple, often rival, states each claiming political legitimacy, sometimes each claiming to be the true heir of a unified "China," at the same historical moment. Understanding Chinese history seriously means taking these complications as central to the story rather than as inconvenient exceptions to it.

Section 1.1

What Chinese History Studies

Chinese history, as a field of study, is the study of the peoples, societies, states, cultures, economies, institutions, and ideas that have developed across the lands associated with "China" over more than three thousand years of written record, and over a much longer span if we include the Paleolithic and Neolithic periods discussed in Chapters 3 and 4. It is not simply the story of emperors, dynasties, and courts, although these provide an important chronological framework. It is equally the story of farmers cultivating millet on the loess soils of the north and rice in the paddies of the south; of merchants moving goods along overland and maritime trade routes; of officials and scholars debating how society should be governed; of religious communities practicing ancestor veneration, Daoist ritual, or Buddhist devotion; of women managing households, weaving textiles, and, in some periods, participating directly in politics and scholarship; of soldiers and conscripted laborers building walls, canals, and roads; and of countless ordinary people whose names were never recorded but whose labor, choices, and experiences shaped the societies historians study.

For much of the twentieth century, popular and even some academic accounts of Chinese history in the English-speaking world emphasized a small number of familiar landmarks: the Great Wall, the terracotta army of the First Emperor, Confucius, the Silk Road, and the more recent history of Communist revolution. These are all significant topics, and this textbook covers each of them in depth. But an accurate account of Chinese history requires much more than a handful of famous images. It requires attention to regional diversity — the profound differences between, for example, the wheat- and millet-growing north and the rice-growing south, or between the densely settled agricultural heartland and the pastoral societies of the northern and western frontiers. It requires attention to periods of political division, which occupy a substantial portion of the historical record and which shaped culture, religion, and technology in their own right rather than functioning simply as gaps between "real" unified dynasties. And it requires attention to the many peoples — Han Chinese and non-Han alike — whose histories are woven into the larger story.

This textbook also insists on distinguishing carefully between different scales of historical analysis. A civilization is a long-term cultural tradition — in this case, one centered on Chinese writing, philosophical traditions, agricultural and administrative practices, and religious life — that persists across many different political regimes and territorial configurations. A dynasty is a sequence of rulers from a single family or lineage who claimed the right to govern, often over a specific, bounded period of time and territory. A state is a more general term for an organized political community with institutions of government, which may or may not be organized dynastically. An empire refers to a large state that incorporates many different peoples, languages, and regions under a single political authority, often through conquest. These categories overlap but are not identical, and confusing them — for example, treating "China" and "the Qing dynasty" as perfectly interchangeable terms — leads to serious historical errors.

Historical Vocabulary
Historiography

Historiography is the history of historical writing — the study of how historians in different times and places have interpreted the Chinese past. China possesses one of the world's longest continuous traditions of official historical writing, beginning with works such as Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian in the Han dynasty and continuing through a long series of officially compiled dynastic histories, each typically written by the succeeding dynasty about its predecessor.

This tradition is an enormous historical resource, but it is not a neutral one. Official histories were usually compiled under the patronage, and often under the direct supervision, of a reigning dynasty with a strong interest in portraying its own rise as justified and its predecessor's fall as deserved. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century historians — both Chinese and international — have also written Chinese history from a range of perspectives: nationalist historians seeking to establish a continuous, unified Chinese nation; Marxist historians in the People's Republic emphasizing class struggle and modes of production; and, more recently, historians emphasizing regional diversity, frontier interactions, environmental history, and the perspectives of non-Han peoples.

Being aware of historiography does not mean treating all interpretations as equally valid. It means asking, of any account of Chinese history: Who wrote this? For what dynasty, government, or institution? For what purpose? Whose perspective does it center, and whose does it leave out?

Section 1.2

What Has "China" Meant Over Time?

One of the most important lessons of this chapter is that "China" has never referred to one fixed territory, one continuous government, or one unchanging people. The term most commonly translated as "China," Zhongguo (中国), literally means something closer to "central state" or "central lands." In its earliest known uses, during the Western Zhou period, it referred to a relatively small core region along the middle and lower Yellow River, distinguishing the Zhou royal domain and allied states from surrounding peoples considered peripheral. Over the following centuries, as different states rose and fell, conquered new territory, and absorbed new populations, the geographic scope that could plausibly be called "the central states" expanded dramatically — but unevenly, and always contested.

During periods of political division, such as the era of Western Jin collapse and the Northern and Southern Dynasties (Chapters 20–23) or the multi-state world of the Song, Liao, Western Xia, and Jin (Chapters 35–36), multiple rival states simultaneously claimed to represent the legitimate center of civilization, each often describing its rivals as illegitimate, barbarian, or peripheral — regardless of which state actually controlled the traditional Yellow River heartland. During the Yuan and Qing dynasties, the territory governed from Beijing was ruled by dynasties of Mongol and Manchu origin respectively, and included vast regions — Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, Manchuria — that had never been part of earlier, smaller conceptions of "China," raising the question of how students and scholars alike should describe the relationship between these expanded, multiethnic empires and the earlier, more geographically limited idea of the central states.

Modern Chinese nationalism, which took shape over the course of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries amid foreign imperialism, internal reform and revolutionary movements, and new global ideas about citizenship and the nation (Chapters 51–57), reshaped the concept of "China" once again — this time into the modern idea of a unified Chinese nation-state with continuous historical roots stretching back to antiquity and encompassing the full territorial extent of the late Qing empire. This modern national narrative has been enormously influential, both within China and internationally, and this textbook takes it seriously as a historical phenomenon in its own right. But it should not be projected backward uncritically onto earlier centuries. A Han-dynasty official, a Song-dynasty poet, and a Qing-dynasty Manchu bannerman would each have had a different understanding of what "China" meant, whom it included, and what its borders were — and none of these historical understandings maps neatly onto the borders of any modern state.

Historical Vocabulary
Dynasty, State, Empire, and Nation-State

A dynasty names a ruling family and the period of its rule (for example, the Tang dynasty, ruled by the Li family, 618–907 CE). A state is any organized political community with institutions of government; several states may exist at once, as during the Warring States period. An empire is a large state incorporating diverse peoples and regions, typically through conquest, and governed — often unevenly — from a single political center. A nation-state is a distinctly modern political form, generally dated no earlier than the eighteenth or nineteenth century worldwide, in which a government claims to represent a single, culturally and often ethnically defined "nation" occupying a clearly bounded territory.

Chinese history includes all of these forms at different times, and mixing them up — calling the Han dynasty a "nation-state," for instance, or assuming that "the Chinese empire" always meant the same territory as the modern People's Republic of China — produces historically inaccurate conclusions.

Historical Vocabulary
Sinicization

Sinicization is a contested historical term for processes by which non-Han individuals, communities, or ruling groups adopted elements of Han Chinese language, dress, institutions, or customs. It is a useful term, but historians disagree sharply about how to apply it. Some processes described as sinicization were driven by state policy; others emerged through generations of trade, intermarriage, and everyday contact; and in some cases, such as the Northern Wei court reforms discussed in Chapter 22, cultural change moved in more than one direction, with Han elites also adopting elements of steppe dress, cuisine, or military practice.

Students should be cautious of using "sinicization" as though it described an automatic, one-way, or complete process. Manchu rulers of the Qing dynasty, for example, adopted many Chinese administrative practices while also deliberately preserving distinct Manchu institutions, language policies, and identity throughout their rule (Chapters 46–48). The term is most useful when historians specify exactly what changed, for whom, under what pressures, and what did not change.

Section 1.3

Chronology, Dynasties, and Historical Periodization

Chronology — the ordering of events in time — is one of the most basic tools historians use, and Chinese history offers an unusually rich chronological record for much of its span, thanks to a long tradition of court record-keeping, astronomical observation, and official historical compilation. But chronology alone does not tell historians how to organize that vast span of time into manageable, meaningful units. That is the task of periodization.

The traditional and most familiar system for periodizing Chinese history is the sequence of dynasties: Xia (traditional), Shang, Zhou, Qin, Han, and so on through to the Qing, followed by the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China. This dynastic framework has real value. It reflects genuine, documented changes in ruling authority, and it is the framework used by Chinese historical sources themselves for most of the imperial period. This textbook uses dynastic names and dates extensively because they are indispensable reference points.

However, organizing Chinese history purely dynasty-by-dynasty, ruler-by-ruler, risks two serious distortions. First, it can make political division look like mere interruption — treating the Three Kingdoms period, or the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, as awkward gaps between "real" unified empires, when in fact these periods produced significant religious, technological, literary, and social change in their own right. Second, a purely dynastic framework can obscure long-term social, economic, environmental, and cultural developments — the spread of rice cultivation, the growth of interregional trade, the transmission of Buddhism, changes in family structure and gender roles — that unfolded across, rather than within, individual dynastic reigns and that often mattered more to the lives of ordinary people than which family occupied the throne.

For this reason, this textbook organizes its seventy-six chapters into twelve parts that combine chronological and thematic organization: China, geography, and the deep past; Bronze Age China and the classical foundations; the Han empire; division, migration, and religious transformation; Sui, Tang, and the post-Tang transition; Song China and the multi-state East Asian world; Mongol, Yuan, and Ming China; the Qing imperial order; nineteenth-century crisis and the end of the Qing; revolution, republic, and war; Maoist China; and reform and contemporary China. Dynasties remain essential chronological markers within this structure, but they are not treated as a substitute for explaining social, economic, environmental, religious, and cultural change.

Cause and Effect

Why Periodization Choices Matter

If historians organize only by dynasty

Periods of division (Three Kingdoms, Northern and Southern Dynasties, Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms) risk being treated as chaotic interludes rather than periods of genuine historical development, and cross-dynastic trends (technology, trade, religion, agriculture) can be obscured.

If historians organize only by broad theme

Students may lose the chronological anchors — reign dates, specific rulers, specific events — that are necessary for precise historical reference and for understanding sequence and causation.

This textbook's approach

Combines chronological, dynastic organization within each Part with explicit thematic sections (economy, religion, society, frontier relations) inside each chapter, so that dynastic sequence and long-term thematic change are both visible.

Effect for students

Students learn to place events precisely in time while also tracing how agriculture, trade, religion, technology, and social structure changed across, not just within, dynastic boundaries.

Historical complexity: No single periodization is "correct." Every choice of periodization highlights some patterns and obscures others; historians must be explicit about the choices they make and why.

Section 1.4

Geography and Historical Space

Geography does not determine history, but it profoundly shapes the possibilities available to human societies, and nowhere is this more evident than in the history of East Asia. Chapter 2 of this textbook is devoted entirely to China's physical geography and environmental regions, but a brief preview belongs here, because geography is one of the essential tools students need before entering the rest of the book.

The North China Plain, watered by the Yellow River, has fertile but easily eroded loess soils and a climate suited to millet and, later, wheat cultivation; it has also long been subject to devastating floods, since the Yellow River's heavy silt load causes its bed to rise, sometimes leading it to change course catastrophically. The Yangtze River basin, further south, has a wetter, warmer climate suited to wet-rice agriculture, and it supported some of the highest population densities and greatest agricultural productivity in the pre-modern world by the Tang and Song periods. The Sichuan Basin, ringed by mountains, developed as a relatively self-contained regional economy with its own distinct history. The south and southeast coast, including regions such as Fujian and Guangdong (historically part of "Lingnan," the region "south of the mountain passes"), developed strong maritime and commercial traditions and were, for much of early Chinese history, considered peripheral to the northern political centers. The vast grasslands, deserts, and mountains of the north and west — Mongolia, the Ordos, the Gansu or Hexi Corridor, the Tarim Basin, and Tibet — were home to pastoral, oasis, and highland societies whose relationship with agricultural China alternated between trade, diplomacy, conflict, and, in some periods, conquest in either direction.

These regional differences mattered enormously for political history. The agriculturally rich but flood-prone north repeatedly posed recurring coordination, water-management, and frontier-defense challenges that could encourage the growth of larger-scale state capacity in some periods — though centralization was never automatic, and different states responded to similar pressures in different ways. States or regimes based in the south, aided somewhat by distance and the Yangtze's natural barriers, sometimes developed different political and cultural patterns, as seen during the Southern Dynasties and the Southern Song. Frontier regions were rarely simply "outside" Chinese history; they were zones of exchange, conflict, and mutual influence that shaped both the settled agricultural core and the surrounding pastoral and highland societies.

Section 1.5

Archaeology and Material Evidence

For the earliest periods discussed in this textbook — the Paleolithic societies of Chapter 3, the Neolithic agricultural communities of Chapter 4, the regional cultures of Chapter 5, and the early states of Chapter 6 — there is little or no surviving contemporary writing. For these periods, archaeology is not a supplementary source of evidence; it is the primary source of evidence. Archaeologists study stone tools, pottery, house foundations, burial sites, human and animal remains, plant remains, and, from the Bronze Age onward, metal objects and early writing, to reconstruct how ancient communities lived, what they ate, how they organized labor and status, and how their societies changed over time.

Archaeological evidence has transformed the study of early Chinese history in the past century. Excavations at sites associated with the Yangshao and Longshan cultures in the north, and the Hemudu and Liangzhu cultures in the lower Yangtze region, have shown that complex, socially stratified societies developed independently in multiple regions of what is now China, rather than spreading outward from a single point of origin. Excavations at Erlitou have revealed a large, planned settlement with palace-like structures, bronze workshops, and evidence of long-distance exchange networks dating to the early second millennium BCE — a site closely associated with, though not conclusively identified as, the traditional Xia dynasty (see the Historical Debate feature below). Excavations at Anyang uncovered the royal cemetery and archive of the late Shang dynasty, including tens of thousands of inscribed oracle bones that transformed Shang history from legend into a period documented by contemporary written sources.

Archaeological evidence has real limits. It rarely preserves names of specific individuals unless writing survives. It can reveal patterns of settlement, diet, technology, and social hierarchy, but it cannot, by itself, reveal political ideology, religious belief in detail, or the content of spoken language, except where these are reflected in surviving material culture such as ritual objects or, later, inscriptions. Archaeological dating methods, including radiocarbon dating and stratigraphic analysis, allow scientists to establish chronological sequences with a stated degree of uncertainty, but these dates are estimates within ranges, not exact calendar years, especially for the earliest periods.

Section 1.6

Inscriptions, Manuscripts, and Written Sources

The history of Chinese writing is itself a central topic for historians. The earliest confirmed Chinese writing appears on oracle bones — ox scapulae and turtle plastrons used in royal divination rituals during the late Shang dynasty, inscribed with questions posed to ancestral spirits and, sometimes, with records of the answers or outcomes. Tens of thousands of these inscriptions, discovered at Anyang beginning in the late nineteenth century, provide direct, contemporary evidence of Shang royal names, military campaigns, agricultural concerns, and religious practice — evidence that in some cases confirms, and in some cases complicates, the picture of the Shang later presented in Han-dynasty histories written more than a thousand years afterward.

Bronze inscriptions, cast into ritual vessels beginning in the late Shang and greatly expanding during the Western Zhou, provide another crucial category of contemporary written evidence, often recording royal grants of land or titles, military achievements, and genealogical information. From the Warring States period onward, historians also have access to an expanding body of excavated manuscripts — texts written on bamboo strips, wooden slips, and silk, recovered from tombs and other archaeological contexts — which have, in recent decades, revealed versions of classical texts, administrative documents, legal codes, and philosophical writings that sometimes differ meaningfully from the versions preserved through later manuscript copying and printing.

Later periods of Chinese history are also documented by an enormous body of official and private written sources: administrative records, law codes, census and tax registers, local gazetteers, private letters, poetry, religious texts, travel accounts, and, especially from the Song dynasty onward, an increasing volume of printed books made possible by advances in printing technology discussed in Chapter 33. Historians must read all of these sources critically, asking who created them, for what purpose, and what perspective — official, elite, male, urban, or otherwise — they represent, and whose experiences they may leave out.

Section 1.7

Official Histories and Historical Memory

China possesses an unusually long and well-organized tradition of official historical writing. Beginning with Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), completed in the early Han dynasty, and continuing through a series of officially sponsored dynastic histories compiled over the following two thousand years, each new dynasty typically commissioned an official history of the dynasty it had replaced. This tradition preserved an immense amount of information that would otherwise have been lost, including administrative records, biographies, economic data, and detailed narrative accounts of political events.

However, students must understand that these official histories were not neutral records compiled by disinterested observers. They were usually written under the sponsorship of the new ruling dynasty, often decades after the events they describe, by court historians with a strong political interest in legitimizing the new dynasty's rise to power. A common narrative pattern in these histories presents the final rulers of a fallen dynasty as personally decadent, cruel, or incompetent, and their fall as a natural consequence of losing the "Mandate of Heaven" (discussed further in Chapter 8 and in Appendix F). While such moral narratives may contain elements of truth, historians today generally look beyond them to identify the actual fiscal, military, environmental, institutional, and social causes of dynastic decline and collapse — causes that this textbook examines in detail for each major transition.

Primary Source Window

Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), c. 94 BCE

Creator: Sima Qian, Han-dynasty court astrologer and historian · Date: completed c. 94 BCE · Type: Historical compilation by a Han court historian, combining chronicles, biographies, and treatises, which became the model later government-sponsored dynastic histories would follow

Context: Sima Qian inherited the position of Grand Historian from his father and undertook the vast project of compiling a history of China from the legendary Yellow Emperor down to his own time under Emperor Wu of Han. He completed the work despite suffering severe punishment at the emperor's hands, and it became the model for nearly all later official Chinese historical writing.

Sima Qian organized his history into chronological annals of rulers, chronological tables, treatises on institutions such as rituals and economics, biographies of hereditary noble houses, and biographies of notable individuals, including officials, philosophers, merchants, and even assassins — an organizational structure later dynastic histories would largely follow.

Analysis: The Shiji is an extraordinarily rich source, but its account of very early periods — the legendary sage-kings, and even the Xia dynasty discussed later in this chapter — was compiled more than a thousand years after the events it describes, drawing on traditions whose historical reliability cannot be independently confirmed for that early period. Its account of the more recent Qin and early Han, closer to Sima Qian's own lifetime, rests on stronger documentary grounding, though it still reflects the historian's own moral and political judgments.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a history written centuries or millennia after the events it describes be less reliable than one written closer to those events?
  2. What political risks did Sima Qian face in writing an official history under a reigning emperor?
  3. How might the model established by the Shiji have shaped the way later dynasties wrote about their predecessors?
  4. What kinds of historical information can official histories like the Shiji provide that archaeology alone cannot?
Section 1.8

Historical Interpretation, Bias, and Modern National Narratives

Historical interpretation of China has been shaped not only by the biases of traditional dynastic historiography but also by more recent political and national narratives. In the twentieth century, Chinese nationalist historians, writing amid a century of foreign imperialism, internal crisis, reform, and revolution (Chapters 51–65), often emphasized a narrative of continuous, unified Chinese civilization stretching back to remote antiquity, and portrayed periods of division or conquest by non-Han dynasties as temporary interruptions in an essentially continuous national story. Marxist historians in the People's Republic of China, especially from the 1950s onward, reinterpreted Chinese history through the lens of modes of production and class struggle, identifying, for example, a "feudal" period lasting from roughly the Warring States through the Qing dynasty.

Outside China, some earlier Western scholarship treated Chinese history as static or unchanging — a stereotype this textbook explicitly rejects — or interpreted Chinese history primarily through the lens of its interactions with, and eventual subordination to, Western imperial powers in the nineteenth century, a framework that risks minimizing the internal dynamism, diversity, and agency of Chinese history across its full span. More recent international scholarship, drawing on archaeology, social and economic history, environmental history, and frontier studies, has worked to present a more regionally, ethnically, and institutionally complex picture — the approach this textbook follows.

Historical Debate

Was the Xia Dynasty a Historical State?

The question: Later Chinese textual tradition, most fully preserved in Sima Qian's Shiji, describes the Xia as the first Chinese dynasty, founded by the legendary flood-control hero Yu the Great and preceding the Shang. But the earliest surviving written sources — Shang oracle-bone inscriptions — do not mention a Xia dynasty by that name, and no writing has been securely identified from sites associated with the traditional Xia period. Historians and archaeologists disagree about how to interpret this gap between later textual tradition and contemporary evidence.

Interpretation A — Erlitou represents the historical Xia: Many Chinese archaeologists argue that the Erlitou culture, with its large palace complexes, bronze industry, and evidence of political centralization dating to roughly the early second millennium BCE, corresponds to the state later remembered as the Xia dynasty, even without inscriptional confirmation, because its scale, timing, and location broadly match the textual tradition.
Interpretation B — The Xia cannot yet be confirmed as a historical dynasty: Other historians, including many outside China, argue that without contemporary written evidence naming a "Xia" state, the identification of Erlitou with the Xia dynasty remains an inference rather than a demonstrated fact. They caution that later textual traditions, especially ones compiled over a thousand years after the events they describe, can preserve genuine historical memory, but can also incorporate legend, retrospective political interpretation, and outright invention, and that these possibilities cannot be fully separated without independent, contemporary evidence.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record at Erlitou and related sites, and on the same later textual tradition; they differ in how much interpretive weight to place on each without a documented link between the two.

Why it matters: This debate illustrates a recurring challenge in the study of any ancient civilization: distinguishing archaeological evidence from later textual tradition, and being honest about what can and cannot be confirmed with the evidence currently available.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, what additional evidence — if discovered — would help resolve this debate? Why is it important for historians to distinguish "traditionally dated" or "debated" from "confirmed"?

Section 1.9

Cause, Effect, Continuity, and Change

As in any field of history, understanding Chinese history requires more than memorizing a sequence of dynasties. It requires asking why things happened, and it requires distinguishing between short-term, immediate triggers and longer-term, structural causes. Consider dynastic transitions, one of the recurring events students will study throughout this book. A dynasty's collapse might have an immediate, short-term trigger — a military defeat, a succession crisis, a specific rebellion — but such triggers are usually the culmination of longer-term causes: fiscal strain from military overextension or tax-collection failures, environmental stress such as flooding or drought, growing landholding inequality, factional conflict at court, or the erosion of administrative capacity in distant provinces. Traditional accounts sometimes reduce this complexity to a simple moral narrative — a "bad last ruler" losing the Mandate of Heaven — but this textbook consistently asks students to look past that narrative to the specific institutional, economic, environmental, and social conditions involved.

Students must also learn to recognize continuity and change operating simultaneously. Political dynasties rose and fell repeatedly, and the territory called "China" expanded, contracted, fragmented, and reunified many times — yet certain institutions and practices, including the tradition of centralized bureaucratic administration, the use of a shared writing system across many spoken languages, and enduring philosophical and religious traditions, persisted and were repeatedly reinterpreted across these political changes. Recognizing both dimensions — the dramatic political discontinuities and the deeper cultural and institutional continuities — without collapsing one into the other, is essential to studying Chinese history honestly.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Idea of "China" from the Zhou to the Present

What Changed

  • The geographic territory associated with "the central states" expanded from a small Yellow River core to include the Yangtze basin, the south, and, under some dynasties, Inner Asian frontier regions
  • Ruling dynasties came from Han Chinese, Mongol, and Manchu backgrounds at different periods
  • Political organization shifted from Bronze Age kin-based states to centralized bureaucratic empires to a modern nation-state
  • The modern concept of a unified "Chinese nation" emerged specifically in response to nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism

What Stayed the Same

  • A shared tradition of Chinese characters (Hanzi), used and adapted across many spoken languages
  • Recurring institutions of centralized bureaucratic government, adapted differently in each period
  • Enduring engagement with Confucian, Daoist, and (from the first centuries CE) Buddhist traditions
  • The recurring political and rhetorical claim to represent the legitimate "central" civilization, even amid division

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "China" as an unchanging, timeless essence, or treating it as nothing more than a modern invention with no historical roots at all.

Section 1.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced the methods, concepts, and habits of mind that will inform the study of Chinese history throughout this textbook. Before moving into the specific content of subsequent chapters, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: Chinese history is the study of many peoples, regions, states, and traditions, not a single continuous national story. The geographic and political meaning of "China" has changed many times. Dynasty, state, empire, civilization, ethnicity, and nation-state are distinct categories that should not be collapsed into one another. Periodization involves interpretive choices that highlight some patterns and obscure others. Geography shaped, without determining, the development of Chinese states and societies. Archaeology is essential, and often the only available evidence, for periods before or without surviving writing. Written sources — oracle bones, bronze inscriptions, excavated manuscripts, and official histories — must each be evaluated according to their own nature, purpose, and distance from the events they describe. Official histories and modern national narratives alike serve political purposes that historians must recognize. Historical causation is complex, and the "Mandate of Heaven" narrative of dynastic decline should not substitute for specific institutional, economic, environmental, and social analysis. Continuity and change coexist throughout Chinese history.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gets to Define "China"? Perspectives on the Frontier

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Han-dynasty court official in the Yellow River heartland: "China" (Zhongguo) meant the settled, agricultural, literate world governed according to ritual and bureaucratic tradition, in contrast to the pastoral peoples of the northern steppe, whom court documents often described as culturally and politically peripheral, even when those peoples were militarily powerful, as in the case of the Xiongnu confederation discussed in Chapter 16.
From the perspective of a Mongol or Manchu ruler of a later conquest dynasty: Rulers of the Yuan and Qing dynasties governed vast multiethnic empires in which the traditional agricultural heartland was one important region among several, alongside Mongolia, Tibet, Manchuria, and Xinjiang, each often administered through distinct institutions rather than being forcibly absorbed into a single uniform "Chinese" identity.
From the perspective of a Uyghur, Tibetan, or Mongol community living on the Inner Asian frontier: Political relationships with dynasties based in the agricultural heartland varied enormously over time — including trade, diplomacy, religious exchange, alliance, and, in some periods, conquest in either direction — and frontier communities maintained their own languages, religious traditions, and political institutions that were not simply extensions of a single "Chinese" civilization.
From the perspective of a modern Chinese nationalist historian: Many of these historically distinct regions and peoples are understood as integral, long-standing parts of a continuous multiethnic Chinese nation, a view that shapes contemporary education, politics, and international relations, and that is itself an important object of historical study, discussed further in Appendix J.
Everyday Life and Social History

Why Ordinary Lives Belong in This Textbook

A history organized only around emperors, dynasties, and elite philosophical texts would tell readers almost nothing about how most people in Chinese history actually lived. Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society, economy, and everyday life examine topics such as: how millet and rice farming shaped daily labor and household organization in the north and south respectively; how family and lineage structures organized inheritance, marriage, and obligation across many centuries and regions; how urban artisans, merchants, and laborers experienced the growth of commercial cities, especially from the Tang and Song periods onward; how women's roles in agriculture, textile production, household management, and, in some periods, scholarship and politics varied by class, region, and era; and how ordinary people experienced war, famine, migration, taxation, and state power differently than the court officials who wrote most surviving records.

Because most surviving written sources before the modern era were produced by a literate elite — officials, scholars, and, less often, merchants — historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own, while also relying heavily on archaeology, material culture, tax and census records, and, for more recent periods, oral history to reconstruct the experiences of farmers, laborers, women, and ethnic minority communities.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced Chinese history as a field of inquiry far broader and more regionally, ethnically, and politically diverse than the popular image of an unbroken line of emperors ruling over an unchanging civilization. Chinese history is the study of many peoples, regions, states, economies, religious and philosophical traditions, and social experiences that have developed across a vast and varied landscape over more than three thousand years of written record and a much longer span of human settlement before that.

The chapter has emphasized that the very meaning of "China" — the term Zhongguo, "the central states" — has changed dramatically over time, from a small core region along the middle Yellow River in the Western Zhou period to the vast multiethnic empires of the Yuan and Qing dynasties to the modern nation-state shaped by nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism. Students have been introduced to essential distinctions between civilization, dynasty, state, empire, ethnicity, and nation-state — distinctions that will recur throughout the textbook and that prevent the common error of assuming that "China" has always meant the same territory, government, or people.

Students have also been introduced to the key tools of historical method as applied to Chinese history: chronology, which establishes sequence; periodization, which organizes the vast span of Chinese history into manageable units while making interpretive choices that this textbook makes explicit; geography, which shaped, without determining, the development of northern and southern agricultural systems, frontier relationships, and regional political traditions; archaeology, which provides the primary evidence for periods before or without surviving writing; and the several distinct categories of written evidence — oracle bones, bronze inscriptions, excavated manuscripts, and official dynastic histories — each of which must be read according to its own nature and limitations.

The chapter paid particular attention to the problem of historiography: the recognition that official Chinese dynastic histories, compiled under the sponsorship of succeeding dynasties, and modern national narratives, shaped by nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism, both serve political purposes that historians must recognize without dismissing their genuine evidentiary value. The Historical Debate feature on the Xia dynasty illustrated this challenge concretely: how should historians treat a later textual tradition that cannot be confirmed by contemporary written evidence, even when archaeological evidence from a relevant time and place, such as Erlitou, exists?

Students also encountered the concepts of causation, continuity, and change as applied to Chinese history specifically. Dynastic transitions, which will recur throughout this textbook, should not be explained through the simplified moral narrative of a corrupt ruler losing the Mandate of Heaven, but through specific fiscal, military, environmental, institutional, and social causes that this textbook will examine in detail for each major transition. At the same time, certain institutions and cultural traditions — a shared writing system, recurring bureaucratic institutions, and enduring philosophical and religious traditions — persisted and were repeatedly reinterpreted across many centuries of political change.

These tools and habits of mind are not abstract exercises. They are the means by which students will read the following seventy-five chapters carefully and critically, distinguishing established evidence from contested interpretation, recognizing regional and ethnic diversity within "Chinese" history, and understanding China as a civilization that has been continuously built, contested, and reinterpreted by many different peoples across an enormous span of time and space.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Zhongguo (中国)
Term commonly translated as "China," literally "central state(s)"; its geographic and political scope has changed repeatedly over Chinese history.
Civilization
A long-term cultural tradition — shared writing, institutions, and belief systems — that persists across many different political regimes and territorial configurations.
Dynasty
A sequence of rulers from a single family or lineage claiming the right to govern, generally over a specific period and territory.
State
An organized political community with institutions of government; multiple states can exist simultaneously.
Empire
A large state incorporating diverse peoples, languages, and regions, typically through conquest, under a single political authority.
Nation-state
A modern political form in which a government claims to represent a culturally defined "nation" within clearly bounded territory; should not be projected backward onto ancient or medieval polities.
Chronology
The sequential ordering of events in time, foundational to historical analysis since cause must precede effect.
Periodization
The practice of dividing the past into named periods; every periodization scheme involves interpretive choices.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of the Chinese past have changed over time and why.
Oracle bone (jiaguwen, 甲骨文)
Inscribed ox scapulae or turtle plastrons used in late Shang royal divination; the earliest confirmed form of Chinese writing.
Bronze inscription
Text cast into ritual bronze vessels, especially common in the Western Zhou period, recording grants, achievements, and genealogy.
Excavated manuscript
A text written on bamboo, wood, or silk recovered through archaeology, sometimes differing from later transmitted versions of the same work.
Official dynastic history
A history of a dynasty compiled, usually under official sponsorship, by its successor dynasty; valuable but shaped by political purpose.
Sima Qian
Han-dynasty court historian, author of the Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), foundational to the Chinese historiographical tradition.
Mandate of Heaven
A political concept, discussed further in Chapter 8, used to explain and legitimize dynastic rule and its loss; should not substitute for concrete historical causation.
Sinicization
A historically contested term describing processes by which non-Han peoples or regions adopted elements of Han Chinese culture, language, or institutions; historians debate its extent, direction, and voluntariness in different periods.
Han Chinese
The largest ethnic group associated with Chinese history; a historically constructed and changing identity rather than a fixed, ancient category (see Appendix E).
Erlitou
Early Bronze Age archaeological site in the Yellow River valley, associated by many scholars, though not conclusively, with the traditional Xia dynasty.
Anyang
Site of the late Shang capital, where oracle-bone inscriptions were discovered.
Contingency
The role of chance, unpredictable events, and human choice in shaping historical outcomes; history did not have to unfold as it did.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.
Causation
The relationship between events in which one condition produces or contributes to another; usually complex and multi-causal.
Short-term cause
An immediate trigger or proximate event, such as a specific military defeat, that sets a historical development in motion.
Long-term cause
A deeper, underlying condition — fiscal, environmental, institutional, or social — that makes a particular outcome possible over time.
Primary source
A document, object, or record created at the time being studied, by a participant or contemporary witness.
Secondary source
A later interpretation of the past, typically by a historian, drawing on primary sources.
Historical memory
The way communities and states collectively remember and use the past to construct identity and meaning in the present.
Ethnicity
A historically changing form of group identity shaped by language, culture, ancestry claims, and political classification, rather than a fixed biological category.
Regional diversity
The recognition that Chinese history varied significantly by region — north versus south, coastal versus interior, agricultural versus pastoral — rather than developing uniformly.
Xia dynasty
A dynasty described in later Chinese textual tradition as preceding the Shang; its historicity and identification with archaeological sites such as Erlitou remain debated (see Chapter 6).
Multi-state world
A historical situation, recurring at several points in Chinese history, in which multiple political entities coexisted and competed, each sometimes claiming legitimate central authority.
Archaeological dating
Scientific methods, such as radiocarbon dating and stratigraphy, used to estimate the age of archaeological materials within a stated range of uncertainty.

Investigating a Historical Debate in Early Chinese History

This project asks you to investigate a scholarly debate about early Chinese history in greater depth than this chapter can provide, using library and reference resources to compare archaeological evidence with textual tradition.

  1. Choose a debated topic. Select a debated question from early Chinese history, such as the historicity of the Xia dynasty, the origins of Chinese writing, or the relationship between Erlitou and later textual tradition.
  2. Research the archaeological evidence. Using library resources, encyclopedias, and reliable academic sources, summarize what archaeological evidence exists for your chosen topic, including site names, approximate dates, and what has been physically discovered.
  3. Research the textual tradition. Identify what later Chinese texts (such as the Shiji) claim about the same topic, and note approximately how many centuries after the events in question those texts were compiled.
  4. Compare the two bodies of evidence. Where do the archaeological and textual records agree? Where do they diverge or remain unconnected? What can be confirmed, and what remains debated?
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay (or equivalent multimedia presentation), explain the debate, present both major scholarly interpretations fairly, and explain your own assessment of what the evidence currently supports, using careful language such as "the evidence suggests" or "this remains debated" rather than overstating certainty.

Library resources to use: Archaeology and early China reference collections, academic encyclopedias, translated primary source collections, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on early Chinese historiography and archaeology (see also Appendix M, Historical Methods for Studying China).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces — distinguishing dynasty from state from empire from nation-state, evaluating archaeological and textual evidence on its own terms, recognizing the political purposes behind official histories and modern national narratives, and tracing continuity and change across an enormous span of time — are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

China's history is one of the longest continuously documented histories of any human civilization, and it is also one of the most regionally, ethnically, linguistically, and politically diverse. It has included periods of extraordinary political unity and periods of prolonged division; periods of Han-led dynastic rule and periods of rule by Mongol, Manchu, and other non-Han dynasties; periods of relative isolation and periods of intense connection with Central Asia, Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean world, and, eventually, the entire globe. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to Chinese civilization's significance — it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than mythologized.

Students who understand that "China" has meant different things at different times, that dynasties and states are not identical to civilizations or nations, and that archaeology and critical source analysis are essential to separating confirmed history from later tradition, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook — from the Paleolithic societies of Chapter 3 through the early states and classical foundations of Parts I and II, the imperial dynasties of Parts III through VIII, the crises and revolutions of Parts IX through XI, and the contemporary developments of Part XII — with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends across more than three thousand years of documented history and thousands of years more of the deeper human past, from the earliest Paleolithic communities discussed in Chapter 3 to the unresolved questions of contemporary Chinese politics, society, and international relations examined in Part XII. It is a story with no single hero, no single unbroken thread, and no predetermined outcome — a story of many peoples, many regions, and many states, continuously building, contesting, and reinterpreting what "China" has meant and what it might yet become. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
