What Is Latin America?

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Region That Did Not Yet Exist

"Latin America" is not a country, not a single people, and not a name that any of the societies this textbook begins with would have recognized. It is a historically constructed region — linguistic, cultural, and political — whose boundaries, meaning, and membership were assembled gradually, largely from the nineteenth century onward, out of an extraordinarily diverse and much older human world.

Open an atlas, a news broadcast, or a general encyclopedia, and "Latin America" appears as though its meaning were self-evident: a great arc of nations running from the Rio Grande to Tierra del Fuego, encompassing Mexico, the isthmus of Central America, most of the Caribbean, and the whole of South America apart from a handful of exceptions. The label feels natural, almost geological, as though the Andes and the Amazon and the plains of the Río de la Plata had always belonged to a single historical story. And yet, thirteen thousand years before that atlas was printed, no one living between the deserts of northern Mexico and the glaciers of Patagonia would have recognized "Latin America" as a meaningful description of anything. A Mexica priest in Tenochtitlan, a K'iche' Maya lord in the Guatemalan highlands, an Inca provincial governor administering the roads of Tawantinsuyu, a Taíno cacique on the island the Spanish would later call Hispaniola, and a Mapuche toqui organizing resistance south of the Bío-Bío River shared a hemisphere, but they did not share a name for it, a common political community, or a sense that their histories were chapters in the same regional story.

This chapter does not begin the narrative of the ancient Olmec, the Maya, the Mexica, the Inca, or any of the other great Indigenous civilizations this textbook will examine in the chapters that follow. It begins with something more basic: an honest reckoning with the fact that the very organizing category of this five-volume textbook — Latin America — is itself a product of history rather than a timeless fact of geography. Understanding how, when, and why this category came into being, what it includes and excludes, and what it risks flattening if used carelessly, is the necessary first step before any of the deeper history can be told responsibly.

Consider a single illustrative complication. A Quechua-speaking farmer in the Peruvian highlands, a Portuguese-speaking descendant of enslaved West Africans in Salvador da Bahia, a Haitian Creole–speaking resident of Haiti, a Guaraní-speaking community member in Paraguay, and a Spanish-speaking mestizo shopkeeper in Mexico City might each be described today, by different observers and for different reasons, as belonging to "Latin America." Yet their languages, ancestries, religious traditions, and historical experiences differ enormously, and the very validity of grouping them under one regional label depends entirely on what criteria — language, colonial history, geography, culture, self-identification — are being applied. None of these figures is a hypothetical curiosity invented to complicate an otherwise simple picture. Each represents a real, historically documented category of person whose presence within the region this textbook studies is central, not peripheral, to understanding its history.

This textbook studies a hemisphere-spanning region defined chiefly by the historical intersection of Indigenous American peoples, Iberian and French colonialism, the Romance languages that colonialism carried and transformed, the forced migration of millions of enslaved Africans, and the political, economic, and cultural processes that followed from those intersections through independence, nation-building, and the contemporary era. Before any of this occurred, the lands that would eventually be called Latin America were home to some of the oldest, most sophisticated, and most varied civilizations in human history — societies with their own political systems, religious worldviews, agricultural technologies, urban centers, and historical trajectories, none of which understood themselves as part of a place called "Latin America," a term that would not exist for centuries, if not millennia, after their own histories began.

Understanding how "Latin America" came to mean what it now means — and understanding what it still does not mean — requires the same historical toolkit historians use to study any large, diverse, and long-lived civilizational space: careful attention to chronology, geography, language, ethnicity, evidence, and the political purposes behind competing national and regional narratives. It also requires humility. Latin Americans themselves have long debated, and continue to debate, where the boundaries of "their" region lie, who belongs within it, what unites Brazil and Bolivia or Haiti and Chile, and what, if anything, that belonging should mean politically and culturally today.

This chapter establishes the vocabulary, geographic scope, chronological boundaries, and historical-methodological commitments that will govern the 114 chapters that follow. It explains why this textbook is titled a history of "Latin America" rather than a history of "Spanish America," "South America," or "the Iberian Americas," and it explains why those distinctions, often blurred in casual usage, matter enormously to writing this history honestly — and why, despite the term's relatively recent invention, the region it now describes has a history stretching back tens of thousands of years before that term existed.

Finally, this chapter asks students to consider why any of this matters beyond the classroom. Treating "Latin America" as though it had always existed, or treating it as simply "south of the United States," or allowing Mexico or the Andes to stand in for the whole region, has had real consequences — in journalism, in foreign policy, in popular stereotype, and in the way hundreds of millions of people's histories and identities are represented or misrepresented by outsiders. Learning to define this region carefully, and to hold its internal diversity in view from the very first page, is not a scholarly exercise in semantics. It is the necessary foundation for understanding, accurately and respectfully, one of the most historically dynamic and internally varied regions on earth.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook — the peopling of the Americas and Indigenous civilizations examined in the remainder of Volume I, the conquest and colonial societies of Volume II, the independence movements and nineteenth-century republics of Volume III, the revolutions and Cold War conflicts of Volume IV, and the contemporary Latin America of Volume V, extending through 2026 — they need a shared, carefully defined vocabulary and a clear sense of the region's scope.

The lands this textbook eventually calls "Latin America" span an extraordinary range of environments and historical experiences: the arid highlands and tropical coasts of Mexico; the volcanic spine of Central America; the island world of the Caribbean, with its own distinct plantation and maritime history; the towering Andes Mountains, running the length of western South America; the vast Amazon Basin, one of the largest tropical forest and river systems on Earth; the Atlantic coastal lowlands and interior plateaus of Brazil, by far the region's largest country; the grasslands of the Río de la Plata; and the long, narrowing landmass of the Southern Cone, reaching toward Antarctica. No single ancient civilization, language, or political authority unified all of these regions before the arrival of Europeans in the late fifteenth century, and none has fully unified them since. Long before any of them could meaningfully be called "Latin American," they were home to some of the most sophisticated and long-lived civilizations in world history — societies covered on their own historical terms throughout the remaining chapters of Volume I.

Historians studying this region must therefore resist two opposite errors. The first error treats "Latin America" as a timeless, unchanging civilizational category stretching back to remote antiquity — projecting a modern regional identity onto the Olmec, the Maya, the Mexica, the Inca, the Taíno, or the Mapuche, none of whom would have recognized such a category and many of whose descendant communities today do not primarily identify with it. The second error, a reaction against the first, sometimes minimizes the genuine historical significance of the shared experiences that did eventually bind much of the region together — Iberian colonial rule, the Roman Catholic Church, the Spanish and Portuguese languages, plantation slavery, and the shared struggle for independence — treating "Latin America" as nothing more than an arbitrary label imposed by outsiders with no real historical substance. This textbook adopts neither error. It describes the pre-Columbian civilizations of the region honestly and on their own terms, and it treats the emergence, consolidation, and ongoing contestation of a Latin American regional identity from the colonial period onward as a significant historical development in its own right — without claiming that this identity was always present or that it ever fully erased the region's Indigenous, African, and locally distinct national and cultural identities.

A further complication concerns language and colonial origin. Most of the territory this textbook studies was colonized by Spain or Portugal, and Spanish and Portuguese — both Romance languages descended from Latin — remain the dominant languages of government, education, and daily life across most of the region today. This has led, in much popular usage, to a rough equation of "Latin America" with "the parts of the Americas where people speak Spanish or Portuguese." But the region as this textbook studies it has never been linguistically or culturally uniform in that simple sense. It includes Haiti, where French and Haitian Creole are the national languages and where the colonial power was France rather than Spain or Portugal; it includes extraordinary numbers of Indigenous-language speakers — Nahuatl, Yucatec Maya and other Maya languages, Quechua, Aymara, Guaraní, Mapudungun, and hundreds of others — many spoken as a first language by millions of people today; and it includes Afro-Latin American communities whose linguistic and cultural inheritance includes West and Central African traditions transformed and blended, over centuries, with Iberian, French, and Indigenous elements. Conversely, some Spanish-speaking Caribbean and South American territories, along with English-, Dutch-, and French-speaking Caribbean states and territories such as Guyana, Suriname, Jamaica, and the smaller island nations, occupy an ambiguous position relative to "Latin America" as this textbook defines it — geographically part of the Americas and often integrated into regional politics and economics, but not always included within the linguistic-cultural definition of Latin America that anchors this book. Section 1.3 of this chapter examines this ambiguity in more depth.

Section 1.1

A Region That Did Not Yet Exist

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France, Japan, or Egypt, the geographic and political referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a currently existing state with deep historical continuity. A history of "Latin America" has no such single anchor. It is a history of a region encompassing more than twenty modern countries, roughly two dozen distinct national histories, hundreds of Indigenous nations and languages, several major waves of forced and voluntary migration, and a shared but uneven colonial experience — one that, crucially, did not produce a single "Latin American" identity until long after the events that most decisively shaped the region had already occurred.

This opening section establishes the central interpretive commitment of the entire textbook: Latin America is a useful modern historical category, but it is not a timeless civilization. This commitment has several consequences that will recur throughout the following chapters. First, this textbook does not begin its narrative by treating Indigenous peoples as an undifferentiated prelude to a story that only becomes historically important once Europeans arrive; the Olmec, the Maya, the Mexica, the Inca, the Taíno, and dozens of other peoples and civilizations receive sustained, independent treatment in Volume I precisely because their histories matter on their own terms, not merely as background to later Latin American nationhood. Second, this textbook does not simply substitute "South America" for "Latin America," because the term as this book uses it includes Mexico and Central America, which are geographically part of North America, while some parts of South America — such as Guyana and Suriname — are not conventionally included in "Latin America" at all. Third, this textbook does not treat "Latin America" as a single ethnic or racial category, because, since the colonial encounter, the populations of the lands later called Latin America have included Indigenous peoples, people of African descent, people of European descent, people of mixed heritage, and, from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries onward, significant populations of Asian and Middle Eastern descent.

None of these clarifications is intended to diminish the historical importance of the shared experiences that did eventually connect much of the region — Iberian colonial rule, Roman Catholicism, the Spanish and Portuguese languages, the plantation economy, and movements for independence. But treating "Latin America" as though it had always existed, or as a synonym for any single sub-region, ethnicity, or language, would misrepresent the actual historical record this textbook sets out to explain.

Historical Vocabulary
Latin America

Latin America is a term whose meaning, like "Arab world" or "Middle East" in other regional histories, has changed and remains contested. The phrase derives from the idea that Spanish, Portuguese, and French are "Latin," or Romance, languages descended from the Latin of the Roman Empire, in contrast to the English of British North America. French writers earlier in the nineteenth century had discussed a broader "Latin race" or "Latin civilization" linking France to Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking America, and this earlier French thought was an intellectual antecedent. But the specific political term "Latin America," used as a regional label, is documented among Spanish American writers themselves, most notably the Chilean Francisco Bilbao and the Colombian José María Torres Caicedo, both writing in 1856 in the context of resistance to United States territorial and commercial expansion following the U.S.–Mexican War, as well as concern about renewed European intervention in the hemisphere. The government of Napoleon III in France subsequently appropriated this "Latin" regional rhetoric for its own imperial purposes, most visibly in connection with the French intervention in Mexico in the 1860s, examined in Chapter 63.

In its most common modern usage, "Latin America" refers to the countries of the Americas south of the United States shaped by Spanish, Portuguese, or French colonization and by the Romance languages that followed. No single list of countries commands universal agreement, and several territories occupy genuine boundary positions rather than fitting neatly inside or outside the category. Section 1.2 sets out this textbook's operational scope in full, including how it treats cases such as Belize, Puerto Rico, French Guiana, Guyana, and Suriname.

It is essential for students to understand that no Indigenous person living in the Americas before 1492, and no colonial-era resident of New Spain, Peru, or Brazil, would have used or recognized the term "Latin America." The category is a product of nineteenth-century political and intellectual history, applied by historians retrospectively — and usefully — to organize the study of an older and more diverse set of societies.

Section 1.2

Defining Latin America

No single, universally accepted list of countries defines "Latin America" precisely, and students should expect definitions to vary somewhat depending on the source and purpose. Most conventional definitions combine geography and colonial-linguistic history. Geographically, the region is generally understood to include Mexico; the Central American countries of Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama; the Latin Caribbean, especially Cuba, the Dominican Republic (both Spanish-speaking), and Haiti (French- and Haitian Creole–speaking); and most of mainland South America — Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and Brazil. Several territories sit at the boundary of this definition and are treated as such throughout this textbook rather than forced into a single tidy category: Belize, part of Central America geographically, was a British colony and remains predominantly English-speaking; Puerto Rico is Spanish-speaking and culturally part of the Hispanic Caribbean but is a territory of the United States rather than a sovereign nation; French Guiana is geographically part of South America and French-speaking but remains an overseas department of France rather than an independent country; and Guyana and Suriname, discussed further in Section 1.3, are geographically South American but developed through British and Dutch rather than Iberian or French colonial rule. This textbook uses "Latin America" as its operational scope — the core set of countries described above — while remaining attentive to these boundary cases wherever they become historically relevant.

Historically, the defining thread running through this list is Iberian or French colonization: nearly every territory in this list was, at some point between the late fifteenth and early nineteenth centuries, ruled by Spain, Portugal, or France, and nearly every territory in this list adopted, to varying degrees, the language, religion, legal traditions, and social hierarchies that colonial rule introduced — while never fully erasing the Indigenous and, in most cases, African populations and traditions that colonialism encountered, enslaved, and transformed. This textbook treats that combination of geography and shared, though not identical, colonial and post-colonial historical experience as the practical basis for its regional scope, while devoting sustained attention throughout to the many peoples, languages, and local histories that this broad definition can obscure if used carelessly.

It is worth stating plainly what this definition does not do. It does not claim that Mexico, Haiti, and Argentina are historically identical, or that a single narrative can adequately describe all of them without careful attention to regional variation. It does not claim that Indigenous peoples within these countries are simply "Latin American" in the same sense as their nations' Spanish- or Portuguese-descended populations. And it does not claim that the region's history began with European colonization; rather, as the remaining twenty-three chapters of Volume I make clear, this textbook insists on precisely the opposite — that Latin America's deepest historical roots lie in societies and civilizations that developed for thousands of years before the term, or the political entities it now describes, existed at all.

Section 1.3

Latin America Is Not the Same as South America

One of the most common errors in popular usage is treating "Latin America" and "South America" as interchangeable. They are not, and the distinction runs in both directions.

In one direction, Latin America includes territory that is not part of South America at all. Mexico and the seven countries of Central America are located in North America by every standard geographic convention, and the Caribbean island nations of Cuba, the Dominican Republic, and Haiti are part of the Caribbean, a distinct island region rather than a South American one. A history of Latin America that focused only on South America would omit Mexico's Aztec and Maya civilizations, the Central American isthmus's own rich pre-Columbian and colonial history, and the entire Caribbean world that produced the Haitian Revolution — an omission this textbook explicitly refuses to make.

In the other direction, South America itself includes territory that most definitions exclude from Latin America. Guyana (a former British colony, independent since 1966, where English is the official language), Suriname (a former Dutch colony, independent since 1975, where Dutch is the official language), and French Guiana (an overseas department of France rather than an independent country) sit on the South American continent but developed through British, Dutch, and French colonial histories quite different from the Spanish and Portuguese colonial systems examined throughout this textbook. These territories will appear at points where their histories intersect directly with those of their Latin American neighbors — in regional diplomacy, migration, and Amazonian and Caribbean history, for example — but they do not receive the sustained, independent treatment this textbook gives to the region's Spanish-, Portuguese-, and French-Haitian-influenced core.

This distinction is not merely pedantic. Confusing Latin America with South America has led, in popular writing and even in some scholarship, to the omission of Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean from analyses that claim to address "Latin America" as a whole — an omission that would eliminate the Mexican Revolution, the Cuban Revolution, the Panama Canal, Central American civil wars, and the entire history of the Haitian Revolution from a supposedly comprehensive regional history. This textbook insists on the full geographic and historical breadth of the term.

Section 1.4

Latin America, Hispanic America, and Ibero-America

Students encountering this textbook for the first time should master several related but distinct terms in addition to "Latin America" itself.

Hispanic America (or Hispanoamérica) refers specifically to the Spanish-speaking countries of the Americas: Mexico, most of Central America, Spanish-speaking South America, and the Spanish-speaking Caribbean — especially Cuba, the Dominican Republic, and Puerto Rico — but excluding Brazil (Portuguese-speaking), Haiti (French- and Haitian Creole–speaking), and the English-, French-, and Dutch-speaking Caribbean. "Hispanic" derives from Hispania, the Roman name for the Iberian Peninsula, and emphasizes specifically Spanish, rather than broader Iberian or Latin, linguistic and cultural heritage.

Spanish America is used similarly to "Hispanic America," often with a more explicitly historical or colonial-era connotation — referring to the former Spanish colonial territories in the Americas, particularly during the colonial period examined in Volume II and the independence era examined in Volume III of this textbook.

Ibero-America (or Iberoamérica) is a broader term than "Hispanic America" because it includes Brazil alongside the Spanish-speaking countries, reflecting the shared Iberian Peninsula origin of Spanish and Portuguese colonization. It does not, however, include Haiti, whose colonial history was French rather than Iberian. "Ibero-America" is frequently used in diplomatic and cultural contexts, including the Ibero-American community of nations, which today comprises the nineteen Latin American countries together with Spain, Portugal, and Andorra (whose official language is Catalan, not a language of the Iberian colonial empires, but which participates as a Pyrenean neighbor of Spain and France within this shared diplomatic and cultural framework).

Latin America, as established above, is the broadest of these related terms, encompassing Hispanic America, Brazil, and Haiti together, on the basis of a shared — though not identical — heritage of Romance-language colonization by Spain, Portugal, or France. This textbook uses "Latin America" as its primary organizing term precisely because its inclusion of Haiti and Brazil alongside the Spanish-speaking countries best matches the Master Instructions governing this textbook's insistence that Brazil and Haiti remain central to the narrative rather than being treated as an appendix to a Spanish-American story.

Students should also distinguish these terms from the Caribbean, a geographic and cultural region defined by the islands and coastal rim of the Caribbean Sea, which includes Spanish-, French-, English-, and Dutch-speaking territories alike and therefore only partially overlaps with "Latin America"; and from the Americas or the Western Hemisphere, the broadest possible geographic terms, encompassing Canada, the United States, and every Caribbean and Latin American nation together.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous

This textbook uses Indigenous peoples as a broad umbrella term for the original inhabitants of the Americas and their descendants, while preferring specific historical names — Mexica, Maya (and, more precisely, the names of individual Maya kingdoms and language groups), Zapotec, Quechua-speaking Andean communities, Aymara, Mapuche, Taíno, Muisca, Guaraní, and many others — whenever those are known and relevant. "Indigenous" is a useful modern category for comparative and analytical purposes, but it can also flatten enormous political, linguistic, and cultural diversity if used as though all Indigenous peoples formed a single undifferentiated group.

Historians must also avoid two related errors: treating Indigenous history as though it ended with European conquest, and treating Indigenous peoples solely as historical victims rather than as political, economic, and cultural actors with their own internal conflicts, alliances, and strategies before, during, and after colonization. This textbook commits explicitly, in every volume, to treating Indigenous peoples as continuous historical actors through the conquest, the colonial period, independence, nation-building, and the contemporary era.

Section 1.5

Where Did the Idea of "Latin America" Come From?

The term "Latin America" is often assumed to be as old as the region's colonial history, but it is not. During the roughly three centuries of Spanish and Portuguese colonial rule, the territories this textbook studies were referred to individually — New Spain, Peru, Brazil, the Captaincy General of Guatemala, and so on — or collectively, when a broader term was needed, as "the Indies," "Spanish America," or "the New World." No widely used term grouped Spanish America, Portuguese Brazil, and French Haiti together as a single cultural-political region during the colonial period itself.

The term "Latin America" began to circulate more widely in the middle of the nineteenth century, in the years following most of the region's independence movements. Its precise origins have been debated among historians, and older accounts often credited French writers and, later, the government of Napoleon III as the term's principal originators, using the idea of a shared "Latin," or Romance-language, civilizational affinity to justify French cultural and political interest in the region, including France's failed intervention in Mexico in the 1860s, examined in Chapter 63. More recent historical scholarship has substantially revised this account. While French writers earlier in the century had discussed a broader "Latin race" or "Latin civilization" linking France to Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking America, the specific political term "Latin America" is documented in 1856 among Spanish American writers themselves — most notably the Chilean Francisco Bilbao, who delivered an address in Paris, and the Colombian José María Torres Caicedo, who composed a poem in Venice that was published in Paris the following year — both writing within months of each other in 1856, in the aftermath of the U.S.–Mexican War and amid concern over renewed European intervention in the hemisphere, discussed further in the Primary Source Window below. Napoleon III's government appropriated this "Latin" regional rhetoric for French imperial purposes only afterward.

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, "Latin America" had become the standard term in much international diplomacy, journalism, and scholarship, gradually displacing more restrictive alternatives such as "Spanish America" in general usage, largely because of its usefulness for including Brazil within a shared regional framework. Twentieth-century political movements, including Latin American nationalism, pan-Latin Americanism, and various regional integration projects, further popularized and politicized the term, using it as a basis for claims about shared economic interests, shared vulnerability to foreign intervention, and shared cultural heritage.

Understanding this history matters for two reasons. First, it explains why "Latin America" does not appear as a meaningful category anywhere in Volume I, and only gradually becomes relevant in the later chapters of Volume III, even though this textbook uses the term as its overall title. Second, it reminds students that regional categories are never simply neutral descriptions of geography; they are historical tools, created by particular people for particular purposes, and always worth examining critically rather than accepting as given.

Section 1.6

The Major Historical Regions

This textbook studies several major sub-regions, each receiving sustained attention across its 115 chapters rather than being subsumed into a single dominant narrative centered on any one country.

Mesoamerica — the cultural-historical region encompassing central and southern Mexico and much of Central America — is the setting for the Olmec, Zapotec, Teotihuacan, Maya, and Mexica (Aztec) civilizations examined in Parts II and III of Volume I, and remains central to Mexican and Central American history throughout the textbook.

The Caribbean — the islands of the Caribbean Sea and their historically linked coastal regions — is the setting for Taíno, Kalinago, and Lucayan societies examined in Chapter 21, for the earliest and most sustained site of European colonization following 1492, for the plantation slave societies of Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico, and for the Haitian Revolution examined in Chapter 50.

The Isthmo-Colombian region — encompassing Panama, Costa Rica, Colombia, and Venezuela — connects Mesoamerican and Andean civilizational spheres and is examined in Chapter 22 and throughout the textbook's treatment of Gran Colombia, Panama, and the northern Andes.

The Andes — the mountain spine running the length of western South America — is the setting for Chavín, Moche, Wari, Tiwanaku, Chimú, and Inca civilizations examined in Part IV of Volume I, and remains central to the history of Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Chile throughout the textbook.

Amazonia — the vast river basin and tropical forest region spanning Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela — receives dedicated attention in Chapter 23 for its pre-Columbian societies and reappears throughout the textbook's treatment of colonization, extraction economies, and twenty-first-century environmental and resource politics.

Brazil, by far the largest country in the region both geographically and demographically, receives sustained, independent treatment throughout every volume, reflecting this textbook's explicit commitment to preventing Brazil from becoming an appendix to Spanish American history.

The Río de la Plata and the Southern Cone — Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, and Chile — are examined as a distinct region with its own Indigenous societies (including the Mapuche, Guaraní, and Charrúa peoples introduced in Chapter 24), its own colonial trajectory, and its own nineteenth- and twentieth-century political history.

Regions adjacent to, but distinct from, Latin America by this textbook's definition — Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, and the English- and Dutch-speaking Caribbean — enter the narrative at the points where their histories directly intersect with their Latin American neighbors, as these Master Instructions specify, rather than receiving independent regional treatment of their own.

Section 1.7

Peoples and Languages of the Region

Latin America is one of the most linguistically diverse regions on earth, a fact easily obscured by the dominance of Spanish and Portuguese in government, media, and international communication. Spanish is the official or dominant language of most of the region's countries, while Portuguese is the official language of Brazil alone — though Brazil's population exceeds that of any single Spanish-speaking Latin American country. Haiti's official languages are French and Haitian Creole, the latter a distinct language that developed among enslaved and free populations in colonial Saint-Domingue, discussed further in Chapter 50.

Beneath and alongside these colonial languages, hundreds of Indigenous languages remain spoken across the region today, in some cases by many millions of people. Nahuatl, the language of the Mexica and numerous other Nahua peoples, remains spoken by well over a million people in Mexico today. Various Maya languages — including Yucatec, K'iche', Kaqchikel, Mam, and dozens of others — remain spoken across Guatemala, southern Mexico, and Belize. Varieties of Quechua, which served as major administrative and lingua-franca languages under the Inca state rather than as a single standardized imperial tongue, remain among the most widely spoken Indigenous language families in the Americas, spoken by millions across Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and neighboring countries. Aymara remains an important language in the Bolivian and Peruvian highlands. Guaraní holds official status alongside Spanish in Paraguay and is spoken by the majority of the Paraguayan population, a rare case of an Indigenous language achieving genuine national co-official status. Mapudungun remains spoken among Mapuche communities in Chile and Argentina. This linguistic diversity is not a historical relic; it reflects the continuous, living presence of Indigenous peoples and cultures throughout Latin American history, a theme this textbook returns to repeatedly.

The region's linguistic map also reflects the African diaspora, immigration, and cultural blending that shaped Latin American societies over five centuries. Afro-Latin American communities in Brazil, the Caribbean, and coastal Spanish America carry linguistic, religious, musical, and culinary traditions traceable to West and Central African origins, transformed through centuries of enslavement, resistance, and cultural exchange. Beginning especially in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, immigration from Italy, Germany, Eastern Europe, the Middle East (particularly Lebanon and Syria), Japan, China, and other parts of Asia added further linguistic and cultural layers to countries such as Argentina, Brazil, Peru, and Mexico, discussed further at relevant points in Volumes III through V.

Section 1.8

Indigenous, European, African, and Asian Latin America

A responsible history of Latin America must establish, from its very first chapter, that the region's societies emerged from the interaction of multiple peoples rather than from the simple expansion of European civilization into an empty or passive landscape. This textbook commits explicitly to this practice throughout, rather than allowing Indigenous and African populations to disappear into a story told primarily from a European vantage point.

Indigenous peoples are not a prelude to Latin American history; they are one of its central, continuous threads, examined on their own terms before conquest in Volume I and as continuous historical actors — through resistance, negotiation, land conflict, labor, migration, and modern political mobilization — in every subsequent volume.

Enslaved Africans and their descendants were forcibly transported to the Americas over more than three centuries; modern estimates based on shipping records, compiled in the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, indicate that approximately 12.5 million people were embarked from Africa and approximately 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage to disembark in the Americas, the great majority of them in Brazil and the Caribbean. This system is examined in depth in Chapter 44 and referenced throughout the textbook's treatment of colonial economies, plantation societies, urban life, resistance, abolition, and modern Afro-Latin American politics and culture. Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other single territory in the Americas, and the African diaspora fundamentally shaped Brazilian, Caribbean, and coastal Spanish American society, religion, music, and cuisine.

European colonizers and their descendants — Spanish, Portuguese, and, in Haiti's case, French — introduced colonial institutions, the Roman Catholic Church, the Spanish and Portuguese languages, and new economic systems, while also intermarrying, sometimes coercively, with Indigenous and African populations to produce the complex racial and social hierarchies examined in Chapter 45.

Asian and Middle Eastern immigrant communities, arriving especially from the mid-nineteenth century onward, became integral to the demographic and cultural fabric of countries such as Peru (with significant Chinese and Japanese immigration), Brazil (with the largest Japanese population outside Japan), and Argentina and Brazil (with significant Lebanese and Syrian immigration), each with their own distinct historical experience discussed at relevant points in later volumes.

Holding all of these populations in view from the outset — rather than treating any single group as the region's default population — reflects the actual historical composition of the societies of the lands later called Latin America that this book examines, from the multiethnic Inca and Mexica empires, which were not themselves "Latin American" in any historically meaningful sense, to the demographically complex modern republics of the twenty-first century.

Section 1.9

Race, Ethnicity, and Identity

Race, ethnicity, and identity are among the most historically important — and most historically variable — categories in Latin American history. This chapter introduces, without exhaustively defining, several concepts that will recur throughout the textbook.

Mestizaje, broadly translated as "racial and cultural mixture," refers to the historical process by which Indigenous, European, and, in many regions, African ancestries and cultural practices blended over generations, producing populations and national identities that many Latin American countries, especially from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries onward, came to celebrate — sometimes selectively and sometimes in ways that obscured ongoing racial inequality — as a defining national characteristic. Mestizaje did not proceed identically or at the same pace across the region, and it did not erase distinct Indigenous or Afro-descendant identities, many of which persisted, and continue to persist, alongside or in tension with national mestizo identities.

Colonial-era racial and caste terminology — including español, criollo, indio, mestizo, mulato, pardo, and other terms examined in Chapter 45 — reflected legal status, ancestry claims, birthplace, and social position in ways that changed considerably across place and time, rather than describing a fixed, scientifically valid biological taxonomy. Modern national censuses and identity categories in Latin American countries today likewise vary considerably in how they define and count Indigenous, Afro-descendant, and mixed-heritage populations, reflecting ongoing political and social debates rather than a single settled definition.

This textbook treats race and ethnicity as historically constructed and changing categories, shaped by law, economics, politics, and culture, rather than as fixed biological facts. It examines how these categories have been used both to build inclusive national identities and to justify exclusion, discrimination, and violence — a tension that recurs from the colonial casta system through nineteenth-century nation-building, twentieth-century populism, and twenty-first-century Indigenous and Afro-descendant political movements.

Section 1.10

Nations versus Historical Regions

A recurring methodological challenge for this textbook, and for students studying it, is the temptation to organize Latin American history around today's national borders — as though "Mexican history," "Peruvian history," and "Chilean history" were self-evident categories stretching back into the ancient past. This temptation must be resisted, especially for the pre-Columbian and early colonial periods examined in Volumes I and II.

The Maya world, at its height, extended across what are today five modern countries: Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador, and Maya civilization was never a single unified political entity but a shifting mosaic of independent and competing city-states sharing linguistic, religious, and cultural traditions. Tawantinsuyu, the Inca imperial state, at its greatest extent stretched across what are today Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Chile, Argentina, and Colombia, administered as a single imperial system with no relationship to the national borders that would eventually divide that same territory centuries later. The Mapuche world consisted of interconnected communities and territories spanning what later became southern Chile and western Argentina, later divided by a national border neither the Mapuche nor the Spanish colonial system had originally drawn in that location. A Maya ruler in 700 CE was not "Mexican" or "Guatemalan." An Inca provincial administrator in the fifteenth century was not "Peruvian" or "Bolivian." Applying modern national identities to these earlier peoples and political systems is not merely imprecise; it actively distorts the political geography within which they actually lived and acted.

This textbook, accordingly, uses historically appropriate regional and political terminology — Mesoamerica, Tawantinsuyu, the Mapuche world, New Spain, the Viceroyalty of Peru, Gran Colombia, and so forth — rather than projecting modern national categories backward, and it explains, at each relevant point, how and when modern national boundaries eventually emerged from these earlier political and cultural geographies.

Section 1.11

Latin America and the Wider Americas

Latin America has never existed in historical isolation. This textbook positions the region within several overlapping wider contexts that recur throughout its chapters.

The Atlantic world connects Latin America to West and Central Africa, through the slave trade and its aftermath, examined in Chapter 26 and Chapter 44, and to Western Europe, through colonization, trade, migration, and intellectual exchange, examined throughout Volumes II and III. The Pacific world connects the region to Asia, through colonial-era trans-Pacific trade routes linking Spanish America to the Philippines and China, and through nineteenth- and twentieth-century migration from China, Japan, and other parts of Asia. The United States enters the narrative repeatedly, from the U.S.–Mexican War and the Spanish-American War through Cold War intervention, the drug trade, and contemporary migration, but, as the Master Instructions governing this textbook specify, the United States must not become the protagonist of Latin American history; Latin American governments, movements, and ordinary people are treated throughout as historical actors with their own interests, strategies, and agency, not merely as objects of U.S. policy.

Canada and the broader English- and Dutch-speaking Caribbean intersect with Latin American history at specific points — migration, trade, and regional diplomacy, for example — without receiving the sustained, independent treatment this textbook gives to the Spanish-, Portuguese-, and French-Haitian-influenced core of the region. Understanding these wider connections from the outset prevents the mistaken impression that Latin America developed in a vacuum, sealed off from the rest of the hemisphere and the world.

Section 1.12

How This Textbook Approaches Latin American History

This textbook is organized into five volumes and 115 chapters, tracing Indigenous civilizations before conquest, the process — not the single event — of conquest and colonization, the formation of colonial societies built on slavery, race, and religion, the era of independence and nineteenth-century nation-building, the twentieth century's revolutions, populist movements, and Cold War dictatorships, and the contemporary era of democratization, organized crime, petroleum politics, migration, and social movements extending through 2026.

Several methodological commitments run through all five volumes. Indigenous history does not end in 1492; it continues, as a central and continuous thread, through every subsequent chapter. Conquest and colonization are treated as related but distinct, prolonged historical processes rather than instantaneous events. Spanish and Portuguese America are compared without treating Brazil as an appendix. Haiti and the Haitian Revolution are treated as integral to the hemisphere's independence history rather than isolated from it. African and Afro-Latin American history continues beyond slavery and emancipation, into republican politics, labor, culture, and contemporary society. Women appear throughout the chronological narrative rather than being confined to a single separate chapter. Organized crime, when it becomes historically significant beginning in Volume V, is examined as a political, economic, and institutional phenomenon rather than a sensationalized true-crime narrative. And events still unfolding as this textbook reaches 2026 are clearly distinguished from historical developments whose outcomes are already established.

This approach reflects a deliberate rejection of two common shortcuts in popular treatments of the region: reducing Latin American history to a Spanish-imperial narrative with a brief Indigenous introduction, and reducing it to a twentieth-century political history that treats everything before 1900 as mere background. Both shortcuts would misrepresent the historical record this textbook sets out to explain in full.

Section 1.13

Why Latin American History Matters

Latin American history is not a regional footnote to world history; it is central to it. The agricultural innovations of Mesoamerican and Andean peoples — including the domestication of maize, the potato, and dozens of other crops — transformed global food systems after 1492. The silver mined at Potosí and Zacatecas financed early modern global trade and helped integrate the Americas, Europe, and Asia into a single commercial system. The Atlantic slave trade, centered substantially on Brazil and the Caribbean, was one of the largest forced migrations in human history and remains foundational to understanding race, labor, and inequality across the Americas. The Haitian Revolution was the only slave revolt of the modern era to overthrow slavery and produce an independent state, and it produced the hemisphere's first independent Black republic. Latin American independence movements, the Mexican and Cuban Revolutions, and the region's Cold War conflicts shaped global ideological competition for over a century. Contemporary Latin America's cocaine and synthetic-drug economies, petroleum politics, migration crises, and environmental significance — particularly the Amazon rainforest's role in global climate systems — place the region at the center of pressing twenty-first-century global concerns.

Understanding this history with the depth, nuance, and regional balance this textbook attempts is not merely an academic exercise. It equips students to engage seriously with one of the most historically significant, culturally rich, and politically consequential regions in the world — a region whose history began tens of thousands of years before the term "Latin America" existed, and whose history will continue to unfold long after this textbook's final chapter.

Primary Source Window

Bilbao and Torres Caicedo Name "Latin America," 1856

Creators: Francisco Bilbao, Chilean writer and political thinker; José María Torres Caicedo, Colombian writer, journalist, and diplomat · Dates: Bilbao delivered Iniciativa de la América as an address in Paris on June 22, 1856. Torres Caicedo composed the poem "Las dos Américas" in Venice on September 26, 1856; it was published some months later, on February 15, 1857, in the Paris-based newspaper El Correo de Ultramar. · Type: Political address (Bilbao) and poem (Torres Caicedo)

Context: Both men were writing in the aftermath of the U.S.–Mexican War (1846–1848), which had cost Mexico roughly half its territory, and amid the activities of U.S. filibusters such as William Walker, who had seized power in Nicaragua earlier in 1856. Both writers were also wary of renewed European ambitions in the hemisphere. Their texts are among the earliest documented uses of "Latin America" or a "Latin race of America" as a deliberate political label for the Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking republics, invoked to express solidarity against both United States expansion and European intervention.

Bilbao, Iniciativa de la América (Paris, 1856): "Pero la América vive, la América latina, sajona e indígena protesta" ("But America lives; Latin, Saxon, and Indigenous America protests").

Torres Caicedo, "Las dos Américas" (composed Venice, 1856; published Paris, 1857): "La raza de la América latina / Al frente tiene la sajona raza" ("The race of Latin America / Has before it the Saxon race").

Analysis: These two short passages show writers reaching independently, within the same year, for a "Latin" label to describe the Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking republics as a unit set against an "Anglo-Saxon" or "Saxon" United States. Read together, they show the term emerging as a political tool of Spanish American self-definition and self-defense, not as a term simply handed down from France. French writers earlier in the century had already been discussing a broader "Latin race" or "Latin civilization" joining France to Spanish and Portuguese America, and that earlier French thought was a real intellectual antecedent; but the government of Napoleon III's later use of "Latin" rhetoric to justify French intervention in Mexico in the 1860s came after, and built upon, this 1856 Spanish American usage rather than originating it. Historians must therefore treat "Latin America" as a term with a genuine Spanish American political origin, later appropriated for different purposes by a European government.

Historical Debate

Is "Latin America" a Useful Historical Category?

The question: Given that "Latin America" is a nineteenth-century political and cultural invention rather than a category the region's own peoples used for most of their history, is it a useful and legitimate framework for organizing a history textbook, or does it risk concealing more than it reveals?

Interpretation A — "Latin America" is a useful and historically grounded category: Proponents of this view argue that, despite its relatively recent origin as a term, the region it describes does share genuine, historically significant common experiences: Iberian or French colonialism, the imposition of Romance languages, Roman Catholic institutions, plantation slavery, racially stratified colonial societies, and, in most cases, nineteenth-century independence movements followed by broadly similar struggles over national consolidation, caudillo politics, and, later, debt, dictatorship, and democratization. On this view, studying these societies together illuminates genuine shared patterns that isolated national histories would obscure.
Interpretation B — "Latin America" risks concealing more than it reveals: Critics of the category point out that it can flatten enormous differences: between Brazil, colonized by Portugal and shaped overwhelmingly by African slavery and later by monarchy rather than early republicanism, and Spanish American republics with quite different colonial administrative traditions; between Haiti's unique history as a slave-revolution republic and the very different independence experiences of Spanish America; between the Indigenous-majority Andean highlands and heavily European-descended Argentina or Uruguay; and between wealthy, urbanized societies and rural, Indigenous-majority regions within the very same country. On this view, "Latin America" can become a lazy shorthand that erases the specific historical experiences of Indigenous peoples, Afro-descendant communities, and individual nations.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same historical record — colonial administrative structures, demographic and economic data, independence-era political writing, and comparative national histories — but differ in how much unifying significance to assign to the region's shared colonial and post-colonial experiences relative to its profound internal diversity.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically rather than doctrinally: it uses "Latin America" as its organizing category because doing so allows sustained comparative attention to conquest, colonization, slavery, independence, and modern political development across an unusually large and consequential part of the world, while committing explicitly, in every chapter, to keeping Indigenous, Afro-descendant, national, and regional diversity visible rather than allowing the broader category to erase it.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which position you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by organizing a textbook around "Latin America" as a single category.

Multiple Perspectives

Naming the Same Land: Perspectives on Region and Identity

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of an Indigenous community member before 1492: Political and cultural identity was organized around one's own nation, city-state, ayllu, or ethnic community — Mexica, K'iche' Maya, Inca, Taíno — rather than around any conception of a shared hemispheric or "Latin American" identity, which would have been entirely unknown.
From the perspective of a colonial-era Spanish or Portuguese administrator: The Americas were understood chiefly through the lens of imperial jurisdiction — viceroyalties, captaincies, audiencias — administered for the benefit of the Spanish or Portuguese Crown, with little conceptual need for a term encompassing all Iberian American territory as a single cultural region distinct from Iberia itself.
From the perspective of a mid-nineteenth-century Spanish American intellectual: "Latin America" offered a way to express solidarity among newly independent, culturally related republics, distinct from both the former Iberian colonial powers and an increasingly powerful and territorially ambitious United States, a view whose diplomatic and territorial context is examined further in Chapter 63.
From the perspective of a twenty-first-century Indigenous or Afro-descendant political activist: National and regional identities such as "Latin American," "Mexican," or "Brazilian" do not automatically capture the distinct political claims, languages, and historical experiences of Indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples, who may identify with their own nation, community, or diaspora identity as strongly as, or more strongly than, with the broader regional or national category.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Idea of "Latin America" from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • The political organization of the region shifted from hundreds of independent Indigenous nations and states, to Iberian and French colonial jurisdictions, to independent nineteenth-century republics, to today's roughly twenty sovereign nations
  • A shared regional identity, "Latin America," emerged only in the mid-nineteenth century, long after most of the demographic and cultural processes that now define the region had already unfolded
  • The population of the region transformed dramatically through Indigenous demographic collapse following conquest, forced African migration under slavery, and later waves of European, Asian, and Middle Eastern immigration
  • Modern political and cultural movements have repeatedly reinterpreted "Latin America" to serve pan-regional solidarity, nationalist consolidation, revolutionary internationalism, and, more recently, Indigenous and Afro-descendant rights claims

What Stayed the Same

  • Extraordinary linguistic, ethnic, and regional diversity has remained a defining feature of the region across every period examined in this textbook
  • Indigenous peoples have remained continuous historical actors, despite dramatic demographic and political change, from the pre-Columbian era to the present
  • Debates over who belongs within the region and what that belonging should mean have recurred across many periods, from colonial-era debates over Indigenous and African legal status to contemporary debates over immigration, Indigenous rights, and national identity
  • The region's internal diversity — between Mesoamerica and the Andes, between Brazil and Spanish America, between the Caribbean and the Southern Cone — has remained a durable feature rather than dissolving into uniformity

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "Latin America" as an ancient, unchanging essence, or treating it as nothing more than a modern political invention with no genuine historical substance.

Cause and Effect

Why Confusion about the Boundaries of Latin America Persists

Long-term causes

The historical overlap between Spanish and Portuguese colonization and the broader geographic space of Central and South America, and the fact that most popular Western engagement with the region has occurred through the lens of geography or language rather than detailed colonial or national history.

Short-term causes

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century media, political, and economic coverage of the region frequently uses "Latin America," "South America," and "Hispanic" interchangeably for narrative convenience, reinforcing imprecise public usage.

Effect

Brazil, Haiti, Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean, and non-Spanish-speaking South American territories are frequently omitted, mislabeled, or rendered invisible in popular understanding of "Latin America."

This textbook's response

Consistent, careful use of "Latin America," "Hispanic America," "Spanish America," "Ibero-America," "South America," and "the Caribbean" as distinct terms throughout all 115 chapters, with explicit and sustained attention to Brazil, Haiti, Central America, and the Caribbean.

Historical complexity: Imprecise terminology is not merely a modern journalistic problem; it has roots in the very origin of "Latin America" as a politically motivated nineteenth-century term rather than a neutral geographic description.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Ordinary Identity Belongs in This Chapter

Definitions of "Latin America," "Indigenous," "mestizo," and national identity are not only matters for scholars and diplomats; they shape how ordinary people have experienced daily life, legal status, and belonging across the centuries this textbook studies. A Quechua-speaking farmer in the Andean highlands, an Afro-Brazilian dockworker in Salvador, a Haitian Creole-speaking market vendor in Port-au-Prince, and a Spanish-speaking shopkeeper in Buenos Aires each navigate daily social, legal, and economic life shaped by their language, ancestry, region, class, and national citizenship — categories that often matter more in practice than any single overarching label such as "Latin American."

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society and everyday life examine how ordinary people across the region experienced markets, neighborhoods, religious festivals, family and community obligation, labor, taxation, and, in the modern era, citizenship and national identity. Because most surviving pre-modern written sources were produced by a literate colonial or national elite, historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own — Indigenous farmers, enslaved and free people of African descent, women, and the rural and urban poor — while also drawing on archaeology, oral tradition, and material culture to reconstruct their experiences more fully.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced "Latin America" as this textbook's organizing subject while carefully distinguishing it from several related but distinct categories with which it is often, and mistakenly, conflated: South America, Spanish America, Hispanic America, and Ibero-America. Latin America, as this textbook defines and studies it, is a region formed principally through the historical intersection of Indigenous American peoples, Iberian and French colonization, the Romance languages that colonization carried and transformed, and the forced migration of millions of enslaved Africans, consolidated politically and culturally through independence and nineteenth-century nation-building.

The chapter has emphasized that the very term "Latin America" is a nineteenth-century political and intellectual invention, documented in 1856 among Spanish American writers such as Francisco Bilbao and José María Torres Caicedo responding to the growing power of the United States, building on an earlier French antecedent idea of "Latin" civilizational affinity that the government of Napoleon III later appropriated for its own imperial purposes — rather than a category that any pre-Columbian, colonial-era, or even early independence-era person would have recognized or used. It has explained why "Latin America" includes Mexico and Central America (part of North America geographically) while excluding Guyana and Suriname (part of South America geographically but shaped by British and Dutch rather than Iberian or French colonial history), and why Haiti and Brazil must remain central to the narrative rather than being treated as exceptions or appendices.

Students have been introduced to the extraordinary linguistic diversity of the region, from Spanish and Portuguese to French and Haitian Creole to the hundreds of Indigenous languages — including Nahuatl, Maya languages, Quechua, Aymara, Guaraní, and Mapudungun — spoken by millions of people today. The chapter has also introduced the region's Indigenous, African, European, and, later, Asian and Middle Eastern populations as jointly constitutive of Latin American history, rather than presenting any single population as the region's default or "normal" inhabitants.

The chapter established the textbook's geographic scope — Mesoamerica, the Caribbean, the Isthmo-Colombian region, the Andes, Amazonia, Brazil, and the Río de la Plata and Southern Cone — and explained why each region receives sustained, independent treatment rather than being absorbed into a single dominant narrative centered on Mexico or the Spanish Andes. It also established the crucial methodological principle that modern national borders cannot be projected backward onto Indigenous political and cultural worlds such as the Maya region, Tawantinsuyu, or the Mapuche world, each of which crossed the boundaries of multiple modern nations.

Finally, the chapter addressed the usefulness and limitations of "Latin America" as a historical category directly, through the Historical Debate feature, concluding that the category is most defensible when its internal diversity — between Brazil and Spanish America, between Haiti and the rest of the Caribbean, between Indigenous-majority and heavily European-descended societies — is kept visible rather than flattened into a single uniform narrative.

These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following 114 chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of Indigenous civilizations, conquest and colonization, independence and nation-building, revolution and Cold War conflict, and the contemporary Latin American order through 2026.

Chapter 1 has asked what region this textbook studies. Chapter 2 turns to an equally fundamental question: how did human beings first arrive in and populate that region, tens of thousands of years before any of the political and cultural categories introduced in this chapter existed at all?

Review Materials

Key Terms

Latin America
A region encompassing Mexico, Central America, most of the Caribbean, and most of South America, defined chiefly by the historical spread of Spanish, Portuguese, or French colonial rule and the Romance languages that followed; a term that emerged in the mid-nineteenth century rather than existing throughout the region's history.
Hispanic America
The Spanish-speaking countries of the Americas, excluding Brazil and Haiti.
Spanish America
A term for the former Spanish colonial territories in the Americas, often used with a historical or colonial-era connotation.
Ibero-America
Hispanic America together with Brazil, reflecting shared Iberian Peninsula colonial origin; excludes Haiti.
Mesoamerica
The cultural-historical region of central and southern Mexico and much of Central America, home to the Olmec, Maya, Mexica, and other major civilizations.
Central America
The isthmus connecting North and South America, comprising Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama.
Caribbean
The island region and coastal rim of the Caribbean Sea, including Spanish-, French-, English-, and Dutch-speaking territories; only partially overlaps with "Latin America."
South America
The southern continent of the Americas; not synonymous with "Latin America," since it excludes Mexico and Central America while including Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana.
Andes
The mountain range running the length of western South America, home to Chavín, Moche, Wari, Tiwanaku, Chimú, and Inca civilizations.
Amazonia
The Amazon River basin and surrounding tropical forest region, spanning Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela.
Southern Cone
Chile, Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay, extending toward Patagonia.
Indigenous
A broad umbrella term for the original inhabitants of the Americas and their descendants; this textbook prefers specific historical and ethnic names whenever known.
Afro-Latin American
Communities descended from enslaved Africans forcibly brought to the Americas, foundational to Brazilian, Caribbean, and coastal Spanish American history and culture.
Mestizaje
The historical process of Indigenous, European, and, in many regions, African ancestral and cultural blending, celebrated unevenly as a national characteristic in many Latin American countries.
Colonialism
The system by which a foreign power establishes and maintains political, economic, and social control over a territory and its inhabitants.
Empire
A large political entity, often multiethnic, in which a central power governs diverse subject territories and peoples.
Nation-state
A modern political entity claiming sovereignty over a defined territory and population, generally understood as an outgrowth of independence-era and nineteenth-century political developments in Latin America.
Ethnicity
A historically changing form of group identity shaped by language, culture, ancestry claims, and political classification, rather than a fixed biological category.
Race
A socially and historically constructed category used to classify people, often based on perceived ancestry or physical characteristics, whose meaning and application have varied considerably across Latin American history.
Diaspora
A population dispersed from its original homeland, such as the African diaspora produced by the Atlantic slave trade.
Romance language
A language descended from Latin, including Spanish, Portuguese, and French, the three principal colonial languages of Latin America.
Cultural region
A geographic area defined by shared cultural, linguistic, or historical characteristics rather than strict political borders.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of Latin American history have changed over time and why.
Mesoamerican civilizations
The Olmec, Zapotec, Teotihuacan, Maya, Mexica, and other complex societies of ancient Mexico and Central America, examined beginning in Part II.
Tawantinsuyu
The Indigenous name for the Inca imperial state, examined in Part IV.
Casta
The colonial system of racial and social classification examined in Chapter 45; a historically specific and changing set of categories rather than a fixed taxonomy.
Primary source
A document, object, or record created at the time being studied, by a participant or contemporary witness.
Secondary source
A later interpretation of the past, typically by a historian, drawing on primary sources.
Minority community
A religious, ethnic, or linguistic group that is not part of the majority population of a given state or region.
Historical memory
The way communities and states collectively remember and use the past to construct identity and meaning in the present.
Regional diversity
The recognition that Latin America varies significantly by sub-region — Mesoamerica, the Caribbean, the Andes, Amazonia, Brazil, and the Southern Cone — rather than forming a single uniform society.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces — distinguishing Latin America from South America, Hispanic America, and Ibero-America; recognizing "Latin America" as a nineteenth-century historical construction rather than a timeless category; keeping Indigenous, African, European, and Asian populations visible together rather than privileging any single group; and refusing to project modern national borders backward onto earlier Indigenous political worlds — are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

Latin America's history is one of the most linguistically, ethnically, and regionally diverse historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included ancient civilizations that developed for thousands of years before any European presence; a catastrophic and transformative era of conquest and colonization; centuries of colonial society built on slavery, race, and religion; a revolutionary Haitian republic born from the hemisphere's only successful large-scale slave uprising; a prolonged and uneven process of independence and nation-building; a twentieth century shaped by revolution, populism, dictatorship, and Cold War conflict; and a contemporary moment shaped by democratization, organized crime, petroleum politics, migration, and social movements extending through 2026. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to Latin America's historical significance — it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that "Latin America" is a useful modern historical category rather than a timeless civilization, that Indigenous peoples remain continuous historical actors rather than a prelude to later history, and that the region's internal diversity — between Mesoamerica and the Andes, between Brazil and Spanish America, between the Caribbean and the Southern Cone — is a feature to be preserved rather than a complication to be smoothed over, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the earliest human settlement of the Americas, examined beginning in Chapter 2, through the unresolved questions of contemporary Venezuela and the wider region examined in Chapter 115. It is a story of many regions, many peoples, and many historical experiences, joined by a shared but uneven colonial past and an evolving regional identity — not a single unbroken thread and not a predetermined outcome. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
