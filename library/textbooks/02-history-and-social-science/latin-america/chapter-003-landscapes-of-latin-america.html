Landscapes of Latin America

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Hemisphere of Extremes

Within a single day's walk in the central Andes, a traveler can pass from a river valley warm enough to ripen maize, through terraced slopes suited to potatoes and quinoa, into high grasslands where only hardy tubers and grazing camelids survive, and finally to bare rock and permanent snow above the limit of any cultivated crop at all. Few inhabited regions on Earth compress so many distinct environments into so short a vertical distance — and no textbook of Latin American history can proceed responsibly without understanding what this extraordinary environmental range meant for the peoples who built their lives, their farms, their cities, and their empires within it.

Chapter 1 established that "Latin America" is a modern historical category applied to a hemisphere of far older and more varied societies. Chapter 2 traced how the first human populations spread across that hemisphere over tens of thousands of years, adapting as they moved to environments no earlier human population had ever encountered. This chapter turns to the environments themselves — the landscapes, climates, and ecological zones that those populations settled, transformed, and were in turn shaped by. It does so not as a detour into physical geography for its own sake, but because the environmental diversity of the lands this textbook studies is one of the central facts historians must understand before any of the civilizations, conquests, colonial economies, or modern political struggles examined in later chapters can be explained with any precision.

Consider the scale of the variation. The volcanic highlands of central Mexico, where cool nights and fertile volcanic soil supported some of the densest populations in the pre-Columbian world, share a hemisphere with the humid, low-lying jungles of the Yucatán Peninsula, where the ancient Maya built cities without the benefit of major rivers, relying instead on rainwater catchment and seasonal wetlands. The narrow, mountainous isthmus of Central America, barely more than a hundred miles wide at its narrowest points, separates two oceans and two very different ecological worlds within a single day's travel. The islands of the Caribbean, though small in land area compared with the American mainland, encompass everything from semi-arid scrubland to dense rainforest, and their surrounding waters connected island societies to one another and to the mainland long before any European ship arrived. The Andes rise higher and run longer than almost any other mountain system on Earth, creating, as the chapter's opening paragraph describes, a vertical mosaic of climates within remarkably short horizontal distances. The Amazon Basin, by contrast, is defined not by elevation but by the sheer scale of its river system and the biological richness of its forest canopy, a landscape that shaped human settlement in ways historians and archaeologists are still working to understand. Brazil's Atlantic coastline and vast interior plateau, the open grasslands of the pampas surrounding the Río de la Plata, and the cold, wind-scoured plains of Patagonia stretching toward the tip of South America complete a picture of environmental diversity unmatched by almost any comparably sized region in world history.

This chapter's central argument is that environment mattered enormously to Latin American history without determining it. Geography set possibilities and imposed constraints — it made certain crops, settlement patterns, trade networks, and political structures more or less likely — but it never dictated a single inevitable outcome. Two societies occupying broadly similar environments could develop very differently, and human beings repeatedly reshaped their environments through irrigation, terracing, forest management, soil improvement, and selective breeding of plants and animals, so that the "natural" landscapes described in this chapter were, in many cases, already substantially the product of earlier human activity by the time later societies encountered them. Historians call the temptation to treat geography as the sole or primary explanation for historical outcomes "environmental determinism," and this chapter explicitly rejects that framework while still insisting that environment cannot be ignored.

Understanding these landscapes also prepares students for a recurring theme of this textbook: that no single region can stand in for the whole of Latin America. Mesoamerica's civilizations developed in dialogue with volcanic highlands and tropical lowlands quite different from the high desert and mountain environments that shaped Andean civilization; Amazonia's societies adapted to river-based settlement and forest management in ways that neither the Mesoamerican nor Andean model can explain; and the temperate grasslands of the Southern Cone supported yet another distinct set of human adaptations. Treating any one of these environments as the "typical" Latin American landscape would falsify the historical record from the outset.

This chapter proceeds region by region — Mesoamerica, Central America, the Caribbean, the Andes, Amazonia, Brazil, the Río de la Plata and Southern Cone, and Patagonia — before turning to the rivers, ocean currents, and climate systems that connected these regions to one another and to the wider Atlantic and Pacific worlds. Along the way, it introduces vocabulary, evidence, and reasoning skills — particularly the distinction between environmental influence and environmental determinism — that will recur throughout the remainder of Volume I, as the specific civilizations that developed within each of these landscapes receive their own sustained treatment in the chapters ahead.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold in mind a caution that will recur throughout this textbook: the landscapes described here are described as they existed, so far as historians and scientists can reconstruct them, in the centuries before and during the periods this textbook examines. Many of these landscapes have since been dramatically altered — by colonial agriculture, ranching, mining, deforestation, urbanization, and, in the twenty-first century, by climate change and industrial development, subjects examined in later volumes. Reading this chapter as a snapshot of a particular, and in some respects vanished, environmental past is essential to using it responsibly.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the major geographic and ecological regions of Latin America, including Mesoamerica, Central America, the Caribbean, the Andes, Amazonia, Brazil, the Río de la Plata and Southern Cone, and Patagonia.
  2. Describe the principal environmental features — climate, elevation, soil, water, and vegetation — that distinguish each major region.
  3. Explain the concept of ecological verticality in the Andes and its significance for Andean agriculture and society.
  4. Analyze how environment shaped, without determining, patterns of settlement, agriculture, and political organization across different regions.
  5. Evaluate the concept of environmental determinism and explain why this textbook rejects it as a primary historical explanation.
  6. Distinguish "natural" landscapes from landscapes substantially shaped by earlier human activity, including agricultural terracing, irrigation, and forest management.
  7. Explain the historical importance of major river systems, ocean currents, and climate patterns for regional and interregional connection.
  8. Assess the strengths and limitations of archaeological, ecological, and documentary evidence used to reconstruct historical landscapes.
  9. Connect the environmental diversity introduced in this chapter to the distinct civilizational trajectories examined throughout the remainder of Volume I.
  10. Contextualize how colonial and modern environmental change has transformed many of the landscapes described in this chapter.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 explained that the earliest human populations entering the Americas encountered an enormous range of environments as they spread from the Arctic fringes of Beringia toward the southern tip of South America, and that this environmental range played a significant role in the regional diversification of technologies, subsistence strategies, and, eventually, cultures described in Section 2.9. This chapter examines that environmental range in its own right, as the essential backdrop against which the civilizations of Volume I — the Olmec, Maya, Mexica, Inca, Taíno, Muisca, Mapuche, Guaraní, and dozens of other peoples — developed their distinctive political, economic, and cultural systems.

Latin America's environmental diversity results chiefly from three interacting factors: latitude, elevation, and the influence of surrounding oceans. The region spans an extraordinary range of latitude, from the tropical zones straddling the equator through temperate regions in both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres to the cold, wind-battered landscapes near Cape Horn — meaning that, unlike most world regions, Latin America contains close analogues of nearly every major climate type found on Earth, sometimes within a single country. Elevation compounds this latitudinal range dramatically: the Andes, running nearly the entire length of western South America, and the volcanic highlands of Mexico and Central America mean that altitude, not merely latitude, determines climate and vegetation across much of the region, producing the dramatic vertical zonation described later in this chapter. Finally, the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans, along with the Caribbean Sea, shape rainfall patterns, ocean currents, and coastal ecosystems in ways that have mattered enormously for human settlement, from the cold, nutrient-rich Humboldt Current off the western coast of South America to the warm currents that helped carry early sailing vessels across the Caribbean and along the Brazilian coast.

Historians studying this environmental diversity must draw on several distinct kinds of evidence. Physical and biological geography — the modern scientific description of climate, soil, vegetation, and hydrology — provides a starting point, but it describes landscapes as they exist today, which are not identical to the landscapes of a thousand or five thousand years ago. Paleoecology and paleoclimatology, which reconstruct past environments through evidence such as pollen cores, sediment layers, tree rings, and ice cores, allow scientists to estimate how climate and vegetation have changed over time. Archaeology contributes evidence about how past societies actually used and modified their environments — through irrigation canals, agricultural terraces, raised fields, managed forests, and constructed wetlands — showing that many landscapes historians once assumed were simply "natural" were in fact substantially shaped by centuries or millennia of human activity. Colonial-era and early modern written descriptions, such as the account by the Spanish chronicler Pedro Cieza de León examined later in this chapter, offer valuable but imperfect evidence, since such observers often misunderstood or misrepresented what they saw, filtered through their own cultural expectations and colonial purposes.

This chapter organizes its treatment of Latin American geography by major historical-ecological region rather than by modern national boundaries, consistent with the methodological principle established in Chapter 1 that modern national borders should not be projected backward onto earlier periods. Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, the Caribbean, and the other regions examined below are geographic and ecological categories that predate, and in important respects do not correspond neatly to, the political map of contemporary Latin America.

Section 3.1

Environment as a Historical Actor

Every civilization examined in this textbook developed within a specific set of environmental conditions — climate, soil, elevation, water availability, and access to particular plants, animals, and mineral resources — that shaped what was possible for the people living there. A society settled along a great river with fertile floodplain soil faced different agricultural possibilities than a society settled on a high, arid plateau; a society with access to the sea could develop maritime trade and fishing in ways unavailable to a landlocked society; a society in a temperate highland climate faced different disease environments than a society in a humid tropical lowland. None of this means that environment alone explains why one society became a centralized empire and another remained a collection of smaller communities, or why one society developed intensive irrigated agriculture and another relied on shifting cultivation. Human choices, political institutions, warfare, trade networks, religious systems, and simple historical contingency all mattered as much as, and often more than, environmental circumstance.

This chapter treats environment as what historians sometimes call a "structuring condition" rather than a determining cause: it shaped the range of realistic possibilities available to a given society without dictating which possibility that society would actually pursue or how successfully it would pursue it. Two societies facing broadly similar environmental conditions frequently developed in different directions, and successful societies frequently overcame or substantially modified environmental constraints that might once have seemed decisive — through irrigation, terracing, plant and animal domestication, storage technology, and long-distance trade that brought resources from environments a given community did not itself occupy.

It is equally important to recognize that the landscapes described in the remainder of this chapter were not simply "given" by nature and then encountered by human societies as passive backdrops. By the time later civilizations examined in this textbook reached their height, many of the environments they occupied had already been substantially reshaped by centuries or millennia of earlier human activity — through cleared and managed forests, terraced hillsides, irrigated valleys, domesticated crop varieties selected over many generations, and, in some regions, deliberately engineered wetlands and raised agricultural fields. The distinction between "natural" and "human-made" landscape is, in much of Latin America, considerably less clear than it might first appear, a theme this chapter returns to throughout and examines directly in Section 3.11.

Section 3.2

Mesoamerica: Highlands, Basins, and Volcanic Coasts

Mesoamerica — the cultural-ecological region encompassing central and southern Mexico and much of Central America — combines an unusually varied set of environments within a relatively compact geographic space. At its core lies a volcanic highland belt running roughly east to west across central Mexico, punctuated by active and dormant volcanoes, including Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl near the Basin of Mexico, whose eruptions over many thousands of years deposited the fertile volcanic soils that supported some of the densest agricultural populations in the pre-Columbian Americas. The Basin of Mexico itself, a large highland valley at roughly 2,200 meters above sea level, once contained a system of interconnected lakes — Texcoco, Xochimilco, Chalco, and others — around and within which the great city of Tenochtitlan and its neighboring city-states would eventually develop, examined in Part III of this volume. Within these lake environments, Mesoamerican farmers eventually constructed chinampas, small raised and highly fertile agricultural plots built up from lakebed mud and vegetation; archaeological evidence places the earliest confirmed chinampas in the Basin of Mexico in the Early Postclassic period, roughly 900 to 1200 CE, with the system expanding substantially in the centuries that followed rather than representing an ancient, continuously used technology stretching back to the region's earliest agricultural settlement.

Surrounding this highland core, Mesoamerica's terrain descends toward tropical coastal lowlands on both the Gulf of Mexico and Pacific sides, each with its own distinct ecology. The Gulf Coast lowlands, home to the Olmec civilization examined in Chapter 6, combine river floodplains, seasonal wetlands, and dense tropical vegetation, offering rich agricultural potential but also posing challenges from flooding, humidity, and disease. The Pacific coastal plain, narrower and drier in many stretches, supported different agricultural and settlement patterns. To the south and east, the Yucatán Peninsula presents a strikingly different environment still: a low, flat limestone platform with little surface water, where the ancient Maya, examined in Chapter 9 and Chapter 12, developed sophisticated systems for capturing and storing rainwater in natural sinkholes called cenotes and constructed reservoirs, since the porous limestone bedrock allows rainfall to drain away too quickly for rivers or lakes to form across much of the peninsula's interior.

Highland Mesoamerica also includes the mountainous valleys of Oaxaca, home to Zapotec and later Mixtec civilization examined in Chapter 7 and Chapter 11, and the volcanic highlands of Guatemala and El Salvador, home to numerous Maya kingdoms examined in Chapter 12. This highland-lowland contrast — cooler, drier highland basins suited to maize, beans, and squash cultivation alongside warmer, wetter lowland zones suited to cacao, cotton, and other tropical crops — created strong incentives for long-distance trade between ecological zones, a pattern that recurs throughout Mesoamerican history and that historians and archaeologists trace through the exchange of obsidian, jade, cacao, feathers, cotton, and other goods examined in later chapters.

Section 3.3

The Isthmus: Central America Between Two Oceans

South and east of the Mesoamerican highland core, the landmass connecting North and South America narrows into the isthmus of Central America, comprising the modern territories of Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama. It is important to distinguish this geographic region from Mesoamerica as a historical culture area introduced in Section 3.2: Mesoamerica's cultural and archaeological boundary has never coincided neatly with the geographic isthmus, and its extent shifted over time. In the conventional scholarly usage this textbook follows, Mesoamerica reaches southeastward to include Belize, Guatemala, and El Salvador and parts of Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica, while the remainder of lower Central America — much of Nicaragua's Caribbean lowlands, most of Costa Rica, and Panama — falls outside Mesoamerica proper and instead connects historically to the Isthmo-Colombian world examined in Chapter 22. This isthmus is defined above all by its narrowness relative to the two oceans it separates: at its narrowest point in Panama, less than fifty miles separate the Pacific Ocean from the Caribbean Sea, a geographic fact that would take on enormous historical significance far beyond the period examined in this volume, discussed in Chapter 70 in connection with the construction of the Panama Canal.

Central America's terrain is dominated by a volcanic mountain spine running roughly parallel to the Pacific coast, producing fertile highland basins similar in character to those of highland Mesoamerica, alongside lower-lying Caribbean coastal plains that are generally wetter and more densely forested, though still home to distinct Indigenous peoples and trade networks examined in Chapter 22 and Chapter 34. The isthmus's mountainous, forested, and in places swampy terrain posed real challenges for overland travel and for the kind of large-scale territorial consolidation achieved in the Basin of Mexico or the central Andes, but terrain alone does not explain the region's political variety. Lower Central America, and Panama in particular, supported a genuinely varied political landscape, including ranked chiefdoms with substantial regional authority, complex ceremonial and craft-production centers, and smaller, more loosely organized communities existing alongside one another. Terrain, demography, trade relationships, and specific historical circumstances all interacted to produce this variety, and this chapter treats environment as one contributing constraint among several rather than as the explanation for why Central America's Indigenous polities generally operated at a smaller territorial scale than the largest Mesoamerican or Andean states.

Central America's position between two oceans and two continents also made it, from very early periods, a corridor for the movement of peoples, plants, animals, and cultural influences between Mesoamerica and South America, a role examined further in Chapter 22's treatment of the Isthmo-Colombian world connecting Central America to Colombia and Venezuela.

Section 3.4

The Caribbean Basin

The Caribbean Sea and its surrounding islands constitute a distinct geographic and historical region rather than a simple extension of either Mesoamerica or South America. The Caribbean's islands range enormously in size, from the large islands of Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico — collectively known as the Greater Antilles — to the much smaller volcanic and coral islands of the Lesser Antilles stretching in an arc toward the coast of South America. Environmental conditions vary considerably across this island world: parts of Cuba and Hispaniola support extensive fertile lowlands suited to intensive agriculture, while other islands, particularly in the drier Lesser Antilles and parts of the Bahamian archipelago, offer more limited freshwater and thinner soils.

What unifies the Caribbean as a historical region is not environmental uniformity but the connective power of the surrounding sea itself. Long before European ships arrived, Indigenous peoples of the Caribbean — including the Taíno, Kalinago, and Lucayan societies examined in Chapter 21 — traveled between islands and to and from the South American mainland using seagoing canoes, exchanging goods, people, and ideas across distances that would otherwise have been formidable barriers. The Caribbean Sea's currents and prevailing winds, which later carried European sailing ships along broadly predictable routes, had already shaped patterns of Indigenous maritime travel for centuries. This maritime connectivity, rather than any single shared terrestrial environment, is what makes it useful for historians to treat the Caribbean as a coherent regional unit.

The Caribbean's climate is predominantly tropical, with a pronounced hurricane season affecting much of the region during the later summer and autumn months — a recurring environmental hazard that shaped settlement patterns, agriculture, and, in later centuries, colonial plantation economies examined in Volume II. The region's relatively limited land area, combined with its rich marine resources and its position astride major sea routes, meant that Caribbean societies, both before and after European colonization, tended to be oriented toward the sea to a degree not always true of mainland societies with access to larger continental interiors.

Section 3.5

The Andes: A Vertical World

The Andes mountain system, running more than four thousand miles along the western edge of South America from Venezuela and Colombia through Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina, is the longest continental mountain range on Earth and among the highest, with numerous peaks exceeding six thousand meters. For the societies examined in Part IV of this volume — including the civilizations centered at Chavín, Wari, Tiwanaku, Chimú, and eventually the Inca imperial state, or Tawantinsuyu — the Andes' defining environmental feature was not simply altitude itself but the phenomenon historians and geographers call ecological verticality: the tendency of climate, vegetation, and agricultural possibility to change dramatically over very short vertical distances, producing a series of distinct ecological zones stacked one above another rather than spread out across a horizontal landscape.

Modern geographers describe this pattern using an altitudinal classification scheme — tierra caliente for the warm, often humid lowland and mid-elevation zones suitable for maize, coca, and fruit cultivation; tierra templada for the intermediate, temperate elevations suited to a wider range of grains and tubers; and tierra fría for the cold high-altitude zones, including the puna grasslands used chiefly for camelid grazing and hardy tuber cultivation. This vocabulary, applied broadly across tropical Latin America by geographers studying both the Andes and highland Mesoamerica, is a useful descriptive tool, but students should not assume it reflects a single vocabulary that Andean societies themselves used before or during Inca rule; specific Andean communities and language groups had their own, more localized ways of naming and distinguishing the ecological zones they occupied and used. The Andean altiplano, a high plateau spanning parts of modern Peru, Bolivia, and Chile at elevations often exceeding 3,600 meters, represents an extreme version of the cold, high-altitude tierra fría environment, supporting the world's highest-altitude complex civilizations, including Tiwanaku, examined in Chapter 18.

This vertical arrangement of ecological zones is a geographic fact distinct from, though closely related to, a specific scholarly model of Andean economic organization that historians and anthropologists must be careful not to conflate with it. The anthropologist John Murra, drawing especially on sixteenth-century Spanish colonial administrative surveys called visitas, proposed that some Andean ethnic groups sought direct access to multiple vertical ecological zones simultaneously by maintaining settlements or access rights at several different elevations at once — a model generally known as the "vertical archipelago." Later research building on and testing Murra's model has shown that direct multi-zone settlement was one strategy among several that Andean communities used across different periods and regions; barter, caravan-based exchange, seasonal labor migration, and, in some places and periods, market-like exchange also connected communities at different elevations, and the relative importance of each strategy varied considerably rather than following one uniform Andes-wide pattern. Nor should this pattern be treated as a strict opposite to Mesoamerican long-distance trade discussed in Section 3.2; Mesoamerican societies also pursued various forms of direct access to resource-producing zones, and Andean communities also engaged extensively in trade and exchange beyond direct settlement. The vertical archipelago model, examined further for the period of Inca rule in Chapter 20, remains a valuable and influential framework, but it describes one documented Andean strategy for managing ecological verticality rather than the sole or inevitable response any society would make to that environmental condition.

The Andes also profoundly shaped Andean agriculture and diet. Andean farmers domesticated and cultivated an unusually wide range of crops adapted to different elevations, including maize at lower and middle elevations, and, at higher and colder elevations where maize could not reliably ripen, an extraordinary diversity of potato varieties along with other high-altitude tubers such as oca and ulluco, and the grain quinoa. At the highest elevations, camelid herding — llamas for transport and meat, alpacas primarily for their fiber — provided a form of subsistence and wealth suited to environments too cold and too high for most cultivated crops. This combination of vertically zoned agriculture and camelid pastoralism gave Andean societies a distinctive subsistence base unlike that of any other major civilizational region examined in this textbook.

Section 3.6

Amazonia and the Tropical Lowlands

East of the Andes, the terrain descends into the vast Amazon Basin, one of the largest tropical forest and river systems on Earth, spanning portions of modern Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela and drained by the Amazon River and its hundreds of tributaries. For much of the twentieth century, many archaeologists and historians assumed that Amazonia's dense forest cover, acidic soils, and challenging tropical climate could support only small, mobile, and relatively simple societies, and that the region therefore played a marginal role in the broader story of pre-Columbian American civilization. Archaeological research conducted especially from the late twentieth century onward has substantially revised this assumption, examined in depth in Chapter 23.

Researchers have identified extensive earthworks, raised agricultural fields, managed forest patches containing unusually high concentrations of economically useful tree species, and large settlement complexes at numerous sites across the Amazon Basin, indicating that some Amazonian societies achieved considerably larger populations and more substantial environmental modification than earlier scholarship had assumed. Older models proposed that dense settlement was largely confined to the Amazon's main channel and major tributaries, where seasonal flooding deposits nutrient-rich sediment, while the more acidic soils of the interfluvial, or terra firme, forest away from major rivers supported only sparse populations. Subsequent research has substantially revised this picture: archaeological survey and, increasingly, airborne laser-scanning (LiDAR) technology capable of detecting earthworks hidden beneath forest canopy have identified substantial pre-Columbian settlement in terra firme zones once assumed to have been sparsely inhabited. A large 2026 study of southwestern Amazonia, combining LiDAR-based survey of sampled areas with satellite data, prior discoveries, and extrapolation across the broader Aquiry cultural area, estimated that the region contained more than twenty thousand precolonial earthworks and supported a population of roughly one and a quarter to three million people around 100–300 CE, within an area representing only a small fraction of the greater Amazon Basin — findings that, as of this textbook's writing, are reshaping scholarly estimates of how many people the Amazonian environment supported and how thoroughly its landscape had been engineered well over a thousand years before European contact. Amazonian settlement remains closely tied to specific hydrological and soil conditions rather than uniform across the entire forest basin, but the simple equation of "riverbank equals dense population" and "interfluvial forest equals sparse population" no longer holds as a general rule. Amazonian societies also produced distinctive dark, fertile anthropogenic soils, sometimes called terra preta, through long-term deposits of charcoal, organic waste, and other human-generated material; while debate continues over the precise processes involved at individual sites, the weight of archaeological and geochemical evidence supports a substantially human origin for these soils, a striking example, examined further in Section 3.11, of how human activity actively improved rather than merely exploited a challenging natural environment.

Amazonia's rivers functioned as the region's primary transportation network, much as the surrounding sea did for Caribbean societies, connecting communities across vast distances that would have been extremely difficult to traverse overland through dense forest. This riverine orientation, combined with the region's exceptional biological diversity, shaped Amazonian subsistence strategies toward a combination of fishing, hunting, forest product gathering, and, increasingly recognized by current archaeological research, deliberate cultivation and forest management, rather than toward the intensive irrigated field agriculture that characterized many highland Mesoamerican and Andean societies.

Section 3.7

Brazil's Coast, Plateau, and Interior

Brazil, by far the largest country in Latin America in both area and population and a region receiving sustained independent attention throughout this textbook, encompasses a genuinely wide range of distinct environmental zones. Brazilian and international geographers generally recognize six major terrestrial biomes within the country: Amazônia, the tropical forest region discussed in Section 3.6; the Atlantic Forest (Mata Atlântica), a once-vast band of humid coastal and near-coastal forest running along much of Brazil's eastern seaboard, historically important for early Portuguese settlement and the sugar plantation economy examined in Chapter 43; the Cerrado, an extensive and ecologically diverse savanna biome covering much of the interior plateau, or planalto, of central Brazil; the Caatinga, a distinctive semi-arid scrubland biome covering much of Brazil's northeastern interior and closely associated with the drought-prone region popularly known as the sertão; the Pantanal, one of the largest tropical wetland systems in the world, located in west-central Brazil; and the Pampa, the temperate grassland biome in Brazil's far south that merges into the broader Río de la Plata grassland region discussed in Section 3.8.

Brazil's Caatinga and the surrounding sertão, subject to periodic severe drought, historically posed significant challenges for agricultural populations and shaped patterns of internal migration and rural hardship that would become historically significant well beyond the period examined in Volume I, discussed further in later volumes. By contrast, the more consistently humid Atlantic Forest coastal zone and certain river valleys of the Brazilian interior supported the intensive agricultural production, first by Indigenous peoples and later by colonial plantation economies, examined throughout the textbook's treatment of Brazilian history. Brazil's geographic scale and internal environmental diversity mean that, much like Mexico or the Andean countries, no single description of "Brazilian geography" adequately captures the range of environments found within its borders — a point this textbook returns to whenever Brazilian history requires attention to specific regional variation rather than generalization about the country as a whole.

Section 3.8

The Río de la Plata, Pampas, and Southern Cone

South of Brazil's planalto, the landscape opens into the broad, fertile grasslands known as the pampas, centered on the Río de la Plata estuary that today divides Argentina from Uruguay. The pampas' deep, fertile soils and temperate climate would, many centuries later, prove highly suitable for cattle, sheep, and cereal grains introduced from Europe, a development examined in Chapter 68 and Chapter 69 that transformed the region through export agriculture and mass immigration. It is important not to read this later suitability backward into the pre-Columbian period, however: cattle and Old World wheat were not resources available to Indigenous societies to select among, and the open pampas grassland was not equally well suited to the maize- and tuber-based cultivation that sustained Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations. During the period examined in Volume I, the open pampas proper supported comparatively small, mobile Indigenous populations, including ancestors of the Charrúa and other peoples introduced in Chapter 24, whose hunting-based economies were well adapted to the grassland environment. This pattern differed from that of Indigenous communities elsewhere in the broader Río de la Plata river system — including Guaraní communities along the Paraná and Uruguay Rivers, discussed further in Chapter 24 — who combined horticulture with fishing and forest use in riverine and forested settings quite different from the open grassland interior.

To the west, the Southern Cone also encompasses the southern reaches of the Andes as they narrow and eventually diminish in elevation, along with the fertile central valley of Chile, wedged between the Andes and the Pacific coastal range, which historically supported denser agricultural populations, including the northern Mapuche communities examined in Chapter 24 and Chapter 38, than the more arid regions to Chile's north or the colder regions to its south. Paraguay, lying inland along the Paraná and Paraguay River systems, occupies a transitional zone between the drier Chaco region to its west and more humid subtropical forest to its east, an environmental setting closely connected to the historical development of Guaraní society examined in Chapter 24.

Section 3.9

Patagonia and the Far South

At the southernmost extreme of South America, the landscape narrows and cools dramatically into Patagonia, a region of wind-scoured steppe, glacial lakes, and, toward its western and southern margins, cold temperate forest and fjord-like coastlines shared between modern Argentina and Chile. Patagonia's climate, among the harshest and windiest of any inhabited region examined in this textbook, historically supported small, highly mobile populations of hunter-gatherers, including groups whose descendants and historical experience are introduced in Chapter 24, adapted to exploiting guanaco herds, marine resources along the coast, and the region's limited but seasonally exploitable plant foods.

Beyond Patagonia's mainland, the archipelago of Tierra del Fuego and the islands approaching Cape Horn represent the southernmost inhabited landscapes in the Americas, where Indigenous peoples developed distinctive and, in important respects, highly successful adaptations to a cold and climatically demanding environment. Along the western fjords and channels of Fuego-Patagonia, peoples such as the Yagán (Yámana) and Kawésqar developed specialized canoe-based, maritime hunter-gatherer economies that drew on locally rich and productive marine resources, including shellfish, fish, and marine mammals, rather than facing a landscape simply "limited" in what it offered a well-adapted population. On the interior grasslands of the main island, Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego, the Selk'nam relied much more heavily on terrestrial resources, particularly guanaco hunting, reflecting a different subsistence strategy suited to that island's more open terrain. This far southern zone illustrates, in especially vivid form, the general principle underlying this entire chapter: human beings successfully settled and adapted to nearly every environment the Latin American landmass presented, however cold or demanding, developing technologies, subsistence strategies, and social organizations suited to conditions ranging from the equatorial rainforest of Amazonia to the sub-Antarctic environments of the far south.

Section 3.10

Rivers, Climate, and Long-Distance Connection

Beyond the region-by-region survey offered in the preceding sections, several broader environmental systems connected otherwise distant parts of Latin America to one another and to the wider Atlantic and Pacific worlds, and these connective systems recur throughout the chapters that follow. Major river systems — the Amazon, the Orinoco in Venezuela and Colombia, the Magdalena in Colombia, the Paraná-Paraguay-Uruguay system draining into the Río de la Plata, and countless smaller rivers throughout the region — functioned as corridors for transportation, trade, and cultural exchange in periods and regions where overland travel was difficult, slow, or dangerous.

Ocean currents and prevailing wind patterns similarly shaped both Indigenous maritime travel and, later, European navigation. Along South America's Pacific coast, the cold, nutrient-rich Humboldt Current supports one of the most productive marine ecosystems on Earth, sustaining large populations of fish and other marine life that, in turn, supported substantial early coastal populations and highly productive fisheries, examined further in Chapters 16–17's treatment of early coastal Andean societies. The precise relative importance of maritime resources compared with early agriculture in the rise of Andean social complexity remains a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion, addressed further in those chapters. In the Caribbean and along the Gulf Coast, prevailing trade winds and current patterns shaped both Indigenous seafaring and, after 1492, the specific sea routes that Spanish, Portuguese, and other European vessels would follow, a subject examined further in Volume II.

Climate systems operating on a still broader scale, including periodic warming and cooling events associated with the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, which produces recurring and sometimes severe disruptions to normal rainfall and ocean-temperature patterns along the Pacific coast of South America, have also periodically reshaped the environmental conditions particular societies faced, sometimes contributing to agricultural failure, coastal ecological disruption, or, conversely, unusually favorable conditions, over timescales ranging from a single season to multiple years. Historians and archaeologists continue to investigate the degree to which such climatic events may have contributed to specific historical developments, including political and social stress within particular Andean and coastal societies, a subject addressed with appropriate caution in later chapters examining specific civilizations.

Section 3.11

Environmental Change and Historical Interpretation

This chapter has repeatedly emphasized that the landscapes it describes were not simply fixed, unchanging backdrops to human history but were themselves shaped and reshaped by human activity over thousands of years, and were also subject to natural processes of climatic and ecological change independent of human action. Historians must therefore exercise care when using modern environmental descriptions, colonial-era observations, or even earlier archaeological reconstructions as though they described a single, timeless Latin American landscape.

Several specific cautions follow from this principle. First, many landscapes that appeared, to early European observers, to be pristine wilderness untouched by human hands were, in fact, substantially modified by earlier Indigenous land use — through managed forests, altered fire regimes, domesticated and semi-domesticated plant communities, and, in some regions, population decline following the demographic catastrophe examined in Chapter 29, which in some areas allowed previously cultivated or managed land to revert to forest before European colonizers arrived to observe it. Second, environmental conditions have themselves changed over the long period this textbook examines, through both natural climatic variation and human-driven transformation, meaning that the environment encountered by, for example, the Classic Maya in the eighth century CE was not necessarily identical to the environment their Postclassic successors encountered several centuries later, a theme explored further in the chapters examining Maya civilization specifically. Third, colonial and modern environmental transformation — deforestation, mining, ranching, export agriculture, urbanization, and industrial development — has altered many of the landscapes described in this chapter so substantially that visiting the same geographic location today often reveals an environment quite different from the one this chapter describes, a subject this textbook returns to directly in its treatment of colonial ecology in Volume II and contemporary resource politics in Volume V.

Recognizing both the reality of environmental influence on human history and the reality of human influence on environmental history equips students to avoid two opposite historical errors: treating Latin American peoples as simply the passive products of an unchanging natural environment, and treating environment as historically irrelevant to a story properly told only through political and cultural developments. The truth, as this chapter has aimed to demonstrate, lies in the constant, two-directional interaction between human societies and the landscapes they inhabited, modified, and were in turn shaped by — an interaction that recurs, in different forms, throughout every volume of this textbook.

Historical Vocabulary
Ecological Zone

An ecological zone is a region characterized by a broadly consistent combination of climate, vegetation, and animal life, distinguished from neighboring zones by differences in temperature, rainfall, elevation, or soil. This chapter uses "ecological zone" as a general descriptive term. Related but more formal classification concepts include the biome — a large-scale regional grouping of vegetation and animal communities defined by shared climate and landscape history, such as the six terrestrial biomes recognized within Brazil and discussed in Section 3.7 — and the life zone, a more technical ecological classification based on precise measures of temperature and precipitation. These concepts operate at different scales and within different classification systems, and students should not treat them as simple synonyms. Latin America contains an unusually large number of distinct ecological zones within a relatively compact geographic area, ranging from tropical rainforest and savanna to temperate grassland, high-altitude desert and grassland, and cold subantarctic steppe.

Historians use the concept of ecological zones to explain why particular crops, subsistence strategies, trade networks, and settlement patterns developed in particular regions, while remaining careful, as this chapter has emphasized throughout, not to treat ecological zone as a sufficient explanation on its own for the political, cultural, and social outcomes examined in later chapters.

Historical Vocabulary
Andean Verticality

Andean verticality, or ecological verticality, refers to the geographic fact that climate and vegetation change dramatically over short vertical distances in the Andes. This is distinct from, though related to, the "vertical archipelago" model, a specific scholarly framework developed by the anthropologist John Murra from evidence including sixteenth-century Spanish colonial administrative surveys, describing how some Andean ethnic groups sought direct access to resources at multiple elevations — coastal, valley, and high-altitude puna zones — through settlement or kinship-based access rights.

These concepts, examined in depth in Section 3.5 and again in Part IV of this volume, help explain distinctive features of Andean economic and political organization, including patterns of settlement, land tenure, and imperial administration under the Inca state. Students should understand ecological verticality as an environmental condition and the vertical archipelago as one documented strategy for managing that condition, not the only strategy Andean societies pursued, since barter, caravan trade, seasonal migration, and exchange also connected communities across elevations in varying combinations by period and region.

Primary Source Window

Pedro Cieza de León Describes the Andes, 1553

Creator: Pedro Cieza de León, Spanish soldier and chronicler · Dates: Composed during the 1540s and 1550s, drawing on travels through the former territories of Tawantinsuyu in the 1540s; the First Part of the Crónica del Perú, describing the geography and peoples of the region, was published in Seville in 1553. The excerpt below is drawn from Chapter XCIV of the First Part, describing the valley of Yucay and surrounding terrain. Cieza de León's later sections on Inca history and administration remained unpublished at his death and did not appear in print until centuries afterward; an English translation of some of this later material was published by the Hakluyt Society in the 1880s. · Type: Colonial-era chronicle combining firsthand observation, secondhand report, and Spanish cultural interpretation

Context: Cieza de León traveled extensively through the former territories of Tawantinsuyu during the 1540s, in the years following the Spanish conquest of the Inca examined in Chapter 35 and Chapter 36, and compiled his observations into one of the earliest and most detailed Spanish descriptions of Andean geography, society, and Inca administration. His work remains a valuable, though not unproblematic, source for historians studying the physical and human landscape of the sixteenth-century Andes.

Describing the mountains he passed through in the Andean highlands, Cieza de León wrote that they were "quite white with snow during the greater part of the year," and that their sides were "lined with terraces one above the other," so that the steep slopes had been made productive nearly to their summits.

Analysis: Cieza de León's account is valuable because it was written by an observer who traveled extensively through the region relatively soon after the conquest, while much of the Inca administrative and agricultural infrastructure remained visible or freshly remembered. It must nonetheless be read critically: Cieza de León wrote as a Spanish soldier with his own cultural assumptions, limited fluency in Andean languages, and, in places, an interest in describing the territory for purposes connected to Spanish administration. His account should be read alongside archaeological evidence and, where available, Indigenous-authored or Indigenous-informed sources, such as those examined in Chapter 4, rather than treated as a neutral or complete description.

  3. Why is it useful for historians to read colonial-era descriptions of landscape alongside archaeological evidence rather than relying on written description alone?
  4. What perspective on the Andean landscape is missing from a chronicle written by a Spanish soldier, and where might historians look to fill that gap?
Historical Debate

Does Geography Explain History?

The question: How much explanatory weight should historians give to environment and geography when accounting for the different historical trajectories of Latin American societies, and at what point does environmental explanation become environmental determinism?

Interpretation A — Environment provides essential structuring conditions for historical development: Proponents of this view argue that a serious history of any region must account for climate, soil, elevation, water availability, and access to domesticable plants and animals, because these factors genuinely constrained and enabled particular economic and social strategies. On this view, the Andes' ecological verticality, Mesoamerica's highland-lowland contrast, and Amazonia's riverine geography are not incidental details but essential explanatory factors for understanding why particular societies developed particular agricultural systems, trade networks, and forms of political organization.
Interpretation B — Overemphasizing environment risks environmental determinism and obscures human agency: Critics caution that explanations leaning too heavily on geography can slide into environmental determinism, a framework historians have widely rejected because it risks implying that societies in supposedly more "difficult" environments were destined to remain less complex or less powerful, an implication with a troubling history of misuse to rank societies or justify inequality. On this view, political choices, religious systems, military organization, trade relationships, and simple historical contingency did at least as much to shape historical outcomes as environment did, and two societies in similar environments frequently developed in very different directions.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological and geographic evidence describing the environments discussed throughout this chapter, but differ in how much causal weight to assign to environmental conditions relative to political, cultural, and historical factors examined in later chapters.

Why it matters: This textbook adopts a middle position, sometimes called environmental possibilism: environment shapes the range of realistic possibilities available to a society without determining which possibility that society will pursue or how successfully it will pursue it. This chapter's regional survey is intended to equip students with the environmental context necessary to understand later chapters, not to suggest that any civilization's specific historical trajectory can be read directly off a map.

Student task: Using examples from this chapter, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain how a historian might combine insights from both without falling into environmental determinism.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Same Landscape: Perspectives on Environment

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a highland Andean farming community before European contact: The landscape offered distinct resources at different elevations, and maintaining direct access to coastal, valley, and puna zones through kinship networks and settlement was a practical strategy for ensuring a secure and varied subsistence base across a challenging vertical terrain.
From the perspective of a Maya community in the Yucatán Peninsula: The absence of major surface rivers required careful management of rainwater through cenotes, reservoirs, and seasonal wetlands, making water storage and distribution a central and continuous concern for community life and agriculture.
From the perspective of an Amazonian riverine community: The river was not an obstacle but the primary means of travel, communication, and access to fertile floodplain soils, shaping settlement directly along its banks and tributaries rather than dispersed evenly across the surrounding forest.
From the perspective of a sixteenth-century Spanish colonial chronicler encountering Andean terrain: The dramatic vertical change in climate and the difficulty of the terrain appeared as formidable obstacles to travel and administration, prompting detailed written description intended partly to help colonial officials understand a landscape very different from that of Iberia.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Landscapes Transformed and Enduring

What Changed

  • Human populations increasingly modified their environments over time through irrigation, terracing, raised-field agriculture, forest management, and soil improvement
  • Population decline following European conquest, examined in Chapter 29, allowed some previously cultivated or managed landscapes to revert toward earlier vegetation states in certain regions
  • Colonial and modern ranching, mining, agriculture, and urbanization have substantially altered many landscapes described in this chapter
  • Scientific understanding of Amazonian and other landscapes has shifted considerably as archaeological evidence of large-scale pre-Columbian environmental modification has accumulated

What Stayed the Same

  • The underlying geological features — the Andes, the volcanic highlands of Mesoamerica, the Amazon Basin — have remained broadly stable across the entire period this textbook examines
  • Ecological verticality has continued to shape Andean agriculture and settlement from the pre-Inca period to the present day
  • Rivers, coastlines, and ocean currents have continued to function as corridors of transportation and exchange across many centuries
  • The basic pattern of environmental diversity distinguishing Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, the Caribbean, and the Southern Cone from one another has persisted throughout the historical period this textbook studies

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the deep stability of Latin America's fundamental geography and the substantial ways human societies have modified their environments over time helps students avoid treating any single landscape description, whether ancient or modern, as a timeless and unchanging fact.

Cause and Effect

Why Environment Shaped, but Did Not Determine, Human Society

Long-term causes

Latin America's exceptional range of latitude, elevation, and oceanic influence produced an unusually large number of distinct ecological zones within a comparatively compact geographic space.

Short-term causes

Specific societies made deliberate choices — irrigation, terracing, vertical resource access, riverine settlement, maritime trade — to exploit, modify, or overcome the particular environmental conditions they encountered.

Effect

An extraordinary diversity of subsistence strategies, settlement patterns, and forms of political and economic organization developed across the region, none of which was strictly dictated by environment alone.

Historical significance

This environmental diversity provides essential context for understanding the distinct civilizational trajectories of Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, and other regions examined throughout the remainder of Volume I.

Historical complexity: No single environmental factor explains any society's historical trajectory; environment interacted continuously with political choice, cultural practice, and historical contingency to produce the outcomes examined in later chapters.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with the Land: Everyday Environmental Knowledge

Behind the large-scale environmental patterns discussed in this chapter lay countless generations of accumulated, practical environmental knowledge held by ordinary farmers, fishers, herders, and foragers. Andean farmers developed detailed knowledge of which potato varieties, among the hundreds they cultivated, tolerated frost, drought, or particular soil types best at particular elevations. Amazonian communities developed sophisticated knowledge of forest ecology, identifying and encouraging useful tree and plant species within managed forest patches. Caribbean fishing communities developed detailed knowledge of seasonal fish migrations, reef ecosystems, and safe canoe routes between islands. Mesoamerican farmers developed calendrical systems, examined further in Chapter 9, closely tied to agricultural cycles and rainfall patterns specific to their local environment.

This everyday environmental knowledge, though rarely recorded in the kinds of documents historians most often rely upon, represents an essential and continuous thread connecting the specialized chapters that follow. Archaeological evidence of crop remains, irrigation systems, and settlement patterns, together with the surviving practices of contemporary Indigenous and rural communities descended from the societies this textbook examines, allow historians to reconstruct at least a partial picture of how ordinary people understood, managed, and depended upon the land, rivers, forests, and seas described throughout this chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has surveyed the major landscapes of Latin America as the essential environmental foundation for the civilizations, conquests, colonial societies, and modern nations examined throughout the remaining chapters of this textbook. It has emphasized, from the outset, that environment shaped historical possibility without determining historical outcome, and that the temptation to treat geography as a sufficient explanation for cultural or political development — environmental determinism — must be rejected even as environment is taken seriously as a genuine historical factor.

Students have examined Mesoamerica's combination of volcanic highlands, lake-filled basins, and tropical coastal lowlands; Central America's narrow isthmus separating two oceans; the Caribbean's island world connected by maritime travel rather than uniform terrestrial environment; the Andes' extraordinary ecological verticality and the distinctive vertical-archipelago economic strategies it encouraged; Amazonia's river-centered geography and its increasingly recognized history of substantial human environmental modification; Brazil's coastal lowlands, interior plateau, and internal environmental diversity; the fertile grasslands of the pampas and Río de la Plata; and the harsh, wind-swept landscapes of Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego at South America's southern extreme.

The chapter has also examined the broader connective systems — rivers, ocean currents, and large-scale climate patterns such as the Humboldt Current and the El Niño–Southern Oscillation — that linked otherwise distant regions of Latin America to one another and to the wider Atlantic and Pacific worlds, preparing students for the extensive treatment of trade, migration, and interregional contact examined throughout the remainder of this textbook.

Finally, the chapter has insisted that the landscapes it describes were not simply fixed, natural backdrops but were themselves substantially shaped by thousands of years of human activity, from Andean terracing and Mesoamerican chinampas to Amazonian raised fields and anthropogenic soils, and that these landscapes have continued to change, through both natural processes and human action, from the colonial period to the present day. Understanding this dynamic, two-directional relationship between human societies and their environments equips students to read the specific civilizational histories examined throughout the remainder of Volume I with appropriate attention to environmental context, without reducing those histories to environmental cause alone.

Chapter 3 has asked what kinds of landscapes shaped the development of Latin American societies. Chapter 4 turns to a different but closely related question: what kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct Indigenous history at all, given the absence of written records for many of the societies and periods this textbook examines?

Investigating a Latin American Landscape

This project asks you to investigate how a specific landscape or environmental feature discussed in this chapter has shaped, and been shaped by, human history across multiple periods.

  1. Choose a landscape or environmental feature. Select a region (such as the Andean altiplano, the Yucatán Peninsula, the Amazon Basin, or the pampas) or a specific environmental feature (such as chinampas, andenes, or terra preta) introduced in this chapter.
  2. Research its pre-Columbian history. Using library resources, academic encyclopedias, and reliable scholarly sources, investigate how Indigenous societies used, modified, or adapted to your chosen landscape or feature.
  3. Research its colonial and modern transformation. Identify how colonization, ranching, mining, agriculture, or modern development has altered your chosen landscape since the period this chapter examines.
  4. Compare past and present. Where has the landscape remained similar to its earlier condition? Where has it changed substantially? What historical processes explain the change?
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay (or equivalent multimedia presentation), explain your chosen landscape's historical development, present relevant differing interpretations fairly, and use careful language such as "researchers generally agree" or "this remains debated" rather than overstating certainty.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look carefully at the physical world within which Latin American history unfolded — not as a static stage on which human events simply took place, but as a dynamic set of conditions and resources that both shaped and were reshaped by the societies examined throughout this textbook. Understanding the volcanic highlands of Mesoamerica, the vertical mosaic of the Andes, the river-bound world of Amazonia, the maritime connectivity of the Caribbean, and the vast environmental range from Brazil's tropical coast to Patagonia's cold plains provides essential context for nearly every chapter that follows.

The chapter has also modeled a form of historical reasoning that will recur throughout this textbook: taking environmental and geographic factors seriously as genuine historical forces while firmly rejecting the reductive claim that geography alone determines a society's fate. This balance — recognizing real constraints and possibilities without erasing human agency, political choice, and historical contingency — will matter again when this textbook examines why particular conquests succeeded or failed, why particular colonial economies developed as they did, and why particular modern states have pursued particular strategies of resource extraction and environmental management.

Students who understand that Latin America's landscapes were, and remain, both a shaping force in human history and a product of human activity are well prepared to read the specific Indigenous civilizations examined in the remainder of Volume I with appropriate attention to their environmental settings, without mistaking environmental context for historical destiny. The story this textbook tells next turns to the evidence historians use to reconstruct those civilizations' histories — a question addressed directly in Chapter 4.
