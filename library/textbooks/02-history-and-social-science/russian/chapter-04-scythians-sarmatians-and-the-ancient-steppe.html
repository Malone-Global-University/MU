Part I — The Lands and Peoples Before Rus'
4

Scythians, Sarmatians, and the Ancient Steppe

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

"The Scythians have made the cleverest discovery in the world, though I do not praise them for it in other respects... they have contrived to have their homes always with them." — a paraphrase of the sense of Herodotus's fifth-century BCE description of the Scythians, whose mobile way of life he presented as a strategic advantage against invaders.

In 1971, Soviet archaeologists excavating a burial mound called Tovsta Mohyla (also transliterated Tolstaya Mogila) near the town of Ordzhonikidze (now Pokrov) in southern Ukraine uncovered a golden pectoral, an elaborate crescent-shaped chest ornament nearly a foot across, weighing over a kilogram and depicting, in three registers of exquisitely rendered gold relief, scenes of grazing horses, mythical griffins attacking horses, and domestic life among people the excavators believed to be Scythian herders and craftsmen. The pectoral was recovered from the central male burial complex of the mound, a complex whose main chamber, historians and archaeologists have established, had already been robbed in antiquity; a separate, undisturbed side chamber contained the burial of a noblewoman and a child. No inscription named the man for whom the complex was built. No chronicle recorded his deeds. Everything historians know about the society that produced this object, and about the people buried at Tovsta Mohyla, comes from the physical evidence of the mound itself — including horse burials and other sacrificed animals associated with the central complex — and from the observations of outside writers, above all the Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, who set down what he had heard, and in some cases apparently witnessed, of a mobile, horse-riding people he called Scythians.

This chapter examines the first peoples of the territory covered by this textbook to enter the written historical record, even though that record was produced almost entirely by outsiders. The Cimmerians, Scythians, and Sarmatians, whose successive prominence on the Pontic-Caspian steppe stretched across roughly a millennium, from perhaps the ninth century BCE to the fourth century CE, left no writing of their own that has survived, and much of what is known about them combines Greek and other outside testimony with the archaeological evidence of their settlements, and especially their graves. This combination of textual and material evidence marks a genuine transition from Chapter 3's exclusively archaeological prehistory toward the fuller documentary record of later chapters, but it is a partial and uneven transition: the textual sources are foreign, often secondhand, sometimes centuries removed from firsthand observation, and shaped by Greek literary conventions about "barbarian" peoples that require careful critical handling.

The societies examined in this chapter were not marginal or peripheral to the ancient world. Scythian elites could tax, protect, or profit from trade routes carrying grain grown by settled agricultural populations of the forest-steppe toward Greek ports on the Black Sea, from which grain moved onward into wider Mediterranean markets, including, in later centuries, Athens; Scythian elites accumulated some of the most spectacular quantities of worked gold recovered anywhere from the ancient world; and Scythian and, later, Sarmatian cavalry forces shaped the military calculations of neighboring settled powers for centuries. Understanding these societies on their own terms, rather than merely as a violent backdrop against which Greek colonists and later chroniclers wrote, is one of this chapter's central purposes.

This chapter also continues, in a new context, the methodological caution introduced in Chapter 3 regarding archaeological cultures and ethnic identity. "Scythian," as this chapter will explain, functioned in Greek usage partly as an ethnonym and partly as a broad, sometimes imprecise label applied to a range of related but distinct steppe populations; the archaeological cultures associated with Scythian and Sarmatian material remains do not map perfectly onto a single, unified political nation, and modern nationalist appropriations of "Scythian heritage" by various claimants require the same critical scrutiny this textbook applied to Bronze Age archaeological cultures in the previous chapter.

Finally, this chapter positions the steppe world explicitly as this textbook's first sustained encounter with the recurring theme of interaction between mobile pastoral societies and settled agricultural and urban populations — a theme Chapter 2 introduced in environmental terms and that will recur, in transformed guises, throughout this textbook's treatment of the Khazars, the Mongols, and the long Russian and Cossack settlement of the steppe frontier. The Scythians and Sarmatians were neither the ancestors of any single modern nation nor simply violent raiders; they were sophisticated, historically consequential societies whose interactions with Greek colonists, Persian emperors, and neighboring forest-zone populations shaped the deep historical fabric of Eurasia.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the political and economic organization of Scythian and Sarmatian society, evaluate the strengths and limitations of Greek textual evidence such as Herodotus, interpret royal kurgan burials as evidence for social hierarchy and belief, and explain why the steppe-sedentary relationship examined in this chapter recurs, in modified form, across the remainder of this textbook.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geographic and environmental setting of the Pontic-Caspian steppe as the stage for Cimmerian, Scythian, and Sarmatian history.
  2. Trace the sequence of prominent steppe powers — Cimmerians, Scythians, and Sarmatians — and the approximate chronology of their rise and decline.
  3. Explain the political and social organization of Scythian society, including the relationship between a ruling elite and subordinate populations.
  4. Analyze Scythian pastoral economy, mounted warfare, and long-distance trade as interlocking features of steppe power.
  5. Evaluate Greek colonization of the Black Sea coast as a distinct but interconnected historical development alongside Scythian power.
  6. Interpret Scythian gold work and kurgan burial evidence as sources for social hierarchy, belief, and cross-cultural artistic exchange.
  7. Compare Sarmatian society and political organization with that of the earlier Scythians.
  8. Assess the ancient reports, archaeological evidence, and later mythologization concerning women in Sarmatian society, including their connection to Greek legends of Amazons.
  9. Critically evaluate Herodotus and other Greek sources as evidence, distinguishing eyewitness observation from secondhand report and literary convention.
  10. Explain the historical relationship between mobile pastoral and sedentary agricultural or urban populations without reducing it to permanent hostility.
  11. Critique nationalist and popular appropriations of "Scythian" identity in later centuries.
  12. Connect the steppe societies examined in this chapter to the later peoples—Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Turkic—and steppe polities—Khazars, Pechenegs, Mongols—examined in subsequent chapters.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the environment of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, introduced in Chapter 2, shape the political and military organization of Cimmerian, Scythian, and Sarmatian societies?
  2. What made Scythian mounted warfare and pastoral mobility such a formidable combination for a settled power, such as Persia, to confront?
  3. Why do historians read Herodotus's account of the Scythians critically rather than as a straightforward ethnographic record?
  4. What can kurgan burials, such as the one at Tovsta Mohyla, reveal about Scythian social hierarchy, religion, and connections to the wider ancient world?
  5. How did Greek colonies along the Black Sea coast interact economically and politically with Scythian power on the steppe?
  6. In what ways did Sarmatian society differ from, and continue, patterns established under Scythian dominance?
  7. Why should historians be cautious about treating "Scythian" as a single, unified ethnic or political identity, or about treating Scythian origins as a single migration event?
  8. How does the relationship between steppe and sedentary societies examined in this chapter prefigure patterns this textbook will trace across many later centuries?

Historical Context

Chapter 3 traced the Bronze Age societies of the Pontic-Caspian steppe — the Yamnaya horizon, the Sintashta-Petrovka culture, and the Srubnaya, or Timber-Grave, culture — as increasingly mobile, increasingly stratified pastoral societies whose fortified settlements, chariot technology, and elaborate burial practices anticipated, without directly and simply producing, the historically documented Iron Age societies this chapter examines. Between the end of the Srubnaya culture, roughly 1200 BCE, and the appearance of the Cimmerians and Scythians in written sources beginning in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, the steppe underwent further transformations, including the wider adoption of iron metallurgy. Iron ore was considerably more widely available across Eurasia than the tin required for bronze, and this availability, rather than any automatic mechanical superiority of early iron over well-made bronze, was the main initial advantage of the new metal; only gradually, as smiths developed techniques of carburization, forging, and heat treatment, did iron and steel tools and weapons become reliably and substantially more effective than bronze equivalents. By many scholars' reckoning, this period also saw an intensification of mounted warfare, as horse riding, rather than only horse-drawn chariotry, became a central feature of steppe military life.

This chapter marks a genuine evidentiary transition. For the first time in this textbook, archaeological evidence can be supplemented, cross-checked, and sometimes contradicted by written testimony, because Greek city-states along the northern Black Sea coast, and Greek writers working farther afield, began recording observations of steppe peoples from the late seventh century BCE onward. The most important such writer, Herodotus of Halicarnassus, composed his Histories in the mid-fifth century BCE, devoting a substantial portion of Book Four to a detailed account of Scythian geography, customs, religion, and recent history, including the failed Persian invasion of Scythia under King Darius I around 513 BCE. Herodotus's account, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window, is invaluable precisely because it offers something Chapter 3's archaeological record could not: named individuals, described customs, and a connected historical narrative. It must nonetheless be read with the same critical caution this textbook has applied to every other source, since Herodotus wrote as a Greek outsider, relying substantially on informants circulating through the northern Black Sea Greek world, on Greek literary conventions about foreign "barbarian" peoples, and on his own selective judgment about what deserved inclusion.

Students should also understand that "Scythian" in ancient Greek usage was not always a precise ethnic label. Greek writers sometimes used the term broadly for a range of steppe peoples with differing languages, customs, and political organization, in much the way that later European writers sometimes used broad and imprecise labels for diverse populations they encountered. Archaeologists today generally understand the Scythians proper as an Iranian-speaking, Indo-European steppe people whose material culture — a specific combination of weaponry, horse harness and gear, and a decorative animal-style art, together known as the "Scythian triad" — spread across an enormous area from the Pontic steppe to the Altai Mountains of Inner Asia, while recognizing that this broad material pattern encompassed numerous regionally and politically distinct groups rather than a single centralized empire. Herodotus reports a tradition that the Scythians themselves arrived on the Pontic steppe from farther east; modern ancient-DNA research complicates a simple single-wave version of this story, suggesting instead that Pontic Scythian populations formed through a more gradual, multiregional process involving substantial gene flow between eastern and western steppe populations rather than one population wholly replacing another, a question examined further in Section 4.3.

Section 4.1

The Steppe World

Chapter 2 described the Pontic-Caspian steppe as a broad band of open grassland extending from the lower Danube across what is now Ukraine and southern Russia to the Caspian Sea and beyond into Kazakhstan, an environment whose drier, more variable rainfall favored mobile pastoralism over dependable rain-fed grain agriculture across most of its extent. This chapter examines the first historically documented societies to exploit that environment on a large political and military scale: the Cimmerians, Scythians, and Sarmatians, whose successive prominence on the steppe corridor stretched across roughly a millennium, from perhaps the ninth century BCE to the fourth century CE.

The steppe's openness, discussed in Chapter 2 as a condition rather than a determinant of history, took on particular significance for these societies because of a specific technological and social combination: the horse. By the early Iron Age, steppe populations had moved decisively beyond earlier patterns of horse herding and horse-drawn chariotry, discussed in Chapter 3, toward mounted horseback riding as a central feature of daily life, herding, and warfare. Mounted riding and archery were widespread among Scythian warriors, and a population that could field large numbers of such mounted archers possessed a form of military mobility that no contemporary settled agricultural society could easily match, allowing steppe confederations to strike, retreat, and regroup across enormous distances that would have exhausted infantry-based armies.

This mobility did not make steppe societies formless or leaderless. Herodotus and other Greek sources describe the Scythians as organized under kings and a ruling elite, controlling subordinate populations and levying tribute or demanding military service from them. Archaeological evidence, particularly the scale and wealth of royal kurgan burials examined in Section 4.6, is consistent with substantial social stratification, though it should be read as material evidence for an elite hierarchy broadly of the kind Herodotus describes rather than as independent, self-sufficient proof of every specific detail of his account. The steppe world this chapter examines was neither a formless mass of interchangeable raiders, as some older popular writing suggested, nor a single unified empire; it was a shifting landscape of confederations, subordinate tribes, and rival elites, whose specific political configuration changed considerably across the centuries this chapter covers.

The Pontic steppe's position, moreover, placed these societies in direct and sustained contact with several of the ancient world's major civilizations: the Greek colonial world along the Black Sea coast, examined in Section 4.5; the Persian Achaemenid Empire, whose invasion under Darius I is examined in Section 4.3; and, at the steppe's eastern reaches, the broader Central Asian and Inner Asian world with which, as this chapter's Historical Context has already noted, Pontic Scythian populations shared significant genetic and cultural connections. This chapter's steppe societies were therefore never isolated; they were active participants in a broader ancient Eurasian world of trade, diplomacy, warfare, and cultural exchange.

Section 4.2

Cimmerians and Early Nomadic Societies

The Cimmerians are the first steppe people of this region named in surviving written sources, appearing in Assyrian records of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE describing raids into Anatolia and the Near East, and later in Greek sources, including Homer's Odyssey, which mentions the Cimmerians in a legendary, quasi-mythical context reflecting how distant and poorly understood they were to early Greek audiences. Later Greek tradition, most fully preserved in Herodotus, associates the Cimmerians with the Pontic steppe before the rise of Scythian prominence there, and describes them retreating in part into Anatolia, where Assyrian records document raiding activity that scholars have often, though not with complete certainty, connected to the same Cimmerian groups.

Archaeologically, the Cimmerians are considerably harder to identify as a distinct material culture than the Scythians who followed them, and even the classical tradition's account of a unified Cimmerian "state" occupying the Pontic steppe before an equally unified Scythian takeover simplifies a considerably less tidy historical reality. Some archaeologists associate certain Late Bronze Age and earliest Iron Age burial and artifact assemblages on the Pontic steppe with a "pre-Scythian" or Cimmerian horizon, but the material record does not permit the same level of confident cultural identification available for the Scythian period, and reconstruction of an earlier Cimmerian homeland and political organization depends substantially on later classical literary tradition rather than on secure independent archaeological evidence. This uncertainty should not be read as evidence that the Cimmerians were unimportant; rather, it illustrates a recurring problem this textbook has examined since Chapter 3: written sources and archaeological cultures do not always align neatly, and a people well attested in later text may be only faintly visible in the excavated record.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Cimmerian prominence on the Pontic steppe represents the first attested instance of the pattern this textbook will trace repeatedly: a steppe people rising to regional prominence, projecting military power beyond the steppe itself (in this case into Anatolia and the Caucasus), and eventually giving way to a subsequent, better-documented steppe power — in this instance, the Scythians, examined beginning in Section 4.3.

Section 4.3

The Scythians

According to Herodotus's account, the Scythians arrived on the Pontic steppe from farther east, driven by conflict with another steppe people, and established dominance over the Cimmerians and other existing populations by the seventh century BCE. This migration tradition should be distinguished carefully from what modern archaeological and genetic evidence actually supports. Ancient DNA research on Scythian-period steppe populations indicates substantial genetic connections and gene flow between eastern (Siberian and Central Asian) and western (Pontic) Scythian-period populations, but the evidence favors a more gradual, multiregional process of formation — involving repeated contact, mixture, and movement across the wider Scythian-period world — rather than a single eastern population wholly displacing or replacing an existing western population in one wave. Herodotus's migration story, in other words, preserves a genuine tradition about eastern connections that modern genetics broadly corroborates in outline, while the specific, simple form of a single decisive migration should not be taken as an established fact.

Linguistic and archaeological evidence broadly supports the picture of an Iranian-speaking steppe population whose material culture — the Scythian triad of characteristic weaponry, horse harness and gear, and animal-style decorative art discussed in the Historical Context above — spread across an enormous stretch of the Eurasian steppe from the Pontic region to the Altai Mountains of Inner Asia, though, as this chapter's introduction cautioned, this broad shared material pattern encompassed numerous regionally distinct and likely politically independent groups rather than a single centralized state.

Herodotus describes Scythian society as organized into several distinct groups occupying different parts of Scythian territory and engaged in different economic activities: "Royal Scythians," whom he presents as the dominant, most powerful group, controlling subordinate populations; agricultural Scythians, who practiced settled farming in parts of the forest-steppe near the Dnieper; and various other named groups. Modern historians read this account with appropriate caution, recognizing that Herodotus's schematic presentation may simplify a more complex and regionally varied social and economic reality, but the basic picture of a politically dominant, primarily pastoral and militarily organized elite ruling over, or coexisting alongside, more agriculturally oriented populations in the fertile forest-steppe zone is broadly consistent with the archaeological evidence and with the general pattern of steppe-forest-steppe interaction Chapter 2 introduced.

Scythian political organization, so far as the sources allow reconstruction, centered on kingship, with royal authority resting on some combination of military leadership, control over tribute and long-distance trade, and religious or ceremonial legitimacy reflected in elaborate royal burial practice. Herodotus's narrative of the Persian King Darius I's invasion of Scythia around 513 BCE — though dates ranging from roughly 520 to 507 BCE have also been proposed by modern scholars, and the campaign's precise objectives and scale remain debated — illustrates both this political organization and Scythian military strategy in unusually vivid detail. According to Herodotus, rather than meeting the vastly larger Persian army in open battle, Scythian forces retreated steadily across the steppe, destroying forage and filling in wells, harassing the Persian column, and drawing the invaders deeper into unfamiliar territory until supply difficulties and exhaustion forced a Persian withdrawal without a decisive engagement. These specific tactical details — the destruction of forage and wells and the precise mechanism of the Persian withdrawal — rest on Herodotus's narrative and should be understood as his account rather than as independently established fact. Whatever the precise historical accuracy of these details, a question this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines further, the broader strategic logic he describes, exploiting mobility, terrain, and logistics against a more heavily equipped but less mobile invading force, recurs as a durable pattern in steppe warfare across many later centuries this textbook will examine, including in the Mongol campaigns of Chapter 16.

Section 4.4

Scythian Economy and Warfare

Scythian economic life combined mobile pastoralism, the herding of horses, cattle, and sheep across the open steppe, with control over, and likely partial reliance upon, the agricultural production of the forest-steppe zone to the north, discussed in Chapter 2 as an especially fertile transitional environment. Herodotus's account of agricultural Scythians, together with archaeological evidence of grain cultivation and storage within Scythian-influenced territory in what is now Ukraine, supports the conclusion that Scythian power rested not solely on pastoral mobility but on a mixed economic base capable of drawing upon substantial grain surpluses — surpluses produced chiefly by settled agricultural populations of the forest-steppe and moved toward Black Sea markets through Greek colonial intermediaries and, in later centuries, the Bosporan Kingdom, examined in Section 4.5. Scythian elites appear to have profited from this trade chiefly by taxing, protecting, or otherwise controlling the routes and populations through which grain and other goods passed, rather than by directly cultivating grain themselves on any large scale.

Scythian military organization built directly upon the pastoral economic base described above. The composite bow, constructed from layered wood, horn, and sinew and capable of considerable power and range despite its compact size well suited to use from horseback, gave Scythian mounted archers a formidable ranged weapon; supplemented by the akinakes — a short, usually straight, double-edged sword or dagger, generally in the range of fifty to seventy centimeters in length, though longer forms are also attested — for close combat, and by spears and light armor for elite warriors, Scythian cavalry combined mobility, ranged firepower, and, when necessary, close combat capability in a package that few contemporary infantry-based armies could match on open terrain. Herodotus's description of Scythian campaign strategy against Darius, discussed in Section 4.3, illustrates the strategic logic this military combination enabled: rather than accepting battle on terms favorable to a heavier, less mobile opponent, Scythian forces could decline engagement, harass supply lines, and control the tempo of a campaign across enormous distances.

Long-distance trade formed a further essential pillar of Scythian economic and political power. Grain, furs, honey, wax, and slaves moved from the forest-steppe and steppe zones toward Greek colonial ports on the Black Sea coast, in exchange for wine, fine pottery, textiles, and metal goods, including the gold and silver objects, often manufactured by Greek or Greek-trained craftsmen working to Scythian taste and commission, that fill the richest Scythian royal kurgans examined in Section 4.6. Control over this trade, and over the routes and populations through which it passed, provided Scythian rulers with resources to reward followers, fund military campaigns, and display status — a pattern of trade-based political power that anticipates, in ancient form, the riverine and steppe trade dynamics Chapter 2 and Chapter 6 examine for the much later Rus' and Khazar periods.

Section 4.5

Greek Colonies of the Black Sea

Beginning in the late seventh century BCE, Greek city-states established a network of colonial settlements along the northern coast of the Black Sea, including Olbia near the mouth of the Southern Bug and Dnieper rivers and Panticapaeum (modern Kerch) commanding the strait between the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov, both founded within this earlier wave of colonization. Tauric Chersonesus, near modern Sevastopol in Crimea, was a later, distinct Dorian Greek foundation of the fifth century BCE, and should not be dated to the same seventh-century wave as Olbia and Panticapaeum. Ancient Greek colonization more generally was driven by a plural set of causes rather than any single factor: the search for arable land, commercial opportunity, competition and political conflict within founding cities, and the practical opportunities particular coastal sites offered for trade and settlement all played a role, in varying combinations for different colonies. These colonies, technically independent city-states in the Greek political tradition rather than administrative outposts of a distant Greek government, developed their own political institutions while maintaining close commercial, religious, and cultural ties to their founding cities and to the broader Greek Mediterranean world.

The relationship between these Greek colonies and neighboring Scythian power was neither purely hostile nor simply subordinate; it was a sustained, mutually beneficial, and at times tense commercial and diplomatic relationship. Greek colonies served as an emporion — a trading place, market, or port, a term Greek writers applied specifically to sites of exchange rather than to every Greek settlement generically — through which grain, furs, and other steppe and forest-steppe products entered Mediterranean trade networks, while Mediterranean wine, olive oil, fine painted pottery, and worked metal moved in the opposite direction toward Scythian elites. Panticapaeum, in particular, grew wealthy as a principal outlet for grain grown by settled agricultural populations in the fertile territory of the Cimmerian Bosporus and adjacent forest-steppe, eventually becoming the center of a substantial local Greek polity, the Bosporan Kingdom, whose rulers maintained close, sometimes intermarried, relationships with neighboring Scythian and later Sarmatian elites. Athenian dependence on Black Sea grain became especially significant in the later fifth and fourth centuries BCE and was closely bound up with commercial relations involving the Bosporan Kingdom specifically, rather than resulting from direct, unmediated Scythian control of grain production or export.

This colonial relationship illustrates a pattern this textbook's later chapters will examine repeatedly: a settled, commercially sophisticated coastal or riverine society coexisting with, trading with, and periodically coming into military tension with a neighboring mobile steppe power, each dependent in some measure on the other's resources and cooperation. Archaeological evidence from Olbia and other Greek colonial sites, including Greek-manufactured objects found in Scythian burials and, conversely, Scythian-style artifacts and burial customs found in some colonial contexts, demonstrates genuine cultural exchange alongside continued distinct identity on both sides.

Section 4.6

Scythian Art and Material Culture

The most spectacular surviving evidence for Scythian society comes from the royal kurgans, elaborately furnished burial mounds conventionally interpreted as the graves of kings or members of the highest Scythian elite described in Herodotus's account. Sites such as Chertomlyk and Tovsta Mohyla, both in what is now southern Ukraine, have yielded extraordinary quantities of worked gold and silver, including weapons, horse trappings, vessels, and personal ornaments, alongside the remains of sacrificed horses and, in some burials, attendants interred alongside the primary occupant — evidence broadly consistent with Herodotus's account of Scythian royal funeral custom, which describes the killing of horses and servants to accompany a dead king, even if the specific numbers and details he reports should be treated with appropriate caution as a Greek outsider's secondhand or embellished account. The material scale and wealth of these burials provides evidence consistent with the elite hierarchy Herodotus describes, though matching any specific kurgan to a specific named ruler generally depends on inference rather than direct proof.

Much of the finest Scythian gold work, including the famous Tovsta Mohyla pectoral described in this chapter's opening, shows very strong Greek technical craftsmanship and iconographic influence, and many scholars attribute major prestige objects of this kind to Greek or Greek-trained, including Bosporan, workshops producing luxury items for Scythian patrons. The precise workshop, origin, and full range of cultural influences behind individual masterpieces such as the Tovsta Mohyla pectoral, however, remain genuinely debated among specialists, with some scholarship also identifying possible Thracian, Achaemenid Persian, and local steppe contributions to particular objects' design and iconography. This scholarly uncertainty should caution students against imagining ancient cultures as sealed, mutually isolated units, or against assuming that a single, simple production story can be assigned to every object; the Scythian gold examined in this section is simultaneously evidence for Scythian wealth and status and for the depth, and complexity, of Greek-Scythian cultural and commercial interpenetration examined in Section 4.5.

Alongside Greek-made or Greek-influenced luxury objects, Scythian material culture also included a distinctive steppe decorative tradition, conventionally called the "animal style," found across the wider Eurasian Scythian-period material horizon from the Pontic steppe to the Altai Mountains, and showing considerable regional variation and evidence of long-distance interaction rather than a single, uniform local origin: dynamic, stylized depictions of animals, particularly deer, felines, birds of prey, and mythical composite creatures, rendered on weapons, harness fittings, and personal ornaments in gold, bronze, and other materials, likely carrying religious, totemic, or status significance beyond pure decoration. The broad geographic spread of this animal-style tradition, sharing recognizable stylistic features across thousands of miles of steppe, provides some of the strongest material evidence for sustained long-distance contact and shared cultural reference points among widely separated Iron Age steppe populations, a pattern of long-distance connection this textbook has traced in less elaborated form since the Paleolithic evidence examined in Chapter 3.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Kurgan

Royal kurgan refers to the largest and most elaborately furnished category of Scythian burial mound, conventionally interpreted by archaeologists as belonging to kings or other members of the highest ruling elite, as distinguished from more modest kurgans built for lesser members of Scythian society. Royal kurgans, such as Chertomlyk and Tovsta Mohyla, are typically distinguished by their scale, the depth and complexity of the burial chamber, the presence of sacrificed horses and, in some cases, attendants, and the extraordinary quantity and quality of gold and silver grave goods, frequently including objects of Greek or Greek-influenced manufacture.

Royal kurgans matter historically because they provide material evidence consistent with the hierarchy Herodotus described in textual form: the existence of a distinct "Royal Scythian" elite exercising authority over a broader Scythian population and controlling sufficient wealth, through trade, tribute, and plunder, to commission extraordinary quantities of imported luxury goods. Assigning a specific kurgan to a specific office or named ruler, however, remains an interpretive act built on comparison with Herodotus's account and on relative scale among excavated burials, rather than a matter of direct documentary proof. Royal kurgans also provide the richest surviving material evidence anywhere in the ancient world for the specific hybrid Greek-Scythian artistic tradition examined in this section.

Section 4.7

Sarmatian Expansion

Beginning around the third century BCE, a related but politically distinct group of Iranian-speaking steppe peoples, whom Greek and later Roman writers collectively called Sarmatians, began displacing Scythian political dominance on the open Pontic-Caspian steppe, moving westward from territory east of the Don River. The Sarmatians were not a single unified nation but a shifting set of related confederations, including groups Greco-Roman sources name as the Roxolani, the Iazyges, and, in later centuries, the Alans, whose relative prominence and territorial reach changed across the several centuries of Sarmatian ascendancy. Scythian power did not disappear from the region all at once with this Sarmatian expansion; a reduced Scythian polity, centered from the second century BCE on the fortified center of Scythian Neapolis near modern Simferopol, persisted in Crimea and parts of the lower Dnieper region from roughly the late third or early second century BCE into the third century CE, remaining significant well into the era of Roman contact even as Sarmatian confederations dominated the open steppe beyond Crimea.

Archaeologically, Sarmatian material culture shares much with the preceding Scythian tradition — kurgan burial, mounted warfare, animal-style decorative art — while also showing distinct developments, including, in later Sarmatian contexts, evidence for heavier cavalry equipment and, according to some Greco-Roman sources, greater emphasis on close-combat lance warfare alongside the mounted archery Scythian forces had emphasized. Ancient writers, including the Roman geographer Strabo and the historian Tacitus, describe Sarmatian society and warfare in terms that, while requiring the same critical caution applied to Herodotus's earlier Scythian account, broadly confirm a continuation of steppe pastoral economy and mounted military organization under new political leadership.

One of the most historically significant, if also historiographically complicated, features of Sarmatian society concerns the reported social and military role of women. Several ancient Greek and Roman sources describe Sarmatian, and specifically some groups sometimes identified with them, as societies in which women participated more directly in warfare, hunting, and religious ritual than was typical in the contemporary Mediterranean world, and ancient Greek writers connected such reports to their own legendary tradition of Amazons, warrior women supposedly inhabiting the region north of the Black Sea. Modern archaeological evidence — including the excavation of steppe burials, particularly from the Sarmatian period, containing weapons alongside female skeletal remains — has lent qualified support to the idea that at least some Sarmatian women held roles, including possible military roles, not typical of most contemporary sedentary Mediterranean societies. Historians nonetheless caution on two related points: first, that the specific, often fantastical, Greek Amazon legend should not be uncritically accepted as literal historical description of Sarmatian society; and second, that the presence of weapons in a woman's grave does not, by itself, prove that she used them in combat, since grave goods can also reflect status, ritual role, family position, or symbolic association rather than lived military activity. The legend, the archaeological evidence, and the question of actual combat participation should therefore be treated as related but analytically distinct questions.

Section 4.8

Nomads and Sedentary Societies

This section draws together the chapter's recurring theme: the relationship between mobile steppe pastoral societies and neighboring sedentary agricultural, urban, and imperial societies was neither simply one of unrelenting hostility nor one of untroubled cooperation, but a complex, historically variable mixture of trade, tribute, diplomacy, intermarriage, warfare, and cultural exchange, whose specific balance shifted with changing political and military circumstances on both sides.

Scythian and Sarmatian relations with the Greek Black Sea colonies, examined in Section 4.5, illustrate the cooperative dimension of this relationship: sustained commercial exchange, cultural interpenetration visible in hybrid art objects, and periods of diplomatic accommodation, including intermarriage between Greek colonial elites and neighboring steppe ruling families, particularly well documented for the Bosporan Kingdom. Scythian relations with Achaemenid Persia, examined in Section 4.3, illustrate the conflictual dimension: a major settled imperial power's campaign of conquest, according to Herodotus's account, defeated not by a decisive battlefield victory but by the strategic exploitation of steppe mobility and logistics.

Between these poles lay a wide range of intermediate relationships: tribute arrangements in which settled or semi-settled agricultural populations, including the "agricultural Scythians" of the forest-steppe Herodotus describes, paid tribute to a dominant steppe elite in exchange for protection or simply to avoid raiding; mercenary service, in which steppe warriors fought for pay in the armies of settled powers, including, in some documented cases, Greek city-states; and gradual absorption or displacement, as one steppe power succeeded another, as the Sarmatians succeeded the Scythians on the open steppe, without necessarily eliminating the preceding population but rather subordinating, absorbing, or pushing them into more restricted territory, as occurred with the persistent but reduced Scythian Neapolis polity examined in Section 4.7.

This pattern — variable, historically contingent interaction rather than fixed civilizational conflict — recurs throughout this textbook's later treatment of the steppe frontier: in the relationship between Rus' principalities and the Pechenegs and Cumans examined in Chapters 12 and 13, in Muscovite relations with the Golden Horde and its successor khanates examined in Chapters 17 and 19–24, and in the long history of Cossack, Russian, and Central Asian frontier interaction examined in later chapters. Students should carry this section's central lesson forward: steppe-sedentary relations require case-by-case historical explanation, not a single overarching narrative of eternal conflict between "civilization" and "barbarism."

Section 4.9

The Steppe in Later Historical Memory

The Scythians and Sarmatians did not disappear from historical memory when Sarmatian prominence on the open steppe itself eventually gave way to new steppe powers, including Goths and, later, Huns, in the centuries examined at the close of this chapter's Chronology Preview and picked up again in Chapters 5 and 6. Instead, "Scythian" became, in later Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and eventually medieval and early modern European usage, a somewhat loose and often anachronistic label frequently applied to a wide range of subsequent steppe and forest-zone peoples — including, at various points, Rus', Mongols, and even early modern Russians themselves — by outside observers drawing on classical literary convention rather than precise ethnographic or historical knowledge. Byzantine writers, for example, sometimes described the Rus' and other northern peoples using classicizing "Scythian" terminology inherited from ancient Greek ethnographic tradition, a usage that tells historians more about Byzantine literary convention than about any genuine historical continuity between ancient Scythians and medieval Rus'.

In the modern period, "Scythian" heritage has periodically been claimed by nationalist movements and popular writers across multiple modern nations occupying territory once dominated by Scythian or Sarmatian power, including at various points in Russian, Ukrainian, and other regional historical and cultural discourse, sometimes asserting direct ethnic or cultural descent from the ancient Scythians in order to establish claims of ancient territorial presence or cultural prestige. Consistent with the methodological caution this textbook established in Chapter 3 regarding archaeological cultures and modern national identity, such claims require the same critical scrutiny: the Scythians were an Iranian-speaking steppe people whose political and cultural world, while occupying territory later inhabited by Slavic-speaking and other populations, cannot be identified in any straightforward genetic, linguistic, or cultural line of descent with any single modern nation, since the intervening centuries brought further waves of migration, conquest, and population mixture that this textbook's later chapters will examine in detail.

The Scythians and Sarmatians also entered Western European artistic and literary imagination from the Renaissance onward, often as a symbol of remote, fierce, freedom-loving, or "barbarian" peoples of the distant steppe, a legacy visible in later European literature and art, and even in some strands of early twentieth-century Russian intellectual self-conception, including a self-consciously provocative Russian literary and artistic circle of the revolutionary era known as the Scythians, who invoked the ancient steppe people as a symbol of Russia's supposed elemental, non-Western, "Eurasian" vitality in deliberate contrast to Western European civilization. This episode belongs to the cultural and intellectual ferment of the revolutionary period rather than to the nineteenth-century intelligentsia debates examined elsewhere in this textbook, and it is examined further, together with other instances of later appropriation of ancient steppe identity, in this textbook's Historical Memory appendix.

Primary Source Window

Herodotus, Histories, Book Four (mid-5th century BCE)

Creator: Herodotus of Halicarnassus, Greek historian · Date of composition: mid-fifth century BCE · Type: Historical-ethnographic narrative

Context: Book Four of Herodotus's Histories describes Scythian geography, customs, religion, and the Persian invasion under Darius I. Herodotus never explicitly states that he personally visited Olbia, though many scholars, noting the density and specificity of his information about the northern Black Sea region, infer that he may have visited the colony and in any case clearly drew heavily on information circulating through the Olbian and wider northern Black Sea Greek world.

Brief paraphrase of the account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

Herodotus describes the Scythians as a people without fixed cities, who carry their households with them on wagons and rely on herds for subsistence, making them, in his assessment, extraordinarily difficult for any invading army to conquer, since they need not defend any fixed territory and can retreat indefinitely while denying an enemy access to food and water. He recounts that Darius's Persian army pursued the retreating Scythians across the steppe without ever forcing a decisive battle, and eventually withdrew after suffering supply shortages and harassment.

Analysis: Herodotus's account is invaluable as the fullest surviving ancient description of Scythian society, but it must be read critically. He was a Greek outsider dependent substantially on informants and, likely, translators, writing partly within a Greek literary tradition that used "barbarian" peoples as a foil for reflecting on Greek values and institutions. Some specific details he reports — including numerical claims and certain customs — cannot be independently verified and are treated with caution by modern historians, while other elements, including the general outline of Scythian mobile strategy against Darius and the practice of elaborate royal burial with sacrificed horses, find broad support in archaeological evidence, including the kurgan excavations examined in Section 4.6.

Questions for students:

  1. What advantages did Herodotus's account offer over purely archaeological evidence, and what limitations remain?
  2. Why might a Greek historian be inclined to present the Scythians as clever and admirable in a specific, narrow military sense while also describing them, elsewhere in his account, in less flattering terms?
  3. What kind of independent evidence would help historians evaluate the accuracy of Herodotus's account of Darius's campaign?
  4. How does Herodotus's description of Scythian mobility connect to the broader environmental and military analysis this chapter presents in Sections 4.1 and 4.4?
Historical Debate

How Reliable Is Herodotus on the Scythians?

The question: How much confidence should historians place in Herodotus's detailed account of Scythian customs, society, and the Darius campaign, given that he was a Greek outsider working substantially from secondhand information?

The skeptical position: Some historians emphasize that Herodotus's own text never claims he traveled onto the open steppe, that he relied on informants of uncertain reliability, wrote within a Greek literary tradition prone to exoticizing and moralizing about "barbarian" peoples, and reported specific numerical and narrative details — including the size of the Persian army and precise campaign movements — that cannot be independently confirmed and may be exaggerated or literary in character.
The corroborationist position: Other historians point out that where Herodotus's account can be checked against archaeological evidence — including burial customs, the general geography of Scythian territory, and the material culture of the "Royal Scythian" elite he describes — it holds up reasonably well, and argue that his access to Greek colonial informants, likely including those at Olbia, with direct commercial and diplomatic contact with Scythian society gives his broad outline real evidentiary value, even where individual details require skepticism.

Evidence: Historians weigh archaeological corroboration (kurgan excavation, settlement patterns), comparison with other ancient sources (including later Greek and Roman geographers), and internal analysis of Herodotus's own text for signs of literary convention versus reported observation.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree Herodotus preserves genuinely valuable information unavailable from any other ancient source and that his account should not be dismissed wholesale.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give specific numerical claims and narrative details, and over how far his schematic division of Scythian society into distinct named groups reflects social reality versus Greek organizing convention.

Student task: Identify one specific claim from Herodotus's account examined in this chapter that archaeological evidence appears to support, and one claim that cannot be independently verified, explaining the difference in evidentiary status between the two.

Multiple Perspectives

Steppe, Forest-Steppe, and Greek Colony

A Royal Scythian elite warrior: For a member of the ruling Scythian elite Herodotus describes, status rested on military prowess, control over tribute and trade, and elaborate display of wealth in life and in death, reflected in the gold-furnished kurgans examined in Section 4.6, and maintained through a combination of mounted warfare, diplomatic relationships with Greek colonial cities, and authority over subordinate populations, including agricultural communities of the forest-steppe.
An agricultural Scythian farming household in the forest-steppe: For a farming family in the fertile Dnieper forest-steppe, life combined the agricultural rhythms examined in Chapter 2 with a subordinate relationship to a dominant steppe elite, likely involving tribute obligations, but also participation in the trade networks that carried grain toward the Black Sea coast.
A Greek colonial merchant at Olbia or Panticapaeum: For a Greek merchant or craftsman in a Black Sea colonial city, Scythian-influenced territory represented both a vital commercial partner, supplying grain and furs in exchange for wine, pottery, and manufactured luxury goods, and a potential security concern, requiring careful diplomatic and commercial management of relations with neighboring steppe power.

These perspectives illustrate why the steppe-sedentary relationship examined throughout this chapter resists any single, simple characterization: cooperation, subordination, and periodic conflict coexisted within the same broad historical system.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Scythian Power

Long-Term Conditions

The environmental structure of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, favoring mobile pastoralism, and the earlier Bronze Age development of steppe horse herding and mobility examined in Chapter 3.

Intermediate Developments

The Iron Age intensification of mounted horseback warfare; the gradual, multiregional formation of Pontic Scythian populations through sustained contact and gene flow with eastern steppe populations, alongside the classical tradition's account of Cimmerian displacement.

Short-Term Triggers

Greek colonization of the Black Sea coast beginning in the late seventh century BCE, opening a lucrative grain and luxury-goods trade that Scythian elites could tax and profit from; the political consolidation of a "Royal Scythian" ruling elite.

Effects

Scythian prominence on the Pontic steppe for roughly four centuries; the accumulation of extraordinary elite wealth reflected in royal kurgan burials; Herodotus's account of the successful repulsion of Darius's Persian invasion around 513 BCE.

Historical complexity: No single cause—not mobility alone, not trade alone, not military technology alone—fully explains Scythian prominence; each factor reinforced the others.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Cimmerians to Sarmatians

What Stayed the Same

  • Mobile pastoral economy and mounted warfare remained the basic organizing features of steppe power across Cimmerian, Scythian, and Sarmatian dominance.
  • Kurgan burial and related elite display practices persisted, in modified form, across all three periods.
  • Sustained trade and diplomatic relationships with Black Sea Greek colonies continued under both Scythian and Sarmatian dominance.

What Changed

  • Political prominence passed from Cimmerian to Scythian to Sarmatian groups, each giving way to but not entirely erasing its predecessor, as the persistence of Scythian Neapolis in Crimea illustrates.
  • Military technology and tactics shifted somewhat, with later Sarmatian warfare showing greater emphasis on heavier cavalry equipment.
  • The social and military role attributed to women appears, on the available evidence, to have been more prominent in some Sarmatian contexts than is documented for the earlier Scythian period, though this evidence should be read cautiously.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to see steppe history as a genuine historical process of successive, related but distinct societies, rather than a single unchanging "nomadic" phenomenon.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Scythian Pastoral Household

For most Scythians, life centered not on royal courts and gold-furnished kurgans but on the ordinary rhythms of pastoral existence: moving herds of horses, cattle, and sheep seasonally in search of pasture and water, processing milk (including fermented mare's milk, a beverage Herodotus and later Greek writers describe among steppe peoples), preparing meat and hides, and maintaining wagons and portable dwellings suited to a mobile way of life. Archaeological evidence from more modest Scythian burials, containing simple weapons, tools, and personal ornaments rather than the extraordinary gold of royal kurgans, suggests a broad population of herders and warriors whose material life, while comfortable by the standards of the ancient steppe, differed enormously from that of the "Royal Scythian" elite.

Women's roles in ordinary Scythian households likely combined herding-related labor, food processing, textile production, and childcare, though direct evidence remains more limited than for elite burials; some scholars, drawing on the fuller Sarmatian evidence examined in Section 4.7, suggest that steppe women in general may have held somewhat more varied economic and, in some cases, military roles than was typical in contemporary sedentary Mediterranean societies, though this generalization should be treated cautiously given the unevenness of the surviving evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Sarmatian

Sarmatian is the collective name Greek and Roman writers applied to a set of related, but politically distinct, Iranian-speaking steppe confederations — including groups they named the Roxolani, the Iazyges, and, in later centuries, the Alans — who succeeded Scythian dominance of the open Pontic-Caspian steppe from roughly the third century BCE onward. Like "Scythian," the term functioned partly as an ethnonym and partly as a broad classical label; it should not be understood as naming a single centralized state or a uniform, unchanging people.

Sarmatian society shared much with the preceding Scythian tradition, including mobile pastoralism, kurgan burial, and animal-style art, while also showing distinct developments in weaponry and cavalry tactics, and, according to ancient testimony examined in Section 4.7, in the social and possibly military prominence of women. The term matters historically both because it names the dominant steppe power of the Pontic region during the period of growing Roman contact with the Black Sea world, and because later writers, including some Byzantine authors, occasionally extended "Sarmatian," much like "Scythian," as a loose classicizing label for later, unrelated peoples of the Eurasian steppe and forest zone.

Regional Context

The Pontic Steppe and Its Neighbors

The Pontic steppe examined in this chapter did not exist in isolation. To its west lay the lower Danube and, beyond it, the wider Balkan and Central European world; to its south lay the Black Sea and, through it, the entire Greek and later Roman Mediterranean world; to its east lay the Caucasus and the Caspian region, gateways toward Persia and the wider Iranian and Central Asian world with which, as this chapter has shown, Pontic Scythian populations shared substantial connections; and to its north lay the forest-steppe and forest zones examined in Chapter 2, home to agricultural populations whose relationship with steppe power this chapter has examined throughout.

This regional position would recur, in transformed guises, throughout later Eurasian history: the same Pontic steppe corridor would host Huns, Avars, Bulgars, Khazars, Pechenegs, Cumans, and Mongols in succession, examined in Chapters 5, 6, 13, and 16, each following a broadly similar pattern of mobile political and military organization interacting with neighboring settled powers to the north, south, east, and west.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Cimmerians, Scythians, and Sarmatians, the first historically documented societies of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, spanning roughly the ninth century BCE to the fourth century CE. Unlike the exclusively archaeological evidence of Chapter 3, this chapter's evidentiary base combines Greek textual sources, above all Herodotus, with the archaeological record of settlements and, especially, elaborately furnished royal kurgan burials.

The chapter traced the succession of steppe powers: poorly documented Cimmerian prominence, well-attested Scythian dominance from the seventh century BCE, and Sarmatian ascendancy from the third century BCE onward, emphasizing continuity in pastoral economy and mounted warfare alongside genuine political and, to some degree, military change across this sequence, while cautioning that ancient DNA evidence favors a gradual, multiregional formation of Pontic Scythian populations rather than a single decisive migratory wave.

It examined Scythian political and social organization as described by Herodotus, including the distinction between a dominant "Royal Scythian" elite and subordinate agricultural and other populations, and analyzed the economic and military foundations of Scythian power: mobile pastoralism, composite-bow and akinakes-armed mounted warfare, and control over long-distance trade linking the forest-steppe to the Greek Black Sea colonial world.

The chapter gave sustained attention to Greek colonization of the northern Black Sea coast, distinguishing the earlier, late seventh/sixth-century founding of Olbia and Panticapaeum from the later, fifth-century founding of Chersonesus, and presenting these colonies as commercially and culturally interconnected with, rather than simply opposed to, neighboring Scythian power. It examined the extraordinary gold work of royal kurgans such as Chertomlyk and Tovsta Mohyla as evidence for both Scythian elite wealth and substantial, though not fully resolved, Greek-Scythian artistic collaboration.

Sarmatian expansion was examined as both a continuation of Scythian-period steppe pastoral warfare and a distinct political and, in some respects, social development, including the historically reported and archaeologically attested, but not conclusively proven, prominence of some women in Sarmatian society and warfare, alongside the persistence of a reduced Scythian polity centered on Scythian Neapolis in Crimea for several centuries after Sarmatian confederations came to dominate the open steppe.

The chapter's central analytical theme—explored directly in Section 4.8 and reinforced throughout—held that steppe-sedentary relations combined trade, tribute, diplomacy, intermarriage, and warfare in historically variable combinations, resisting any single narrative of permanent civilizational conflict.

Finally, the chapter examined the later historical memory and repeated appropriation of "Scythian" identity, from Byzantine classicizing usage through modern nationalist and artistic movements, cautioning against uncritical claims of direct descent from the ancient Scythians by any single modern nation.

These lessons—critical use of outside textual evidence, careful reading of archaeological corroboration, attention to the full range of steppe-sedentary interaction, and skepticism toward nationalist appropriation of ancient peoples—prepare students directly for Chapter 5's introduction of the Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples who inhabited this region before the formation of Rus', and for Chapter 6's examination of the Khazars, Volga Bulgars, and Varangian trade networks.

A loose political and military alliance of related but distinct groups, the likely organizational form of Sarmatian society.

Speakers of Iranian branch Indo-European languages, the linguistic classification generally applied to the Scythians and Sarmatians.
Herodotus
Fifth-century BCE Greek historian whose Histories, Book Four, is the principal ancient textual source on the Scythians.
Ethnography
The descriptive study of a people's customs and society; Herodotus's Scythian account is an early example, requiring critical evaluation.
Emporion
A Greek trading place, market, or port serving as a hub of exchange with a neighboring inland population.
Grain trade
The export of forest-steppe grain to Mediterranean markets via Black Sea Greek colonies, chiefly the Bosporan Kingdom, a source of profit that Scythian elites could tax or control.
Scythia
The Greek name for the territory associated with Scythian power, roughly the Pontic steppe and adjacent forest-steppe.
Sarmatians
A related Iranian-speaking steppe confederation succeeding Scythian dominance of the open steppe from roughly the third century BCE.
Alans
A later Sarmatian-associated group, prominent especially from the early centuries CE onward.
Amazon legend
A Greek mythological tradition of warrior women, connected by some ancient and modern observers to reports of Sarmatian women's roles, though grave goods alone do not prove combat activity.
Barbarian (Greek usage)
A Greek term for non-Greek-speaking peoples, carrying cultural and literary connotations that shape, and sometimes distort, Greek accounts such as Herodotus's.
Scythian gold
The extraordinary quantity of worked gold recovered from Scythian royal kurgans, much of it showing strong Greek technical and iconographic influence though precise workshop origins remain debated.
Bosporan Kingdom
A Greek polity centered on Panticapaeum, engaged in sustained commercial and dynastic relations with neighboring Scythian and Sarmatian elites and a key intermediary in the Black Sea grain trade.
Akinakes
A short, usually straight, double-edged sword or dagger characteristic of Scythian close-combat weaponry, generally around fifty to seventy centimeters in length.

Fortified center of the persistent Late Scythian polity in Crimea, founded in the second century BCE.

MGU Library Research Project

Investigating a Scythian Royal Kurgan

This project asks you to investigate one Scythian royal kurgan—such as Chertomlyk or Tovsta Mohyla—in greater depth, evaluating what its material evidence reveals and how it corroborates or complicates Herodotus's textual account.

  1. Select a kurgan. Choose one royal Scythian burial site discussed in this chapter or identified through library research.
  2. Investigate the material evidence. Research the burial's structure, grave goods, and any human or animal remains, noting which parts of the burial were disturbed or robbed in antiquity and which were found intact.
  3. Compare to Herodotus. Identify specific claims in Herodotus's account that this evidence supports, contradicts, or cannot address.
  4. Evaluate cross-cultural production. Research whether any objects show evidence of Greek manufacture or Greek-Scythian collaboration, and note whether their specific workshop origin is considered settled or still debated.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, describe the site, evaluate the evidence, and explain what can and cannot be responsibly concluded about Scythian society from this single burial.

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's ancient Eurasian archaeology collection, academic databases on Scythian and Sarmatian archaeology, and translated editions of Herodotus's Histories.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students from the dim, largely archaeological Cimmerian world through the richly documented Scythians to their Sarmatian successors, marking this textbook's first sustained encounter with the combination of textual and material evidence that will characterize most of its remaining chapters. The Scythians and Sarmatians were neither simple raiders nor the direct ancestors of any single modern nation; they were sophisticated, historically consequential societies whose mobile pastoral economy, mounted warfare, and long-distance trade shaped the Pontic-Caspian steppe for roughly a millennium.

The chapter's central methodological lesson—that steppe-sedentary relations require case-by-case explanation rather than a single narrative of civilizational conflict—will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in later chapters examining the Khazars and Volga Bulgars, the relationship between Rus' and the Pechenegs and Cumans, and the Mongol conquest.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 5's introduction of the Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples who inhabited this wider region before the formation of Rus', students should carry forward this chapter's critical approach to outside textual evidence, its attention to the full range of steppe-sedentary interaction, and its skepticism toward nationalist appropriation of ancient peoples for modern political purposes.
