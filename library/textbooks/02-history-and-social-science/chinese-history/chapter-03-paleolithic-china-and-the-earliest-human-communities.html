Paleolithic China and the Earliest Human Communities

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Long before any dynasty, any city, any written character, or any name that could be translated as "China," small bands of human beings and their evolutionary relatives were already living, hunting, gathering, and adapting across the landscapes this textbook studies — for a span of time so much longer than recorded history that the entire historical span from the Shang period to the present occupies only the last sliver of a much deeper human story.

In 1929, a young Chinese paleontologist named Pei Wenzhong knelt in the dust of a limestone cave near a village called Zhoukoudian, not far from present-day Beijing, and carefully lifted a fossilized human skullcap from the sediment where it had lain buried for hundreds of thousands of years. The discovery, which came to be associated with the name "Peking Man," electrified scientists around the world and placed China at the center of a global conversation about human origins that continues today. But the story this chapter tells did not begin with that skullcap, and it does not end there either. It begins much earlier, with the movements of early hominins across the landmass of Asia hundreds of thousands, and by some interpretations well over a million, years before the present, and it extends forward through tens of thousands of years of anatomically modern human life before any of the developments discussed in later chapters — agriculture, villages, writing, kingship — had yet appeared.

This chapter asks students to set aside, for a time, the great themes of dynasty, empire, and civilization that will dominate the rest of this textbook, and to consider instead a much longer and, in some ways, much stranger period: the Paleolithic, or "Old Stone Age," during which small, mobile communities of hunter-gatherers and their hominin ancestors adapted to the varied and changing environments of East Asia using tools made of stone, bone, wood, and other perishable materials, most of which have long since decayed. For the greater part of this period, no writing existed anywhere in the world, and for most of it, no permanent villages, no farming, and no metal tools existed either. Understanding this period requires a different set of historical tools than those used to study, say, the Han dynasty or the Tang dynasty. Archaeology, geology, paleoanthropology, and the natural sciences take the place of court records and philosophical texts, because for the overwhelming majority of the Paleolithic, no other kind of evidence survives.

Why should a history of Chinese civilization begin so far back — hundreds of thousands of years before anything resembling a state, a city, or an agricultural village existed in the region? The answer is that human beings did not arrive in the lands later associated with China as a blank slate the moment agriculture or writing appeared. The peoples who eventually developed millet farming in the north and rice farming in the south, discussed in Chapter 4, were themselves descended from, or closely related to, communities of hunter-gatherers who had already spent an immense span of time learning the rhythms, resources, dangers, and possibilities of the East Asian landscape. The deep human past shaped later history in less visible but still important ways: it established long-term patterns of regional adaptation to different environments, it left behind the very physical spaces — river valleys, cave systems, mountain corridors — that later societies would continue to inhabit, and it provides a crucial reminder that "Chinese history," in the broadest sense, is inseparable from the broader story of human evolution and migration across the earth.

This chapter also introduces students to some of the most contested and actively debated questions in the study of human origins. Did modern humans in East Asia descend, at least in part, from earlier hominin populations that had lived in the region for hundreds of thousands of years, or did anatomically modern Homo sapiens who evolved in Africa migrate into East Asia and largely, or entirely, replace earlier populations there? This question, sometimes framed as a debate between "multiregional continuity" and "recent African origin" models of human evolution, has been argued over by paleoanthropologists and geneticists for decades, and it has also, at times, become entangled with modern national pride and political sensitivities, since some popular Chinese accounts have emphasized continuity between ancient hominin fossils such as those found at Zhoukoudian and modern East Asian populations, while the great majority of current genetic evidence points toward a predominantly African origin for all anatomically modern humans, including those in East Asia, with more limited and complex patterns of interbreeding with earlier hominin groups. This chapter presents this debate carefully, using it as an opportunity to teach students how scientific evidence, including genetic evidence unavailable to earlier generations of scholars, can reshape long-standing historical and scientific narratives.

Consider, too, what the study of Paleolithic China asks historians to give up: the certainty of names, dates, and individual biographies that later chapters will offer in abundance. No Paleolithic inhabitant of the caves at Zhoukoudian left behind a name, a spoken sentence, or a personal account of daily life. What survives are stone tools shaped by human hands, fragments of bone bearing cut marks left by ancient butchery, traces of ancient hearths, and the fossilized remains of the people themselves. From this fragmentary evidence, archaeologists and paleoanthropologists reconstruct, with great care and considerable uncertainty, how these ancient communities lived: what they ate, how they made and used tools, how they responded to a dramatically changing climate across hundreds of thousands of years of alternating glacial and interglacial periods, and how, over an immense span of time, human populations gradually spread across, and adapted to, the tremendous environmental diversity of the East Asian landmass described in Chapter 2.

Finally, this chapter situates China within a genuinely global story. The hominins and early modern humans who inhabited the region during the Paleolithic were part of a much larger pattern of human dispersal across Africa, Europe, and Asia, and the archaeological record in China — including some of the most important and long-studied hominin fossil sites anywhere in the world — has contributed enormously to scientific understanding of human evolution as a worldwide, not merely a regional, process. Reading Chinese history back to its very beginning therefore means reading it, from the outset, as a chapter within the still longer history of humanity as a whole, a theme students should carry forward as they encounter, in later chapters, the increasingly distinct regional societies, states, and civilizations that eventually developed from these ancient beginnings.

By the end of this chapter, students should be equipped to think carefully and critically about how historians and scientists reconstruct a period with no written sources at all, to understand the major archaeological sites and fossil discoveries associated with Paleolithic China, and to appreciate both the achievements and the deep uncertainties that surround the study of human origins in East Asia — a foundation that will make the transition to Neolithic agriculture in Chapter 4, and to early complex societies in Chapter 5, considerably easier to understand in proper historical context.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the Paleolithic period is and why it constitutes the vast majority of the human presence in the region later associated with China.
  2. Describe the major categories of evidence — fossil, lithic, faunal, and geological — that archaeologists and paleoanthropologists use to study Paleolithic China.
  3. Trace the discovery, excavation, and scientific significance of the Zhoukoudian site and the fossils historically associated with "Peking Man."
  4. Distinguish between different hominin species and populations discussed in the East Asian fossil record, including earlier hominins and anatomically modern Homo sapiens.
  5. Evaluate the scientific debate over human origins in East Asia, explaining why neither the older multiregional continuity model nor a strict replacement model fully matches current evidence, and how the current African-origin-with-admixture synthesis differs from both.
  6. Analyze the development of stone tool technologies in East Asia and compare them, where relevant, to stone tool traditions elsewhere in the world.
  7. Describe hunting, gathering, and other subsistence strategies used by Paleolithic communities in different regions of East Asia.
  8. Explain how Ice Age climate fluctuations shaped patterns of human settlement, migration, and adaptation in the region.
  9. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of archaeological dating methods, including stratigraphy and radiometric techniques, for reconstructing the deep past.
  10. Contextualize Paleolithic China within the broader global story of human evolution and migration.
  11. Identify continuities and discontinuities between Paleolithic communities and the Neolithic societies discussed in Chapter 4.
  12. Assess why questions of human origins in China have sometimes intersected with modern national narratives, and why scientific caution is necessary when interpreting ancient fossil evidence.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 introduced the tools historians use to study Chinese history critically, and Chapter 2 surveyed the physical geography within which that history unfolds. This chapter applies both sets of tools to the very beginning of the human story in East Asia — a period so far removed from written history that historians must rely almost entirely on archaeology, paleontology, geology, and, increasingly, genetics to reconstruct it.

The Paleolithic, or "Old Stone Age," is conventionally divided by archaeologists into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases, a scheme originally developed from European evidence and organized primarily around changes in stone tool industries. Students should understand that this three-part scheme is a heuristic archaeological periodization, not a biological evolutionary sequence: it does not map hominin species neatly onto "phases," and its application to China is itself debated, with some specialists arguing that a simpler two-stage Early and Late Paleolithic framework better fits the East Asian evidence than the European-derived tripartite scheme. In global terms, the Lower Paleolithic extends back well over two million years in Africa, while in East Asia the earliest currently well-supported archaeological evidence of hominin presence dates to approximately 2.1 million years ago, based on stone artifacts recovered from the Loess Plateau, discussed further in Section 3.1.

It is essential for students to understand that dates for this period are almost always approximate, frequently revised as new discoveries and new dating techniques emerge, and often the subject of ongoing scientific debate. Unlike the chronology of the Han or Tang dynasties, which can often be fixed to a specific year through court records and calendrical systems, Paleolithic chronology is typically expressed in ranges of tens of thousands or hundreds of thousands of years, with real uncertainty at both ends of any given range. This chapter uses cautious language — "approximately," "estimated," "the evidence suggests" — throughout, and students should adopt the same cautious habits of expression in their own writing about this period.

Geographically, the story of Paleolithic China unfolds across the same diverse landscape introduced in Chapter 2, though during much of the Paleolithic period the climate, sea levels, vegetation, and even the outlines of coastlines differed considerably from those of the present day. During colder glacial periods, global sea levels dropped significantly as water became locked in ice sheets, at times expanding continental shelves, connecting some islands and landmasses that are separated by water today, and narrowing important straits — though not eliminating every maritime barrier, since even at the height of the most recent Ice Age the crossing to the Japanese islands still required travel across open water — and shaping patterns of both human and animal migration across a landscape considerably different from the one described in Chapter 2. During warmer interglacial periods, conditions shifted again, sometimes dramatically, altering the distribution of forests, grasslands, and available food resources across the region.

Students should also understand that "China" as a geographic or political concept, as Chapter 1 explained, did not exist in any meaningful sense during the Paleolithic. The hominins and early modern humans discussed in this chapter did not think of themselves as living in a place called "China," did not organize themselves into anything resembling a state, and would not have recognized any of the political or cultural categories later chapters will introduce. This chapter studies the deep human past of a geographic region — the landmass later encompassed, at various times and to varying degrees, within states associated with Chinese history — not the early history of an already-existing "Chinese people" or "Chinese civilization" projected backward in time. Historians and scientists take particular care on this point, since some popular and nationalist narratives have, at times, sought to draw a direct, unbroken line from ancient hominin fossils to modern national identity, a claim that the evidence, examined carefully in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, does not straightforwardly support.

Section 3.1

The Earliest Human Presence

Establishing when hominins first arrived in the region later associated with China is one of the most difficult and actively contested questions in the field of paleoanthropology. Unlike questions about, for example, the founding of the Han dynasty, which can be answered with a specific year drawn from surviving records, questions about the earliest hominin presence in East Asia must be answered through the patient accumulation and reinterpretation of physical evidence — stone tools, fossilized bones, and the geological layers in which these materials are found — much of which is fragmentary, difficult to date precisely, and open to more than one reasonable scientific interpretation.

For much of the twentieth century, the East Asian fossil and archaeological record was thought to represent a relatively later chapter in the story of human dispersal out of Africa, generally dated to within the last one million years or so. Excavations on the Loess Plateau in Shaanxi province, however, have since documented a long sequence of in-situ stone artifacts at the site of Shangchen, with the earliest layers dated using paleomagnetic methods to approximately 2.1 million years ago. This evidence currently constitutes the earliest widely accepted archaeological evidence of hominin presence in East Asia, and it is routinely cited by specialists as among the earliest well-documented evidence of hominins anywhere outside Africa. Students should still apply appropriate scientific caution: palaeomagnetic chronology always carries interpretive assumptions, no hominin fossils have been recovered at Shangchen, so the identity of the toolmakers who produced these artifacts remains unknown, and any single very early date of this kind should be understood as a current best estimate rather than a final, unrevisable conclusion.

Other sites provide somewhat more numerous and geographically distributed evidence, though generally somewhat later in date. Stone tool assemblages and, in some cases, associated fossil remains from sites in northern China, the Loess Plateau, and parts of southwestern China have produced age estimates ranging from roughly 1.2 million to 780,000 years ago, a range accepted by most specialists as demonstrating a sustained, if not necessarily continuous, hominin presence in the region well before the more famous and more extensively documented occupation at Zhoukoudian discussed in Section 3.3.

Students should draw two lessons from this section. First, the deep human past of East Asia extends back an extraordinarily long time — likely well over a million years, and possibly more than two million years, before the present — a span of time so much longer than the roughly three-thousand-year span of documented Chinese history that it can be difficult to grasp intuitively. Second, and just as importantly, the precise dating and interpretation of the earliest sites remain genuinely uncertain and actively debated among specialists, and students should resist the temptation to treat any single date as a settled, final answer. Scientific understanding of the earliest human presence in East Asia continues to change as new sites are discovered, as existing sites are re-examined using improved dating technologies, and as genetic and fossil evidence from around the world is compared and re-interpreted.

Section 3.2

Paleolithic Archaeology in China

The systematic archaeological study of the Paleolithic period in China began in the early twentieth century, initially led by a combination of Chinese and international scientists working together, often under difficult political and logistical conditions, including foreign concessions, warlord conflict, and eventually full-scale war. Since that time, Chinese Paleolithic archaeology has grown into a substantial and increasingly sophisticated scientific field, supported by government research institutions, universities, and international scientific collaboration, and it has produced one of the richest and most geographically extensive Paleolithic archaeological records anywhere in the world.

Paleolithic archaeologists working in China study several overlapping types of evidence. Lithic, or stone tool, assemblages provide the most abundant and durable form of evidence, since stone survives in the archaeological record far better than bone, wood, or other organic materials. Fossilized hominin remains, though far rarer than stone tools, provide direct physical evidence of the hominins themselves, allowing scientists to study anatomical features, estimate age and sex, and, in some cases, extract genetic material for further analysis. Faunal remains — the fossilized bones of animals that lived alongside, and were often hunted or scavenged by, ancient hominins — help scientists reconstruct ancient environments, climates, and diets. And geological evidence, including the layered sediments in which artifacts and fossils are found, allows scientists to establish relative and, increasingly, absolute chronologies for these remains.

China's Paleolithic archaeological record is notable for its geographic breadth, encompassing sites across the North China Plain and Loess Plateau, the mountainous southwest, the Yangtze basin, and more scattered evidence from other regions described in Chapter 2. This geographic spread demonstrates that hominins and, later, modern humans adapted to a wide range of environments across the region over an immense span of time, rather than remaining confined to a single core area. At the same time, the archaeological record is uneven: some regions and time periods are far better documented than others, reflecting both genuine differences in ancient population density and, just as importantly, differences in where and how intensively archaeological research has been conducted. Students should be cautious about assuming that regions or periods with fewer known sites were necessarily less inhabited; in many cases, they may simply be less thoroughly studied, or their evidence may have been destroyed by erosion, later construction, or unfavorable preservation conditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Paleolithic

The Paleolithic, or "Old Stone Age," is the name archaeologists give to the earliest and by far the longest period of human technological history, characterized by the use of stone tools produced without polishing or grinding, generally by striking one stone against another to remove sharp flakes or to shape a core into a usable implement. The Paleolithic began with the earliest known stone tool use, more than three million years ago in parts of Africa, and, in most regions of the world including East Asia, ended with the emergence of settled agricultural societies and, often, new stone tool technologies such as polished and ground stone tools associated with the Neolithic period discussed in Chapter 4.

Archaeologists commonly divide the Paleolithic into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases, based primarily on changes in stone tool technology and, where available, associated hominin fossil evidence. These divisions are useful organizing tools, but students should understand that the transitions between them were gradual, uneven across different regions, and do not correspond to sharp, universally applicable dates. In China specifically, archaeologists sometimes describe regional stone tool traditions that developed somewhat differently from the classic sequences first defined in Europe and Africa, a point discussed further in Section 3.5.

Section 3.3

Zhoukoudian and Peking Man

No single site has shaped the study of Paleolithic China as profoundly as Zhoukoudian, a complex of limestone caves located roughly forty kilometers southwest of Beijing. Beginning with early exploratory work in the 1920s and continuing through more intensive excavations led by the Chinese paleontologist Pei Wenzhong from 1929 onward, Zhoukoudian yielded an extraordinary assemblage of hominin fossils, animal bones, and stone tools that transformed scientific understanding of human evolution in East Asia and established China as a central location for global paleoanthropological research.

The fossils recovered at Zhoukoudian, popularly known as "Peking Man," were initially classified by the Canadian anatomist Davidson Black, who worked in China during this period, and were later studied extensively by Chinese and international scientists. These fossils are now generally classified within the species Homo erectus, an early hominin species found at sites across Africa and Asia, and the Zhoukoudian material remains, decades later, one of the largest and most thoroughly documented Homo erectus fossil assemblages anywhere in the world, including multiple skulls, teeth, and other skeletal remains representing dozens of individuals across a long span of time, generally estimated to range from roughly 780,000 to 300,000 years ago, based on a combination of geological, paleomagnetic, cosmogenic isotope, and other dating methods; cosmogenic isotope dating published in 2009 secured the lower deposits at approximately 770,000 years ago. A major study published in 2026 further reported the successful extraction and analysis of ancient enamel proteins from several Chinese Homo erectus specimens, including material from Zhoukoudian — a significant methodological advance, since proteins can sometimes survive in fossil enamel far longer than DNA does, opening a new avenue for studying the biological relationships of East Asian Homo erectus even where ancient DNA cannot be recovered.

Excavations at Zhoukoudian also revealed extensive stone tool assemblages, animal bones bearing evidence of butchery, and, more controversially, deposits that early excavators interpreted as evidence of the controlled use of fire, including apparent ash layers and burned bone. This interpretation, once widely accepted as evidence that Homo erectus at Zhoukoudian regularly used fire for warmth, cooking, or protection, has been revisited by later researchers using more rigorous taphonomic methods — the scientific study of how bones and other materials become altered, buried, and preserved after death or deposition. Some of these later studies have raised questions about whether all of the deposits originally interpreted as evidence of fire use were, in fact, produced by hominin activity rather than by natural processes such as spontaneous combustion of organic material, bat guano decomposition, or other non-human causes. This reassessment is an important lesson in scientific self-correction: an initially confident interpretation, based on the best available evidence and methods at the time, was later reconsidered as new analytical techniques became available, and current scholarly opinion is more cautious about the extent and regularity of fire use at Zhoukoudian than the earliest excavation reports suggested, even though evidence for fire use by Homo erectus does exist at some other sites worldwide.

The historical fate of the original Zhoukoudian fossils adds a poignant, and instructive, chapter to the site's story. With Beijing already under Japanese occupation since 1937 and tensions between the United States and Japan escalating sharply in late 1941, Chinese and American scientists arranged to transfer the original fossils out of the city into American custody for safekeeping. The fossils disappeared during transit under wartime conditions and have never been recovered, despite extensive searches conducted over the following decades. Fortunately, detailed casts, photographs, and scientific descriptions made before the fossils' disappearance have allowed research to continue, and additional fossil material discovered at Zhoukoudian and related sites in subsequent decades has further expanded the scientific record. The loss of the original fossils is frequently cited as one of the great unsolved mysteries of twentieth-century science, and it also illustrates how war, political upheaval, and historical contingency can directly affect even the physical evidence historians and scientists rely upon.

Section 3.4

Homo sapiens in East Asia

The emergence and spread of anatomically modern Homo sapiens in East Asia represents one of the most scientifically significant, and most actively debated, chapters in the region's deep history. The overwhelming majority of current genetic evidence, drawn from studies of both modern human populations and ancient DNA recovered from fossil remains, supports a model in which all anatomically modern humans, including those who eventually populated East Asia, share a common origin among populations that evolved in Africa, with subsequent migration and dispersal across Eurasia, Australia, and eventually the Americas. Earlier, smaller dispersals of Homo sapiens beyond Africa appear to have occurred before 100,000 years ago, but genetic evidence indicates that the major, ancestry-forming dispersal from which most present-day non-African populations descend occurred more recently, generally placed by current research at roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, with more widespread and stable colonization of Eurasia well underway by approximately 45,000 years ago.

At the same time, the fossil and archaeological record in East Asia includes several sites with more debated and complex implications, and students benefit from distinguishing two broad categories of evidence. The first category consists of earlier, more contested claims: fossil remains from sites such as Fuyan Cave in southern China have, in some cases, been interpreted by their discoverers as showing anatomically modern features at dates in the range of roughly 120,000 to 80,000 years ago, considerably earlier than the most widely accepted models of modern human dispersal into East Asia would predict; these early dates remain actively debated among specialists and are not yet treated as securely established. The second category consists of later, more securely dated evidence: sites such as Shiyu in northern China, with a sophisticated toolkit dated to approximately 45,000 years ago and associated by its excavators with Homo sapiens, alongside Tianyuan Cave, dated to approximately 40,800 to 38,100 years ago, and the Upper Cave deposits at Zhoukoudian, dated to approximately 39,000 to 36,300 years ago, provide considerably firmer evidence for widespread Homo sapiens populations across the region. A high-profile earlier case illustrates the value of this caution: the well-known Liujiang skeleton from southern China, once reported in some accounts as tens of thousands of years older, was redated in 2024 to approximately 33,000 to 23,000 years ago, placing it comfortably within the securely documented range rather than among the earliest disputed claims. Even some of the more recent, better-documented evidence remains subject to scholarly disagreement: the classification of the Shiyu assemblage as an "Initial Upper Paleolithic" industry, for example, has itself generated published debate among specialists over how the site should be categorized.

Genetic evidence has also revealed that modern human populations, including ancestors of East Asian populations, carry small but measurable amounts of DNA inherited from interbreeding with other, now-extinct hominin populations, including Neanderthals, whose range extended primarily across western Eurasia, and Denisovans, a hominin population identified initially through genetic analysis of fossil remains from Siberia and later confirmed at Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau, where Denisovan-associated remains and proteins have been documented across a long span extending from roughly 160,000 to perhaps as recently as 45,000 years ago. Denisovan genetic legacy is measurable in various East and Southeast Asian populations today, though it is present in smaller amounts and reflects a more complicated history of multiple, distinct introgression events than once assumed; the largest known proportions of Denisovan ancestry are instead found among populations in New Guinea and other parts of Oceania. This evidence complicates any simple story of modern humans "replacing" earlier hominins in East Asia without any biological connection whatsoever; rather, current scientific understanding suggests a more complex pattern in which expanding modern human populations occasionally interbred with earlier hominin groups they encountered, leaving a small but real genetic legacy in some present-day populations, while the overwhelming majority of ancestry in modern East Asian populations, as in populations worldwide, traces to the African-origin modern human dispersal.

This section's central lesson is one of appropriate scientific caution and humility. The precise sequence of hominin and modern human occupation, replacement, and interbreeding in East Asia remains an active area of ongoing research, with new fossil discoveries, new genetic studies, and new dating techniques regularly refining or revising earlier conclusions. Students encountering popular or older textbook claims about "Peking Man being the direct ancestor of modern Chinese people" should understand that this specific claim is not supported by the great majority of current genetic evidence, which instead points to a predominantly African-origin ancestry for all modern human populations, including those in East Asia, alongside the smaller and more complex patterns of ancient interbreeding described above. This chapter's Historical Debate feature examines this question in greater depth.

Historical Vocabulary
Hominin

Hominin is the scientific term for the group of species that includes modern humans and our closest fossil relatives after the evolutionary split from the ancestors of chimpanzees and bonobos, our closest living primate relatives. The hominin fossil record includes many different species across several million years, including early African hominins, later species such as Homo erectus, and eventually anatomically modern Homo sapiens. Not all hominin species were direct ancestors of modern humans; many represent side branches of the human evolutionary tree that ultimately went extinct without descendants.

When discussing fossils such as those found at Zhoukoudian, scientists use the term hominin carefully, distinguishing between a fossil's classification within a broader evolutionary tree and any claim about direct ancestry to modern human populations. Homo erectus, the species to which the Zhoukoudian fossils are generally assigned, is understood by most current researchers to represent an earlier branch of the human evolutionary tree that does not appear, based on current genetic evidence, to be the direct ancestor of modern East Asian populations in any simple or exclusive sense, even though Homo erectus populations lived in the region for an extremely long span of time before eventually disappearing from the fossil record.

Section 3.5

Stone Tools and Technology

Stone tools provide the single most abundant, and often the most informative, category of evidence available to archaeologists studying Paleolithic China. Because stone survives burial, weathering, and the passage of hundreds of thousands of years far better than bone, wood, or other organic materials, lithic assemblages allow scientists to trace long-term patterns of technological change even in the absence of associated fossil remains.

Early stone tool technologies in East Asia, as elsewhere in the world, generally involved simple techniques for striking flakes from a stone core using another stone as a hammer, producing sharp-edged flakes and choppers useful for tasks such as butchering animals, processing plant material, and working wood or bone. Over the course of the Paleolithic, stone tool technologies in East Asia did not develop along a single, steadily rising ladder of sophistication culminating only in the Upper Paleolithic; instead, the evidence shows a more uneven pattern marked by periods of genuine innovation, long stretches of technological persistence, regional specialization suited to local raw materials and tasks, and, in some cases, the recombination of existing techniques into new forms. Some of this innovation occurred far earlier than once assumed: prepared-core technology, in which a stone core is carefully shaped in advance so that a flake of a predetermined size and form can be struck from it, is documented at sites in northern China dating to the Early Pleistocene, considerably earlier than the technique's appearance was once thought possible in East Asia.

A particularly important example of this uneven pattern comes from Xigou, a site in central China with an occupation sequence dated to roughly 160,000 to 72,000 years ago. Excavations there have documented organized core reduction, a diverse range of specialized tools, and what the excavating team has identified as the earliest known evidence of hafted, or composite, tools in eastern Asia — implements in which a stone tool was mounted onto a handle or shaft made of wood, bone, or another material, a technique requiring planning, the use of adhesives or bindings, and a more complex production sequence than simple handheld tools. This evidence directly challenges an older assumption, once common in the field, that significant technological complexity did not appear in China until close to the end of the Paleolithic, roughly 40,000 years ago, alongside the arrival of fully modern Homo sapiens. The Xigou evidence instead demonstrates that considerable technological sophistication existed in the region tens of thousands of years earlier, associated with hominin populations whose precise taxonomic identity remains under study.

One long-standing scholarly discussion concerning East Asian Paleolithic archaeology concerns the relative rarity, compared to some assemblages in Africa and western Eurasia, of certain more standardized bifacial tool forms, such as the classic symmetrical handaxes associated with the Acheulean tradition of stone tool manufacture. Early and mid-twentieth-century scholars sometimes interpreted this apparent difference as evidence that East Asian hominin populations were technologically or cognitively less advanced than their African and western Eurasian counterparts — an interpretation now widely rejected by specialists as both scientifically unsupported and reflective of outdated and biased assumptions about human development. More recent research has instead emphasized that East Asian stone tool traditions likely reflect a combination of factors, including differences in the availability and quality of suitable stone raw material in different regions, differences in the kinds of tasks tools were needed for in different environments, and possibly the greater use, in East Asian contexts, of tools made from bamboo, wood, and other perishable materials that have not survived in the archaeological record but that may have served functions similar to some stone tool types used elsewhere. This reinterpretation illustrates an important historical thinking skill: recognizing when an apparent difference in the archaeological record reflects genuine historical variation rather than differences in preservation, sampling, or the biased assumptions of earlier researchers.

By the Upper Paleolithic period, generally associated with fully modern Homo sapiens populations, stone tool technologies across East Asia, as in many other regions of the world, became markedly more diverse and specialized, including smaller, more finely worked blade and microlith technologies — very small, carefully shaped stone tools often mounted in composite tools made from wood, bone, or antler. Some Upper Paleolithic sites in China have also yielded evidence of tools made from bone and antler, ornamental objects such as perforated shells and beads, and, at a few sites, pigments that may have been used for symbolic or decorative purposes, suggesting an increasing degree of behavioral and cultural complexity among modern human populations in the region during the final millennia of the Paleolithic period.

Section 3.6

Hunting, Gathering, and Adaptation

Paleolithic communities across East Asia obtained food through a combination of hunting, scavenging, fishing, and the gathering of wild plant foods, with the specific balance among these strategies varying considerably depending on regional environment, season, and available technology. Faunal remains recovered from sites such as Zhoukoudian include the bones of a wide range of animals, including deer, horses, and other large mammals, some bearing cut marks consistent with butchery by stone tools, providing direct evidence that hominins at the site consumed meat, whether obtained through active hunting, scavenging of animals killed by other predators, or some combination of both strategies, a question that remains debated for specific assemblages.

Plant foods almost certainly formed an important, though archaeologically less visible, part of the diet for most Paleolithic communities, since plant remains generally preserve far less well than bone or stone in the archaeological record. Nuts, seeds, tubers, and other wild plant foods likely provided essential nutrition, particularly in regions and seasons where hunting yields were unpredictable, and ethnographic studies of more recent hunter-gatherer societies worldwide suggest that gathered plant foods often supplied a larger proportion of caloric intake than commonly assumed in popular depictions that emphasize big-game hunting.

Paleolithic communities were generally small and highly mobile, moving across the landscape in response to the seasonal availability of food resources, water, and other necessities, rather than maintaining permanent, year-round settlements of the kind discussed in Chapter 4. This mobility required a body of practical, transmitted knowledge — about seasonal patterns of plant growth, animal migration, water sources, and useful raw materials — that would have been passed down through direct instruction, observation, and practice across generations, even though no written record of this knowledge survives.

Adaptation to environmental diversity is a central theme of this chapter, echoing the geographic diversity introduced in Chapter 2. Paleolithic communities in the colder, drier environments of the Loess Plateau and northern China likely relied more heavily on hunting large mammals adapted to grassland and steppe environments, while communities in the warmer, wetter environments of southern China had access to a different range of plant and animal resources, including a greater variety of edible wild plants and aquatic resources. This regional diversity in subsistence strategies, evident even in the Paleolithic period, foreshadows the later independent development of millet agriculture in the north and rice agriculture in the south discussed in Chapter 4, suggesting that regional environmental adaptation has deep roots extending back well before the origins of farming.

Section 3.7

Climate and Environmental Change

The Paleolithic period in East Asia unfolded against a backdrop of dramatic long-term climate change, driven by the cyclical pattern of glacial and interglacial periods that characterized the Pleistocene epoch, the geological era encompassing nearly the entire span of hominin presence discussed in this chapter. During colder glacial periods, extensive ice sheets locked up a significant proportion of the world's water, causing global sea levels to drop by well over one hundred meters at the coldest points, exposing land that is submerged under coastal waters today and, at times, creating land connections between parts of East Asia and neighboring regions, including areas now separated by the Yellow Sea, the Bohai Sea, and the waters separating the Asian mainland from Taiwan and parts of Southeast Asia.

These climate fluctuations had profound consequences for human and animal populations alike. Colder, drier glacial periods generally reduced the extent of forested land in favor of grassland and steppe environments across much of northern and central East Asia, altering the distribution of plant and animal food resources available to Paleolithic communities and likely requiring significant adaptations in subsistence strategy, mobility patterns, and, potentially, social organization. The most recent and best-studied of these cold periods, the Last Glacial Maximum, occurred roughly between 26,500 and 19,000 years ago, and represents one of the most severe climatic challenges faced by human populations anywhere in the world during the Paleolithic period, with extremely cold, dry, and resource-scarce conditions across much of the region.

Following the Last Glacial Maximum, global climate began a gradual, though not perfectly smooth, warming trend, eventually giving way to the more stable and generally warmer conditions of the current geological epoch, the Holocene, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago. This warming trend brought significant environmental changes across East Asia, including rising sea levels that gradually submerged previously exposed coastal land, changing patterns of rainfall and vegetation, and, ultimately, the environmental conditions that made possible the agricultural developments discussed in the next chapter. Understanding this long process of climate change is essential context for Chapter 4, since the origins of agriculture in both northern and southern China are closely tied to the environmental transformations that accompanied the end of the last Ice Age.

Cause and Effect

Ice Age Climate and Human Settlement in East Asia

Underlying causes

Long-term cyclical changes in the Earth's orbit and axial tilt (Milankovitch cycles) drove alternating glacial and interglacial periods throughout the Pleistocene epoch, altering global temperature, ice volume, and sea level over tens of thousands of years.

Triggering conditions

During glacial maxima, expanding ice sheets locked up ocean water, lowering global sea levels by over one hundred meters at the coldest points and altering coastlines, river systems, and vegetation zones across East Asia.

Immediate effects on human populations

Colder, drier conditions reduced forest cover in favor of grassland and steppe in many areas, shifted the distribution of huntable animals and edible plants, and likely required Paleolithic communities to adjust mobility patterns, tool technology, and subsistence strategies.

Longer-term consequences

The eventual warming and stabilization of climate at the start of the Holocene created environmental conditions — including the spread of wild ancestors of millet and rice in different regions — that set the stage for the agricultural developments examined in Chapter 4.

Historical complexity: Climate change did not affect all regions of East Asia equally or simultaneously; local topography, latitude, and proximity to coastlines or mountains produced considerable regional variation in how severely, and how quickly, environmental conditions shifted.

Section 3.8

Archaeological Evidence and Scientific Dating

Because the Paleolithic period predates writing by an enormous margin, historians and scientists reconstruct it entirely through physical and scientific evidence, and understanding the strengths and limitations of the relevant dating methods is essential to evaluating claims about Paleolithic China responsibly.

Stratigraphy, the study of the layered sequence of sediments in which archaeological materials are found, provides a foundational relative dating method: in an undisturbed sequence, deeper layers are generally older than the layers above them, allowing archaeologists to establish the relative order in which materials were deposited even without an absolute date. Stratigraphic analysis at multi-layered sites such as Zhoukoudian has allowed scientists to reconstruct long sequences of hominin occupation and associated environmental change spanning hundreds of thousands of years.

Absolute, or chronometric, dating methods provide estimated calendar-year ages, generally expressed with a margin of uncertainty rather than as a single precise figure. Radiocarbon dating, which measures the decay of a radioactive isotope of carbon present in organic materials, is useful for materials up to roughly 50,000 years old, making it valuable for the more recent portions of the Upper Paleolithic but not for the much older hominin sites discussed earlier in this chapter. For older materials, scientists rely on other radiometric methods, including techniques that measure the decay of radioactive isotopes in volcanic materials or other minerals, as well as paleomagnetic dating, which uses periodic reversals in the Earth's magnetic field recorded in certain sediments and rocks to establish approximate ages. Each of these methods carries its own margin of error and its own set of assumptions, and responsible scientific practice generally involves cross-checking results from multiple independent dating methods wherever possible, rather than relying on a single technique alone.

Students should understand that disagreements among specialists about the age of a particular site or fossil often reflect genuine scientific uncertainty about which dating method, or combination of methods, provides the most reliable result for a given context, rather than careless or unscientific work. The dates given throughout this chapter, and throughout the discipline of Paleolithic archaeology generally, should be understood as current best estimates, subject to revision as dating techniques improve and as new evidence is discovered — a pattern of ongoing scientific refinement that mirrors, in its own way, the historiographical caution about later written sources introduced in Chapter 1.

Historical Vocabulary
Stratigraphy

Stratigraphy is the study of layered rock and sediment deposits, based on the principle that, in an undisturbed sequence, lower layers were generally deposited earlier than the layers above them. Archaeologists use stratigraphy to establish the relative sequence in which artifacts, fossils, and other materials were deposited at a site, providing an essential framework for interpreting a site's history even before absolute dates can be established.

Stratigraphic interpretation can be complicated by natural or human disturbances, such as erosion, animal burrowing, cave collapse, or later digging, which can mix layers or displace materials from their original context. For this reason, careful excavation technique and detailed recording of exactly where each artifact or fossil was found are essential to reliable stratigraphic interpretation, and disputes about a site's chronology sometimes hinge on questions about whether its stratigraphic sequence was disturbed in ways that complicate straightforward interpretation.

Section 3.9

China in the Wider Human Story

The Paleolithic archaeological record in East Asia is not an isolated regional story; it forms an integral part of the global scientific effort to understand human evolution and the spread of hominin and, later, modern human populations across the planet. Sites such as Zhoukoudian have long occupied a central place in this global scientific conversation, and Chinese Paleolithic archaeology has, over the past century, made significant contributions to broader debates about hominin dispersal, tool technology, environmental adaptation, and the ultimate origins of modern human populations worldwide.

At the same time, East Asia's Paleolithic record raises distinctive scientific questions of its own. With the currently accepted range for Homo erectus at Zhoukoudian ending at roughly 300,000 years ago — well before the more securely dated evidence for widespread Homo sapiens populations in China after approximately 45,000 years ago — the two populations do not appear, based on current evidence, to have directly overlapped at that particular site. The more scientifically significant pattern of overlap and interaction in the East Asian record instead involves the substantial diversity of archaic hominin populations documented across the Middle and Late Pleistocene, including Denisovan-related populations such as those identified at Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau, discussed in Section 3.4. Recent research emphasizes that hominin diversity in eastern Asia during this period was considerably greater than the older, simplified scheme of a single lineage progressing neatly from Homo erectus to "archaic" and then "modern" Homo sapiens once implied, and that questions about the relationships, distribution, and possible interaction among these diverse populations remain an active area of ongoing research.

Understanding Paleolithic China as part of this wider human story serves an important pedagogical purpose for this textbook. It reminds students, at the very outset of a long history of a specific civilization, that the human beings who eventually developed that civilization were part of a much older, larger, and shared human story that connects the peoples later associated with China to every other human population on Earth. This global framing does not diminish the distinctiveness or importance of the later historical developments this textbook will examine; rather, it provides an accurate and scientifically grounded starting point from which those later, more culturally specific, and more historically documented developments can be properly understood.

Section 3.10

Chapter Review and Paleolithic China Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced students to the deep human past of the region later associated with Chinese history, a period spanning well over a million years and constituting, by an enormous margin, the longest single phase of human presence in the region — far longer than the roughly three-thousand-year span of documented dynastic history that occupies the great majority of this textbook's remaining chapters.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. The earliest currently well-supported archaeological evidence of hominin presence in East Asia comes from Shangchen on the Loess Plateau, at approximately 2.1 million years ago, though the identity of the toolmakers and the true date of first arrival remain uncertain, and this conclusion, like all conclusions in this field, is likely to be refined as new discoveries emerge. The site of Zhoukoudian, associated with the fossils historically called "Peking Man" and now classified within the species Homo erectus, represents one of the most important and thoroughly documented hominin fossil assemblages in the world, with its occupation sequence now dated to approximately 780,000 to 300,000 years ago, even though some of its early interpretations, particularly regarding fire use, have been revised by later, more rigorous scientific analysis. The emergence of anatomically modern Homo sapiens in East Asia is best understood, based on the great weight of current genomic evidence, as part of a predominantly African-origin dispersal complicated by real but limited interbreeding with archaic populations, including Neanderthals and multiple distinct Denisovan-related groups such as those documented at Baishiya Karst Cave. Stone tool technologies in East Asia developed unevenly, marked by periods of innovation, persistence, and regional specialization, including notable early complexity at sites such as Xigou, and apparent differences from tool traditions in other world regions should not be interpreted as evidence of lesser cognitive or technological sophistication. Paleolithic communities adapted their subsistence strategies to a wide range of regional environments, foreshadowing the later regional agricultural diversity discussed in Chapter 4. Dramatic long-term climate change, especially the cycle of glacial and interglacial periods culminating in the Last Glacial Maximum, profoundly shaped where and how Paleolithic communities could live. And all of these conclusions rest on archaeological, geological, and genomic evidence subject to ongoing scientific revision, requiring careful, cautious historical and scientific language rather than false certainty.

Historical Debate

From Multiregionalism and Replacement to African Origin With Admixture

The question: Did the anatomically modern human populations who eventually inhabited East Asia descend, at least in significant part, from earlier hominin populations such as Homo erectus who had already lived in the region for hundreds of thousands of years, or did modern Homo sapiens, having evolved in Africa, migrate into East Asia relatively recently and simply replace earlier populations there without any biological connection at all? Tracing how this scientific debate has developed helps students understand both where the evidence currently stands and how much scientific understanding of this question has changed within the past two decades.

The older multiregional continuity model: An earlier generation of paleoanthropological scholarship, still cited in some popular and a minority of scholarly accounts, argued that certain skeletal and dental features observed in some East Asian fossil remains suggested a degree of biological continuity between earlier regional hominin populations, including Homo erectus in East Asia, and later anatomically modern populations in the same region, implying substantial regional evolutionary continuity across enormous spans of time.
The older strict replacement model: A competing, simplified version of the "Out of Africa" model held that expanding Homo sapiens populations originating in Africa replaced earlier hominin populations across Eurasia essentially completely, with little or no interbreeding between the two, treating the process as a clean substitution of one population for another.
The current synthesis — African origin with multiple dispersals and archaic admixture: Neither older model fully matches the evidence available today. Genomic research over roughly the past fifteen years has established that the overwhelming majority of ancestry in all living human populations, including in East Asia, does trace to a predominantly African-origin dispersal of Homo sapiens, largely confirming the core of the replacement model over multiregional continuity. At the same time, that dispersal was not a single clean substitution: genomic evidence has identified interbreeding with Neanderthals and with at least three genetically distinct Denisovan-related populations, producing a more complex pattern of "African origin with archaic admixture" rather than either strict multiregional continuity or total replacement without any biological connection.

Evidence: The older multiregional model drew primarily on morphological comparisons of skeletal and dental traits in the fossil record. The current synthesis instead rests on a much larger and rapidly growing body of genomic evidence — whole-genome comparisons of living populations worldwide, ancient DNA recovered from fossil remains where preservation allows, and, increasingly, ancient proteins recovered from fossil enamel even where DNA has not survived — which most specialists consider considerably more decisive for reconstructing population history than morphological comparison alone, though morphological evidence remains valuable for classifying fossils that cannot yield molecular data.

Why it matters: This debate has scientific significance for understanding the true pattern of human dispersal and evolution, but it has also, at times, become entangled with questions of national identity and pride, since a strong version of multiregional continuity has occasionally been invoked in popular narratives to suggest a uniquely ancient and unbroken biological lineage for a specific modern national population — a claim the current weight of genomic evidence does not support. Historians and scientists must be especially careful to separate well-supported scientific conclusions from politically or nationally motivated interpretations of ancient evidence, while also avoiding oversimplified "replacement" narratives that ignore genuine, if limited, archaic admixture.

From the perspective of an early twentieth-century international paleoanthropologist: The discovery at Zhoukoudian offered thrilling new evidence for understanding human evolution on a global scale, situating East Asia alongside Africa and Europe as a critical region for testing scientific theories about the origins and spread of the human species, at a time when relatively few well-documented hominin fossil sites had been discovered anywhere in the world.

From the perspective of a historian of science studying how national narratives shape scientific interpretation: The history of research at Zhoukoudian illustrates how scientific questions about human origins can become entangled with modern national identity and pride, making it especially important for historians and scientists alike to distinguish rigorously supported conclusions from narratives shaped by contemporary political or nationalist considerations.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Human Adaptation in East Asia From the Paleolithic Onward

What Changed

  • Hominin and, later, modern human populations shifted from simple to increasingly sophisticated and specialized stone, bone, and antler tool technologies over hundreds of thousands of years
  • Subsistence strategies evolved from opportunistic hunting, scavenging, and gathering toward increasingly systematic exploitation of specific plant and animal resources by the end of the Paleolithic
  • Climate shifted repeatedly between glacial and interglacial extremes, culminating in the long-term warming that began roughly 11,700 years ago
  • Population mobility patterns and, eventually, the beginnings of more settled behavior began to change as the Paleolithic period drew to a close, discussed further in Chapter 4

What Stayed the Same

  • Regional environmental diversity, introduced in Chapter 2, continued to shape distinct patterns of human adaptation in the north versus the south throughout the Paleolithic and into later periods
  • Human communities throughout this period remained small, mobile, and dependent on wild rather than cultivated food resources
  • The reliance on stone as a primary and highly durable tool-making material persisted across the entire span of the Paleolithic, even as specific tool forms changed considerably

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the profound technological and environmental changes of the Paleolithic and the underlying continuities of regional adaptation helps students understand the Neolithic agricultural transition in Chapter 4 not as a sudden, unexplained event, but as the culmination of an extremely long period of accumulated human knowledge and environmental adaptation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in a Paleolithic Community

Although no written record survives to describe daily life in a Paleolithic community in East Asia, archaeological evidence, combined with careful comparison to well-documented hunter-gatherer societies studied by anthropologists in more recent history, allows historians to offer a cautious, evidence-based reconstruction of some likely features of daily life. A small band of related individuals — likely numbering anywhere from a handful to a few dozen people, based on patterns commonly observed among mobile hunter-gatherer societies studied elsewhere — would have organized daily activity around the practical necessities of finding food, water, and shelter, tasks that likely involved most or all able-bodied members of the group in some capacity, though the precise division of labor by age or gender in any specific ancient community cannot be determined with certainty from the archaeological evidence alone.

Toolmaking, a skill requiring considerable practice and instruction, would have been an important and probably routine activity, with knowledge of how to select suitable stone, strike appropriate flakes, and shape tools for specific tasks passed down through direct demonstration and practice across generations. Food preparation, using both stone tools and, at some sites and periods, fire, would have similarly required accumulated practical knowledge about which plants were safe and nutritious to eat, how to process tough or fibrous plant materials, and how to butcher and prepare animal remains.

Shelter likely took a range of forms depending on region, season, and available materials, including the use of natural caves and rock shelters, such as those at Zhoukoudian, as well as more temporary structures built from wood, brush, or animal hides in the open, though evidence for such perishable structures is understandably far rarer in the archaeological record than evidence from cave sites where organic and inorganic materials are more likely to be preserved. Social life, cooperation, and the transmission of practical knowledge across generations would have been essential to the survival of any Paleolithic community, even though the specific content of that social life — language, belief, kinship structure — remains almost entirely invisible to archaeologists working with only stone tools, bones, and sediment as their evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced students to the Paleolithic period in East Asia, a span of human presence stretching back well over a million years and constituting, by an overwhelming margin, the longest single phase of human history in the region — far longer than the roughly three-thousand-year span of documented dynastic history that occupies most of this textbook. Understanding this deep past provides an essential, if often overlooked, foundation for the study of later Chinese history, reminding students that the peoples who eventually developed agriculture, states, and civilizations in the region were themselves the descendants, in a broad evolutionary and historical sense, of an immensely long process of human adaptation, migration, and technological development.

Students have learned that the earliest currently well-supported archaeological evidence of hominin presence in East Asia comes from Shangchen on the Loess Plateau, at approximately 2.1 million years ago, though the precise chronology and identity of the toolmakers remain uncertain, no hominin fossils have been recovered there, and the true date of first hominin arrival in the region is not known with certainty. More securely and extensively documented evidence confirms sustained hominin activity across the region by at least 1.2 million to 780,000 years ago. The site of Zhoukoudian, associated with the fossils historically known as "Peking Man" and now classified within the species Homo erectus, has played a central role in shaping global scientific understanding of human evolution, with its occupation sequence currently dated to approximately 780,000 to 300,000 years ago and its fossil enamel recently yielding ancient protein evidence, even as some of its early interpretations, particularly regarding fire use, have been revised through later, more rigorous scientific analysis — a valuable lesson in the self-correcting nature of scientific inquiry.

The chapter examined the complex and still-developing scientific understanding of how anatomically modern Homo sapiens came to inhabit East Asia, emphasizing that the great weight of current genetic evidence supports a predominantly African-origin model for modern human ancestry worldwide, complicated by real but limited patterns of interbreeding with earlier hominin populations such as Neanderthals and Denisovans. This section modeled an important historical and scientific thinking skill: evaluating competing interpretations of evidence, understanding why molecular evidence — including ancient DNA and, increasingly, ancient proteins recovered from fossil enamel — has transformed and supplemented morphological comparison as a tool for reconstructing ancient population history, and remaining alert to the ways in which questions about human origins can become entangled with modern national narratives that the underlying scientific evidence does not straightforwardly support.

Students also examined the development of stone tool technologies across the Paleolithic period, learning to recognize and reject outdated and biased interpretations that once characterized East Asian tool traditions as evidence of lesser technological or cognitive sophistication, in favor of more careful interpretations that account for regional differences in raw material availability, task requirements, and the probable use of now-vanished perishable materials such as bamboo and wood.

The chapter's discussion of subsistence strategies, climate change, and environmental adaptation demonstrated that Paleolithic communities in East Asia developed distinct regional patterns of hunting, gathering, and mobility in response to the same environmental diversity introduced in Chapter 2, foreshadowing the later, independent development of millet and rice agriculture examined in Chapter 4. The severe climatic challenges of the Last Glacial Maximum, followed by the long-term warming that ushered in the Holocene epoch, provided the essential environmental backdrop against which the agricultural revolution of the following chapter would eventually take shape.

Finally, this chapter emphasized the methodological foundations that make the scientific study of the deep human past possible: stratigraphy, radiometric and other chronometric dating techniques, faunal and taphonomic analysis, and, increasingly, genetic evidence, each with its own strengths, limitations, and margins of uncertainty. Students have learned to approach claims about Paleolithic China with appropriate scientific caution, recognizing that dates and interpretations in this field are frequently revised as new evidence and new techniques emerge, and that historians and scientists alike must resist the temptation to present uncertain or contested conclusions as settled fact.

Armed with this foundation in the deep human past, students are now prepared to move into Chapter 4's examination of the Neolithic agricultural revolution, a period during which the descendants of the Paleolithic communities examined in this chapter began, independently in different regions, to cultivate plants, domesticate animals, and establish the first permanent villages — developments that would, over thousands of years, lay the groundwork for the early complex societies, states, and civilizations examined throughout the remainder of this textbook.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The "Old Stone Age," the earliest and longest period of human technological history, characterized by unpolished stone tools; concluded with the rise of agriculture in most world regions.
Hominin
The group of species including modern humans and our closest fossil relatives after the evolutionary split from ancestors of chimpanzees and bonobos.
Homo erectus
An early hominin species found at Zhoukoudian and other East Asian sites, generally dated across a wide range extending from roughly 1.8 million to several hundred thousand years ago.
Homo sapiens
The species of all anatomically modern humans; current genetic evidence traces the majority of modern human ancestry, including in East Asia, to a predominantly African-origin dispersal.
Zhoukoudian
A cave complex near Beijing yielding one of the world's most extensively studied Homo erectus fossil and archaeological assemblages.
Peking Man
The historical popular name for the Homo erectus fossils recovered at Zhoukoudian; the original fossils were lost during wartime transport in 1941.
Pei Wenzhong
Chinese paleontologist who led key Zhoukoudian excavations beginning in 1929 and helped establish Chinese paleoanthropology as a field.
Lithic technology
Stone tool technology, the most abundant and durable form of Paleolithic archaeological evidence.
Core and flake tools
Basic stone tool types produced by striking flakes from a stone core, among the earliest and most widespread tool forms.
Handaxe
A shaped, often symmetrical bifacial stone tool associated with the Acheulean tradition, comparatively less common in some East Asian assemblages than in parts of Africa and western Eurasia.
Microlith
A very small, carefully shaped stone tool, often mounted in a composite tool of wood, bone, or antler; associated with the Upper Paleolithic.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered sediment deposits, used to establish the relative age sequence of archaeological materials.
Radiometric dating
A family of chronometric dating techniques based on the predictable decay of radioactive isotopes, used to estimate the absolute age of archaeological and geological materials.
Radiocarbon dating
A radiometric dating method effective for organic materials up to roughly 50,000 years old.
Paleomagnetic dating
A dating method using recorded reversals in the Earth's magnetic field preserved in certain sediments and rocks.
Taphonomy
The scientific study of how bones and other remains become altered, buried, and preserved after death or deposition, essential for evaluating claims such as ancient fire use.
Faunal remains
The fossilized bones of animals found at archaeological sites, used to reconstruct ancient diet, environment, and climate.
Glacial period
A cold phase of the Pleistocene epoch marked by expanded ice sheets and lower global sea levels.
Interglacial period
A warmer phase between glacial periods, marked by higher global sea levels and different vegetation patterns.
Last Glacial Maximum
The most recent peak of Ice Age conditions, roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, marked by extreme cold and low sea levels.
Land bridge
Land exposed by lowered sea levels during glacial periods, connecting regions separated by water today.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago, marked by warmer and more stable climate conditions.
Multiregional continuity hypothesis
An older model, now largely superseded, proposing substantial regional evolutionary continuity between earlier hominins and later modern human populations in the same region.
African origin with admixture
The current scientific synthesis: anatomically modern humans trace the great majority of their ancestry to a predominantly African-origin dispersal, complicated by real but limited interbreeding with archaic populations such as Neanderthals and multiple Denisovan-related groups along the way.
Ancient DNA and ancient proteins
Molecular evidence recovered from fossil remains, used to reconstruct ancestry and population relationships; ancient DNA survives in only some preservation conditions, while ancient proteins, including those recently recovered from Chinese Homo erectus tooth enamel, can sometimes survive far longer and offer an alternative source of molecular evidence.
Denisovans
An extinct hominin population identified through genetic analysis; measurable Denisovan ancestry is present in various Asian populations today, though the highest known proportions occur among populations in New Guinea and other parts of Oceania.
Neanderthals
An extinct hominin population primarily associated with western Eurasia, which also interbred to a limited degree with dispersing modern human populations.
Hunter-gatherer
A society that obtains food primarily through hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants rather than through farming.
Subsistence strategy
The methods a society uses to obtain food and other necessities, varying by environment, season, and available technology.
Upper Paleolithic
The final phase of the Paleolithic, generally associated with anatomically modern humans; assemblages in many regions display distinctive blade, microlithic, bone, antler, and ornamental technologies, though notable technological complexity is also documented earlier at sites such as Xigou.
Shangchen
A Loess Plateau site with in-situ stone tools dated to approximately 2.1 million years ago, currently the earliest widely accepted archaeological evidence of hominin presence in East Asia; no hominin fossils have been found there.
Xigou
A central Chinese site with an occupation sequence dated to roughly 160,000–72,000 years ago, documenting organized core reduction and what excavators identify as the earliest known hafted or composite tools in eastern Asia.
Baishiya Karst Cave
A Tibetan Plateau site providing Denisovan skeletal, dental, sedimentary-DNA, and protein evidence; occupation is documented from at least approximately 160,000 to 60,000 years ago and may have continued to roughly 45,000 years ago.
Symbolic behavior
Evidence, such as ornamental beads or pigments, suggesting non-utilitarian, socially or culturally meaningful human activity.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch preceding the Holocene, spanning roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago and encompassing nearly the entire span of hominin presence discussed in this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has taken students to the very beginning of the human story in the region later associated with Chinese history, a beginning that lies not in a royal court, a founding dynasty, or a sacred text, but in stone tools, fossilized bones, and layers of ancient sediment stretching back well over a million years. Understanding this deep past matters because it grounds the rest of this textbook in an accurate, evidence-based account of where the human beings who eventually built the societies, states, and civilizations of later chapters actually came from — not through a simple, unbroken national lineage stretching back to a single ancient fossil, but through the same long, shared, and still not fully understood process of human evolution and migration that connects every human population on Earth.

The discoveries at Zhoukoudian, and the broader scientific effort to understand Paleolithic China, illustrate themes that will recur throughout this textbook: the importance of careful, evidence-based historical and scientific reasoning; the danger of allowing national pride or political considerations to distort the interpretation of evidence; and the value of remaining open to revising even well-established conclusions in light of new evidence and improved methods. These are not merely abstract methodological lessons — they are essential habits of mind for any student who wishes to understand Chinese history, or any history, honestly and rigorously.

This chapter also establishes an essential foundation for Chapter 4's examination of the Neolithic agricultural revolution. The regional environmental adaptations, subsistence strategies, and technological developments examined in this chapter did not disappear when farming began; they were built upon, transformed, and, in many respects, continued by the Neolithic communities who would soon begin cultivating millet in the north and rice in the south, developments that depended directly on the long-term climate stabilization that followed the Last Glacial Maximum discussed in Section 3.7.

As students move forward into the following chapters, they should carry with them an appreciation for the immense scale of time that precedes even the earliest developments most people associate with "Chinese history" — the Bronze Age states, the philosophical traditions, the imperial dynasties. Those developments occupy only the most recent, and by far the shortest, chapter of a much longer human story in this region, a story that began not with an emperor or a text, but with small groups of hominins and, eventually, fully modern humans, learning to survive and adapt across one of the most environmentally diverse landscapes on Earth.
