Prehistoric Peoples and Archaeological Cultures

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

"Long before any chronicle was written, before any prince ruled, and before any nation imagined itself as ancient, human beings had already lived, hunted, buried their dead, and exchanged goods and ideas across northern Eurasia for tens of thousands of years. Their history survives not in words but in bone, stone, ash, and soil — and it belongs to no single modern people."

In 1965, villagers near Mezhirich (also transliterated Mezhyrich), in the Dnieper valley south of Kyiv, accidentally uncovered, during agricultural work, a startling structure buried beneath more than a meter of loess soil: a roughly circular structure built almost entirely from the bones of woolly mammoths. Excavation eventually revealed four such structures at the site, each built from dozens of mammoth skulls, jawbones, tusks, and long bones, carefully stacked and arranged to form a substantial circular or oval structure. A revised radiocarbon chronology published in 2025 places at least one of these structures and its associated deposits at approximately eighteen thousand years before the present — somewhat earlier than the roughly fifteen-thousand-year estimate once commonly cited — though the precise chronology, duration of occupation, and even the exact function of these structures (whether year-round dwellings, seasonal shelters, or storage and ritual features) remain subjects of active archaeological debate. The people who built these structures left no written word, no name for themselves that has come down to us, and no chronicle recording their achievements. What they left instead was material evidence: bone architecture, stone tools, hearths, food refuse, and, in nearby pits, stores of fuel and supplies that allowed a small community to survive an Ice Age winter on the open, treeless steppe-tundra of the East European Plain. Their descendants — if any single line of descent can even be traced across such a span of time — cannot be identified with any people known to later history. They were neither Slavs nor Balts nor Finns nor any other group this textbook will name in later chapters. They were, simply, some of the first inhabitants of the land upon which all of that later history would eventually unfold.

This chapter studies the tens of thousands of years of human presence in northern Eurasia that preceded the appearance of any written record, any named people, or any state. It is, in an important sense, the most difficult chapter historians face in constructing this textbook, because none of the ordinary tools of historical analysis — chronicles, laws, letters, treaties — exist for this period. Everything historians and archaeologists know about the Paleolithic hunters of Mezhirich, the Mesolithic fisherfolk who colonized the forests after the ice retreated, the first Neolithic farmers and herders, and the metal-working communities of the Bronze Age comes from the patient, technical work of archaeology: the excavation of settlements, the analysis of tools and pottery, the study of burial sites, and, increasingly in recent decades, the extraction of ancient DNA from human remains. This chapter asks students to think carefully about what this kind of evidence can, and cannot, tell us, and to resist the temptation — one that popular writing about prehistory frequently indulges — to assign ancient peoples national identities, political loyalties, or cultural characteristics that the evidence simply does not support.

The scale of time this chapter covers is genuinely difficult to grasp. Human beings, in some form, have been present in parts of the territory this textbook studies for many tens of thousands of years, and behaviorally modern humans — anatomically and cognitively similar to people living today — for at least forty thousand years. Compare this to the roughly eleven and a half centuries since the traditional date for the founding of Rus', or the little more than three centuries since Peter the Great proclaimed the Russian Empire, and the disproportion becomes clear: the overwhelming majority of the human story in this region occurred before any of the events later chapters will narrate, and it occurred among peoples who cannot be claimed by any single modern nation without serious historical distortion.

This chapter also introduces students to archaeology's central interpretive tool: the concept of the archaeological culture, a term this chapter will define carefully in Section 3.8, referring to a recurring, geographically bounded pattern of artifacts, technologies, and practices that archaeologists identify in the material record. Archaeological cultures — with names such as the Yamnaya, the Corded Ware, the Pit-Comb Ware, or the Fatyanovo cultures that this chapter will introduce — are not the same thing as peoples, tribes, ethnic groups, or nations, even though popular and sometimes even academic writing has, at various points, blurred this distinction in ways that served later political agendas. Learning to distinguish an archaeological culture, defined by shared material practices, from an ethnic or linguistic group, defined by shared identity and speech, is one of the chapter's central analytical tasks.

The prehistoric peoples examined in this chapter were not idle or static. Across tens of thousands of years, human communities in this region adapted to dramatic environmental transformation, including the retreat of continental ice sheets, the stabilization of the forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones described in Chapter 2, and repeated waves of migration, technological innovation, and cultural exchange. They domesticated plants and animals, worked copper and then bronze, built increasingly complex forms of social organization, buried their dead with growing attention to status and ritual, and participated in exchange networks that moved valued materials — amber, copper, exotic stone, and eventually metal ornaments — across enormous distances. Understanding these developments prepares students for Chapter 4's detailed examination of the Scythians and other historically documented steppe societies, and for Chapter 5's introduction of the Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples whose emergence from this deep prehistoric background forms the immediate prelude to the formation of Rus'.

Finally, this chapter asks students to confront directly a problem that recurs, in different forms, throughout this textbook: the danger of projecting later identities backward onto earlier peoples for the sake of a tidier, more flattering national story. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalist writers in many European countries, including Russia and its neighbors, sometimes claimed direct, unbroken descent from prehistoric archaeological cultures in order to establish ancient territorial claims or demonstrate national antiquity. Contemporary archaeology and genetics have complicated, and in many cases directly contradicted, these claims, revealing instead a long history of migration, mixture, replacement, and cultural exchange that resists any simple story of one people, in one place, since time immemorial. This chapter presents the evidence as archaeologists and geneticists currently understand it, while remaining honest about what remains uncertain, contested, or actively revised as new scientific techniques continue to reshape the field.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the major phases of prehistoric settlement in northern Eurasia, distinguish carefully between archaeological cultures and ethnic or national identities, evaluate what different kinds of prehistoric evidence can and cannot establish, and explain why the transition to agriculture, the emergence of metalworking, and the development of long-distance exchange networks each represent genuine historical transformations worth studying in their own right — not merely as a prologue to be rushed through on the way to more "important" later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the major phases of human settlement in northern Eurasia — Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, and Copper and Bronze Age — and identify the environmental and technological conditions associated with each.
  2. Explain how Paleolithic communities adapted to Ice Age conditions on the East European Plain, including through distinctive technologies such as mammoth-bone architecture.
  3. Trace the environmental and cultural adaptations that followed the retreat of the continental ice sheets during the Mesolithic period.
  4. Analyze the Neolithic transition to agriculture and animal husbandry as a gradual, regionally uneven process rather than a single sudden event.
  5. Compare the economic and social organization of Copper and Bronze Age societies across the forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for long-distance exchange networks connecting prehistoric communities across enormous distances, including amber, copper, and other valued materials.
  7. Interpret burial evidence as a source for understanding social differentiation, status, ritual, and belief in prehistoric societies.
  8. Distinguish clearly between an archaeological culture, defined by shared material practices, and an ethnic, linguistic, or national identity, defined by shared self-understanding and speech.
  9. Assess what archaeological, linguistic, and genetic evidence can and cannot establish about prehistoric migration and population movement.
  10. Critique nationalist or politically motivated claims that assert direct, unbroken descent from specific prehistoric archaeological cultures.
  11. Explain the specific methodological challenges involved in reconstructing prehistoric identity, given the absence of written sources.
  12. Connect the developments examined in this chapter to the ancient steppe societies examined in Chapter 4 and the historically documented peoples examined in Chapter 5.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 established the environmental zones of northern Eurasia — tundra, taiga, mixed forest, forest-steppe, and steppe — as the physical stage on which human history in this region unfolded. This chapter now populates that stage with its earliest known inhabitants, tracing human presence from the Paleolithic period, when small bands of hunter-gatherers survived on the margins of continental ice sheets, through the Mesolithic adaptations that followed the ice's retreat, the Neolithic period — during which farming and herding took root in the forest-steppe and other southern zones while pottery-using hunting, fishing, and gathering communities persisted much longer farther north — and the Copper and Bronze Age societies whose metalworking, long-distance exchange, and increasingly differentiated burial practices mark a substantial elaboration of social and economic life. This sequence — Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, Copper Age (sometimes called the Chalcolithic), and Bronze Age — is a standard archaeological periodization used across Europe and much of Eurasia, based primarily on changing stone, and later metal, tool technologies, though the specific dates at which each period began and ended varied considerably by region, since technological change did not occur simultaneously or uniformly across so vast a landmass.

Students should understand from the outset that this periodization, like all periodization discussed in Chapter 1, is an analytical tool rather than a set of natural, self-evident divisions. The people living through what archaeologists now call the "Mesolithic-Neolithic transition," for example, did not experience their lives as a transition between two named periods; they experienced gradual changes in available food sources, tools, and social arrangements across many generations. Archaeologists impose these period labels retrospectively, based on patterns visible in the surviving material record, and different regions of northern Eurasia moved through these stages at markedly different times: farming reached the forest-steppe zone of what is now Ukraine and southern Russia considerably earlier than it reached the taiga zone further north and east, where hunting, fishing, and gathering remained the dominant subsistence strategies for millennia longer.

This chapter's evidentiary foundation is exclusively archaeological and, increasingly, genetic, since no written sources exist for any of the periods this chapter examines within the territory this textbook studies. Chapter 1 already introduced archaeology's methods — stratigraphic excavation, radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, and isotopic analysis among them — and this chapter puts those methods to sustained use. In recent decades, a further method has transformed the study of prehistoric Eurasia: the analysis of ancient DNA extracted from human skeletal remains, which allows geneticists to trace, with a precision unavailable to earlier generations of scholars, patterns of population movement, mixture, and replacement across thousands of years. This "ancient DNA revolution," as many scholars have called it, has confirmed some long-standing archaeological hypotheses, overturned others, and introduced genuinely new evidence bearing on questions — including the spread of Indo-European languages, discussed further in Section 3.8 — that archaeology and linguistics alone had been unable to resolve.

It is essential to state clearly, before proceeding to the chapter's numbered sections, that none of the peoples examined in this chapter can be identified as "Russian," "Ukrainian," "Slavic," or any other later ethnic or national category. The earliest textually attested Slavic-speaking populations do not appear in the historical record until the middle of the first millennium CE, a topic Chapter 5 examines in detail; the archaeological cultures discussed in this chapter predate that appearance by anywhere from several centuries to tens of thousands of years, depending on the period in question. Attempting to draw a straight line from a specific Bronze Age archaeological culture to a specific modern nation is a category error this textbook explicitly rejects, for reasons this chapter's later sections will explain in detail.

Section 3.1

Paleolithic Settlement in Northern Eurasia

The Paleolithic, or "Old Stone Age," refers to the vast span of human prehistory characterized by stone tool technologies preceding the development of agriculture, extending from the earliest evidence of hominin presence in Eurasia hundreds of thousands of years ago down to the end of the last Ice Age, roughly twelve thousand years ago. Within the territory this textbook studies, the earliest evidence of hominin activity — associated with earlier hominin species rather than anatomically modern humans — dates back several hundred thousand years in some southern regions, though the archaeological record from these earliest periods is sparse and unevenly distributed. This chapter's central concern, however, is the Upper Paleolithic period, beginning roughly forty-five to forty thousand years ago, when anatomically and behaviorally modern humans — Homo sapiens, possessing the same cognitive and physical capacities as people living today — became established across much of the East European Plain, in some regions alongside surviving populations of Neanderthals, a related but distinct hominin species that had inhabited Eurasia for a much longer period and that disappeared from the archaeological record by approximately forty thousand years ago for reasons that remain a subject of ongoing scientific investigation, likely involving some combination of environmental pressure, competition with incoming modern human populations, and, as ancient DNA evidence has now confirmed, at least limited interbreeding between the two populations.

The Upper Paleolithic communities of the East European Plain lived through the final and most severe phase of the last glacial period, when much of the plain's northern reaches lay under or near continental ice sheets and the unglaciated regions to the south supported a distinctive, cold, largely treeless environment sometimes called steppe-tundra or mammoth steppe: a landscape combining grassland vegetation with extremely cold, dry conditions, quite different from either the modern steppe or modern tundra, and capable of supporting large herds of grazing megafauna, including woolly mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, reindeer, horses, and bison. Human communities living in this environment developed specialized hunting technologies and strategies adapted to pursuing these large, migratory animal populations, including sophisticated stone-tool traditions (blade technology, in which long, thin stone blades were struck from a prepared core, allowing more efficient use of raw material and the production of specialized tool types), bone and antler tools, and, crucially, sewn clothing, evidenced indirectly by the discovery of bone needles at several sites, which would have been essential for survival in such an extreme climate.

The mammoth-bone structures at Mezhirich and the related site of Mezin, both in the Dnieper valley south of Kyiv, discussed in this chapter's opening, represent one of the most remarkable and well-studied categories of evidence for Upper Paleolithic adaptation to this environment. On a landscape with virtually no timber available for construction — the treeless steppe-tundra offered little wood — Upper Paleolithic communities of the middle and later Upper Paleolithic period that drew on mammoth remains, whether through direct hunting, scavenging, or the collection of older bones from natural mortality sites, constructed structures, often interpreted as dwellings or shelters, using the most abundant large structural material available. Excavations have revealed structures built with dozens of mammoth skulls arranged in careful patterns, forming a stable foundation and wall structure, likely covered with hides for insulation and weatherproofing, and associated with hearths, storage pits (some evidently used for preserving meat in permanently frozen ground, a form of natural refrigeration), and a range of tools, ornaments, and artistic objects, including engraved bone and ivory pieces that provide some of the earliest evidence for symbolic and artistic behavior in this region.

These Paleolithic communities were not large by later historical standards; population densities across the Ice Age steppe-tundra were almost certainly very low, and, on the interpretation that these structures served primarily as habitation, archaeologists estimate that individual sites, even substantial ones such as Mezhirich, supported only a few dozen people at most, organized in small, mobile or semi-mobile bands that moved across the landscape in relationship to the seasonal movement of the animal herds on which they depended. Nonetheless, the sophistication of their technology, their capacity for large-scale cooperative construction projects such as the mammoth-bone structures, and the evidence for symbolic and artistic behavior all demonstrate that these were fully modern human communities, cognitively and socially comparable to any later human population, adapted with remarkable success to one of the most challenging environments human beings have ever inhabited.

Students should resist any temptation to view Paleolithic peoples as "primitive" in a pejorative sense, or as somehow less fully human than later populations. The archaeological evidence instead reveals resourceful, technologically sophisticated communities who solved genuine and severe environmental challenges through ingenuity, cooperation, and accumulated cultural knowledge transmitted across generations — the same basic human capacities, applied to a very different set of circumstances, that later chapters will trace through agriculture, metalworking, state formation, and beyond.

Archaeologists have documented, at the largest of the four mammoth-bone structures, the remains of at least 149 mammoth bones incorporated into the structure, including skulls arranged in an interlocking pattern to form a stable circular or oval wall base, long bones and tusks likely used to support a covering (probably hide, now decayed and unrecoverable), and associated hearths, worked flint tools, ornaments made from fox teeth and shell (the shell apparently originating from a considerable distance away, suggesting exchange or long-distance movement), and fragments of engraved bone and ivory bearing geometric decoration.

The presence of imported shell ornaments indicates that even small, mobile Ice Age communities participated in exchange networks extending well beyond their immediate territory. The scale and organization of the mammoth-bone construction suggests a substantial degree of planning and cooperative labor, although, as the opening of this chapter noted, the structures' precise function and duration of use remain debated among archaeologists, and should not be assumed to represent the purely transient campsites sometimes imagined for Paleolithic hunter-gatherers.

Section 3.2

Mesolithic Adaptations After the Ice Age

The Mesolithic, or "Middle Stone Age," describes the period following the retreat of continental ice sheets at the end of the last glacial period, roughly twelve thousand years ago, and preceding the widespread adoption of agriculture — a period during which human communities across northern Eurasia adapted their technologies and subsistence strategies to the newly forming environmental zones described in Chapter 2. As the steppe-tundra environment of the Ice Age gave way to expanding forests, wetlands, and eventually the distinct tundra, taiga, mixed-forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones familiar from Chapter 2's discussion, the large migratory herd animals that Upper Paleolithic hunters had depended upon — mammoths (which became extinct across most of their range by the end of the Pleistocene), woolly rhinoceroses, and the vast open-country herds of horses and bison — declined sharply or disappeared entirely from the region, forcing human communities to develop new strategies suited to the more varied, more heavily forested, and generally more resource-dispersed environment that replaced the Ice Age steppe-tundra.

Mesolithic technology reflects this adaptation. Archaeologists identify Mesolithic assemblages partly through the presence of microliths: small, carefully shaped stone tool components, often only a few centimeters in length, that were combined, several at a time, into composite tools and weapons — for example, set into a wooden or bone shaft to form a serrated arrow point or knife edge. Microlithic technology allowed for more efficient use of high-quality stone material and permitted the rapid replacement of damaged tool components without discarding an entire tool, an adaptation well suited to a more mobile, forest-adapted way of life focused on smaller, more dispersed prey — deer, elk, wild boar, and a wide range of fish and waterfowl — rather than the large migratory herds of the Ice Age steppe-tundra.

Fishing and the exploitation of aquatic resources became substantially more important during the Mesolithic period than they had been previously, reflecting both the expansion of lakes, rivers, and wetlands as the climate warmed and glacial meltwater reshaped the landscape, and the development of specialized fishing technology, including bone and antler fish hooks, harpoons, and, in some regions, early evidence for basketry and possibly netting, though organic materials of this kind rarely survive in the archaeological record except under unusually favorable preservation conditions. Sites along rivers, lakeshores, and coastal areas — including, in later Mesolithic contexts, along the shores of what would become the Baltic Sea as postglacial sea levels stabilized — show intensive exploitation of fish, shellfish, and waterfowl, supplementing continued hunting of forest game.

Mesolithic communities generally remained mobile or semi-mobile, organized in relatively small groups, though the increased reliability and diversity of resources available in richer environments — particularly along major rivers and lakes — allowed some communities to establish longer-term or seasonally recurring settlements, a development that in some regions anticipates the greater sedentism, meaning a settled, non-mobile way of life, that would become far more pronounced with the adoption of farming in the following Neolithic period. Archaeologists have identified extensive Mesolithic sites, particularly in the lake and wetland districts of the northern forest zone, with evidence of repeated, long-term seasonal occupation, indicating that these communities possessed detailed, place-specific knowledge of local resources accumulated and transmitted across many generations.

It is important to emphasize that the Mesolithic period should not be understood merely as a transitional or intermediate stage of lesser interest than the periods that preceded or followed it. Mesolithic communities developed sophisticated, sustainable, and in many respects highly successful adaptations to the postglacial environment, adaptations that in the northern forest and tundra zones, where farming remained impractical or marginal for thousands of years longer than in the forest-steppe, persisted for millennia after agriculture had already transformed subsistence strategies farther south — a point of considerable importance for understanding the very different historical trajectories of the northern forest zone, discussed further in Chapter 5, compared to the forest-steppe and steppe zones examined in Section 3.4 below and in Chapter 4.

Section 3.3

Neolithic Communities and New Technologies

The Neolithic, or "New Stone Age," is conventionally defined by archaeologists through the presence of several interrelated developments: polished (rather than merely flaked) stone tools, pottery production, and, in many though not all regional contexts, the beginnings of plant cultivation and animal domestication. Students should understand from the outset that the meaning of "Neolithic" varies considerably by region within the territory this textbook studies. In the forest-steppe and other southern regions examined later in this section, the label is closely tied to the arrival of farming and herding, and marks a genuine transformation in subsistence. Across much of the northern and eastern forest zone, however, archaeologists apply the same period label to pottery-producing communities that continued to rely primarily on hunting, fishing, and gathering for many centuries, or even millennia, without adopting agriculture at all — a pattern this section returns to below in discussing Pit-Comb Ware pottery. "Neolithic," in other words, should be understood here as a label for a related but not inseparable bundle of technological developments, one that occurred gradually, unevenly, and through processes considerably more complex than a single "Neolithic Revolution" sweeping uniformly across the landscape.

Agriculture and animal domestication did not originate within the territory this textbook studies. Farming and herding arose independently in several distinct regions of the world, but the specific agricultural complex relevant to this chapter — based on wheat, barley, and legumes alongside sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs — developed first in southwest Asia, in the region sometimes called the Fertile Crescent, beginning around ten thousand years before the present. These agricultural practices, along with associated technologies including pottery, spread gradually into southeastern and then central Europe over the following millennia, reaching the forest-steppe zone of what is now Ukraine and Moldova — the western edge of the territory this textbook studies — by approximately the sixth millennium BCE, considerably earlier than farming reached the forest zones farther north and east, where hunting, fishing, and gathering remained economically dominant for a much longer period, in some northern and Siberian regions well into the historical period covered by later chapters of this textbook.

The spread of agriculture into this region raises a question that has occupied archaeologists for generations and that connects directly to the methodological concerns discussed further in Sections 3.8 and 3.9: did farming spread primarily through the migration of farming populations displacing or absorbing existing hunter-gatherer communities, or did existing populations adopt agricultural techniques and domesticated species through cultural contact and exchange, without significant population replacement? The evidence, including increasingly detailed ancient DNA studies, suggests that both processes occurred, in different combinations in different regions and periods: some areas show evidence of substantial genetic input from incoming farming populations with ancestry ultimately traceable to Anatolia and the Near East, while other areas show greater continuity with pre-existing local hunter-gatherer populations who appear to have adopted agricultural techniques and domesticated species through contact and exchange rather than through wholesale population replacement. This mixed picture cautions against any simple, single-cause explanation for the spread of farming across so large and environmentally varied a region.

Pottery production, closely associated with Neolithic communities though not invariably confined to them, provided a durable technology for storage, cooking, and, importantly for archaeologists, cultural classification: distinctive pottery styles, decorated in characteristic ways, allow archaeologists to identify and distinguish different archaeological cultures across time and space, a method this chapter will discuss further in Section 3.8. In the forest zone of the East European Plain, archaeologists have identified pottery-producing cultures — including cultures conventionally labeled by their characteristic decoration, such as Pit-Comb Ware cultures, named for pottery decorated with pits and comb-like stamped impressions — among communities that continued to rely primarily on hunting, fishing, and gathering rather than farming, demonstrating that pottery production and agriculture, though often associated, were not inseparable developments and could occur independently of one another.

The Neolithic transition, wherever and whenever it occurred, brought profound social consequences beyond subsistence alone. Agricultural communities generally required, and enabled, greater sedentism: farming, unlike most forms of hunting and gathering, ties a community more closely to specific plots of cultivated land and requires investment in structures, storage facilities, and, eventually, more substantial and durable housing. Greater sedentism, in turn, is generally associated with larger settlement sizes, increased population density, more complex forms of social organization, and, as the following sections will examine, an increasing tendency toward social differentiation reflected in burial practices, the accumulation and inheritance of property, and the eventual emergence of distinct social ranks or classes — developments that reach a particularly striking expression in the exceptionally large settlements of the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture discussed in Section 3.5.

Section 3.4

Agriculture and Pastoralism

Building on the Neolithic developments introduced in the previous section, this section examines more closely how agriculture and pastoralism developed as distinct, though sometimes overlapping and interacting, economic adaptations across the different environmental zones of northern Eurasia — a pattern of environmental specialization that anticipates, in prehistoric form, the enduring forest-versus-steppe economic distinction that Chapter 2 introduced and that recurs throughout this textbook's later treatment of historically documented peoples.

In the forest-steppe zone, where fertile chernozem soils and moderate rainfall permitted productive grain cultivation, Neolithic and later Copper Age communities developed increasingly intensive and increasingly large-scale farming economies, cultivating wheat, barley, and other cereal crops alongside legumes, and raising domesticated cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. The scale this mixed farming economy could support is dramatically illustrated by the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, examined further in Section 3.5, whose settlements, some covering several hundred hectares and housing what some archaeologists estimate at several thousand to, in exceptional cases, more than ten thousand inhabitants, rank among the largest human settlements anywhere in the world during the fourth millennium BCE, well before comparable urban scale developed in most of the rest of Europe. These population estimates remain debated: archaeologists disagree about whether such settlements were occupied continuously by their full estimated population or instead supported large but more seasonal or fluctuating aggregations, and recent household-level research at several Trypillia mega-sites suggests that, despite their enormous scale, material differences between individual households may have remained comparatively modest — a finding that cautions against assuming that settlement size alone translated automatically into steep social hierarchy.

On the open steppe, by contrast, where rainfall was less reliable and soils, though often fertile, could not support dependable rain-fed grain agriculture across most of the zone (as Chapter 2 explained in detail), Neolithic and Copper Age communities increasingly turned toward a pastoral economy centered on the herding of domesticated cattle, sheep, and goats. The history of the horse in this region is considerably more complicated, and has been considerably more recently revised, than older accounts often suggest, and it is important to distinguish several separate developments rather than treating "horse domestication" as a single fourth-millennium event. Intensive horse husbandry is archaeologically documented at Botai, in what is now northern Kazakhstan, by approximately 3500 BCE, but large-scale ancient-genome research published in 2024 demonstrated that the Botai horses belonged to a distinct lineage that is not the direct ancestor of nearly all modern domestic horses. That modern lineage, which geneticists refer to as DOM2, underwent the reproductive management and rapid, continent-wide expansion associated with true domestication only around 2200 BCE — considerably later than once assumed — and the same research concludes that widespread horse-based mobility did not drive the Yamnaya expansions of roughly 3300–2600 BCE examined in Section 3.5, since those expansions occurred before DOM2 horses became widespread across the steppe. Horseback riding on any significant scale, and, still later, horse-drawn chariot technology — well attested at Sintashta-Petrovka sites by approximately 2000–1800 BCE, discussed further in Section 3.5 — therefore represent separate and later developments building upon, rather than identical with, this earlier and more limited history of horse herding. Taken together, these developments, combined with the increasing mobility of pastoral economies more generally, fundamentally transformed the steppe's economic and, eventually, military potential over the course of the third and second millennia BCE, laying groundwork for the historically documented mobile pastoral societies — the Scythians and Sarmatians, among others — that Chapter 4 examines in detail.

The forest-steppe and, to a lesser extent, the northern edge of the open steppe functioned, from the Neolithic period onward, as a genuine frontier zone of contact and exchange between farming and pastoral economies, a pattern this chapter's Multiple Perspectives feature below and Chapter 2's Section 2.8 both examine from different angles. Farming communities in the forest-steppe likely maintained herds of their own alongside cultivated fields, while steppe pastoral communities almost certainly obtained grain, and perhaps other agricultural products, through exchange with neighboring farming populations, a pattern of interdependence between mobile pastoral and settled agricultural economies that, in modified form, would persist for thousands of years and that this textbook's later chapters — on the Scythians, the Khazars, the Mongols, and Cossack frontier society — will examine repeatedly in historically documented contexts.

Farther north, in the mixed-forest and taiga zones, farming spread far more slowly and unevenly, and in the far north — the tundra and much of the taiga — hunting, fishing, and gathering, sometimes eventually supplemented by reindeer herding (itself a distinctive and much later form of animal domestication, associated with the ancestors of the Sámi, Nenets, and other Arctic and sub-Arctic peoples discussed in Chapter 5), remained the dominant subsistence strategies for millennia after farming had already transformed economic life in the forest-steppe and steppe zones to the south. This persistent north-south economic differentiation, rooted in the environmental conditions Chapter 2 examined, recurs as a durable pattern throughout much of the history this textbook will narrate.

Cause and Effect

From Foraging to Farming: A Multi-Causal Transformation

Long-Term Conditions

Postglacial climatic stabilization; the prior domestication of cereals and livestock in southwest Asia several millennia earlier; the existence of the fertile forest-steppe chernozem zone as a receptive environment for grain cultivation.

Intermediate Developments

Gradual westward and northward spread of farming communities and techniques across southeastern and central Europe over several thousand years; increasing contact between incoming farming populations and existing Mesolithic hunter-gatherer communities in the forest-steppe zone.

Short-Term Triggers

Localized population pressure, resource decisions, and cultural contact events (varying by region) that led specific communities to adopt cultivation, animal husbandry, or both, whether through migration, intermarriage, exchange, or direct cultural borrowing.

Effects

Increased sedentism, larger settlements, growing social differentiation (examined in Section 3.7), the eventual emergence of exceptionally large Copper Age settlements such as those of the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, and a durable long-term economic distinction between farming and pastoral zones that persisted for millennia.

Historical complexity: Ancient DNA evidence indicates that the spread of farming into this region involved both migration of farming populations and cultural adoption by existing populations, in varying combinations by region; no single mechanism explains the transition everywhere.

Section 3.5

Copper and Bronze Age Societies

The Copper Age, sometimes called the Chalcolithic (from Greek roots meaning "copper" and "stone," reflecting the continued use of stone tools alongside emerging copper metallurgy), and the subsequent Bronze Age, defined by the development of bronze — an alloy of copper and tin, harder and more versatile than copper alone — mark a further major transformation in the technological, economic, and social organization of northern Eurasia's prehistoric communities, occurring across roughly the fourth through the early first millennia BCE, with considerable regional variation in timing.

The Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, already introduced in Section 3.4, represents one of the most remarkable Copper Age developments in this region. Centered on the forest-steppe of what is now Moldova, western Ukraine, and northeastern Romania, and flourishing roughly between 4800 and 3000 BCE, this culture is known for its exceptionally large settlements — some of the largest known human settlements anywhere in the world during this period — organized in concentric or radial patterns of substantial timber-framed houses, some with two stories, and, remarkably, showing evidence that many houses — in some excavated settlements, a large majority of them — were deliberately burned, particularly at the time of abandonment; the social and ritual meaning of this practice (proposed explanations include ritual closure, hygienic concerns, and soil fertility management, among others) remains debated among archaeologists, as does the question of how consistently this pattern extended across entire settlements rather than particular houses or neighborhoods. Cucuteni-Trypillia communities produced distinctive, elaborately painted pottery, cultivated wheat, barley, and other crops, raised cattle, sheep, and pigs, and worked copper, obtained through exchange networks extending toward the Balkan Peninsula, one of the significant early sources of copper ore and copper-working technology in this broader region.

Farther east and north, the Yamnaya horizon (sometimes called the Pit Grave culture, after its characteristic burial practice, discussed further in Section 3.7) spread across the Pontic-Caspian steppe beginning around the thirty-third century BCE, representing a major and historiographically significant development in steppe pastoral economy and mobility, and one that archaeologists generally classify as an Early Bronze Age horizon even though it emerged directly out of the preceding Eneolithic, or Copper Age, period. Yamnaya communities practiced a mobile pastoral economy centered on cattle and sheep herding and are associated by many archaeologists and geneticists with a substantial and rapid population expansion across the steppe and, in the following centuries, into parts of Europe — a population movement that, as Section 3.8 will examine in detail, many scholars connect to the early dispersal of Indo-European languages, though this connection remains an active subject of scholarly interpretation rather than a matter of unanimous agreement. Yamnaya-related ancestry also became an important component of later, second-millennium BCE steppe populations, including the Sintashta-Petrovka and related Andronovo-horizon communities discussed below; it was the subsequent movement of these later populations through Central Asia, rather than any direct Yamnaya migration, that eventually carried steppe-derived ancestry into South Asia after approximately 2000 BCE.

In the forest zone, the Corded Ware cultural horizon (also called Battle Axe culture in some regional contexts, after a characteristic stone tool type), spreading across much of forested Europe including parts of the East European Plain from roughly 2900 to 2350 BCE, introduced new burial practices — typically single burials, often with distinctive cord-decorated pottery and stone battle-axes as grave goods — that many archaeologists interpret as reflecting new social structures, quite possibly associated with population movement from the steppe region into the forest zone, a movement substantially confirmed by ancient DNA studies published over the past decade. The Fatyanovo culture, a regional expression of this broader Corded Ware phenomenon in the upper Volga and Oka river basins, provides significant evidence for early metalworking and changing burial customs in the central forest zone of what is now European Russia.

Archaeologists classify the beginning of the Bronze Age somewhat differently from region to region: across much of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, the Yamnaya expansion discussed above, beginning around 3300 BCE, is itself generally classified as Early Bronze Age, while other regional sequences place a formally defined Bronze Age somewhat later, following an intervening Eneolithic or transitional period. What is sometimes called the "Bronze Age proper" in this region — the period of widespread bronze metallurgy, increasingly fortified settlements, and, in some areas, chariot technology — is generally dated from roughly the twenty-first through the earlier first millennium BCE, again with considerable local variation in both starting and ending dates. The Sintashta-Petrovka culture of the southern Ural steppe, flourishing roughly 2100–1800 BCE, is particularly significant for archaeological evidence of fortified, carefully planned circular settlements and some of the earliest well-documented spoked-wheel chariots anywhere in the world, technology of considerable later military significance across Eurasia. Farther west and south, the Srubnaya, or Timber-Grave, culture (flourishing roughly 1850–1200 BCE) dominated much of the Pontic-Caspian steppe in the later Bronze Age, named for its characteristic burial chambers lined with wooden timbers, and associated with extensive bronze metalworking, a substantially pastoral economy, and, in its material culture, connections extending across a very wide area of the Eurasian steppe.

These Bronze Age developments — fortified settlements, chariot warfare, extensive metalworking, and increasingly organized, hierarchical societies, evidenced particularly through burial practices examined in Section 3.7 — form part of the deeper archaeological background from which the historically documented Iron Age steppe societies examined in Chapter 4 eventually emerged. Students should not, however, picture a single, unbroken line running directly from Srubnaya to the Scythians: further Late Bronze Age and Early Iron Age transformations, not examined in detail in this textbook, intervened between cultures such as Srubnaya and Sintashta-Petrovka and the historically documented Cimmerians and Scythians that Chapter 4 discusses using both archaeological and, increasingly, textual evidence from Greek and other contemporary observers.

Section 3.6

The Development of Long-Distance Exchange

Chapter 2 emphasized that the river-and-portage trade networks of the historically documented Rus' period had deep environmental preconditions extending far back before any written record. This section demonstrates that long-distance exchange itself, not merely the environmental corridors that would later support it, has a documented prehistory reaching back to the Paleolithic period, and that the scale and complexity of prehistoric exchange networks expanded substantially across the Neolithic, Copper, and Bronze Ages examined in this chapter.

Even Upper Paleolithic communities, as the Mezhirich evidence discussed in Section 3.1 demonstrated, obtained materials — in that case, shell ornaments — from sources located a considerable distance from their settlements, indicating some combination of direct long-distance movement, exchange between neighboring groups passing goods along a chain of intermediaries, or both. Similar evidence of long-distance material movement, including certain distinctive stone types used for tool production that can be traced, through geological sourcing analysis, to specific and sometimes distant quarry sites, recurs throughout the Paleolithic and Mesolithic archaeological record, indicating that human communities in this region were never entirely isolated, self-contained units even at the earliest periods this chapter examines.

The Neolithic and Copper Age periods saw a substantial elaboration of these exchange patterns. The copper used by Cucuteni-Trypillia communities, as noted in Section 3.5, arrived through exchange networks extending toward the Balkans, one of early Europe's most significant sources of copper ore and metalworking expertise; in return, forest-steppe communities likely exchanged agricultural products, textiles, or other goods not directly documented in the archaeological record, since organic materials of this kind rarely survive. Amber, the fossilized tree resin found in significant quantities along the Baltic Sea coast, provides one of the best-documented examples of a specific, traceable long-distance exchange commodity: Baltic amber has been found, through analysis of its distinctive chemical and physical properties, at archaeological sites hundreds and even thousands of kilometers from its Baltic source, including sites associated with the Bronze Age cultures examined in Section 3.5, demonstrating that exchange networks connecting the Baltic region to more southern and eastern parts of the East European Plain, and even beyond, were already well established by the Bronze Age, long before the historically documented Varangian and Rus' trade networks examined in Chapter 6.

Bronze Age exchange networks also moved metal itself, and the technological knowledge required to work it, across substantial distances. Tin, essential for bronze production and considerably rarer in its natural distribution than copper, had to be obtained, in many cases, from sources located far from where bronze objects were ultimately produced and used, requiring exchange networks capable of moving this scarce and valuable material across considerable distances. The similarities in metal object types, decorative styles, and metalworking techniques across widely separated Bronze Age archaeological cultures — similarities that allow archaeologists to trace connections between, for example, steppe Bronze Age cultures and contemporary societies in the Caucasus, Central Asia, and even further afield — provide indirect but compelling evidence for sustained long-distance contact, whether through direct trade, the movement of specialist metalworkers, or the diffusion of technological knowledge through intermediary populations.

Understanding this deep prehistory of exchange matters for how students approach later chapters of this textbook. The elaborate riverine and steppe trade networks examined in Chapter 6 — connecting the Baltic, the Volga, the Black Sea, and the Islamic world — did not spring into existence with the Varangians or the Khazars; they built upon environmental corridors and, in some respects, upon patterns of long-distance contact and exchange that had already been developing, in progressively more elaborate forms, for many thousands of years. This continuity should not be overstated into a claim of unbroken historical connection — the specific participants, goods, and political arrangements changed dramatically across this span of time — but it does demonstrate that long-distance connection, rather than isolation, has been a persistent feature of this region's history since the very earliest periods for which evidence survives.

Section 3.7

Burial Practices and Social Differentiation

In the absence of written records, burial practices provide historians and archaeologists with some of the richest available evidence for prehistoric social organization, belief, and status differentiation, because the way a community buries its dead — the location and structure of a grave, the presence or absence and character of accompanying grave goods, and the treatment of the body itself — typically reflects, in some form, that community's beliefs about death, its social hierarchy, and its sense of identity and community membership.

Upper Paleolithic burials in this region, though relatively rare in the surviving archaeological record (likely reflecting both genuine rarity of formal burial and the poor preservation or non-discovery of many burial sites), sometimes show evidence of elaborate treatment, including red ochre pigment application, personal ornaments, and careful body positioning, suggesting ritual significance and, in some interpreted cases, differential treatment that may indicate emerging status distinctions even within small, mobile Paleolithic bands, though archaeologists exercise considerable caution in inferring social structure from a small number of exceptional burials.

Neolithic and Copper Age burial practices generally show greater elaboration and, in many cases, clearer evidence of social differentiation. Communal burial structures, in which multiple individuals were interred together over an extended period, appear at various Neolithic sites and are sometimes interpreted as reflecting a communal, less individually differentiated social structure, particularly in earlier farming communities. By the Copper Age, however, a shift toward practices that more clearly distinguish specific individuals, including through the quantity and quality of grave goods, becomes archaeologically visible in various regional contexts, suggesting the gradual emergence of more clearly defined individual status and, potentially, inherited social position.

The kurgan, an earthen burial mound raised over a grave, often containing a single primary burial along with grave goods and sometimes accompanied by additional later burials inserted into the same mound, has roots earlier than the Yamnaya horizon: modest earthen mounds are archaeologically documented in Eneolithic, or Copper Age, steppe contexts, including sites in the North Caucasus, from at least the mid-fifth millennium BCE onward. It was with the Yamnaya horizon, however, that mound burial became an especially widespread and archaeologically prominent feature of steppe burial practice across an enormous stretch of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, and it remained a characteristic feature of steppe burial practice for thousands of years afterward, including, as later chapters will note, among the historically documented Scythian and Sarmatian societies examined in Chapter 4. Kurgan burials vary enormously in scale and elaboration, from relatively modest mounds with simple grave goods to, in later periods and among higher-status individuals, substantial mounds containing elaborate grave assemblages including weapons, ornaments, wheeled vehicles, and, in some documented cases, evidence of sacrificed animals or, controversially and much debated among archaeologists, possible evidence of human sacrifice accompanying particularly high-status burials. The variation in kurgan scale and grave-good elaboration provides archaeologists with one of their most direct lines of evidence for the emergence of social stratification — meaning a society organized into distinct ranks or classes with unequal access to resources, status, and authority — among prehistoric steppe populations.

Bronze Age burial evidence, including the fortified settlements and associated cemeteries of the Sintashta-Petrovka culture and the timber-chamber graves of the Srubnaya culture discussed in Section 3.5, similarly demonstrates increasing social differentiation, with certain burials — often, though not exclusively, associated with male individuals and containing weapons, chariot equipment, and other high-status items — clearly distinguished from more modest surrounding burials. Archaeologists interpret this pattern as evidence for the emergence of a warrior elite or similarly defined high-status social group within these Bronze Age steppe societies, a development with obvious continuity toward the more elaborately documented social and political hierarchies of the historically attested Scythian and Sarmatian societies examined in the following chapter.

Students should approach burial evidence with appropriate methodological caution. A grave assemblage reflects the choices and resources of the survivors who conducted the burial, not necessarily a direct, unmediated reflection of the deceased individual's actual lifetime status, wealth, or role; burial practices are themselves a form of cultural and, at times, political expression, shaped by ritual convention, religious belief, and social display, rather than a simple photographic record of social reality. Nonetheless, when burial evidence is considered across many graves and over extended time periods, consistent patterns — such as the progressive emergence of markedly unequal grave-good distribution — provide historians with genuinely valuable, if indirect, evidence for broader processes of social change.

Historical Vocabulary
Kurgan

Kurgan is the term, adopted into English and international archaeological usage from a Turkic-derived word used widely across the Eurasian steppe, for an artificial earthen burial mound raised over one or more graves. Modest kurgan-style mound burials are documented in Eneolithic steppe contexts predating Yamnaya by well over a thousand years, but it was with the Yamnaya horizon of the Pontic-Caspian steppe that mound burial became an especially widespread and defining feature of steppe burial practice, continuing for thousands of years afterward, including among the historically documented Scythian and Sarmatian societies examined in Chapter 4, whose elaborately furnished kurgan burials — containing gold and silver artifacts, weapons, and sometimes sacrificed horses — provide some of the richest surviving evidence for these ancient steppe civilizations.

Kurgans matter historically for two distinct reasons. First, they provide direct physical evidence — through their scale, construction, and grave contents — for social stratification, wealth, and status distinctions in societies that otherwise left no written record. Second, because kurgans are visible, durable features on the steppe landscape, they became, in some contexts, sites of later memory, reuse, and even reverence by much later populations who had no direct historical connection to the original builders but who nonetheless incorporated these ancient monuments into their own understanding of the landscape they inhabited.

Section 3.8

Languages, Migrations, and Archaeological Interpretation

This section addresses directly one of the most historiographically sensitive and scientifically active areas of prehistoric research relevant to this textbook: the relationship between archaeological cultures, patterns of prehistoric migration, and the spread of language families, particularly the Indo-European language family, to which most of the languages examined in this textbook's later chapters — including Slavic, Baltic, and Iranian languages, along with most European and many South Asian languages — ultimately belong.

The concept of an archaeological culture requires careful initial definition, since it will recur throughout the remainder of this section. An archaeological culture is a recurring, geographically and chronologically bounded assemblage of artifact types, technologies, burial practices, and other material patterns that archaeologists identify in the excavated record and to which they assign a conventional label, typically derived from a type site (the location where the pattern was first identified or is best documented) or from a distinctive artifact type (as with Corded Ware or Pit-Comb Ware pottery). Crucially, an archaeological culture is defined entirely by shared material patterns visible in the archaeological record; it is not, and cannot be, defined by shared language, shared ethnic self-identification, or shared political organization, none of which leave direct traces in the material record of a non-literate society.

This distinction matters enormously because a persistent temptation, evident in scholarship from the nineteenth century onward and periodically revived for political purposes since, has been to treat an archaeological culture as though it corresponded neatly to a single ethnic group, a single language community, or even a direct ancestor of a specific modern nation. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalist scholarship in numerous European countries, including within the Russian Empire and later the Soviet Union, as well as in many of Russia's neighboring states, at various points advanced claims of this kind, asserting direct, unbroken descent from specific prehistoric archaeological cultures in order to establish claims of national antiquity, cultural priority, or territorial entitlement. Contemporary archaeology and, especially, ancient DNA analysis have demonstrated that the actual historical relationship between archaeological cultures, language spread, and population movement is considerably more complex, involving repeated episodes of migration, population mixture, cultural adoption without population replacement, and language shift without accompanying population replacement, in varying combinations across different times and places.

The question of Indo-European origins illustrates these complexities directly. Linguists have long recognized that the Indo-European language family — encompassing most languages of Europe, Iran, and northern India, and reconstructed by comparative linguistic methods to derive from a single ancestral language, conventionally called Proto-Indo-European, spoken at some point in prehistory — must have spread from some original homeland across an enormous geographic range through some combination of migration and language shift. Several competing hypotheses regarding the location and timing of this Proto-Indo-European homeland have been proposed over more than a century of scholarly debate. The hypothesis that has gained the strongest support from recent archaeological and, especially, ancient DNA evidence — sometimes called the steppe hypothesis or the Kurgan hypothesis, associated originally with the twentieth-century archaeologist Marija Gimbutas — locates the deeper origins of the Indo-European languages broadly within the Pontic-Caspian steppe and adjacent regions, building on still earlier Eneolithic steppe traditions such as the Khvalynsk complex of the middle Volga, with the Yamnaya horizon examined in Section 3.5 playing the central role in their subsequent, wide geographic dispersal. Large-scale ancient-DNA research published in 2025 has proposed a more finely resolved version of this model: it identifies an earlier population associated with the Caucasus and Lower Volga region as the likely setting for a shared Proto-Indo-Anatolian ancestral stage around 4400–4000 BCE, and it identifies the Serednii Stih archaeological complex of the Dnipro-Don region as more immediately ancestral to the later, more widely dispersed Indo-European branches. On this reconstruction, the populations who became archaeologically visible as Yamnaya formed from this broader lineage and then carried Indo-European speech outward across an enormous area beginning around 3300 BCE. Because this remains an active and rapidly developing field of research, students should treat this specific sequence as the strongest current reconstruction supported by the available evidence, not as a final, permanently settled conclusion; continuing archaeogenetic and linguistic research may yet refine or revise it further.

Ancient DNA studies published over the past decade have provided striking, though not entirely uncontested, support for significant elements of this hypothesis, documenting substantial genetic input from steppe-associated populations into the ancestry of later European populations, coinciding chronologically with the archaeologically documented spread of the Corded Ware cultural horizon discussed in Section 3.5. This evidence has been widely, though not universally, interpreted as supporting a real and substantial migration of Yamnaya-descended, likely Indo-European-speaking, populations from the steppe into the forest zone of Europe, including parts of the East European Plain, during the third millennium BCE — a genuinely significant historical population movement, though one that occurred among peoples who cannot be identified with any later named ethnic or linguistic group discussed in this textbook's subsequent chapters, since Slavic, Baltic, Germanic, and other historically attested Indo-European branches did not begin to differentiate as distinct, identifiable language communities until many centuries, in most cases well over a thousand years, after the population movements this section describes.

Students should draw several careful conclusions from this evidence. First, prehistoric population movement across northern Eurasia was real, substantial, and archaeologically and genetically documented, particularly regarding the steppe-to-forest population movement associated with the Yamnaya and Corded Ware horizons. Second, this movement cannot be equated with the emergence of any specific later historical people; the ancestors of the historically attested Slavs, discussed in Chapter 5, descend, in whatever genetic and cultural proportions modern research continues to refine, from a long and complex history of population mixture involving Yamnaya-descended, pre-existing local, and, in later periods, additional incoming populations, rather than from any single, cleanly identifiable prehistoric group. Third, language spread and population movement, though frequently correlated in the evidence this section has examined, are not identical processes; languages can and do spread through cultural adoption, elite dominance, or gradual shift without wholesale population replacement, just as significant population movement can occur without producing a corresponding, easily traceable language shift, particularly over the very long, multi-stage timeframes this chapter examines. Historians and archaeologists must therefore evaluate migration, language, and archaeological-culture evidence as three related but analytically distinct lines of inquiry, each with its own evidentiary basis and its own limitations, rather than collapsing them into a single simple narrative.

Historical Vocabulary
Archaeological Culture

Archaeological culture refers to a recurring, geographically and chronologically bounded pattern of material remains — pottery styles, tool types, burial practices, settlement forms, and related evidence — identified by archaeologists in the excavated record and given a conventional name, usually derived from a type site or a distinctive artifact. Examples introduced in this chapter include the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, the Yamnaya horizon, the Corded Ware culture, the Fatyanovo culture, the Sintashta-Petrovka culture, and the Srubnaya culture.

An archaeological culture is not the same thing as an ethnic group, a tribe, a nation, or a language community. Material culture can spread through trade, intermarriage, and cultural imitation among people who spoke different languages and considered themselves distinct communities, just as a single language community can produce, over time and across different regions, quite different material cultures. Historians and archaeologists must therefore avoid the common but methodologically unsound practice of treating an archaeological-culture label as though it named an ancient people in the same sense that "the Rus'" or "the Mongols," discussed in later chapters, name historically documented political and social communities. This distinction is essential preparation for Section 3.9's discussion of the problems involved in reconstructing prehistoric identity.

Historical Debate

How Should Archaeologists Interpret Archaeological Cultures?

The question: When archaeologists identify a recurring pattern of artifacts and practices and label it an archaeological culture, what, if anything, can they responsibly infer about the identity, language, and self-understanding of the people who produced that pattern?

The older "culture-historical" interpretation (dominant through much of the early-to-mid twentieth century): Many earlier archaeologists, working within a tradition sometimes called culture-historical archaeology, treated a clearly defined archaeological culture as a reasonably direct proxy for a specific ancient people or ethnic group, and used the geographic spread or contraction of a given archaeological culture over time as evidence for the migration, expansion, or decline of that "people." This approach produced influential early syntheses of Indo-European origins and other major prehistoric questions, but it was also, at times, adapted for nationalist political purposes, since it lent itself readily to claims of ancient ethnic continuity in a given territory.
The processual and post-processual critique (from the 1960s onward): Beginning particularly in the 1960s, archaeologists increasingly challenged the assumption that material culture patterns map neatly onto ethnic or linguistic groups, pointing out that material culture can spread through trade, imitation, intermarriage, and other forms of contact without any accompanying population movement or shared identity, and that a single, self-identified community can produce quite varied material culture across different contexts. This critique pushed archaeology toward greater caution in inferring identity, language, or ethnicity directly from artifact patterns alone.
The ancient DNA synthesis (2010s–present): The advent of ancient DNA analysis has, in some specific and well-documented cases — including the steppe-to-forest population movement associated with the Yamnaya and Corded Ware horizons discussed in this section — provided genuinely new, independent evidence for substantial population movement accompanying certain archaeological-culture transitions, lending renewed, though more carefully qualified, support to migration-based interpretations in specific instances, while still cautioning against assuming that every archaeological-culture change reflects population movement, since ancient DNA evidence must be evaluated case by case.

Evidence: Historians and archaeologists draw on stratigraphic excavation, artifact typology, radiocarbon dating, isotopic analysis of diet and geographic origin, comparative and historical linguistics, and, increasingly, ancient DNA sequencing, weighing each line of evidence against the others rather than relying on any single method alone.

Points of broad agreement: Most archaeologists today agree that some but not all archaeological-culture transitions reflect genuine population movement, that the relationship between material culture, language, and identity must be evaluated case by case, and that nationalist appropriations of archaeological evidence to support modern political claims are methodologically unsound.

Points of continued disagreement: Historians and archaeologists continue to debate the specific scale, mechanism, and social character of particular population movements (including, for example, the precise nature of the Yamnaya-associated migration into the forest zone), and the degree to which ancient DNA evidence should be allowed to revise longstanding archaeological interpretations built on other kinds of evidence.

Section 3.9

Problems in Reconstructing Prehistoric Identity

Building directly on the methodological groundwork laid in Section 3.8, this section addresses more broadly the specific problems historians and archaeologists face when attempting to reconstruct any form of identity — ethnic, linguistic, political, or social — for prehistoric peoples who left no written record explaining how they understood themselves.

The most fundamental problem is the complete absence, by definition, of any self-description. Every named archaeological culture discussed in this chapter — Cucuteni-Trypillia, Yamnaya, Corded Ware, Fatyanovo, Sintashta-Petrovka, Srubnaya — bears a name invented by modern archaeologists, based on a type site or characteristic artifact, not a name the people themselves used or would have recognized. Historians studying literate societies can, with appropriate critical caution, draw on how a society described itself, its neighbors, and its history; historians and archaeologists studying prehistoric societies have no comparable access to self-description and must instead infer social and cultural patterns entirely from material remains, a fundamentally different and more limited evidentiary situation.

A second problem concerns the scale and pace of prehistoric time compared to the resolution available in the archaeological record. Radiocarbon dating, even at its most precise, typically carries an uncertainty range of decades to a few centuries, meaning that events, migrations, or cultural changes that occurred within a human lifetime, or even across several generations, frequently cannot be distinguished from longer-term, more gradual processes using archaeological dating methods alone. This limitation means that archaeologists studying, for example, the spread of the Yamnaya horizon or the Corded Ware culture across enormous distances cannot always determine whether a given cultural or genetic pattern reflects a single, relatively rapid migratory event, several distinct waves of movement over an extended period, or a more gradual, generation-by-generation process of expansion — distinctions that matter considerably for understanding the social and political character of the movement in question.

A third problem, already introduced in Section 3.8, concerns the relationship between material culture, genetic ancestry, and language, three lines of evidence that do not always move together and that require independent verification wherever possible. A community's pottery style, its genetic ancestry, and the language its members spoke could, in principle, each follow a different historical trajectory: a population could adopt a new language while retaining its existing genetic ancestry and much of its existing material culture (a process well documented in more recent historical periods through cultural assimilation and elite dominance), or a population could experience substantial genetic input from an incoming group while retaining its existing language and only partially adopting new material culture. Responsible historical and archaeological interpretation must therefore resist the temptation to assume that a single, unified "people," possessing a shared genetic ancestry, a shared language, and a shared, clearly bounded material culture, existed at any given moment in prehistory in a form directly analogous to a modern ethnic group or nation.

A fourth and directly related problem concerns the persistent political misuse of prehistoric evidence for modern nationalist purposes, a pattern already noted in Section 3.8 but worth examining somewhat further here because of its continuing relevance. Various nationalist movements across Europe and Eurasia, over the past two centuries, have at different points claimed ancient, even prehistoric, origins for their modern national community, using archaeological evidence selectively to support claims of ancient territorial presence, cultural priority, or ethnic continuity extending back to the Bronze Age or earlier. Such claims, when examined critically against the actual archaeological, linguistic, and genetic evidence, typically prove considerably more complicated, and often considerably less supportive of straightforward ethnic continuity, than the nationalist narrative asserts. This textbook does not adjudicate contemporary political disputes, but it insists, as a matter of basic historical method, that no modern nation, including Russia and its neighbors, can responsibly claim exclusive or uniquely privileged descent from any specific prehistoric archaeological culture discussed in this chapter, since the evidence instead points toward long, complex histories of migration, mixture, and cultural exchange involving many different populations across many thousands of years, culminating, many centuries after the periods this chapter examines, in the emergence of the historically attested Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples that Chapter 5 will introduce.

None of these methodological cautions should be read as suggesting that prehistoric archaeology cannot produce reliable historical knowledge. On the contrary, the combination of careful excavation, artifact analysis, radiocarbon dating, comparative linguistics, and ancient DNA analysis has produced an increasingly detailed and increasingly well-supported picture of prehistoric population movement, technological change, and social development across northern Eurasia, as this chapter's preceding sections have demonstrated. The caution this section urges is specifically against overinterpreting that evidence — against converting well-supported archaeological and genetic findings about population movement and cultural change into unsupported claims about ancient ethnic or national identity that the evidence itself cannot sustain.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Neolithic Transition

A farming household in the forest-steppe: For a household that had adopted cereal cultivation and animal husbandry, life involved new forms of labor tied to the agricultural calendar, greater investment in a fixed dwelling and cultivated plots, and, over generations, the accumulation of stored grain and livestock that could support larger households and, eventually, more differentiated social status, but also new vulnerabilities to crop failure and new forms of labor obligation not present in a purely foraging economy.
A hunter-gatherer community encountering incoming farmers: For communities continuing to rely on hunting, fishing, and gathering in the forest zone, contact with incoming or neighboring farming populations offered opportunities for exchange of goods, ideas, and, in some documented cases, intermarriage and gradual adoption of agricultural techniques, but it could also bring competition for land and resources, and, in some regions supported by genetic evidence, eventual demographic absorption or replacement by incoming farming populations.
A pastoral household on the open steppe: For a household whose livelihood depended on herding cattle, sheep, and eventually horses, the steppe's unreliable rainfall made grain cultivation impractical across most of the zone, but exchange with forest-steppe farming communities, discussed in Section 3.4, could supply grain and other agricultural products in return for livestock, hides, and access to the growing mobility that horse domestication increasingly made possible.
A modern archaeologist studying the transition: For the archaeologist attempting to reconstruct these lived experiences many thousands of years later, the available evidence — pottery, tools, plant and animal remains, burial patterns, and, increasingly, ancient DNA — offers genuine but incomplete insight; the archaeologist must constantly ask which changes reflect a single household's lived choices, which reflect a slower, generation-spanning cultural shift, and which distinctions the evidence simply cannot resolve.

These four perspectives illustrate why the Neolithic transition, examined in Sections 3.3 and 3.4, is best understood as a varied, regionally uneven, and individually experienced process rather than a single uniform event affecting all communities identically and simultaneously.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Deep Time on the Plain

What Changed

  • Subsistence strategies shifted, unevenly across regions, from Paleolithic big-game hunting through Mesolithic diversified foraging to Neolithic farming and pastoralism.
  • Tool technology progressed from flaked stone blades through microliths, polished stone, and eventually copper and bronze metallurgy.
  • Settlement scale grew dramatically in some regions, from small mobile Paleolithic bands to the large, planned Copper Age settlements of the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture.
  • Burial evidence shows an increasing, though uneven, trend toward social differentiation and, eventually, clearly marked status distinctions.

What Stayed the Same

  • Long-distance exchange of valued materials — shell, amber, copper, tin — persisted, in progressively more elaborate form, from the Upper Paleolithic through the Bronze Age.
  • The basic environmental distinction between forest and steppe economies, established by the ecological zones described in Chapter 2, continued to shape economic adaptation throughout the periods this chapter examines and would continue to do so for thousands of years afterward.
  • Human communities throughout this entire span remained capable of cooperation, technological innovation, symbolic expression, and long-distance contact — capacities this chapter emphasizes were fully present even in the earliest periods it examines.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine transformation and the genuine continuity across this immense span of prehistory equips students to avoid two opposite errors: treating prehistoric peoples as static or unchanging, and treating the very real changes that did occur as evidence for a single, continuous national story.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Bronze Age Household on the Steppe

Archaeological evidence from Sintashta-Petrovka and Srubnaya settlements allows historians to reconstruct, in careful outline, elements of daily life in a Bronze Age steppe household. Excavated house floors reveal evidence of mixed activities within a single dwelling: hearths for cooking and warmth, evidence of textile production (spindle whorls used in spinning thread), storage areas for grain and other foodstuffs obtained partly through exchange with forest-steppe farming neighbors, and, in many Sintashta-Petrovka contexts, evidence of on-site bronze metalworking, including molds, crucibles, and slag, suggesting that metalworking was integrated into ordinary household or community life rather than confined to a small number of specialized workshops.

Livestock management occupied a central place in daily and seasonal life: cattle, sheep, and horses required regular care, seasonal movement to available pasture, and protection from both predators and, increasingly as settlements grew more numerous, competition with neighboring communities for grazing land and water access — a competitive pressure that likely contributed to the fortified character of many Sintashta-Petrovka settlements. Some archaeologists, drawing cautiously on ethnographic analogy with more recent pastoral societies, have suggested that textile production, food preparation, and childcare may often have occupied a substantial share of the labor of women and older children within the household, while herding, hunting, and warfare may often have fallen more heavily to adult men. This suggestion should be treated as a plausible hypothesis rather than an established fact: site-specific archaeological evidence for Sintashta-Petrovka and Srubnaya communities does not, by itself, consistently establish a rigid, universal division of labor by sex, and burial and activity evidence from some steppe contexts complicates any simple assumption of this kind.

This glimpse of ordinary Bronze Age life reminds students that the archaeological cultures named and mapped throughout this chapter were composed of real households engaged in the same basic human activities — cooking, raising children, managing animals, making tools, and building community — as any later society this textbook will examine, even though the specific technologies, environments, and social structures differed enormously across the millennia this chapter has surveyed.

Regional Context

The Yamnaya Horizon Across the Pontic-Caspian Steppe

The Yamnaya horizon, introduced in Section 3.5 and central to the Indo-European debate examined in Section 3.8, was never confined to a single, narrow locality; it extended across an enormous stretch of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, from the lower Danube region in the west to the southern Ural steppe in the east, encompassing territory that today falls within several modern states, including Moldova, Ukraine, Russia, and Kazakhstan. Students should resist imagining this horizon as a single unified political entity — no evidence supports the existence of centralized Yamnaya political authority extending across so vast a territory — and should instead understand it as a broadly shared pastoral economy, burial practice, and set of technologies shared across a network of related, but almost certainly politically independent, communities linked by common ancestry, mobility, and exchange rather than by any overarching state structure.

This regional perspective matters for understanding later chapters as well. The Pontic-Caspian steppe would remain, for thousands of years after the Yamnaya period, a corridor of exceptional historical significance, home in turn to the Cimmerians, Scythians, and Sarmatians examined in Chapter 4, the Huns, Avars, and Khazars examined in Chapters 5 and 6, and eventually the Mongols examined in Chapter 16 — a recurring pattern of steppe mobility and political formation of which the Yamnaya horizon, examined in this section, is an early and exceptionally well-documented large-scale expression, itself building upon still earlier Eneolithic steppe traditions — including the Khvalynsk and Serednii Stih complexes discussed in Section 3.8 — that already show evidence of mobility, livestock economy, and long-distance exchange.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the deep prehistory of northern Eurasia, from the earliest well-attested presence of anatomically modern humans on the East European Plain more than forty thousand years ago through the complex, metal-working, socially differentiated societies of the Bronze Age, immediately preceding the historically documented steppe civilizations examined in Chapter 4. Across this immense span of time, human communities adapted successfully to dramatic environmental transformation, from the extreme cold of the last Ice Age's steppe-tundra to the forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones that stabilized after the ice retreated.

The chapter examined Upper Paleolithic communities who drew on mammoth remains for construction, whose remarkable mammoth-bone structures at sites such as Mezhirich demonstrate sophisticated technological and social adaptation to an extremely challenging environment, and whose imported ornaments provide early evidence for long-distance exchange. It then traced the Mesolithic adaptations that followed the retreat of the ice sheets, as communities developed new technologies, including microlithic tools, suited to a more forested, more resource-dispersed postglacial landscape.

The chapter examined the regionally uneven spread of the Southwest Asian farming package — the specific wheat, barley, legume, sheep, goat, cattle, and pig complex relevant to this region, one of several agricultural traditions that arose independently in different parts of the world — into the forest-steppe zone of this textbook's territory by roughly the sixth millennium BCE, while pottery-using hunting, fishing, and gathering communities persisted for centuries or millennia longer in the forest and tundra zones farther north. It distinguished the increasingly intensive grain-and-livestock farming economy of the fertile forest-steppe, exemplified by the exceptionally large settlements of the Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, from the increasingly pastoral economy that developed on the more arid open steppe, while showing that horse husbandry, the later domestication and expansion of the modern DOM2 horse lineage, riding, and chariot traction were separate developments unfolding over a long chronological span rather than a single fourth-millennium event.

The chapter then examined the Copper and Bronze Ages, tracing the emergence of metalworking, increasingly fortified settlements, chariot technology, and — through the evidence of burial practices examined in Section 3.7 — increasingly pronounced social stratification, from the Yamnaya horizon of the Pontic-Caspian steppe through the Corded Ware and Fatyanovo cultures of the forest zone to the Sintashta-Petrovka and Srubnaya cultures of the later Bronze Age steppe.

Sections 3.6 through 3.9 addressed the chapter's central methodological concerns. The chapter demonstrated that long-distance exchange networks, later elaborated into the historically documented riverine trade systems examined in Chapter 6, had genuine prehistoric antecedents reaching back to the Paleolithic period. It introduced the concept of the archaeological culture as a pattern of material remains, carefully distinguished from ethnic, linguistic, or national identity, and examined the historiographically significant and scientifically active debate over the relationship between the Yamnaya horizon, the spread of Corded Ware material culture, and the spread of Indo-European languages, an area substantially reshaped in recent years by ancient DNA evidence. Finally, the chapter examined, in general terms, the specific evidentiary problems involved in reconstructing prehistoric identity, and explicitly rejected nationalist claims of direct, exclusive descent from any specific prehistoric archaeological culture.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized that the peoples examined here cannot be identified with any later named ethnic or national group discussed in this textbook's subsequent chapters. The historically attested Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples that Chapter 5 will introduce emerged, many centuries after the periods this chapter has examined, from a long, complex history of population movement, mixture, and cultural exchange involving many different populations, rather than from any single, cleanly identifiable prehistoric ancestor.

This chapter's methodological lessons — evidentiary caution in the absence of written sources, attention to gradual and regionally uneven historical processes, careful distinction between material culture and identity, and resistance to nationalist appropriation of prehistoric evidence — will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in Chapter 4's examination of the historically documented Scythians and Sarmatians, and in Chapter 5's introduction of the specific peoples whose emergence forms the immediate prelude to the formation of Rus', examined beginning in Chapter 7.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The "Old Stone Age," the earliest and longest period of human prehistory, characterized by flaked stone tool technology and, in this region, adaptation to Ice Age steppe-tundra conditions.
Mesolithic
The "Middle Stone Age," the period following the retreat of continental ice sheets, characterized by microlithic technology and adaptation to newly forming forest and wetland environments.
Neolithic
The "New Stone Age," characterized by polished stone tools and pottery, and, in southern regions such as the forest-steppe, by the beginnings of plant cultivation and animal domestication; in much of the northern forest zone, pottery-using "Neolithic" communities continued to rely on hunting, fishing, and gathering without adopting farming.
Chalcolithic (Copper Age)
The period marked by the emergence of copper metallurgy alongside continued stone tool use, associated in this region with cultures such as Cucuteni-Trypillia and with the Eneolithic steppe traditions that preceded the Early Bronze Age Yamnaya horizon.
Bronze Age
The period marked by the widespread adoption of bronze (a copper-tin alloy), associated with increasingly fortified settlements, chariot technology, and pronounced social stratification.
Archaeological culture
A recurring, geographically and chronologically bounded pattern of artifacts and practices identified by archaeologists, not to be equated with an ethnic group, tribe, or nation.
Kurgan
An earthen burial mound with roots in pre-Yamnaya Eneolithic steppe traditions; mound burial became an especially widespread and defining feature of steppe burial practice during the Early Bronze Age Yamnaya horizon and for thousands of years afterward.
Domestication
The process by which humans bring plant or animal species under human control through selective breeding, fundamentally transforming both the domesticated species and human subsistence strategies.
Sedentism
A settled, non-mobile way of life, generally associated with, though not exclusively caused by, the adoption of agriculture.
Microlith
A small, carefully shaped stone tool component, characteristic of Mesolithic technology, combined with other components to form composite tools and weapons.
Loess
A fine, wind-deposited soil common across parts of the East European Plain, within which many Paleolithic sites, including Mezhirich, are preserved.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered deposits in an archaeological or geological site, used to establish relative chronology.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific dating method measuring the decay of radioactive carbon-14 in organic remains, used to establish chronologies independent of written records.
Ancient DNA (aDNA)
Genetic material extracted from ancient human or animal remains, used increasingly since the 2010s to trace prehistoric population movement, mixture, and replacement.
Indo-European
A large language family encompassing most languages of Europe, Iran, and northern India, reconstructed to derive from a single ancestral language whose original homeland and spread remain the subject of the debate examined in Section 3.8.
Steppe hypothesis (Kurgan hypothesis)
A family of models locating the deeper origins and major dispersals of Indo-European languages in the Pontic-Caspian steppe and adjacent regions. Recent archaeogenetic research has proposed a more detailed sequence involving an earlier Caucasus–Lower Volga population, the Serednii Stih complex, and the later Yamnaya expansion beginning around 3300 BCE, though the field continues to develop.
Social stratification
The organization of a society into distinct ranks or classes with unequal access to resources, status, and authority, traced archaeologically primarily through burial evidence.
Grave goods
Objects placed with a burial, used by archaeologists as evidence for status, belief, and social differentiation.
Cucuteni-Trypillia culture
A Copper Age archaeological culture of the forest-steppe, known for exceptionally large settlements and painted pottery, flourishing roughly 4800–3000 BCE.
Yamnaya horizon
A Bronze Age archaeological horizon of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, associated with pit-grave kurgan burials and, by many scholars, with early Indo-European-speaking populations.
Corded Ware culture
A broad archaeological horizon spreading across forested Europe, including parts of the East European Plain, associated with cord-decorated pottery and, per ancient DNA evidence, substantial steppe-derived population input.
Sintashta-Petrovka culture
A Bronze Age archaeological culture of the southern Ural steppe, notable for fortified settlements and early spoked-wheel chariot technology.
Srubnaya (Timber-Grave) culture
A later Bronze Age archaeological culture of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, named for its timber-lined burial chambers.
Population replacement
A demographic process in which an incoming population substantially or entirely displaces a pre-existing population, distinguished by geneticists and archaeologists from cultural diffusion without population change.
Cultural diffusion
The spread of technologies, practices, or ideas between communities through contact and exchange, without necessarily involving population movement or replacement.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students across an almost unimaginable span of time — from Ice Age hunters constructing shelters from mammoth bone to Bronze Age communities working metal, building fortified settlements, and burying their dead beneath earthen mounds still visible on the steppe landscape today. None of these communities can be claimed by any single modern nation, and none of them left a written word explaining how they understood themselves. And yet their history is not therefore unknowable, or unimportant: careful archaeological and scientific method has recovered a genuinely rich, if always partial and continually revised, picture of how human beings first settled, adapted to, and transformed the environments that Chapter 2 described.

The chapter's central methodological lesson — that an archaeological culture is not the same thing as an ethnic group or a nation — is not a narrow technical point relevant only to specialists. It is a caution students should carry forward into every later chapter of this textbook, since the temptation to read modern national identities backward into the deep past recurs, in different forms, throughout the study of Rus', Muscovy, the Russian Empire, and the Soviet Union, wherever later political movements have sought to claim ancient legitimacy for contested modern boundaries or identities.

At the same time, this chapter has shown that the deep prehistory of northern Eurasia is not simply a blank prologue to be passed over quickly on the way to "real" history. The transition to agriculture, the emergence of metalworking, the development of long-distance exchange networks, and the gradual emergence of social stratification are each genuine, historically significant transformations, whose consequences — an agricultural forest-steppe heartland, a mobile pastoral steppe economy, and enduring networks of exchange connecting forest and steppe, north and south — would continue to shape the region's history for thousands of years afterward, into the periods this textbook's later chapters will examine in far greater documentary detail.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 4's examination of the Scythians, Sarmatians, and the ancient steppe world — the first peoples of this region to appear, however incompletely, in written sources produced by outside observers — students should carry forward the archaeological vocabulary, the methodological caution against equating material culture with ethnic identity, and the appreciation for deep historical time that this chapter has introduced. The peoples examined in this chapter had no name that has come down to us, but their achievements — technological, social, and artistic — form part of the deep, shared inheritance of everyone who now lives upon the land they once inhabited.
