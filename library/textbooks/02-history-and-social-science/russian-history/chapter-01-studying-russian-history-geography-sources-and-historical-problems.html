Part I — The Lands and Peoples Before Rus'

Studying Russian History: Geography, Sources, and Historical Problems

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

"To study Russian history honestly is to study more than one nation. It is to study a vast and shifting Eurasian world of forests and steppes, rivers and frontiers, in which many peoples, states, and traditions have met, competed, and changed one another across more than a thousand years."

On July 26, 1951, the archaeologist Nina Akulova, working on an excavation along the ancient wooden pavement of Kholopia Street in the northern Russian city of Novgorod, uncovered a small strip of birch bark, rolled tight and buried in damp clay for nearly a thousand years. When conservators carefully unrolled it, they found scratched letters in an early form of the East Slavic language recording feudal dues owed to a local landholder — not a literary or religious text, but a routine administrative note. It was the first of what would eventually become more than eleven hundred birch-bark documents recovered from Novgorod's waterlogged soil, a form of preservation so effective that the clay had protected organic material for centuries. Later finds in the same growing corpus include debts, marriage arrangements, children's writing exercises, business disputes, and everyday complaints written by merchants, craftsmen, priests, and, remarkably, children learning to write. They were not composed for historians. They were composed by ordinary medieval townspeople going about ordinary business, and their survival transformed what historians could know about literacy, commerce, and daily life in medieval Rus' — the loose federation of East Slavic principalities from which later Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian history would all, in different ways, trace part of their descent.

The birch-bark letters of Novgorod illustrate a central truth about the study of Russian history: the evidence historians use to reconstruct the past is neither uniform nor complete, and much of what is known today has come not from grand chronicles or royal archives but from careful, patient work — digging in wet clay, deciphering faded ink, comparing chronicle accounts against archaeological finds, and cross-checking oral traditions against inscriptions. Russian history spans more than a millennium of written record and tens of thousands of years of human habitation across a landmass that stretches from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean. No single document, no single dynasty, and no single people can account for the whole of it. This textbook studies that vast history — not as the story of one uninterrupted nation marching from a distant origin to a predetermined destination, but as the history of many overlapping peoples, states, environments, and traditions that intersected across the Eurasian landmass and whose descendants today live in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and beyond.

This is also, unavoidably, a contested history. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the history of the lands that became the Russian Empire and later the Soviet Union was written to serve particular political purposes: to justify the authority of tsars claiming descent from ancient rulers, to defend the international position of the Soviet state, to support nationalist claims by successor states after 1991, or to explain, after the fact, why a particular outcome — autocracy, empire, revolution, or collapse — was supposedly inevitable. Some of this history was written by outsiders who knew the region only through the reports of others and who carried their own assumptions about what a "normal" pattern of state development should look like. Other accounts were produced under governments that suppressed inconvenient evidence or required scholars to reach predetermined conclusions. Untangling careful historical reconstruction from politically motivated myth-making is one of the central intellectual tasks of this textbook, and it begins in this opening chapter.

Students beginning this subject often arrive with a simplified mental map: a single line running from ancient Kyiv to medieval Moscow to the tsars to the Soviet Union to modern Russia, as though each stage were the natural, inevitable successor to the one before it. This chapter asks students to set that simplified map aside. Kyiv, Novgorod, Vladimir, and dozens of other Rus' principalities coexisted, competed, and diverged over centuries. Moscow's later dominance was neither automatic nor foreordained; other cities, other dynasties, and other political traditions might plausibly have prevailed instead. The lands eventually gathered under Muscovite, then imperial, then Soviet rule were never ethnically, linguistically, or religiously uniform, and many of the peoples living within those borders — Ukrainians, Belarusians, Tatars, Bashkirs, peoples of the Caucasus and Central Asia, Siberian indigenous communities, Finns, Baltic peoples, Poles, Jews, and many others — experienced Russian and Soviet rule differently from ethnic Russians, and often understood their own histories in terms quite distinct from a Moscow-centered narrative.

At the same time, this chapter does not ask students to abandon the idea that there is a coherent subject called Russian history worth studying carefully. There plainly is. The political structures that emerged from medieval Rus', the Orthodox Christian civilization that took root along the Dnieper and Volga rivers, the Muscovite state that expanded across northern Eurasia, the Russian Empire that became one of the great powers of the modern world, the Soviet Union that reshaped the twentieth century, and the Russian Federation that emerged from its collapse together constitute one of the most consequential and least well understood historical trajectories in world history for many students outside the region. Studying it seriously requires geographic literacy across an extraordinary landmass, careful attention to several distinct kinds of historical evidence, precise use of terminology that changes meaning across periods, and constant vigilance against national mythology — whether Russian, Soviet, or of any other successor state — that would flatten this complexity into a simple story.

This chapter, then, is a toolkit. It introduces the geography that shaped where people settled, how they moved, and how states formed. It introduces the evidence — archaeological, written, oral, visual, and statistical — that historians use to reconstruct more than a thousand years of change. It introduces the vocabulary that distinguishes Rus' from Muscovy from the Russian Empire from the Soviet Union from the Russian Federation, terms that are easy to blur together but that denote genuinely different political entities occupying different territories with different populations at different times. And it introduces the historiographical debates — over the origins of Rus', the impact of Mongol rule, the nature of Russian autocracy, the causes of revolution, the character of Stalinism, and the causes of the Soviet collapse — that historians continue to argue about today, often for good reason, because the evidence itself is incomplete, ambiguous, or open to more than one responsible interpretation.

By the end of this chapter, students should be equipped not merely with facts to memorize but with a disciplined way of asking questions of the material that follows in the textbook's remaining seventy-one chapters: What kind of evidence supports this claim? Whose perspective does this source represent, and whose is missing? What political purpose might an account of the past have served for the people who wrote or preserved it? Did a described development actually follow the tidy causal line often presented in popular retellings, or was it the product of multiple interacting causes, human choices, and historical accidents? These are the habits of mind that separate serious historical study from national legend, and they will serve students well not only in the study of Russia but in the study of any subject where the past has been made to serve the political needs of the present.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what Russian history studies and why it must be approached as the history of a vast and diverse Eurasian region rather than the biography of a single, uninterrupted nation.
  2. Describe the major features of the East European Plain, the forest and steppe zones, the principal river systems, and the seas bordering the region, and explain how these features shaped settlement, migration, trade, and state formation.
  3. Situate Russian history within the broader histories of Eastern Europe, the Baltic world, the Black Sea world, the Caucasus, Central Asia, Siberia, and the North Pacific.
  4. Distinguish among archaeological, chronicle, legal, religious, diplomatic, oral, literary, statistical, and visual sources, and explain the distinctive strengths and limitations of each in reconstructing the Russian past.
  5. Define and correctly distinguish among Rus', Muscovy, the Tsardom of Russia, the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, and the Russian Federation.
  6. Explain why applying modern national identities, borders, or political categories to earlier periods can distort historical understanding.
  7. Identify major historiographical debates in Russian history, including the origins of Rus', the impact of Mongol rule, the rise of Moscow, the character of Russian imperial expansion, the causes of revolution, and the causes of the Soviet collapse.
  8. Analyze how transliteration systems and changing place names affect the study of Russian, Ukrainian, and Eurasian history.
  9. Evaluate different systems of periodization and explain the interpretive choices embedded in dividing Russian history into eras.
  10. Apply the historical thinking skills of causation, continuity and change, corroboration, and multiple perspectives to specific problems in Russian history.
  11. Recognize the political uses of historical narrative, including Russian, Soviet, and post-Soviet national mythologies, and explain how historians work to correct for bias and teleology.
  12. Connect the methods introduced in this chapter to the study of subsequent chapters throughout the textbook.

Historical Context

Before turning to the specific content of Russian history, students need to understand something about how the field of Russian historical study developed, because that development has shaped — and sometimes distorted — the very narrative students encounter in popular accounts. For centuries, the history of the lands that became Russia was written primarily to serve the political and religious legitimacy of ruling authorities. Medieval chroniclers working in monasteries under princely patronage recorded the deeds of ruling dynasties and framed political events within a providential Christian narrative in which the fortunes of princes reflected divine favor or displeasure. This was not unique to Rus'; medieval chroniclers across Europe wrote history in similar ways. But it means that the earliest written accounts of Rus' history are not neutral, disinterested records. They are documents produced by specific institutions, for specific patrons, embedded within specific religious and political assumptions, and historians must read them accordingly.

From the sixteenth century onward, the growing Muscovite state and later the Russian Empire actively cultivated historical narratives that presented the expansion of Moscow's authority as the natural, divinely sanctioned "gathering of the Russian lands" — a phrase still sometimes used uncritically today. Imperial-era historians, some of them serious scholars and others closer to court propagandists, often wrote history that treated the incorporation of Novgorod, Ukraine, Siberia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and other regions into an expanding Russian state as an unproblematic and even inevitable unification, rather than as a complex history of conquest, negotiation, resistance, migration, and accommodation involving diverse peoples with their own political traditions and interests.

The nineteenth century added new layers of complexity. Russian intellectuals divided sharply over how to understand their own country's relationship to Western Europe, producing rival schools of thought — Westernizers, who argued Russia should follow European patterns of development, and Slavophiles, who argued Russia possessed a distinct historical and spiritual path — whose disagreements shaped how historians interpreted everything from the Mongol period to Peter the Great's reforms. Meanwhile, the rise of professional, university-based historical scholarship in Russia and across Europe brought new standards of archival research and source criticism, even as national historical narratives across Europe increasingly served the ideological needs of emerging nation-states and empires competing for prestige and legitimacy.

The Soviet period introduced a further transformation, and in some respects a further distortion. Soviet historical scholarship after the 1920s was required to conform to Marxist-Leninist theories of historical development, which explained change primarily through class struggle and economic stages, and later through interpretations that served the political needs of the ruling Communist Party. Access to archives was tightly controlled. Certain topics — the scale of Stalin-era repression, the famines of the early 1930s, the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, the treatment of deported nationalities, and many others — were suppressed, minimized, or falsified in officially sanctioned Soviet historical writing for decades. At the same time, Soviet historians, working within these constraints, also produced genuinely valuable archaeological, archival, and source-critical scholarship, particularly on medieval and early modern topics less directly threatening to the regime's legitimacy. Distinguishing solid Soviet-era scholarship from politically compromised Soviet-era propaganda is itself a skill this textbook will ask students to practice.

The end of Soviet censorship after 1991 opened long-closed archives and allowed a generation of Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and international historians to reexamine major questions with far greater access to evidence. This transformation has been genuinely significant, comparable in some respects to the mid-twentieth-century transformation of African historical scholarship discussed in other regional histories: newly available archival material has clarified the scale of Stalin-era terror, the mechanics of the early 1930s famines, the internal workings of the Communist Party, and much else. At the same time, the post-Soviet period has also produced new national narratives — in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and the other post-Soviet states — that sometimes serve contemporary political purposes as readily as earlier imperial and Soviet narratives once did. A responsible history of Russia written today must draw on the best available evidence and scholarship while remaining alert to the ways that political interests, on multiple sides, continue to shape how this history is told.

Students should also grasp, before proceeding, the sheer physical and demographic scale of the subject. The Russian Federation today is the largest country on Earth by land area, spanning eleven time zones and more than six million square miles, from the Baltic Sea and the plains of Eastern Europe across the Ural Mountains, the forests and steppes of Siberia, to the Pacific coast and the Bering Strait. Its population of roughly 145 million people includes ethnic Russians alongside more than one hundred other recognized ethnic groups, speaking dozens of distinct languages and following Orthodox Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Judaism, indigenous spiritual traditions, and no religion at all. The lands and peoples this textbook examines were, across most of the past millennium, considerably more diverse still, since the territories once ruled by Kyiv, Muscovy, the Russian Empire, and the Soviet Union extended across Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, Poland, Finland, the Caucasus, and Central Asia at various points — regions with their own distinct histories that this textbook treats as substantive subjects in their own right, not merely as backdrops to a Moscow-centered story.

Section 1.1

What Is Russian History?

Russian history, as this textbook treats it, is the study of the peoples, societies, states, institutions, economies, religions, and ideas that developed across the lands of Rus', Muscovy, the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, and the Russian Federation from the deep prehistoric past to the present day. This definition sounds straightforward, but each of its component ideas requires immediate qualification, because a careless reading could imply a single continuous national subject where, in reality, several distinct political communities, occupying overlapping but not identical territories, populated by overlapping but not identical peoples, are involved.

Consider first the geographic scope. The territory ruled from Moscow or St. Petersburg at any given moment in the past thousand years has never matched the borders of the present-day Russian Federation, and it has rarely matched the borders of any earlier period either. Medieval Rus' extended across territory now divided among Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, with political centers at Kyiv, Novgorod, and Vladimir among others — cities that are today located in three different sovereign countries. The Russian Empire at its height incorporated Poland, Finland, the Baltic provinces, much of Ukraine, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, along with Siberia and, until 1867, Alaska. The Soviet Union was a federal union of fifteen constituent republics, only one of which — the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic — corresponded to what most people today think of as Russia. Studying "Russian history" without acknowledging these shifting and overlapping territorial realities risks projecting the borders of any one period backward or forward onto periods where they did not apply.

Consider next the question of population and identity. The peoples who lived within the various states this textbook studies were never uniformly "Russian" in any straightforward ethnic, linguistic, or religious sense. Medieval Rus' encompassed East Slavic populations alongside Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples. The Russian Empire governed an extraordinary range of nationalities, religions, and languages: the 1897 imperial census, which measured mother tongue rather than ethnicity in a modern sense, recorded Great Russian speakers at just over forty percent of the population, meaning they did not constitute an absolute majority of the empire's subjects even before accounting for the empire's many other Slavic, Turkic, Caucasian, Baltic, and other communities. The Soviet Union explicitly organized itself as a multinational federation, with policies that alternately promoted and suppressed the cultural expression of dozens of recognized nationalities. Treating the history of these states as simply the history of "the Russians" erases the historical experience of Ukrainians, Belarusians, Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Georgians, Armenians, Kazakhs, Uzbeks, Siberian indigenous peoples, Jews, Poles, Finns, Baltic peoples, and many others whose histories are inseparably interwoven with, but not identical to, the history of ethnic Russians and the Russian state.

Given this complexity, why does this textbook nonetheless treat "Russian history" as a coherent subject worth a unified course of study? The answer lies not in any seamless institutional succession but in a set of overlapping inheritances, institutions, political claims, and territorial centers that later states selectively inherited, adapted, and reinterpreted: an Orthodox Christian religious tradition (later joined by state atheism and, since 1991, religious pluralism), a dominant political language, evolving legal and administrative practices, and a shifting but repeatedly reasserted center of political gravity that, despite competing claimants and changing capitals, organized enormous Eurasian territories at successive points across many centuries. Studying this history carefully, while remaining honest about its discontinuities, its internal diversity, and its contested legacies, allows students to understand one of the most consequential political and cultural formations in world history: a formation that has shaped, and been shaped by, the histories of Eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, Siberia, and much of the modern international system.

Russian history, properly studied, therefore includes: the deep prehistoric and ancient history of the East European Plain and the Eurasian steppe; the formation and Christianization of Rus'; the political fragmentation of Rus' and the Mongol conquest; the rise of Moscow and the construction of a centralized Muscovite state; the transformation of Muscovy into a European great power under Peter the Great and his successors; the vast territorial expansion of the Russian Empire across Siberia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia; the social and political crises that produced revolution in 1917; the construction, transformation, and eventual collapse of the Soviet Union; and the ongoing history of the post-Soviet Russian Federation through the present day. At every stage, this textbook asks students to attend not only to rulers, wars, and revolutions, but to peasants, merchants, clergy, soldiers, workers, women, children, ethnic and religious minorities, and the enormous non-Russian populations whose experiences are as much a part of this history as the decisions made in Moscow, St. Petersburg, or the Kremlin.

Historical Vocabulary
Historiography

Historiography is the history of historical writing itself — the study of how scholars in different periods, political systems, and national traditions have interpreted a given subject, and why those interpretations have changed. Historiography matters with particular force in Russian history because political authorities, from medieval princely courts through the imperial government to the Soviet state and various post-Soviet governments, have repeatedly shaped, funded, censored, or suppressed historical scholarship to serve their own legitimacy. A student who does not understand this history of historical writing risks mistaking a politically constructed narrative — Muscovite dynastic legitimation, imperial Russian nationalism, Soviet Marxist-Leninist theory, or a contemporary national myth of any post-Soviet state — for a neutral account of what actually happened.

Understanding historiography equips students to ask, of any source or textbook passage encountered in this course: Who wrote this, and when? What government or institution controlled the archives and publishing outlets available to the author? What political or national narrative might this account be serving, intentionally or not? What evidence actually supports the claims being made, and what is merely asserted? These questions apply to every chapter of this textbook, including this one.

Section 1.2

Geography and the Eurasian Setting

No history of Russia can proceed responsibly without first grasping the physical geography that shaped, without determining, the region's political and social development. The core of the territory this textbook studies is the East European Plain, one of the largest continuous lowland regions on Earth, stretching from the Baltic Sea and the Carpathian Mountains in the west to the Ural Mountains in the east, and from the Arctic coastline in the north to the Black Sea, the Caucasus, and the Caspian Sea in the south. This plain lacks the dramatic mountain barriers that divide much of Western Europe into distinct, defensible regions; instead, it presents a relatively open, traversable landscape across enormous distances. This openness created conditions and constraints — ease of long-distance movement, exposure to raiding and invasion, the logistical difficulty of defending distant frontiers — whose actual political consequences in any given period depended on the institutions, military organization, technology, demography, and state capacity of the peoples living there, not on geography alone.

Running roughly east to west across this plain are several broad environmental zones. In the far north lies tundra, a treeless landscape of permafrost supporting limited agriculture but historically sustaining hunting, herding, and fishing communities, including the ancestors of today's Sámi, Nenets, and other Arctic peoples. South of the tundra stretches an immense forest zone — the taiga in its northern reaches, giving way to mixed forest further south — that historically supplied furs, timber, honey, and other forest products that became central to the region's earliest long-distance trade. South of the forest lies a transitional forest-steppe zone, and beyond that the open steppe: a band of grassland stretching from the lower Danube across southern Russia, Ukraine, and Kazakhstan into Central Asia and Mongolia, historically the domain of mobile pastoral peoples whose horse-based societies repeatedly shaped, disrupted, and connected the settled agricultural populations to their north.

This forest-steppe boundary was never a fixed line but a zone of constant interaction: settled agricultural communities pushing southward when steppe powers were weak or fragmented, and mobile steppe confederations pressing northward when strong nomadic states could organize sufficient military force. Scythians, Sarmatians, Huns, Avars, Khazars, Pechenegs, Cumans, and eventually the Mongols each in turn dominated portions of this steppe corridor, and each shaped the political and economic development of the settled populations to the north in ways this textbook will examine in detail in Part I and Part III.

Rivers provided the primary arteries of long-distance transportation and communication across this otherwise open terrain. The Dnieper River, flowing south from the forests near Smolensk through Kyiv to the Black Sea, formed the backbone of the famous trade route linking Scandinavia to Byzantium — sometimes called, in later historical writing, "the route from the Varangians to the Greeks." The Volga River, Europe's longest, flows southeast from its source northwest of Moscow to the Caspian Sea, linking the forest zone to the Islamic world via Volga Bulgaria and the Khazar Khaganate, and later becoming the central artery of the expanding Muscovite and Russian state. The Don River connects the southern forest-steppe to the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea. Farther north, the Northern Dvina and other rivers connected the forest zone to the White Sea and Arctic trade. To the west, the Western Dvina and the Neman linked the region to the Baltic Sea. These river systems, connected by relatively short portages where they did not directly meet, allowed remarkably long-distance movement of people, goods, and ideas across a landmass that lacked the natural harbors and short sea crossings that facilitated trade and communication in much of maritime Europe.

Climate imposed real constraints on agriculture and settlement. Much of the East European Plain lies at latitudes where growing seasons are short and unpredictable frosts common, producing chronically low and unreliable agricultural yields compared to much of Western Europe. These conditions created recurring pressures on rural society that later chapters will examine in detail, but the specific institutions that emerged in response — communal landholding practices, the legal construction of serfdom, and patterns of periodic famine — were the product of particular political decisions, fiscal demands, and social structures built over centuries, not a direct or inevitable outcome of climate itself. At the same time, the region's forests, rivers, and eventually its mineral and energy resources provided other forms of wealth — furs, timber, honey, wax, fish, and, from the nineteenth century onward, coal, iron, and petroleum — that shaped its economic development in different ways across different periods.

Seas bordered the region on several sides, each carrying distinct historical significance. The Baltic Sea connected the region to Scandinavia, the Hanseatic trading world, and northern Europe, though Muscovy's access to Baltic ports was contested and limited until Peter the Great's conquests in the early eighteenth century. The Black Sea connected the southern steppe to Byzantium, the Ottoman Empire, and the wider Mediterranean world, though direct Muscovite and Russian access to its shores was not secured until the later eighteenth century under Catherine II. The Caspian Sea, the world's largest inland body of water, linked the lower Volga to Persia and Central Asia. The Arctic Ocean, forbidding and ice-bound for much of the year, nonetheless supported a distinctive northern Russian maritime and trading culture from the medieval period onward and later became strategically significant for Arctic exploration and, in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, resource extraction and shipping.

Section 1.3

Russia, Eurasia, and Eastern Europe

Where does Russia belong on the map of world regions — Europe, Asia, or somewhere distinct from both? This is not merely a cartographic question but a historical and cultural one that has been argued over for centuries, by Russians and non-Russians alike, and the argument itself is part of the history this textbook studies. Different frameworks for locating Russia carry different implications for how its history is told, and students should understand the competing frameworks rather than assume any one of them is simply correct.

One framework treats Russia as fundamentally part of Eastern Europe: a Christian, initially Byzantine-influenced civilization that developed along the same East European Plain as Poland, Ukraine, Belarus, and the Baltic states, sharing patterns of agrarian society, serfdom, and later industrialization with its immediate neighbors, and whose major political and cultural turning points — Christianization, princely fragmentation, absolutist monarchy, industrialization, and twentieth-century Soviet and post-Soviet political transformation — parallel developments elsewhere on the European continent, even when Russia's particular trajectory diverged from Western European patterns.

A second framework, associated with the intellectual movement known as Eurasianism that emerged among Russian émigré thinkers in the 1920s, argues that Russia constitutes a distinct civilizational zone — neither simply European nor simply Asian — shaped as much by its centuries of interaction with steppe nomadic peoples, the Mongol conquest, and its enormous Asian territories as by its Byzantine Christian and European inheritance. Eurasianist thinkers argued that Russia's vast size, its multiethnic population, and its historical experience of absorbing and being absorbed into Inner Asian political systems gave it a distinctive historical character that neither a purely European nor a purely Asian framework could capture. This idea has had a long afterlife, resurfacing in various forms in Soviet-era geopolitical thinking and in some strands of post-Soviet Russian political and intellectual discourse, where "Eurasianism" is sometimes invoked to support arguments about Russia's distinct civilizational mission or its natural sphere of influence across the former Soviet space.

A third framework emphasizes Russia's specifically Asian and Pacific dimensions: the fact that roughly three-quarters of the territory of the Russian Federation lies geographically in Asia, that Russian expansion across Siberia from the sixteenth century onward brought the state into sustained contact and conflict with Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, and Paleo-Siberian peoples, that Russian imperial and Soviet history includes extensive involvement in Central Asia, the Caucasus, China, Japan, Korea, and the North Pacific, and that treating Russia as simply a European power obscures this vast Asian dimension of its history.

This textbook does not adopt any single one of these frameworks as definitively correct, because each captures a genuine dimension of a genuinely complex historical reality. Instead, it treats Russian history as fundamentally Eurasian: shaped by European Christian and political traditions inherited via Byzantium and later direct contact with Western Europe, by centuries of interaction, conflict, and exchange with the peoples of the Eurasian steppe and Inner Asia, and by a sustained history of expansion across Siberia and into the Caucasus, Central Asia, and the North Pacific that gave the resulting state and empire a character genuinely distinct from any purely European or purely Asian polity. Students should resist any temptation to resolve this complexity by simply picking one framework and discarding the others; each framework illuminates real historical patterns, and each has also been mobilized, at different times, for political purposes that go beyond disinterested historical analysis.

This chapter's geographic and civilizational framing also has direct consequences for how this textbook is organized. Because Russian history cannot be separated from the histories of Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, Poland, Finland, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Siberia's indigenous peoples, this textbook devotes sustained attention to these regions throughout its chapters — not as mere appendages to a Moscow-centered narrative, but as substantive historical subjects whose own political institutions, social structures, and historical experiences deserve serious treatment. Where Moscow, St. Petersburg, or the central Soviet government dominates a chapter's narrative, later regional-context features in this textbook will regularly ask students to consider how the same events looked from Kyiv, Novgorod, Tbilisi, Almaty, Tashkent, or Vladivostok.

Section 1.4

Archaeological and Material Evidence

For the vast majority of the human past in the lands this textbook studies — and for the entirety of human history everywhere before the invention of writing — archaeology provides the only available evidence. Archaeology is the systematic study of past human life through physical remains: stone tools, pottery, dwellings, burial sites, metal artifacts, plant and animal remains, and the broader landscapes and settlements people shaped. In the study of Russian and Eurasian history, archaeology has been transformative both for understanding the deep prehistoric past and for reconstructing aspects of more recent societies whose written record is thin, biased, or entirely absent.

Paleolithic sites across the East European Plain and Siberia, some dating back tens of thousands of years, have revealed evidence of early human habitation, mammoth-bone dwellings, and sophisticated tool traditions, establishing the deep antiquity of human presence in the region long before any written record existed. Bronze and Iron Age archaeological cultures, identified largely through distinctive pottery styles, burial practices, and metalworking traditions, allow historians to trace patterns of technological development, trade, and population movement across the steppe and forest zones. The elaborate burial mounds, or kurgans, of Scythian and Sarmatian elites, excavated extensively across the Pontic steppe, have yielded extraordinary quantities of gold and silver artifacts that reveal the wealth, artistic traditions, and long-distance trade connections of these mobile nomadic societies, discussed further in Part I of this textbook.

Archaeology has also proven indispensable for the study of Rus' itself. Excavations at Novgorod, Kyiv, Staraya Ladoga, and other early medieval sites have revealed evidence of Scandinavian material culture intermixed with Slavic and Finnic artifacts, informing (though not single-handedly resolving) the long-running historiographical debate over the relative Scandinavian and Slavic contributions to the formation of Rus', discussed later in this chapter and in Chapter 7. The Novgorod excavations in particular, conducted continuously since the 1930s, have produced not only the celebrated birch-bark documents but detailed stratigraphic evidence of urban development, craft production, trade connections, and daily life across many centuries, made possible by the region's waterlogged, oxygen-poor soil, which preserves organic material — wood, leather, textiles, and birch bark — that would decay in most archaeological contexts.

Modern archaeological science has added further precision to these methods. Radiocarbon dating allows historians to establish chronologies for organic remains independent of any written record. Dendrochronology — the analysis of tree-ring patterns in preserved wood, particularly effective in Novgorod's well-preserved wooden pavements — allows extraordinarily precise dating, in some cases to within a single year, of archaeological layers. Isotopic analysis of human remains can reveal information about diet, geographic origin, and migration patterns, adding a further layer of evidence to questions about the movement of peoples across the Eurasian landmass that written sources alone cannot answer.

Archaeology has real limitations, however, that historians must bear in mind. Physical remains can reveal a great deal about technology, economy, trade connections, and, with careful interpretation, aspects of social organization and religious practice, but they generally cannot reveal the names of individuals, the content of political negotiations, or the specific motivations behind particular decisions, except in the rare cases where inscriptions or other texts survive alongside the physical evidence. For this reason, archaeology works most effectively in combination with chronicle, documentary, and linguistic evidence rather than in isolation — a principle this textbook will apply repeatedly, particularly in its treatment of periods and regions, such as early Rus' and the peoples of the pre-Rus' steppe, where written sources are scarce, secondhand, or written by outside observers with limited direct knowledge.

Section 1.5

Chronicles and Written Sources

Written sources for Russian history begin, in a meaningful sense, with the Christianization of Rus' in the late tenth century, though earlier written references to the peoples of the region appear in Byzantine, Arabic, and Scandinavian sources produced by outside observers. The adoption of Byzantine Christianity connected Rus' more deeply to an already developing Slavonic Christian literary tradition that had originated further south and west, among South Slavic peoples, in the ninth and tenth centuries. That tradition's earliest liturgical texts had been rendered in Glagolitic, a distinctive script devised for Slavic-language use in connection with the missionary work of Cyril and Methodius; the Cyrillic alphabet that reached Rus' and gradually superseded Glagolitic across much of the Slavic Orthodox world was a separate, later script based largely on Greek letterforms, with additional characters devised to represent Slavic sounds absent from Greek. Through this broader South Slavic and Byzantine cultural network, Rus' gained not only an alphabet but, over the following generations, a tradition of monastic chronicle-writing modeled on Byzantine historical writing but applied to the specific dynastic and religious concerns of Rus'.

The most important early chronicle is the Primary Chronicle, known in Old East Slavic as the Povest' vremennykh let ("Tale of Bygone Years"), compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, traditionally associated with the monk Nestor, though modern scholarship recognizes the text as the product of multiple compilers, revisions, and later interpolations rather than the work of a single identifiable author. The Primary Chronicle narrates the legendary and semi-legendary origins of Rus', the deeds of early princes, the Christianization under Vladimir, and events down to the early twelfth century, all organized according to a year-by-year annalistic structure and framed within a providential Christian narrative that interprets political success and failure as reflections of divine will and princely piety or sinfulness.

Historians value the Primary Chronicle enormously, but they do not read it as a neutral factual record. It was composed more than a century after many of the events it describes, drawing on oral tradition, earlier (now-lost) written sources, Byzantine chronicles, and the political and religious concerns of the monastic institution and princely patrons for whom it was produced. Its account of the "calling of the Varangians" — the story that Slavic and Finnic tribes, unable to govern themselves, invited the Scandinavian prince Rurik and his brothers to rule over them — has been read by different generations of historians as anything from a literal historical account to a later literary and political construction serving specific twelfth-century dynastic interests. This textbook treats the Primary Chronicle as an invaluable but critically evaluated source, corroborated where possible against archaeological, Byzantine, and Arabic evidence, rather than as a straightforward factual record.

Beyond chronicles, Russian and Rus' history is documented through an increasingly rich array of written sources across succeeding centuries. Legal codes — beginning with the Russkaya Pravda compiled under Yaroslav the Wise in the eleventh century, continuing through the Muscovite Sudebniki of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and the comprehensive Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, and eventually the codified law of the imperial and Soviet periods — reveal social structure, property relations, criminal justice, and the changing legal status of different social classes, including the gradual legal construction of serfdom. Religious texts, including saints' lives, sermons, and church council records, illuminate religious belief, monastic institutions, and the relationship between church and state. Diplomatic records, including treaties between Rus' princes and Byzantium preserved within the Primary Chronicle itself, and later Muscovite and imperial diplomatic correspondence, document international relations and, often incidentally, provide outside perspectives on Russian institutions and society.

From the seventeenth century onward, government administrative records multiply dramatically: tax registers, census and revision lists (used to track the taxable and conscriptable population, including serfs), military records, and the voluminous output of the expanding Muscovite and later imperial bureaucracy. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries add newspapers, published statistics, and a growing body of private correspondence, memoirs, and diaries produced by a literate elite and, increasingly, by educated commoners. The twentieth century adds an entirely new category of evidence: the vast documentary output of the Soviet party-state, including Communist Party records, security-service files, planning documents, and propaganda, alongside memoirs, samizdat (self-published underground literature), and, since the 1991 opening of many previously closed archives, a flood of material that continues to reshape scholarly understanding of the Soviet period.

Primary Source Window

The Primary Chronicle, "The Calling of the Varangians" (early 12th century, describing events traditionally dated to 862)

Creator: Compiled by monks of the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves, traditionally associated with Nestor · Date of compilation: early twelfth century · Type: Monastic annalistic chronicle

Context: The Primary Chronicle describes a period of internal conflict among Slavic and Finnic tribes around Novgorod, after which, according to the chronicle, the tribes agreed to invite Scandinavian (Varangian) princes to rule and bring order to their lands.

Brief paraphrase of the chronicle's account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The chronicle recounts that the assembled tribes, finding no justice among themselves, resolved to seek a prince from among the Varangian Rus' who might rule over them and render judgment according to law, and that Rurik and his kinsmen thereafter came to rule at Novgorod and neighboring towns.

Analysis: This passage has shaped, and distorted, popular understanding of the origins of Rus' for centuries. Read literally, it suggests that Rus' statehood originated entirely from outside intervention by Scandinavian rulers. Read critically, it is a twelfth-century narrative composed more than two hundred years after the events it describes, shaped by the political interests of the Rurikid dynasty then ruling Kyiv, which had every reason to present its own founding as ancient, legitimate, and providentially sanctioned. Archaeological evidence confirms substantial Scandinavian presence in the region during the relevant period but also reveals extensive prior Slavic and Finnic settlement and political organization, suggesting a more gradual and mutual process of state formation than the chronicle's dramatic "calling" narrative implies.

Questions for students:

  1. What political interests might have motivated a twelfth-century Kyivan chronicler to present the origins of princely rule in this particular way?
  2. What kind of corroborating evidence would historians need before accepting this account as an accurate description of ninth-century events?
  3. Why might later Russian, Scandinavian, and other historians have found this passage useful or troubling for different political reasons?
  4. What does the passage's own internal claim — that local tribes could not govern themselves — suggest about the chronicle's perspective and possible bias?
Section 1.6

Oral Tradition, Literature, and Cultural Sources

Written chronicles and government records are not the only sources historians use to reconstruct Russian and Eurasian history. Oral tradition, folklore, epic literature, visual art, architecture, and music each preserve historical information — sometimes factual, sometimes symbolic, but always historically significant — that complements and sometimes challenges the documentary record.

The byliny, a genre of oral epic poetry recorded from Russian peasant singers primarily in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (though describing, in idealized and often anachronistic form, heroic figures and events associated with the Kyivan period), illustrate both the value and the limitations of oral tradition as historical evidence. The byliny preserve names, motifs, and cultural memories associated with early Rus', including legendary warrior figures serving at the court of a prince remembered as "Vladimir the Fair Sun," but centuries of oral transmission before these songs were finally written down by folklorists have reshaped, conflated, and reordered their historical content. Historians use the byliny cautiously, as evidence of later popular historical memory and cultural values rather than as a reliable narrative of specific eleventh-century events.

Among the peoples of the wider Eurasian world discussed in this textbook, oral tradition served, and in some communities continues to serve, more structured historical functions. Among various Turkic and Mongolic peoples of the steppe and Siberia, oral genealogies and epic traditions preserved historically significant information about lineage, migration, and political authority, requiring the same kind of methodological care that historians of oral tradition elsewhere in the world — using methods pioneered by scholars such as Jan Vansina in the African context — have developed: attention to genre, transmission process, social function, and corroboration against independent evidence.

Literature more broadly constitutes an essential historical source for Russian history from the eighteenth century onward, when a distinctive Russian literary tradition emerged and rapidly achieved extraordinary sophistication. The works of writers such as Alexander Pushkin, Nikolai Gogol, Ivan Turgenev, Fyodor Dostoevsky, and Leo Tolstoy do not merely reflect their historical moment; they actively participated in the era's central political and philosophical debates, including the Westernizer-Slavophile controversy discussed in Chapter 40, the moral and social status of serfdom, and the psychological and spiritual crises of a rapidly changing society. Soviet-era literature, including officially sanctioned socialist realism and unofficial or banned works circulated through samizdat, likewise offers historians insight into both the values the state sought to promote and the alternative perspectives that persisted despite censorship.

Visual and material culture — icons, church architecture, palace design, painting, and, from the nineteenth century onward, photography and film — provide further evidence of religious belief, artistic development, political self-presentation, and everyday life. Soviet-era propaganda posters, photographs, and film represent a particularly important and particularly complex category of visual evidence: powerful sources for understanding what the state wanted citizens to believe and value, but sources that require active skepticism regarding their claims to represent objective reality, since they were explicitly designed to shape public perception rather than record it neutrally.

Historical Vocabulary
Rus'

Rus' refers to the medieval East Slavic political and cultural world centered initially on Kyiv, Novgorod, and other principalities from roughly the ninth through the thirteenth centuries, before its political fragmentation and the Mongol conquest. The term derives from the name applied, in contemporary Byzantine, Arabic, and western European sources, to the Scandinavian-linked traders and rulers active along the Dnieper and Volga trade routes, though the term's precise origin and early meaning remain debated.

Crucially, Rus' is not a synonym for modern Russia. Kyiv, the political and religious center of early Rus', is today the capital of independent Ukraine. Novgorod and Vladimir, other major Rus' centers, lie within the modern Russian Federation. The medieval Rus' inheritance — its Christianization, its legal traditions, its dynastic structures — is claimed, in different ways and for different purposes, by the modern national historical narratives of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus alike. This textbook uses "Rus'" specifically for the medieval period and its immediate successor principalities, and uses "Russia," "Muscovy," "the Russian Empire," and other terms only for the later, more specific political entities to which they properly refer.

Section 1.7

Names, Transliteration, and Geographic Terminology

Studying Russian and Eurasian history in English requires constant engagement with the problem of rendering names, terms, and places written originally in the Cyrillic alphabet, in historical languages no longer spoken in their original form, or in the scripts and naming conventions of other Eurasian peoples — Ukrainian, Belarusian, Polish, Lithuanian, Turkic, Mongolic, Caucasian, and others — into readable English text. This is not a trivial technical problem. Transliteration choices can carry political significance, and inconsistent practice can confuse students trying to track the same person, place, or institution across different sources.

This textbook follows a consistent, readable transliteration system for Russian-language names and terms, avoiding excessive diacritical marks while preserving enough precision for students to recognize terms in other sources. Well-established conventional English forms are used where they exist and are widely recognized — for example, "Peter the Great" rather than a transliterated Russian form, "Moscow" rather than "Moskva," and "Tsar" rather than an alternative rendering. Where no firmly established English convention exists, a consistent transliteration is applied and, where historically useful, alternate forms are noted at first occurrence.

Place names present a related but distinct challenge, because many places within the territories this textbook studies have been renamed, sometimes repeatedly, reflecting changes in political control, ideology, or national identity. St. Petersburg, founded by Peter the Great in 1703, was renamed Petrograd in 1914 (to remove its German-sounding suffix during the First World War), then Leningrad in 1924 (in honor of the recently deceased Lenin), and then restored to St. Petersburg in 1991 following a popular referendum. This textbook uses the name appropriate to the period under discussion — St. Petersburg for the imperial period before 1914, Petrograd for 1914–1924, Leningrad for the Soviet period from 1924 to 1991, and St. Petersburg again thereafter — while making the successive renamings explicit so that students understand these are the same city under different names at different times, not different cities.

The case of Kyiv/Kiev illustrates a related but more directly consequential naming issue. "Kiev" is a transliteration of the city's name derived from Russian-language pronunciation, historically standard in English-language writing during the imperial and Soviet periods. "Kyiv" is a transliteration derived from Ukrainian-language pronunciation, and has become the internationally standard English spelling since Ukrainian independence in 1991, a shift formally adopted by most English-language style guides, news organizations, and governments especially after 2014 and 2022. This textbook uses "Kyiv" throughout, including when discussing the medieval and early modern periods, both because it reflects current international convention and because it avoids implying that the city's historical identity was inherently or exclusively Russian. Where a historical source itself uses "Kiev," this textbook identifies that as the source's own usage rather than silently changing quoted material.

Personal names present their own complexities. Russian names traditionally include a given name, a patronymic (derived from the father's given name, such as Ivanovich, "son of Ivan," or Ivanovna, "daughter of Ivan"), and a family surname, with the patronymic commonly used in formal address. Russian surnames typically carry distinct masculine and feminine grammatical forms. Rulers are conventionally known in English by regnal names and, where applicable, ordinal numbers — Ivan IV, Peter I, Catherine II — following European monarchical convention, while Soviet leaders are generally known by their party names or noms de guerre (Lenin, Stalin, Trotsky) rather than their birth names (respectively, Vladimir Ulyanov, Joseph Jughashvili, Lev Bronstein), a naming practice with its own historical significance related to the revolutionary movement's culture of underground political activity.

Finally, students must reckon with the calendar itself. The Russian Empire used the Julian calendar for civil purposes until February 1918, when the newly established Soviet government adopted the Gregorian calendar already in use across most of Europe. By the twentieth century, the Julian calendar had fallen thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar. This is why the "February Revolution" of 1917, which began on 23 February by the Julian calendar then in use in Russia, corresponds to 8 March by the Gregorian calendar used in the rest of Europe and in this textbook's dating conventions; and why the "October Revolution," which began on 25 October by the Julian calendar, corresponds to 7 November by the Gregorian calendar. This textbook identifies Old Style (O.S., Julian) and New Style (N.S., Gregorian) dates explicitly wherever the distinction could otherwise cause confusion, particularly in the chapters covering 1917.

Section 1.8

Periodization and Historical Interpretation

Periodization — the practice of dividing a long historical narrative into named eras — is a necessary tool for organizing more than a millennium of history into a manageable textbook, but it is also, inescapably, an interpretive act. Every choice about where to place a dividing line between periods embeds a judgment about what mattered most: political rupture, social transformation, or cultural change. Students should understand the periodization this textbook uses while recognizing that other reasonable periodizations exist and that historians continue to debate the merits of different schemes.

This textbook organizes Russian and Eurasian history into twelve parts, moving from the prehistoric peoples of northern Eurasia (Part I) through the formation of Rus' (Part II), its political fragmentation and the Mongol era (Part III), the rise of Moscow and the Muscovite state (Part IV), the crises and recovery of the seventeenth century (Part V), the eighteenth-century empire built by Peter the Great and his successors (Part VI), the autocratic and reform-era nineteenth century (Part VII), the crisis of the late imperial period (Part VIII), the revolutionary birth of the Soviet Union (Part IX), Stalinism and the Second World War (Part X), the post-Stalin Soviet Union (Part XI), and finally the Russian Federation (Part XII). This scheme, like any periodization, has real limitations. Political turning points — a new ruler, a revolution, a change of regime — do not always correspond to equally significant changes in the everyday lives of peasants, workers, or provincial townspeople, who often experienced far greater continuity across a supposed "period break" than the political narrative alone would suggest.

Students should also be alert to a specific danger inherent in periodizing Russian history: the temptation toward teleology, meaning historical narrative that interprets earlier periods primarily as steps toward a known later outcome, as though history were moving inevitably toward some destination. A teleological account of medieval Rus' might present its fragmentation solely as a prelude to Moscow's later triumph, implying that Moscow's rise was the "true" purpose toward which earlier Rus' history was building. A teleological account of the nineteenth century might present every reform, every act of repression, and every revolutionary movement solely as steps on an inevitable path toward the 1917 revolutions, as though no other outcome had ever been genuinely possible. A teleological account of the late Soviet period might present every economic difficulty and every act of political dissent as evidence that Soviet collapse was inevitable from a much earlier date than the actual, highly contingent sequence of events in 1989–1991 would support.

This textbook explicitly rejects teleological narration. Historical actors at every point examined in this textbook faced genuine alternatives, uncertain outcomes, and the possibility that events might have unfolded differently. Medieval Novgorod, Lithuania, and Tver each represented plausible alternative centers of political consolidation to Moscow at various points before the fifteenth century. The October Revolution of 1917 was not the automatic result of the February Revolution eight months earlier, but the product of specific political choices, organizational advantages, and contingent circumstances that this textbook examines in Part IX. The Soviet Union's collapse in 1991 resulted from a specific, closely contested sequence of political events in the late 1980s that could plausibly have concluded differently — with a reformed but intact union, for example — rather than from some inevitable, predetermined logic embedded in the Soviet system from its founding.

Historical Vocabulary
Periodization

Periodization is the practice of dividing the past into named blocks of time to make a vast historical narrative more comprehensible. In Russian history, periodization is complicated by the coexistence of several overlapping schemes: dynastic periodization (organized around ruling houses, such as the Rurikids and the Romanovs), political-systemic periodization (Rus', Muscovy, the Tsardom, the Empire, the Soviet Union, the Federation), and thematic periodization (feudal fragmentation, absolutism, industrialization, totalitarianism, post-totalitarian transition) borrowed, sometimes uncritically, from broader European or global historical frameworks. Students should recognize that no periodization scheme is neutral, and should ask, of any period label encountered in this or any other textbook, what assumptions about historical significance and causation that label embeds.

Section 1.9

Historiographical Debates and National Narratives

Few fields of historical study are as thoroughly shaped by ongoing scholarly and political controversy as the history of Russia and its neighboring peoples. This section introduces several of the most significant debates students will encounter in later chapters, not to resolve them here but to establish the habits of critical evaluation that will make it possible to engage with them responsibly when they arise in full.

The Normanist controversy, concerning the relative Scandinavian and Slavic contributions to the formation of Rus', dates back to the eighteenth century, when German scholars working in the newly founded Russian Academy of Sciences proposed that Scandinavian Varangians had been the primary founders of Rus' statehood. This "Normanist" thesis provoked immediate and lasting controversy, since Russian scholars including Mikhail Lomonosov objected that it appeared to deny East Slavic peoples the capacity for independent political development — an objection with obvious resonance for national pride, but also a legitimate historiographical concern about evidence and interpretation. The debate intensified further during the Soviet period, when the Communist Party at times actively promoted "anti-Normanist" positions for ideological reasons connected to Soviet patriotism, particularly during and after the Second World War. Contemporary historians, drawing on archaeological, linguistic, and documentary evidence unavailable to earlier disputants, generally accept that both Scandinavian and Slavic (along with Finnic) populations and political traditions contributed to the formation of Rus', in a process of gradual state formation and cultural fusion rather than either a purely external Scandinavian imposition or a purely indigenous Slavic development. Chapter 7 examines this debate in full.

The legacy of Kyivan Rus' constitutes a second major and politically charged historiographical problem. Modern Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian national historical narratives each claim aspects of the medieval Rus' inheritance as foundational to their own national history, and each narrative has, at times, minimized or denied the others' claims. Russian imperial historiography traditionally presented Muscovy as the direct political and cultural heir of Kyivan Rus', with Kyiv's later incorporation into the Russian Empire framed as a natural reunification of a single Russian people artificially divided by Mongol conquest and Lithuanian-Polish rule. Ukrainian national historiography, developing especially from the nineteenth century onward, has emphasized the distinctiveness of the Rus' inheritance centered on Kyiv itself, and has argued that Muscovy represented a distinct, northeastern political development only loosely connected to Kyivan traditions. This textbook does not adjudicate which modern nation is the "true" heir of Rus', because the question itself rests on a problematic assumption — that medieval political communities map neatly onto modern national identities. Instead, it presents Rus' as a shared medieval inheritance whose legacy has been variously interpreted, and sometimes contested, by multiple modern national traditions, a topic examined further in Chapter 12.

The historiography of Mongol influence on Muscovite political development constitutes a third significant debate. Some historians, particularly in the nineteenth-century Russian tradition, argued that Mongol rule imposed a decisively despotic, "Asiatic" model of governance on Rus', explaining later Muscovite and Russian autocracy as essentially a Mongol import onto an otherwise more "European" political tradition. Other historians have argued that continuities with earlier Rus' and Byzantine political traditions, alongside genuinely indigenous institutional developments, provide a more persuasive explanation for Muscovite centralization, with Mongol administrative and fiscal practices (particularly around taxation and communication systems) contributing specific, identifiable institutional borrowings rather than the whole architecture of autocratic rule. Chapter 17 examines this question with the detail it deserves.

Twentieth-century Russian and Soviet history contains its own dense thicket of historiographical controversy. Whether the October Revolution should be understood as a genuine popular revolution, a military insurrection, or effectively a coup d'état carried out by a disciplined minority party remains debated among historians, as does the degree to which Stalin's rise to power and later policies represented a natural continuation of Leninist practice or a significant departure from it. The causes and character of the early 1930s Soviet famines, including the intensely disputed question of whether Soviet policy toward Ukraine specifically constituted genocide (the position associated with the term "Holodomor"), involve both genuinely contested historical interpretation and, in some contemporary political contexts, questions of legal and diplomatic recognition that go beyond historical scholarship alone. The causes of the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991 remain debated among historians who variously emphasize economic stagnation, nationality politics, elite decisions, and international pressures. This textbook addresses each of these debates in the relevant later chapters (particularly Chapters 51, 54, 56, and 66), presenting the evidence and the range of scholarly interpretation rather than asserting a single settled answer where genuine disagreement persists, while being equally clear about matters — such as the scale of Stalin-era political repression or the basic sequence of events in 1917 — where the evidence supports strong scholarly consensus.

Historical Debate

The Normanist Controversy: Who Founded Rus'?

The question: Did Scandinavian Varangians play the decisive role in founding the political structures of Rus', or did indigenous Slavic and Finnic populations develop these structures largely on their own, with Scandinavian involvement being secondary or exaggerated by later chronicle tradition? This controversy is better understood not as a permanent standoff between two fixed camps but as a debate that has evolved through several distinct historiographical stages.

Stage One — The Older Normanist Interpretation (18th–19th centuries): Early proponents, beginning with eighteenth-century scholars in the Russian Academy of Sciences, emphasized the Primary Chronicle's account of the "calling of the Varangians," the likely Scandinavian origin of the term "Rus'" itself, and later archaeological evidence of Scandinavian material culture at sites such as Staraya Ladoga and Rurikovo Gorodishche, to argue that Scandinavian traders and warriors were the primary founders of Rus' political and commercial structures, largely imposing order on populations otherwise incapable of state formation.
Stage Two — The Older Anti-Normanist Interpretation (19th–20th centuries): Responding partly to the political implications of Stage One and partly to genuine methodological concerns, historians including Mikhail Lomonosov in the eighteenth century and many Soviet-era scholars emphasized evidence of substantial pre-existing Slavic settlement, trade, and political organization in the region, arguing that Scandinavian involvement, while real, had been overstated, and that indigenous East Slavic development provided the more important explanation for Rus' state formation.
Stage Three — Contemporary Interaction and Synthesis Approaches: Most historians working today, drawing on archaeological, linguistic, and documentary evidence unavailable to earlier disputants, move past the binary framing altogether. They treat Rus' state formation as a gradual process of interaction, intermarriage, and cultural fusion among Scandinavian, Slavic, and Finnic populations and political traditions, in which Scandinavian, Slavic, and Finnic elements were each present, rather than as a contest between a purely external imposition and a purely indigenous development.

Points of broad agreement today: Archaeological and linguistic evidence supports the presence of both Scandinavian and Slavic (and Finnic) populations in the relevant regions and periods, and most contemporary historians accept the Stage Three synthesis as the best-supported account of Rus' state formation.

Points of continued disagreement: Historians continue to debate the relative weight of Scandinavian versus indigenous contributions in particular places and periods, the reliability of the Primary Chronicle's specific narrative details, and the pace and mechanism by which a distinct "Rus'" political identity formed and became linked to Slavic-speaking populations.

Why it matters: This debate has carried disproportionate political weight because it has been read, rightly or wrongly, as bearing on questions of national pride and the capacity of Slavic peoples for independent political development — questions that a careful historian recognizes as separate from the actual historical evidence about ninth- and tenth-century state formation.

Student task: Explain why a debate about ninth-century state formation became entangled with much later questions of national identity, and evaluate what kind of additional evidence might help historians resolve remaining points of disagreement.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Were the Rus'? Competing Modern Claims on a Medieval Inheritance

One influential strand of Russian imperial historiography: Kyivan Rus' represents the earliest chapter of a single, continuous Russian national history, later interrupted by Mongol conquest and Lithuanian-Polish rule, and eventually restored through Muscovite and imperial "reunification" of the Rus' lands. Not all Russian historians, past or present, accept this framing, and the tradition itself has been debated and revised across different periods.
One strand of Ukrainian national historiography: Kyiv was the authentic political and cultural center of Rus', and the medieval Rus' inheritance belongs most directly to the population that has continuously inhabited the Dnieper region, while Muscovy's later claim to the Rus' inheritance reflects a later, politically motivated appropriation. This view, too, is one position within a broader and internally diverse Ukrainian scholarly tradition rather than a single settled consensus.
One strand of Belarusian national historiography: The western and northwestern Rus' lands, later absorbed into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, preserved distinct linguistic and cultural traditions that developed into a separate Belarusian identity, one often overshadowed in historical narratives dominated by Russian and Ukrainian claims. Belarusian historians themselves have offered varying accounts of how directly this identity traces to the medieval period.
A view common among internationally comparative historians: Medieval Rus' was a loosely connected federation of principalities whose populations did not think of themselves in terms of any modern national category. Treating Rus' as the exclusive property of any one modern nation projects later political identities backward onto a medieval world that did not share them, while denying any nation's claim to a meaningful historical connection to that inheritance would be equally inaccurate. This view, like the others above, represents one scholarly position among several, not a final adjudication.
Cause and Effect

Building a Rigorous Historical Discipline After 1991

Long-Term Causes

Decades of Soviet-era archival restriction and Marxist-Leninist ideological requirements shaping officially permitted historical interpretation; the accumulation, even under these constraints, of genuine archaeological and source-critical scholarship on earlier periods.

Short-Term Causes

Glasnost-era relaxation of censorship in the late 1980s; the 1991 dissolution of the Soviet Union and the resulting opening of previously closed party, state, and security-service archives; the emergence of independent national historiographies in Ukraine, Belarus, and other post-Soviet states.

The Transformation

Reassessment of major historiographical questions — the scale of Stalinist repression, the mechanics of the 1932–1933 famines, the internal workings of the Communist Party — using archival evidence unavailable to earlier scholars, alongside the growth of new national historical narratives in successor states.

Effects

A far richer, more accurate, and more contested scholarly landscape than existed before 1991, in which historians continue to debate major questions using substantially better evidence than earlier generations possessed, even as new political pressures in some post-Soviet states have periodically constrained free historical inquiry.

Historical complexity: The post-1991 transformation of Russian and Soviet historiography cannot be reduced to a single cause, and it remains an ongoing, unfinished process rather than a completed achievement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Evidence for Russian History Across a Millennium

What Changed

  • The volume and diversity of written evidence expanded enormously from the sparse chronicle record of the eleventh and twelfth centuries to the vast bureaucratic, statistical, and media archives of the imperial and Soviet periods.
  • Access to archival evidence has alternated between relative openness and severe restriction, particularly across the transitions from imperial to Soviet rule and from Soviet to post-Soviet rule.
  • Modern scientific dating and analytical methods have transformed what archaeology alone can reveal about periods with little or no written record.

What Stayed the Same

  • Political authorities across the medieval, Muscovite, imperial, and Soviet periods have repeatedly used control over historical narrative to bolster their own legitimacy.
  • Historians have consistently needed to cross-check any single source — chronicle, archival document, oral tradition, or statistical record — against independent evidence before accepting its claims.
  • The peoples and regions at the margins of centralized political power — peasants, non-Russian nationalities, frontier populations — have persistently been underrepresented in sources produced by and for central political authorities, requiring historians to seek out other kinds of evidence to recover their experience.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the transformation and the persistence of these evidentiary patterns equips students to read every subsequent chapter of this textbook with appropriate care about what its sources can, and cannot, reliably establish.

Everyday Life and Social History

Finding Ordinary People in the Historical Record

Much of the documentary record surviving from Rus', Muscovy, and the Russian Empire was produced by and for rulers, clergy, and administrators, creating a persistent challenge for historians who want to understand the lives of peasants, artisans, soldiers, and other ordinary people who left few direct written records of their own. Historians have developed several strategies for recovering this history. Novgorod's birch-bark letters, discussed at the opening of this chapter, are exceptional precisely because they were written by and for ordinary townspeople rather than princes or clergy, offering a rare direct window into medieval commercial and family life. Tax and census records, though compiled for the state's own fiscal purposes, incidentally document household composition, landholding, and economic activity among the peasantry. Court records documenting disputes among peasants, townspeople, and lesser clergy reveal social tensions, gender relations, and community norms that rarely appear in chronicles focused on princely deeds. Later folklore collection, ethnographic study, and, for the twentieth century, oral history interviews and Soviet-era workplace and village records extend this kind of evidence into more recent periods.

Historians must nonetheless proceed carefully. A tax record compiled to maximize state revenue, a court record compiled to enforce social order, or a folklore collection compiled by nineteenth-century ethnographers with their own romantic assumptions about peasant life are each shaped by the purposes and perspectives of the people who created them, no less than a princely chronicle is shaped by dynastic interests. Recovering the history of ordinary people requires the same critical evaluation of sources that this chapter has emphasized throughout, applied to a different and often more fragmentary body of evidence.

Regional Context

A History Without a Single Center

Because this textbook necessarily follows political developments centered, across most periods, on Kyiv, Novgorod, Moscow, St. Petersburg, or the central Soviet government, students should make a deliberate habit of asking how the same events looked from elsewhere. When Chapter 19 explains why Moscow rose to prominence among competing Rus' successor states, it is equally worth asking why Novgorod's republican commercial model, examined in Chapter 14, or the Grand Duchy of Lithuania's absorption of western Rus' lands, examined in Chapter 18, represented genuinely plausible alternative outcomes. When later chapters examine imperial expansion into Siberia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, or Soviet nationality policy across fifteen union republics, students should ask how the populations of Novgorod, Kazan, Tbilisi, Almaty, or Vladivostok experienced the same developments differently from the perspective of Moscow or St. Petersburg. This textbook's regional-context features throughout later chapters are designed to reinforce this habit of decentered historical thinking.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced Russian history as the study of the peoples, societies, states, institutions, economies, religions, and ideas that developed across the lands of Rus', Muscovy, the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, and the Russian Federation from the deep prehistoric past to the present day. Several distinct, related political communities have occupied shifting and overlapping territories, populated by diverse and changing peoples, across more than a thousand years, and this textbook studies their connected but discontinuous history rather than the biography of a single uninterrupted nation.

The chapter has situated this history within its Eurasian geographic setting: the East European Plain, the forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones whose interaction shaped patterns of settlement and conflict for millennia, the great river systems — the Dnieper, Volga, and Don above all — that provided the primary arteries of trade and communication, and the Baltic, Black, Caspian, and Arctic Seas, each connecting the region to different wider worlds. It has explored competing frameworks for locating Russia geographically and civilizationally — as fundamentally European, as a distinct Eurasian civilization, or as substantially Asian and Pacific — concluding that Russian history is best understood as genuinely Eurasian, shaped by all of these dimensions simultaneously.

A recurring caution throughout the geography sections has been the danger of geographic determinism. The plain's openness, the steppe's aridity, and the region's harsh growing season created real conditions, constraints, and opportunities, but the specific political and social institutions that emerged in response — centralized states, serfdom, or patterns of invasion and defense — depended on human choices, technology, military organization, and state capacity, not on geography by itself.

Students have been introduced to the distinctive evidentiary toolkit historians use to reconstruct this history: archaeology, which recovers the deep prehistoric past and illuminates periods and populations with thin written records, as the discovery and study of Novgorod's birch-bark documents illustrates; chronicles, beginning with the Primary Chronicle, which preserve invaluable but politically shaped narratives requiring careful critical evaluation and, where quoted, careful distinction between paraphrase and verified translation; legal codes, religious texts, and diplomatic records, which document social structure, belief, and international relations; oral tradition and literature, which preserve cultural memory and, from the eighteenth century onward, actively participate in the era's central intellectual debates; and the vast archival and statistical record of the imperial and Soviet periods, whose interpretation continues to be transformed by ongoing archival access since 1991.

The chapter has established essential terminological distinctions — among Rus', Muscovy, the Tsardom of Russia, the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, and the Russian Federation — that students must maintain carefully throughout the textbook, alongside careful attention to transliteration, historical place names such as Kyiv and St. Petersburg/Petrograd/Leningrad, and calendar systems, including the Old Style and New Style dating distinction that materially affects the interpretation of events such as the 1917 revolutions. The chapter has also emphasized the importance of grounding demographic and cultural generalizations in specific evidence, as illustrated by the 1897 imperial census and its classification of the population by mother tongue.

The chapter has also introduced several of the major historiographical debates that recur throughout this textbook: the Normanist controversy over the origins of Rus', which this chapter has shown evolving through successive historiographical stages rather than remaining frozen between two fixed camps; the competing, and internally diverse, national claims of Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian scholarly traditions on the medieval Rus' inheritance; the debated extent of Mongol influence on Muscovite political institutions; and the ongoing scholarly controversies surrounding twentieth-century Soviet history, including the character of the 1917 revolutions, the causes of Stalinism and the 1930s famines, and the causes of the Soviet collapse.

In each case, the chapter has modeled the critical evaluation of evidence and interpretation that students should apply throughout their study of this textbook: distinguishing established scholarly consensus from genuine ongoing disagreement, resisting the temptation to flatten evolving historiographical traditions into two permanent opposing sides, and recognizing that national historiographies themselves are internally diverse rather than monolithic.

Students have practiced applying the core conceptual tools of historical thinking — geography without determinism, evidentiary corroboration, precise terminology, periodization and its limitations, causation as multiple interacting factors, continuity and change, multiple perspectives, and resistance to teleological narrative — to the specific evidentiary and interpretive challenges of Russian and Eurasian history. These are the practical skills students will use throughout this textbook's remaining seventy-one chapters, spanning prehistoric Eurasia, the formation and fragmentation of Rus', the Mongol era, the rise of Moscow, the Muscovite and imperial Russian states, revolution, the Soviet Union, and the contemporary Russian Federation through 2026.

Throughout, the organizing commitment of this textbook remains constant: to present the history of Russia and the wider Eurasian world with the depth, complexity, evidentiary rigor, and interpretive honesty that this subject's scale and significance demand — neither reduced to a single national narrative nor stripped of the coherent political and cultural threads that make its long history a subject worth studying as a connected whole.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Archive
A collection of primary sources preserved in libraries, religious institutions, or government repositories, used by historians as raw material for research.
Bias
A perspective or inclination embedded in a source that may distort its representation of events; a persistent concern in state-produced and politically motivated sources about Russian and Soviet history.
Causation
The relationship between events in which one condition produces or contributes to another; historical causation in this textbook is understood as involving multiple interacting long-term and short-term causes.
Chronicle
A year-by-year narrative historical account, typically compiled by medieval monastic writers; the Primary Chronicle is the foundational example for early Rus' history.
Chronology
The sequential ordering of events in time, foundational to all historical analysis.
Continuity
Patterns or conditions that persist over time without fundamental transformation, often alongside significant change in other dimensions.
Corroboration
The process of checking whether multiple independent sources — archaeological, chronicle, and documentary — support the same historical claim.
Eurasianism
An intellectual movement, originating among Russian émigré thinkers in the 1920s, arguing that Russia constitutes a distinct civilizational zone, neither simply European nor Asian, shaped by its historical interaction with Inner Asian peoples.
Historical memory
The way communities collectively remember, commemorate, and use the past to construct identity and political meaning in the present, distinct from the historical events themselves.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of the past have changed over time, why, and under what political conditions.
Muscovy
The state centered on the Grand Principality, later Tsardom, of Moscow from roughly the fourteenth through the late seventeenth centuries, distinct from both medieval Rus' and the later Russian Empire.
Normanist controversy
The long-running historiographical debate over the relative Scandinavian and Slavic contributions to the formation of Rus' as a political entity.
Old Style / New Style dating
The distinction between dates given according to the Julian calendar (Old Style), used in Russia until February 1918, and the Gregorian calendar (New Style), adopted thereafter and used throughout most of Europe earlier.
Periodization
The practice of dividing the past into named periods or eras to make it more manageable, while recognizing that such divisions involve interpretive choices about historical significance.
Primary source
A document, object, or record created at the time being studied, by someone who participated in, witnessed, or was affected by the events in question.
Rus'
The medieval East Slavic political and cultural world centered initially on Kyiv, Novgorod, and other principalities from roughly the ninth through the thirteenth centuries; not synonymous with modern Russia.
Russian Empire
The Russian state from Peter I's imperial proclamation in 1721 to the abdication of Nicholas II in 1917, encompassing a vast multiethnic and multireligious territory.
Russian Federation
The sovereign state that emerged from the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, distinct from both the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic that preceded it and the Soviet Union as a whole.
Secondary source
A work created after the fact, typically by a historian, that interprets and analyzes primary sources to construct an account of what happened and why.
Soviet Union (USSR)
The federal union of socialist republics formed in 1922 and dissolved in 1991, encompassing Russia and fourteen other constituent republics; not synonymous with Russia.
Teleology
A mode of historical narration that interprets earlier events primarily as steps toward a known later outcome, obscuring the genuine uncertainty and contingency historical actors actually faced.
Transliteration
The systematic conversion of text from one alphabet (such as Cyrillic) into another (such as the Latin alphabet used in English), a process that involves interpretive choices affecting how names and terms appear in English-language scholarship.
Tsardom of Russia
The Russian state from Ivan IV's coronation as tsar in 1547 to Peter I's imperial proclamation in 1721, distinct from both Muscovy in its earlier form and the later Russian Empire.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The methods and habits of mind it introduces — geographic literacy across an enormous Eurasian landmass, careful evaluation of archaeological, chronicle, documentary, oral, and statistical evidence, precise terminology distinguishing Rus' from Muscovy from the Russian Empire from the Soviet Union from the Russian Federation, critical attention to historiographical bias and national mythology, and vigilance against teleological narrative — are not isolated skills to be applied once and set aside. They are the tools students will use in every subsequent chapter, from the Eurasian environment examined in Chapter 2 and the prehistoric peoples examined in Chapter 3 through the contemporary Russian Federation examined in the textbook's final chapters.

The history of Russia and the wider Eurasian world it inhabits is one of the most consequential and, for many students, least well understood historical trajectories in the modern world: a history encompassing the formation of a medieval East Slavic civilization, its fragmentation and conquest, the rise of a Muscovite state that would expand across northern Eurasia, the construction of one of history's largest land empires, one of the twentieth century's most consequential revolutionary experiments, and the ongoing, unfinished history of the post-Soviet Russian Federation and its neighbors. Understanding this history requires more than memorizing rulers and dates. It requires disciplined evaluation of evidence, honest reckoning with how political power has repeatedly shaped historical narrative, and genuine attentiveness to the many peoples — Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Tatar, Caucasian, Central Asian, Siberian, and many others — whose histories are inseparably woven into this larger story.

The example of the Novgorod birch-bark letters, with which this chapter began, captures something essential about the spirit in which this textbook approaches its subject: the history of Russia and Eurasia is not simply the history of rulers and their decrees, preserved for posterity because power decided it should be. It is also, and just as importantly, the history of ordinary merchants, craftsmen, and children scratching notes into birch bark, whose survival depended on chance, on wet clay, and on the patient work of twentieth-century archaeologists who understood that the past is not only what the powerful chose to record.

As this textbook proceeds through seventy-one more chapters — tracing the deep prehistory of northern Eurasia, the formation and Christianization of Rus', its fragmentation and the Mongol conquest, the rise of Moscow and the Muscovite state, the transformation of Russia into a European and Eurasian empire, the crises of the late imperial period, revolution and the Soviet experiment, the trauma and triumph of the Second World War, the long arc of Soviet history from Stalin to Gorbachev, and the ongoing history of the post-Soviet Russian Federation — students should carry forward the tools introduced here: ask what evidence supports a given claim; distinguish tradition from contemporary evidence and both from later interpretation; weigh multiple perspectives, especially those of peoples and regions at the margins of central political power; watch for teleology and national mythology in every direction; and always ask what changed, what remained, who experienced it, and why it still matters.
