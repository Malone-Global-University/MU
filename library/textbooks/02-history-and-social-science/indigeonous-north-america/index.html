Indigenous Peoples of North America

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History traces Indigenous societies from the earliest human presence on the continent through ancient regional worlds, continental networks, colonial encounters, Indigenous diplomacy and resistance, reservation and assimilation systems, political resurgence, sovereignty, cultural renewal, and Indigenous nationhood in the twenty-first century.

Overview

Indigenous North American history is a continental history of peoples, nations, communities, political systems, economies, migrations, religions, languages, technologies, ecological knowledge, diplomacy, warfare, and cultural change extending from Greenland and the Arctic through Canada, the present-day United States, and Mexico.

This textbook is organized around Indigenous historical experience rather than modern international borders or a simple division between “pre-contact” and “post-contact.” It begins with the peopling of the continent and the development of ancient regional societies; follows agriculture, cities, mound-building traditions, Pueblo worlds, continental exchange, and the diverse nations and homelands of North America; then examines encounters with European empires, epidemics, trade, diplomacy, warfare, conquest, removal, reservation systems, allotment, boarding and residential schools, and Indigenous resistance. The later volumes trace political reorganization, wartime service, termination and relocation, Red Power, treaty-rights struggles, self-determination, land and water claims, economic development, language revitalization, repatriation, environmental sovereignty, reconciliation, cultural resurgence, and contemporary Indigenous nationhood.

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain the major chronological transformations in Indigenous North American history from the earliest human presence on the continent through the twenty-first century.
  • Analyze Indigenous societies within their own political, economic, cultural, linguistic, ecological, and diplomatic frameworks rather than treating European arrival or modern national borders as the default organizing structure.
  • Compare the development of regional societies and continental networks across the Arctic, Subarctic, Northwest Coast, California, Great Basin, Plateau, Plains, Southwest, Southeast, Northeast, Great Lakes, northern Mexico, and other interconnected Indigenous homelands.
  • Evaluate how migration, agriculture, exchange, urbanism, ecological management, warfare, diplomacy, kinship, religion, language, and technological adaptation shaped Indigenous histories before and after sustained colonial expansion.
  • Assess Indigenous strategies of accommodation, alliance, resistance, survival, political reorganization, and nation-building in response to Spanish, French, British, Russian, American, Canadian, and Mexican expansion.
  • Trace the continuing histories of Indigenous sovereignty, treaty rights, self-government, cultural resurgence, language revitalization, land and water defense, knowledge sovereignty, and contemporary nationhood across North America.
