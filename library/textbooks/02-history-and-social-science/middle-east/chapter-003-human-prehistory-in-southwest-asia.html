Human Prehistory in Southwest Asia

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the limestone hill country above the Mediterranean coast, in caves that shepherds and travelers have walked past for thousands of years, archaeologists have found something that no ancient chronicle, inscription, or sacred text could ever record: the deliberately buried bodies of human beings who lived roughly one hundred thousand years ago, long before agriculture, cities, writing, kingship, or any of the institutions examined in the chapters that follow. At sites such as Qafzeh and Skhul, near modern Nazareth and on the slopes of Mount Carmel, excavators uncovered skeletons placed in prepared pits, in some cases accompanied by red ochre pigment or, in one especially discussed case, the antlers of a deer positioned across a burial. These are not the remains of "early Middle Easterners" in any meaningful modern sense, since neither the region's name nor any of its later political or religious identities existed at the time. They are, however, direct physical evidence that anatomically modern humans were living, dying, and treating their dead with evident care in Southwest Asia tens of thousands of years before the developments that later chapters of this textbook will describe.

Chapter 2 surveyed the physical stage of Middle Eastern history: the exotic rivers, the mountain frontiers, the deserts, and the connecting seas within which human societies would eventually build cities, empires, and states. This chapter turns to the first human actors to occupy that stage, beginning not with farmers, kings, or priests but with small, mobile groups of hominins whose presence in the region can be traced back at least 1.5 million years, and quite possibly close to two million years according to a 2026 geochronological reassessment discussed further below, and possibly further still, through stone tools, animal bones bearing cut marks, and, more rarely, fossilized human remains themselves. For the overwhelming majority of the time that human beings — and their evolutionary predecessors — have occupied Southwest Asia, there was no agriculture, no permanent village, no writing, and no political authority beyond the immediate band or family group. Understanding this immense span of prehistory matters not as a curiosity preceding the "real" history that begins with cities and texts, but because the environmental adaptations, technologies, social patterns, and population movements developed during the Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic periods created the human foundation on which the transition toward food production, examined across Chapters 5 and 6, and the first villages and cities, examined through Chapter 8, would later build.

Southwest Asia occupies a position of unusual importance in the study of human prehistory because it forms a land corridor connecting Africa, where the hominin lineage originated, to Europe and Asia, where hominins and, later, modern humans eventually spread. The Levant in particular — the narrow strip of land examined in Chapter 2 as a corridor between Africa and Asia — functioned during the Pleistocene epoch as a genuine biogeographic bridge, a route that successive waves of hominins, and eventually multiple human species, used to move out of Africa and into the wider Old World. This chapter treats the region not as a peripheral footnote to human evolution, which occurred principally in Africa, but as a critical junction through which that evolutionary story passed repeatedly, sometimes with lasting consequences and sometimes, as far as the archaeological record can presently show, without them.

The evidence available for this period differs fundamentally from the evidence historians will use in later chapters. There are no royal inscriptions, no administrative archives, no religious texts, and no named individuals. Instead, prehistorians and archaeologists reconstruct this era from stone tools, animal and plant remains, sediment layers, pollen cores, isotopic analysis of bones and teeth, ancient DNA extracted from fossils and, increasingly, from sediment itself, and the spatial layout of hearths, burials, and living surfaces preserved in caves and open-air sites. This chapter introduces both the outline of what is currently understood about human prehistory in Southwest Asia and the methods by which that understanding has been built, methods examined more fully in Chapter 4's treatment of historical reconstruction. Readers should expect considerably more uncertainty, revision, and open debate in this chapter than in chapters dealing with periods that left written records, and this uncertainty is not a weakness of the evidence but an honest reflection of how difficult, and how actively contested, the study of deep human prehistory remains.

Finally, this chapter insists on a distinction that will recur throughout this textbook: the deep human past examined here belongs to no single modern nation, ethnicity, or religious tradition. The hominins and early modern humans who moved through the Levantine corridor, hunted gazelle on the steppes of the Fertile Crescent, and sheltered in the caves of Mount Carmel and the Zagros foothills were not Arabs, Israelis, Iranians, Turks, or Kurds, categories that would not exist for many tens of thousands of years. Their story belongs to the broader human species and to the deep prehistory of a landscape that later peoples would inhabit, name, and reshape in radically different ways. Understanding this shared and impersonal deep past is itself part of understanding why this textbook, throughout its nine volumes, resists projecting later identities backward onto times and places to which they do not belong.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 established the physical landscapes of Southwest Asia — the exotic rivers, the mountain frontiers, the deserts, and the connecting seas — within which human history would eventually unfold. This chapter examines the earliest human occupation of that landscape, a period conventionally divided by archaeologists into the Paleolithic, meaning "Old Stone Age," and the subsequent Epipaleolithic, a transitional period leading toward the developments in sedentism and food production examined across Chapters 5 and 6. The Paleolithic itself is customarily divided into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases, distinguished primarily by changes in stone-tool technology, though these divisions vary somewhat by region and are used differently by different specialists.

Southwest Asia's prehistoric importance rests substantially on its geographic position. As Chapter 2 explained, the Levant forms a land bridge between Africa and Eurasia, and the Sinai Peninsula provides the only wholly overland connection by which hominins originating in Africa could reach the rest of the Old World without crossing open water. For this reason, the Levantine corridor has long attracted intensive archaeological investigation as a probable route, and in some periods a probable staging ground, for successive dispersals of hominins and, eventually, anatomically modern humans out of Africa. It was not, however, necessarily the only route: archaeological and genetic evidence increasingly documents hominin and, later, Homo sapiens occupation of the Arabian Peninsula during humid climatic phases, including modern human remains at the Arabian site of Al Wusta dated to roughly 95,000 to 86,000 years ago, raising the possibility that a more southerly route across the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, discussed in Chapter 2, supplemented movement through the Sinai-Levant corridor at various points. This does not mean that Southwest Asia was simply a passageway devoid of independent significance; the region also supported long periods of local occupation, adaptation, and cultural development in its own right, examined throughout this chapter.

The evidentiary basis for this period differs sharply from later chapters. Prehistorians rely on lithic technology (stone tools and the debris from their manufacture), faunal remains (animal bones, often bearing butchery marks), floral remains (preserved seeds, phytoliths, and pollen), skeletal remains of hominins themselves, sediment stratigraphy, and a growing array of scientific dating and analytical techniques, including radiocarbon dating, optically stimulated luminescence, uranium-series dating, and ancient DNA analysis. Chapter 4 examines these methods, along with textual and epigraphic evidence relevant to later periods, in greater depth; this chapter applies them specifically to the problem of reconstructing Southwest Asia's earliest human occupation.

Section 3.1

Reconstructing a World before Writing

Before examining the specific evidence for early human occupation of Southwest Asia, students must understand how radically different the methods and the certainty of this chapter are from those of chapters dealing with periods that left written records. No hominin, and no anatomically modern human living in this region before roughly the fourth millennium BCE, left behind a name, a spoken sentence, or a written account of their own life. What survives instead is material evidence: stone tools shaped by human hands, the butchered bones of animals eaten thousands or hundreds of thousands of years ago, the ash and charcoal of ancient hearths, the postholes and floor surfaces of long-vanished shelters, and, far more rarely, the fossilized remains of the people themselves.

Archaeologists date this evidence using a combination of methods. Radiocarbon dating measures the decay of a radioactive isotope of carbon in organic remains such as charcoal, bone collagen, or shell, and is generally reliable for material up to about fifty thousand years old, though its precision decreases with age and requires careful calibration. For older material, archaeologists rely on techniques such as optically stimulated luminescence, which measures the time elapsed since sediment grains were last exposed to sunlight, uranium-series dating of certain minerals, and, where volcanic ash layers are present, argon-argon dating. Relative dating through stratigraphy — the layered sequence of sediments in a site, with older material generally lying beneath younger material — remains fundamental to interpreting the order of events even where absolute dates are uncertain or disputed.

Genetic evidence has transformed the study of human prehistory since the early twenty-first century. Ancient DNA extracted from Neanderthal and other archaic hominin remains, compared with the genomes of living humans, has revealed that some interbreeding occurred between Neanderthals and the ancestors of modern non-African populations, a finding with particular relevance to the Levantine corridor examined in this chapter. Ancient DNA extracted directly from cave sediments, even without identifiable bone fragments, has become an increasingly important tool for detecting the presence of hominin populations at sites where skeletal remains have not survived. These genetic techniques complement, but do not replace, the archaeological and paleoenvironmental evidence that remains the primary basis for reconstructing this period.

Interpretation of this evidence is frequently contested among specialists, and this chapter presents current understanding with appropriate caution rather than false certainty. Dates for individual sites are regularly revised as dating techniques improve; the taxonomic status of some hominin fossils remains debated; and questions about behavior, cognition, language, and social organization can rarely be answered directly from stone tools and bones alone, requiring careful, sometimes contested, inference. Students should read this chapter's claims about deep prehistory as representing the current state of an active and evolving field of research, not as settled and unchanging fact.

Historical Vocabulary
Hominins, Species, and the Paleolithic Sequence

The term hominin refers to modern humans and our extinct close relatives and ancestors, following the branching of the human lineage from the ancestors of chimpanzees, our closest living relatives. Several hominin species or populations are relevant to Southwest Asian prehistory, including early members of the genus Homo associated with the earliest sites discussed in this chapter, Homo neanderthalensis (Neanderthals), and Homo sapiens, the species to which all living humans belong.

Archaeologists divide the long span of hominin stone-tool use into the Lower Paleolithic (associated with simple core-and-flake tools and, later, the more standardized Acheulean handaxe tradition), the Middle Paleolithic (associated with more sophisticated flake-based technologies such as the Levallois technique, and, in the Levant, with both Neanderthal and early modern human toolmakers), and the Upper Paleolithic (associated with blade-based technologies, more elaborate tool kits, and an expanding record of symbolic behavior). These divisions are useful analytical tools rather than sharp, universally applicable boundaries; the transitions between them were gradual, uneven across different regions, and are defined somewhat differently by different specialists.

Historical Vocabulary
Dating Methods and the Limits of Chronology

Most dates in this chapter are approximate, and many carry margins of uncertainty far larger than anything encountered in later, text-based chapters of this textbook. Radiocarbon dating, which measures the decay of radioactive carbon-14 in organic remains such as charcoal, bone collagen, or shell, is generally reliable only for material younger than about fifty thousand years and requires careful calibration against independent records. For older material, archaeologists turn to techniques such as optically stimulated luminescence, which measures the time elapsed since sediment grains were last exposed to sunlight, uranium-series dating, and electron spin resonance dating, both often applied to tooth enamel and associated minerals, and, where volcanic layers are present, argon-argon dating.

These methods do not always agree. The Tabun Cave sequence, discussed in Section 3.4, illustrates the problem directly: different dating studies applying different methods and different assumptions about site formation have produced substantially different age estimates for the same Neanderthal-associated deposits, with some placing them within roughly the same broad window as the early modern humans of Skhul and Qafzeh and others placing them considerably later. Rather than treating any single published date as final, careful readers of prehistoric archaeology look for convergence among multiple independent dating methods and remain alert to the possibility that new techniques, or new applications of existing techniques, will revise currently accepted chronologies, as has already happened repeatedly at several of the sites discussed in this chapter.

Section 3.2

The Earliest Hominin Presence in Southwest Asia

The earliest widely accepted evidence for hominin presence in Southwest Asia comes from the site of 'Ubeidiya, located in the Jordan Valley near the southern end of the Sea of Galilee, within the same Dead Sea Transform system that Chapter 2 examined as the region's principal zone of tectonic activity. Excavations at 'Ubeidiya, conducted across several decades beginning in the 1960s, have long been dated to approximately 1.5 to 1.4 million years ago, a figure repeated in much of the twentieth- and early twenty-first-century literature and still widely cited as of this textbook's composition. A 2026 study applying cosmogenic burial dating, paleomagnetic analysis, and uranium-lead dating to the 'Ubeidiya Formation, however, concluded that the site's most probable age is considerably older, at least 1.9 million years, with the study's authors favoring an even older modeled range over a younger alternative closer to the traditional estimate. Because this reassessment is very recent relative to this textbook's composition and applies newly combined methods to a complex and recycled sedimentary sequence, its precise implications remain under active discussion among specialists, and this textbook presents both the traditional c. 1.5–1.4 million-year figure and the 2026 revision as reflecting a site whose exact age is still being worked out, rather than treating either figure as definitively settled. What is not in serious dispute is that 'Ubeidiya documents hominin presence in the Jordan Valley over a million years ago, making the site one of the oldest well-documented hominin occupations anywhere outside Africa regardless of which chronological model is eventually confirmed. The stone tools recovered at 'Ubeidiya belong to the Acheulean tradition, characterized by bifacially worked handaxes and other core tools, a technology that first appeared in Africa and that hominins carried with them as they moved into Southwest Asia and, eventually, other parts of Eurasia.

The presence of hominins at 'Ubeidiya so early in the Pleistocene supports the general scholarly understanding that the Levantine corridor served as the principal overland route by which early members of the genus Homo first left Africa, since the Sinai Peninsula provided (and provides) the only land connection between Africa and the rest of the Old World. This does not mean that a single dramatic migration event carried hominins out of Africa on one occasion; rather, current evidence suggests a more complex pattern of repeated, and possibly intermittent, movements of hominin populations into and out of Southwest Asia over hundreds of thousands of years, likely tracking changes in climate, vegetation, and the availability of game and water discussed further in Section 3.6.

Other Lower Paleolithic sites across the region, including localities in the Jordan Valley, the southern Levant, and parts of the Zagros foothills, provide additional though generally less securely dated evidence for hominin presence during the Early and early Middle Pleistocene. The site of Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, also located in the Jordan Valley and dated to roughly 780,000 years ago, preserves strong evidence for repeated anthropogenic fire use, including burned wood, flint, and seeds concentrated in discrete spatial clusters interpreted as hearths, along with fish remains showing patterns of controlled, low-temperature heating that specialists have interpreted as evidence of cooking. Taken together, this evidence makes Gesher Benot Ya'aqov one of the strongest currently documented cases for repeated fire use and cooking by hominins outside Africa. It is important to distinguish, however, between hominins capturing, maintaining, and repeatedly using naturally occurring fire, which the Gesher Benot Ya'aqov evidence documents reasonably well, and hominins possessing the technological ability to deliberately ignite fire on demand, a capability for which the earliest compelling evidence currently comes from a considerably later period, roughly four hundred thousand years ago, at sites far to the north in Britain. The Gesher Benot Ya'aqov evidence should therefore be read as demonstrating sustained fire use and cooking rather than as proof that these hominins could kindle fire from scratch, a distinction examined further in the Historical Vocabulary box on dating methods above.

Taken together, the evidence from 'Ubeidiya, Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, and related sites establishes that Southwest Asia has been intermittently occupied by hominins for well over a million years, making the region's human history, in the broadest sense, vastly older than the roughly five-thousand-year span of urban civilization that occupies the bulk of this textbook's later volumes. This deep chronology should reorient students' sense of historical scale: the developments examined in Chapters 5 through 8 — agriculture, villages, and the first cities — occupy only the final, and in evolutionary terms extremely recent, portion of a much longer human presence in the region.

Section 3.3

The Lower and Middle Paleolithic: Multiple Human Species in One Corridor

Across the Middle and early Late Pleistocene, from roughly 780,000 to perhaps 250,000 years ago, hominin populations in Southwest Asia continued to use and gradually refine Acheulean handaxe technology before transitioning, at various times and places, toward the flake-based Levallois technique that characterizes much of the Middle Paleolithic across Africa and western Eurasia. The Levallois technique involves carefully preparing a stone core so that a single strike detaches a flake of a predetermined size and shape, a method requiring considerable planning and manual skill, and one that appears at multiple sites across the Levant, Anatolia, and the Zagros region during this period.

The taxonomic identity of the hominins responsible for this Middle Pleistocene occupation is not always clear, since skeletal remains from this period are rare and fragmentary compared with the more abundant stone-tool evidence. Some remains from this period and region have been attributed to archaic populations sometimes grouped loosely under the heading of "archaic Homo sapiens" or associated with populations ancestral to, or related to, Neanderthals, though the precise classification of specific fossils remains a matter of ongoing paleoanthropological debate. What can be said with greater confidence is that Southwest Asia was intermittently occupied throughout this long period, that occupation appears to have fluctuated with climatic conditions discussed in Section 3.6, and that the region's toolmakers were part of the same broad technological traditions found across contemporary Africa and western Eurasia rather than developing in complete isolation.

By the later Middle Paleolithic, beginning perhaps around 250,000 to 200,000 years ago and continuing through roughly 45,000 years ago, the archaeological and fossil record of the Levant becomes considerably richer, and it is during this period that the region's most historically significant Paleolithic evidence — the coexistence, in the same geographic corridor, of both Neanderthal and anatomically modern human populations — becomes clearly documented, a subject examined in detail in Section 3.4.

Section 3.4

Neanderthals, Early Modern Humans, and the Levantine Encounter

The Levant occupies a uniquely important place in paleoanthropology because it is one of the few regions in the world where both Neanderthal and anatomically modern human skeletal remains have been recovered from broadly overlapping time periods and closely spaced sites, allowing researchers to investigate questions about the relationship between these two human populations more directly than is possible almost anywhere else. The current earliest skeletal evidence for anatomically modern humans in the region comes not from Skhul or Qafzeh but from Misliya Cave, another Mount Carmel site, where a partial upper jaw known as Misliya-1 has been attributed to Homo sapiens and dated, through a combination of methods, to approximately 177,000 to 194,000 years ago. This date would place modern humans in the Levant more than fifty thousand years earlier than the Skhul and Qafzeh evidence discussed below. The Misliya dating has been challenged in published scholarly exchanges, particularly regarding aspects of the uranium-series analysis, and the original researchers have defended their chronology using multiple independent dating methods; subsequent literature has generally continued to cite the roughly 177,000-to-194,000-year range, but students should understand this as a currently well-supported rather than universally uncontested date.

At the cave and rock-shelter sites of Skhul and Qafzeh, located respectively on the slopes of Mount Carmel and near the base of the Nazareth hills, excavators recovered the remains of anatomically modern humans in layers now dated, through a combination of thermoluminescence and electron spin resonance dating of associated burnt flint and tooth enamel, to approximately 90,000 to 130,000 years ago. Given the earlier Misliya evidence, Skhul and Qafzeh are best described not as the earliest presence of modern humans in the Levant but as among the earliest well-documented instances anywhere of deliberate burial practice by anatomically modern humans, a distinction examined further in Section 3.5 and the Primary Evidence Window below.

Neanderthal occupation is securely documented at several Levantine sites during the later Middle Paleolithic, including Tabun, Kebara, and Amud, but the chronology of this occupation — especially at Tabun — remains genuinely contested rather than settled. Tabun Cave in particular has been the subject of repeated, substantially revised dating studies over several decades, employing different methods and different assumptions about the site's complex depositional history; some published age estimates for the Neanderthal-associated Tabun C1 layer fall broadly within roughly 100,000 to 130,000 years ago, an age range that would place it in approximate overlap with the Skhul and Qafzeh moderns rather than clearly afterward, while other dating models applied to the same or related deposits have produced considerably younger ages. Kebara and Amud, by contrast, generally produce more consistent Neanderthal-associated dates falling within roughly the last seventy thousand years. Rather than presenting a single clean sequence in which modern humans occupied the Levant first and Neanderthals arrived afterward, the current evidence is better described as documenting both populations present in the same broad corridor across a long and only partially resolved span of the later Middle Paleolithic, with the degree of direct temporal overlap between them varying by site and remaining an active subject of dating research.

This chronological uncertainty has generated extensive scholarly discussion about the nature of Neanderthal–modern human interaction in the region, examined further in the Historical Debate feature below. Genetic evidence, established through ancient DNA studies since the early twenty-first century and refined considerably by research through the mid-2020s, confirms that interbreeding did occur between Neanderthals and the ancestors of present-day non-African modern human populations, with current genomic estimates placing the major admixture episode ancestral to sampled non-African genomes at roughly forty-five thousand to forty-nine thousand years ago and typical non-African populations today carrying on the order of two to three percent Neanderthal-derived ancestry; smaller, more recent traces of Neanderthal ancestry are also detectable in some African populations, largely attributable to later back-migration from Eurasia. This genomic evidence establishes that interbreeding happened and provides an approximate window for the dominant admixture pulse, but it cannot by itself specify precisely where any given interbreeding event took place. Southwest Asia remains a strong geographic candidate, given the region's well-documented, long-term co-presence of both populations, but the genetic record alone does not prove that the relevant contact occurred specifically within the Levantine corridor rather than elsewhere along the broader zone of Neanderthal-modern human contact across western Eurasia.

Beyond the question of interaction between populations, the Skhul and Qafzeh burials provide some of the earliest and most widely discussed evidence anywhere for deliberate burial and possible symbolic behavior among anatomically modern humans. Several individuals at both sites were interred in prepared pits, with body positioning that appears deliberate rather than accidental. The best-documented case of associated grave material is Qafzeh 11, an adolescent burial in which two deer antlers were found positioned across the upper chest near the hands, while red ochre, including scraped pigment and ochre-stained marine shells, is documented more broadly within the burial-bearing layers at Qafzeh as part of the wider symbolic assemblage of the site rather than as an item placed directly within a single grave. At Skhul, one burial included the mandible of a wild boar placed in apparent association with the skeleton. These findings are examined in greater depth in the Primary Evidence Window below, along with the interpretive caution that modern historians and archaeologists must exercise when inferring belief, ritual, or emotion from material remains this old.

Section 3.5

The Upper Paleolithic: Expanding Technological and Symbolic Traditions

From roughly 48,000 to 25,000 years ago, with regional boundaries that vary somewhat by subregion and specialist, the archaeological record of Southwest Asia documents a series of technological and behavioral changes generally associated with the Upper Paleolithic across much of Africa and Eurasia. This chapter's title for the period is deliberately framed as one of expansion and elaboration rather than sudden onset, since, as Section 3.4 has already shown, symbolic behavior — deliberate burial, pigment use, and possible ornamentation — is documented at Qafzeh as early as roughly ninety-two thousand years ago, tens of thousands of years before the start of the Upper Paleolithic as conventionally defined. Older models describing a sudden Upper Paleolithic "behavioral revolution," in which fully modern cognition supposedly emerged abruptly during this period, sit uneasily with this earlier Levantine evidence, and this textbook instead presents the Upper Paleolithic as a period of intensification and diversification within an already-present capacity for symbolic behavior rather than its first appearance.

Stone-tool technology shifted increasingly toward the production of long, narrow blades struck from carefully prepared cylindrical cores, a method that produced more standardized tools using less raw material than earlier flake-based techniques, and that supported an expanded tool kit including specialized implements for working bone, antler, wood, and hide. Bone and antler tools, comparatively rare in earlier periods, become more common in Upper Paleolithic assemblages across the Levant and the Zagros region, including awls, points, and other implements associated with the production of clothing, containers, and other perishable items that themselves rarely survive in the archaeological record.

Personal ornamentation, including perforated shells, some transported considerable distances from their marine sources, and, at some sites, worked pieces of ochre and other pigments, becomes more archaeologically visible during this period, though earlier and more sporadic evidence for pigment use and possible ornamentation exists at some Middle Paleolithic sites as well. Many archaeologists interpret the increasing frequency and elaboration of such material during the Upper Paleolithic as evidence for expanding symbolic communication, social differentiation, and the exchange networks linking increasingly distant and interconnected human groups, since the transport of marine shells to inland sites implies either direct travel or the existence of exchange relationships extending across considerable distances.

Settlement patterns during this period continued to reflect a highly mobile foraging economy, discussed further in Section 3.7, with populations moving seasonally in response to the availability of game, wild plant foods, and water, but some sites show evidence of more intensive and repeated occupation than earlier periods, suggesting the beginnings of the more structured and sometimes prolonged site use that would become increasingly pronounced during the subsequent Epipaleolithic period examined in Section 3.9. Archaeologists debate how much of this Upper Paleolithic behavioral change reflects genuine cognitive or social innovation specific to this period, as opposed to better archaeological visibility of behaviors that may have existed, in less well-preserved forms, considerably earlier; this debate parallels similar discussions of the "human revolution" question in African and European Paleolithic archaeology, and this textbook presents it as an active area of scholarly disagreement rather than a settled conclusion.

Section 3.6

Climate, Ice Age Cycles, and Human Mobility

Throughout the Pleistocene epoch, global climate alternated between colder glacial periods, during which large ice sheets covered much of the Northern Hemisphere and global sea levels fell significantly, and warmer interglacial periods similar in some respects to the present Holocene climate. Southwest Asia, though never covered by continental ice sheets itself, experienced significant climatic fluctuations tied to these global cycles, including changes in temperature, precipitation, and the extent of forest, steppe, and desert vegetation zones described in Chapter 2's discussion of the region's ecological zones.

During colder and drier glacial phases, desert and steppe environments generally expanded across the Arabian Peninsula and the Syrian steppe, while the Mediterranean woodland zone of the Levant likely contracted, concentrating both human and animal populations into more limited, better-watered refuge areas along the coast and in mountain valleys. During warmer and wetter interglacial and interstadial phases, by contrast, the region's habitable zones likely expanded, potentially easing the movement of both animal populations and the human groups that depended on them across wider areas, including into the interior of the Arabian Peninsula during especially favorable humid phases, some of which are discussed further in Chapter 2's treatment of Holocene climate change and are relevant background for understanding the deeper Pleistocene humid phases that affected earlier hominin movement as well.

The Last Glacial Maximum, occurring roughly twenty-six thousand to nineteen thousand years ago, represents the most extreme and best-documented cold, arid phase within the period covered by this chapter. Paleoenvironmental evidence, including pollen cores and sediment records, indicates that this period brought increased aridity to much of Southwest Asia, likely contracting habitable zones and placing pressure on human foraging populations, some of whom appear to have concentrated into refuge areas along the Mediterranean coast and in well-watered valley systems. The subsequent warming and climatic amelioration following the Last Glacial Maximum, though not without further short-term fluctuations, including a documented cold and arid interval known as the Younger Dryas occurring toward the very end of the Pleistocene, set the stage for the Epipaleolithic developments examined in Section 3.9 and for the eventual emergence of agriculture examined in Chapter 6.

These climatic cycles should not be understood as directly and mechanically determining human behavior, in keeping with the possibilist approach to environmental explanation introduced in Chapter 2. Rather, climate change altered the distribution and abundance of the plant and animal resources on which foraging populations depended, creating shifting constraints and opportunities to which human groups responded through changes in mobility, technology, diet, and social organization, discussed further in Section 3.7. Archaeologists continue to debate the precise relationship between specific climatic events and specific archaeological changes, and this textbook presents proposed connections between climate and cultural change as reasonable hypotheses supported by converging evidence rather than as established, one-to-one causal relationships.

Section 3.7

Hunting, Gathering, and the Rhythms of Foraging Life

For the entire span of time covered by this chapter, subsistence in Southwest Asia remained overwhelmingly based on hunting, gathering, scavenging, and fishing rather than on a livestock- or crop-based food-producing economy, which lay tens of thousands of years in the future for most of this period and is examined beginning in Chapter 6. This general picture requires one important qualification: the dog is now understood to be the first domesticated animal and the only domestic animal firmly documented to predate the origins of agriculture, with genomic research indicating that dog lineages were already diversifying by at least roughly 14,200 years ago, and archaeological evidence from the Natufian site of Ain Mallaha, discussed further in Section 3.9, includes a human burial accompanied by a puppy. This chapter's later Epipaleolithic sections therefore describe an economy still overwhelmingly reliant on foraging, but not one entirely without domesticated species or, as Section 3.9 discusses, without emerging forms of deliberate plant management. Faunal remains recovered from Paleolithic sites across the region indicate that foraging populations hunted a range of wild animals appropriate to the varying environments in which they lived, including gazelle, fallow deer, wild cattle (aurochs), wild goats and sheep in mountainous areas, and, at various points, larger and more dangerous game, with the relative importance of different species shifting over time and by region in ways that likely reflect both environmental change and evolving hunting technology and strategy.

Plant foods, though generally less well preserved in the archaeological record than animal bone, almost certainly formed a substantial part of the diet throughout this period, and increasing archaeobotanical evidence — including the recovery of carbonized plant remains, phytoliths (microscopic plant silica bodies), and starch grain residues on stone tools — has allowed researchers to reconstruct at least a partial picture of plant use, including the collection of wild cereals, legumes, nuts, and fruits, particularly in the Fertile Crescent zone examined in Chapter 2. This evidence is central to understanding the Epipaleolithic intensification of plant use examined in Section 3.9, which historians and archaeologists increasingly recognize as building on a much longer prior history of plant exploitation rather than representing a sudden, unprecedented innovation.

Paleolithic foraging populations in Southwest Asia were almost certainly organized into small, mobile residential groups, though the size and composition of these groups likely varied considerably across time, region, and season, with periodic larger aggregations plausible at particular times of year, moving seasonally across the landscape in patterns shaped by the availability of water, game, and plant resources. Archaeologists infer this pattern both from ethnographic comparison with more recent foraging societies living in broadly similar environments — a method that must be applied cautiously, since no living or recently documented society is a direct analogue for deep prehistoric populations — and from the archaeological evidence itself, including the generally small size of most Paleolithic occupation sites, the frequent presence of only temporary hearths and shelters, and the absence, for most of this period, of the more substantial and repeatedly used structures that begin to appear in the Epipaleolithic.

Toward the later part of the period examined in this chapter, especially during the Upper Paleolithic and the subsequent Epipaleolithic, some evidence suggests a trend toward what archaeologists call "broad-spectrum foraging," meaning increased reliance on a wider range of smaller and more labor-intensive food resources, including small game, birds, fish and other aquatic resources, and a broader array of plant foods, rather than a narrower focus on larger and more easily obtained game. This concept remains useful, but reassessments of the Levantine evidence caution against treating it as a simple, steady, region-wide march toward agriculture: exploitation of a fairly wide range of plant and animal resources is documented at some sites well back into the Middle Paleolithic, and the degree to which taxonomic diversity in faunal and floral assemblages increased in a genuinely linear fashion toward the end of the Pleistocene, as opposed to fluctuating unevenly by site, season, and local environment, is itself debated among specialists. Some researchers have linked periods of apparent broadening to population growth placing pressure on preferred resources, others to changing climate and vegetation patterns affecting resource availability, and still others to changing technology making the exploitation of previously less accessible resources more efficient; whether these factors combined into a single, coherent "Broad Spectrum Revolution" late in the Pleistocene, as earlier models proposed, remains an open and actively debated question, examined further in Chapters 5 and 6's discussion of the origins of agriculture.

Section 3.8

Southwest Asia within Wider Human Migrations

The prehistory of Southwest Asia cannot be understood in isolation from the much larger story of human evolution and dispersal, which occurred principally in Africa before extending, at various points and through various routes, across the rest of the Old World and eventually into the Americas and Oceania. Current genetic and archaeological evidence indicates that anatomically modern humans evolved in Africa, with the oldest currently known fossils attributed to Homo sapiens dating to roughly three hundred thousand years ago, and that subsequent dispersals of modern human populations out of Africa occurred over an extended period rather than through a single migration event.

The early modern human presence documented at Skhul and Qafzeh, examined in Section 3.4, appears to represent one such early dispersal, though current genetic evidence suggests that the modern human populations descended from this particular Levantine presence may not have contributed substantially, or at all, to the ancestry of most present-day non-African populations, whose ancestors appear instead to trace their dispersal out of Africa to a later period, generally placed somewhere in the range of seventy to fifty thousand years ago, though the exact timing, number, and routes of these later dispersals remain subjects of ongoing research and revision. Southwest Asia likely served as a corridor, and quite possibly also as a staging area for a period of continued residence, for at least some portion of this later and more demographically significant dispersal, before descendant populations continued onward into Europe, Central and South Asia, and ultimately every other inhabited continent.

Ancient DNA research has also clarified the genetic legacy of interbreeding between modern humans and Neanderthals, indicating that most present-day human populations outside sub-Saharan Africa carry a small percentage, on the order of two to three percent, of Neanderthal-derived DNA, tracing to a major admixture episode currently dated to roughly forty-five to forty-nine thousand years ago. As Section 3.4 explains, this genomic evidence establishes that interbreeding occurred and provides an approximate date for the dominant admixture pulse, but it does not by itself identify where the relevant contact took place; Southwest Asia remains a strong geographic candidate given the region's documented long-term co-presence of both populations, but the genetic record alone cannot confirm that the decisive contact occurred specifically within this corridor rather than elsewhere across the broader zone of Neanderthal-modern human contact in western Eurasia. Genetic evidence has additionally identified traces of interbreeding with other archaic hominin populations, including Denisovans, whose remains are documented in Siberia and whose genetic legacy is more pronounced in some Asian and Oceanian populations than in populations more directly connected to the Southwest Asian corridor; the precise geographic extent of Denisovan populations, and whether they were ever present in Southwest Asia itself, remains an open question actively being investigated through both fossil and genetic research.

This global context matters for understanding Southwest Asian prehistory because it clarifies that the region's importance lies not in being an isolated cradle of human development but in its function as a connective corridor within a far larger, continent-spanning story, a role echoing the connective function that Chapter 2 assigned to the region's rivers, mountains, deserts, and seas across every later period this textbook examines. Even in the deepest prehistoric past, long before agriculture, cities, or writing, Southwest Asia's history was already a history of movement, connection, and exchange rather than isolation.

Section 3.9

The Epipaleolithic Threshold: Toward Sedentism

The final period examined in this chapter, the Epipaleolithic, spans roughly twenty-five thousand to eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, with the precise start of the period varying somewhat among regional schemes and specialists, and encompasses a series of archaeological cultures documenting a gradual, uneven, and regionally variable trend toward more intensive resource use, more prolonged site occupation, and, by the culture's later phases, increasingly sedentary or semi-sedentary settlement. The earlier part of this period in the Levant is associated with the Kebaran culture, named for the site of Kebara previously discussed in connection with Neanderthal remains, whose later Epipaleolithic layers document a distinct and later occupation by anatomically modern human foraging populations employing increasingly specialized microlithic stone-tool technology — very small, standardized stone blade segments, often mounted in composite tools using wood, bone, or bitumen hafts.

Kebaran and related Epipaleolithic populations continued to practice a substantially mobile foraging economy, but archaeological evidence, including increasingly repeated and sometimes more substantial site occupation, suggests a gradual trend toward reduced mobility and more intensive exploitation of resources within smaller territories compared with earlier Upper Paleolithic populations, a trend some researchers connect to population growth, to the resource pressures associated with Last Glacial Maximum aridity discussed in Section 3.6, or to a combination of factors that remains actively debated.

By the final Epipaleolithic phase, generally dated from roughly fourteen and a half thousand to eleven and a half thousand years ago, the Levant was home to the Natufian culture, whose communities are documented at sites including Ain Mallaha (Eynan), Hayonim, el-Wad, and Salibiya I, among others. Natufian communities constructed semi-subterranean stone-founded dwellings, in some cases occupied repeatedly or continuously over extended periods, buried their dead, sometimes elaborately and in dedicated cemetery areas — one burial at Ain Mallaha, notably, paired a human individual with a puppy, consistent with the genomic evidence discussed in Section 3.7 indicating that dogs were already a distinct, domesticated lineage by this period — and left abundant evidence, including specialized ground-stone tools such as mortars, pestles, and sickle blades bearing characteristic wear patterns, for intensive collection and processing of wild cereals and other plant foods, alongside continued hunting of gazelle and other game.

A detailed study published in 2026, examining wear patterns on 158 Natufian sickle blades from el-Wad Terrace and Salibiya I, identified harvesting and stripping patterns that its authors interpret as evidence for intensive, possibly deliberate cereal cultivation — meaning the planned sowing of collected seed to produce a future harvest — occurring roughly four and a half thousand years before the morphological changes conventionally used to identify fully domesticated cereals. If this interpretation holds up under further research, it would mean that some Natufian communities were already managing cereal stands in a directed way well before the Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 6, even though the plants themselves remained morphologically wild and lacked the fully domesticated non-shattering phenotype associated with later Neolithic crops. This finding should be read as an important and actively developing area of research rather than as settled consensus, and it reinforces this chapter's broader caution against drawing a sharp line between "foraging" and "farming": Natufian subsistence remained overwhelmingly a foraging economy, but it may already have included forms of deliberate wild-cereal cultivation long before domesticated crops appeared. The full examination of Natufian society, its significance for the origins of sedentism, and its relationship to the subsequent Neolithic transition belongs properly to Chapters 5 and 6, which this chapter's closing discussion previews rather than replaces.

What this chapter's brief preview of the Natufian and related Epipaleolithic developments is intended to establish is continuity rather than rupture, while resisting an overly tidy or inevitable narrative. The trend toward more intensive resource use, reduced residential mobility, and eventually sedentary settlement that fed into the Neolithic developments of Chapters 5 through 8 did not emerge suddenly, without deep roots, or along a single predetermined path. It developed unevenly, over thousands of years and with considerable regional and local variation in mobility, settlement permanence, and subsistence strategy, out of the long foraging adaptations, technological traditions, and environmental relationships that this entire chapter has traced from the earliest hominin presence at 'Ubeidiya through the increasingly specialized and settled societies of the terminal Pleistocene. The Kebaran, Natufian, and related Epipaleolithic cultures are best understood as important antecedents to, and one of the principal historical pathways toward, the later Neolithic transition, rather than as a single, uniform, or inevitable march toward agriculture.

Section 3.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has surveyed well over a million years of human and pre-human occupation of Southwest Asia, establishing the deep prehistoric foundation on which the agricultural, urban, and imperial developments examined throughout the remainder of this textbook would eventually be built. The earliest evidence, examined in Section 3.2, places hominins in the Jordan Valley by at least 1.5 million years ago on the traditional chronology, and quite possibly earlier still according to a 2026 reassessment, confirming the Levantine corridor's importance as an early route by which hominins first moved out of Africa into the wider Old World.

Sections 3.3 and 3.4 traced the long Middle Pleistocene occupation of the region and the historically significant later co-presence, within the same general corridor, of Neanderthal and anatomically modern human populations, documented most vividly at Misliya, Skhul, Qafzeh, Tabun, Kebara, and Amud. The Misliya-1 maxilla currently represents the earliest skeletal evidence for modern humans in the Levant, at roughly 177,000 to 194,000 years ago, while the Skhul and Qafzeh burials provide some of the world's earliest evidence for deliberate burial practices and possible symbolic behavior among anatomically modern humans. The Neanderthal-associated chronology at Tabun, Kebara, and Amud remains only partially resolved, with some dating models placing Tabun in approximate overlap with Skhul and Qafzeh rather than clearly afterward, and ongoing genetic and archaeological research continues to refine understanding of how, when, and where Neanderthal and modern human populations may have interacted.

Section 3.5 examined the Upper Paleolithic's technological and behavioral changes, including blade technology, expanded tool kits, and increasing evidence for personal ornamentation and long-distance exchange, while Section 3.6 situated all of this development within the broader context of Pleistocene climate cycles, whose alternating glacial and interglacial phases shaped the shifting distribution of habitable environments and resources across the region without mechanically determining human behavior. Section 3.7 examined the hunting and gathering economies that sustained human populations throughout this entire period, emphasizing both the diversity of resources exploited and the mobile, small-scale social organization inferred from the archaeological record.

Section 3.8 placed Southwest Asian prehistory within the still larger context of global human dispersal out of Africa, emphasizing the region's role as a connective corridor within a continent-spanning story rather than an isolated center of independent development, a theme consistent with this textbook's broader argument, introduced in Chapter 2, that connection rather than isolation has been the historical norm for this region across every period it examines. Finally, Section 3.9 previewed the Epipaleolithic Kebaran and Natufian cultures, whose gradual trend toward intensified resource use and reduced mobility, including possible early forms of deliberate cereal cultivation documented by recent research, represented important antecedents to the transition toward food production examined across Chapters 5 and 6.

Throughout this chapter, students should have noted the recurring methodological caution appropriate to a period without written records: dates are subject to revision, taxonomic and behavioral interpretations remain debated among specialists, and inferences about cognition, belief, and social organization must be drawn cautiously from indirect material evidence. These same habits of careful, evidence-based reasoning about uncertainty, introduced here in the context of deep prehistory, will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of every subsequent period, including periods with abundant written evidence whose interpretation nonetheless remains genuinely contested.

Primary Evidence Window

The Skhul and Qafzeh Burials

Type of evidence: Archaeological — skeletal remains and associated grave features · Location: Skhul Cave, Mount Carmel, and Qafzeh Cave, near Nazareth, present-day Israel · Approximate date: c. 130,000–90,000 years ago, based on thermoluminescence and electron spin resonance dating of associated burnt flint and dental enamel

Context: Excavations conducted at Skhul beginning in the 1930s and at Qafzeh across several campaigns from the 1930s through the 1970s and beyond recovered the skeletal remains of numerous anatomically modern humans, several found in what excavators interpreted as deliberately prepared burial pits rather than natural or accidental deposits.

Description of the evidence: The best-documented grave association at Qafzeh comes from Qafzeh 11, an adolescent burial in which two deer antlers were found positioned across the upper chest near the hands. Red ochre pigment, including scraped fragments and ochre-stained marine shells, is documented more broadly within the burial-bearing layers at Qafzeh as part of the site's wider symbolic assemblage. At Skhul, one adult burial was found with the mandible of a wild boar positioned in apparent association with the body's hands. Several other individuals at both sites were found in body positions — flexed limbs, consistent orientation — that most excavators interpret as the result of deliberate placement rather than natural accumulation.

Analysis: These burials are widely cited as among the earliest evidence anywhere for deliberate mortuary treatment among anatomically modern humans, and some researchers interpret the antlers, the associated ochre, and the boar mandible as evidence for early symbolic or ritual behavior. Other researchers urge caution, noting that deliberate burial does not by itself prove complex ritual belief, that ochre could have practical as well as symbolic uses, and that inferring specific beliefs about death, an afterlife, or social status from a small number of burials many tens of thousands of years old requires a degree of interpretive caution that responsible historians and archaeologists must maintain.

Limitations: The excavation methods used in the 1930s were less precise by modern standards than later archaeological techniques, complicating some aspects of stratigraphic interpretation; sample sizes remain small; and the absence of any written or oral testimony from this period means that all interpretation of motive or belief remains inferential rather than direct.

Historical Debate

How Should We Understand Neanderthal–Modern Human Interaction?

The question: What was the nature of the relationship between Neanderthal and anatomically modern human populations who occupied the Levantine corridor across tens of thousands of years? Did the two populations directly encounter one another, compete for the same resources, coexist with minimal contact, or interbreed within the region itself?

Interpretation A — A pattern of alternating occupation tied to climate: Some researchers argue that the currently available dates support a model in which modern humans occupied the Levant primarily during warmer interglacial phases, represented by Skhul and Qafzeh, while Neanderthals, whose broader population is well documented in colder European contexts, occupied the same region primarily during subsequent cooler phases, represented by Tabun, Kebara, and Amud, with direct contact between the two populations correspondingly limited or absent within the region itself.
Interpretation B — A more continuous zone of contact and periodic interbreeding: Other researchers argue that dating uncertainties for several key sites leave open considerable chronological overlap between the two populations, that genetic evidence for interbreeding between Neanderthals and the ancestors of non-African modern humans most plausibly locates at least some contact events in Southwest Asia or an immediately adjacent region, and that the Levantine corridor should therefore be understood as a more continuous zone of at least intermittent contact across a long period rather than a location of strictly separated, alternating occupation.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same body of stratigraphic, dating, and genetic evidence but differ in how they weigh the uncertainties in that evidence and in how directly they connect regional archaeological patterns to genome-wide genetic findings about interbreeding, which cannot by themselves specify an exact geographic location or date for any particular interbreeding event.

Why it matters: This debate illustrates a recurring challenge in prehistoric archaeology: genetic evidence can establish that certain population-level events occurred, such as interbreeding between Neanderthals and modern humans, without being able to specify precisely where or when those events took place, requiring careful, sometimes provisional, integration with the independently uncertain archaeological and dating evidence from specific sites.

Student task: Using the evidence and reasoning described above, explain which interpretation you find more persuasive, and identify what kind of new evidence — additional fossils, more precise dating, or further genetic research — might help resolve the debate more conclusively.

Multiple Perspectives

Imagining Foraging Life Responsibly

From the perspective of a Middle Paleolithic hunting group in the Levant: Daily and seasonal decisions are shaped by tracking the movement of gazelle and other game across a landscape whose vegetation and water sources shift with the seasons and with longer-term climatic changes discussed in Section 3.6, requiring detailed shared knowledge of the terrain passed between generations.

From the perspective of a modern archaeologist interpreting this evidence: Reconstructing the beliefs, emotions, and social relationships of Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic people requires combining stone tools, animal bones, burial evidence, and paleoenvironmental data with great caution, resisting the temptation to project modern assumptions about religion, family, or social organization onto evidence that can rarely speak to such questions directly.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Mobile Bands to the Edge of Settlement

What Changed

  • Stone-tool technology developed from simple Lower Paleolithic core-and-flake and Acheulean handaxe traditions, through Middle Paleolithic Levallois flake production, to Upper Paleolithic blade technology and Epipaleolithic microliths
  • Hominin and human populations shifted from intermittent, climate-dependent occupation toward more continuous and eventually semi-sedentary settlement by the Natufian period
  • Resource use varied considerably across time and place; some later assemblages show intensified exploitation of smaller game, aquatic resources, and plant foods, but current scholarship disputes whether this amounted to a uniform, region-wide "Broad Spectrum Revolution" rather than an uneven, site-by-site pattern
  • Evidence for personal ornamentation, long-distance exchange, and possible symbolic or ritual behavior became more archaeologically visible from the Middle Paleolithic onward, particularly during the Upper Paleolithic

What Stayed the Same

  • Subsistence remained overwhelmingly foraging-based throughout this period; domesticated dogs were present by the late Epipaleolithic, and some Natufian groups may already have practiced forms of deliberate wild-cereal cultivation, though crop and livestock agriculture proper still lay in the future
  • The Levantine corridor consistently functioned as a route of movement between Africa and the rest of Eurasia across more than a million years
  • Human and pre-human populations throughout this period remained small in scale, organized into mobile or semi-mobile bands rather than large, centrally governed communities
  • Climate remained a persistent shaping influence on where and how people could live, from the earliest hominin occupation through the final Epipaleolithic phase

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the profound changes and the enduring continuities across this immense span of time helps students understand the Neolithic developments of Chapters 5 and 6 not as a sudden break with the past but as building on trends already underway, unevenly and with considerable regional variation, for thousands of years by the end of the Epipaleolithic.

Cause and Effect

Climate, Corridor, and Movement

Long-term causes

The Levant's geographic position as the only overland corridor linking Africa to the rest of the Old World made it a persistent route for hominin and, later, modern human movement across more than a million years.

Contributing conditions

Recurring Pleistocene glacial and interglacial cycles altered the extent of habitable woodland, steppe, and desert zones, periodically expanding and contracting the resource base available to foraging populations.

Effect

Southwest Asia experienced repeated, climate-linked episodes of hominin and human presence, population movement, and, during the Epipaleolithic, a gradual shift toward reduced mobility, intensified resource use, and eventual sedentism.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats climate and geography as significant contributing conditions that shaped, without mechanically determining, the specific technological, social, and behavioral choices made by prehistoric populations, consistent with the possibilist approach introduced in Chapter 2.

Historical complexity: No single climatic or geographic factor fully explains the pace or character of change across this long period; population dynamics, technological innovation, and processes not yet well understood by current research likely all played contributing roles alongside environmental conditions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living as a Forager in Prehistoric Southwest Asia

Although no direct testimony survives from this period, archaeological evidence allows historians to reconstruct, with appropriate caution, some outlines of daily life. Paleolithic foraging groups likely organized their days and seasons around securing food, water, and shelter, tasks that required detailed environmental knowledge transmitted across generations through observation and practice rather than through writing. Toolmaking, whether producing an Acheulean handaxe, a Levallois flake, or an Upper Paleolithic blade, required years of learned skill, suggesting sustained relationships between more experienced and less experienced group members, plausibly including relationships between adults and children, though the precise social structure of any specific group cannot be recovered directly from stone tools alone.

Evidence for shelter includes both the use of natural caves and rock shelters, well documented at sites such as Skhul, Qafzeh, Tabun, Kebara, and Amud, and, particularly by the Epipaleolithic Natufian period, constructed dwellings with stone foundations. Health and mortality patterns inferred from skeletal remains suggest lives that were, by later historical standards, often short and physically demanding, though such inferences must be drawn cautiously from small and unevenly preserved skeletal samples. Because the surviving evidence for this period consists overwhelmingly of durable materials such as stone, bone, and, more rarely, preserved plant remains, historians must remain alert to the likelihood that perishable technologies, including textiles, basketry, and wooden tools, played a significant but largely invisible role in daily life throughout the period this chapter examines.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has surveyed more than a million years of human and pre-human occupation of Southwest Asia, establishing the deep prehistoric foundation that underlies the entire nine-volume history this textbook presents. The earliest evidence, examined in Section 3.2, documents hominin presence in the Jordan Valley traditionally dated to roughly 1.5 million years ago at the site of 'Ubeidiya, an estimate a 2026 geochronological reassessment argues may be too young by several hundred thousand years, confirming the Levantine corridor's importance as an early route by which hominins first moved out of Africa into the wider Old World, a role reinforced by the strong evidence for repeated fire use and cooking at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov roughly 780,000 years ago.

Sections 3.3 and 3.4 traced the long Middle Pleistocene occupation of the region through Acheulean and Levallois stone-tool traditions and examined the historically significant Levantine record documenting, within the same broad corridor, both anatomically modern humans — represented first by the Misliya-1 maxilla, dated to roughly 177,000 to 194,000 years ago and currently the earliest skeletal evidence for Homo sapiens in the Levant, and subsequently by the deliberate burials at Skhul and Qafzeh dated to roughly 130,000 to 90,000 years ago — and Neanderthals, represented at Tabun, Kebara, and Amud in a chronology that remains only partially resolved, with some dating models for Tabun in particular overlapping substantially with the Skhul/Qafzeh moderns rather than following clearly afterward. The Skhul and Qafzeh burials, examined further in the Primary Evidence Window, provide some of the world's earliest evidence for deliberate mortuary treatment and possible symbolic behavior among modern humans, while ongoing genetic and archaeological research continues to refine scholarly understanding of the relationship between these two human populations, a subject examined critically in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Section 3.5 examined the Upper Paleolithic's technological and behavioral changes, including blade-based tool production, expanded tool kits incorporating bone and antler implements, and increasing evidence for personal ornamentation and long-distance exchange networks, developments that many archaeologists connect to expanding symbolic communication and social complexity, though the precise causes and significance of these changes remain subjects of active scholarly discussion.

Section 3.6 situated this long developmental sequence within the broader context of Pleistocene climate cycles, explaining how alternating glacial and interglacial phases, including the especially severe Last Glacial Maximum roughly twenty-six thousand to nineteen thousand years ago, shaped the shifting distribution of habitable environments and resources across the region without mechanically determining specific human choices, consistent with the possibilist approach to environmental explanation introduced in Chapter 2.

Section 3.7 examined the hunting and gathering economies that sustained human and hominin populations throughout this entire period, describing the animals hunted, the plant foods gathered, the small-scale and mobile social organization inferred from archaeological evidence, and the debated later pattern often called "broad-spectrum foraging," in which some later assemblages show intensified exploitation of smaller and more labor-intensive resources, though current scholarship disputes whether this amounted to a uniform, region-wide progression rather than an uneven pattern varying by site and period.

Section 3.8 placed Southwest Asian prehistory within the still larger context of global human evolution and dispersal out of Africa, emphasizing the region's role as a connective corridor for successive waves of hominin and modern human movement, and examining the genetic evidence for interbreeding between modern humans and Neanderthals, and possibly other archaic hominin populations, evidence that establishes when major admixture occurred without independently confirming exactly where within the broader zone of contact it took place, though Southwest Asia remains a strong candidate.

Finally, Section 3.9 previewed the Epipaleolithic Kebaran and Natufian cultures, whose increasingly intensive resource use, reduced residential mobility, and, by the Natufian period, semi-sedentary settlement, elaborate mortuary practices, the presence of the domesticated dog, and possible early deliberate cereal cultivation documented by recent research, together represent important antecedents to — and one of the principal historical pathways toward — the Neolithic transition examined fully in Chapters 5 through 8.

Throughout this chapter, sustained attention to methodological caution — the uncertainty of dating, the provisional nature of taxonomic and behavioral interpretation, and the need for careful, evidence-based inference in the absence of written testimony — modeled the kind of careful historical reasoning that this textbook will continue to apply, in different forms appropriate to different kinds of evidence, throughout its treatment of every subsequent period covered across its nine volumes.

These deep prehistoric foundations are not merely a preface to the "real" history that begins with cities, writing, and states. They represent the overwhelming majority of the total span of human presence in Southwest Asia, and the technological, environmental, and social adaptations developed across this immense period directly shaped the conditions under which the agricultural, urban, and political developments examined throughout this textbook's later volumes became possible.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Hominin
Modern humans and their extinct close relatives and ancestors following the divergence of the human lineage from the ancestors of chimpanzees.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch spanning roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated glacial and interglacial climate cycles, during which most of the human prehistory examined in this chapter took place.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, marking the end of the last glacial period and the climatic conditions within which agriculture later developed.
Paleolithic
The "Old Stone Age," the long period of human prehistory characterized by the use of chipped stone tools, conventionally divided into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases.
Lower Paleolithic
The earliest Paleolithic phase, associated with simple core-and-flake tools and, later, the Acheulean handaxe tradition.
Middle Paleolithic
The Paleolithic phase associated with more sophisticated flake-based technologies, including the Levallois technique, and, in the Levant, with both Neanderthal and early modern human toolmakers.
Upper Paleolithic
The Paleolithic phase associated with blade-based stone-tool technology, expanded tool kits, and increasing evidence for symbolic behavior and long-distance exchange.
Epipaleolithic
The transitional period following the Upper Paleolithic, encompassing the Kebaran and Natufian cultures of the Levant, marked by increasingly intensive resource use and, eventually, semi-sedentary settlement.
Acheulean
A Lower Paleolithic stone-tool tradition characterized by bifacially worked handaxes, first developed in Africa and carried into Southwest Asia by early hominin populations.
Levallois technique
A Middle Paleolithic method of preparing a stone core so that a single strike produces a flake of predetermined shape, associated with both Neanderthal and early modern human toolmakers in the region.
Blade technology
A stone-tool production method emphasizing long, narrow blades struck from prepared cylindrical cores, characteristic of the Upper Paleolithic.
Hominin dispersal
The movement of hominin populations out of their region of origin into new territories, a process in which Southwest Asia played a repeated and important role.
Out of Africa
The general scientific model holding that hominins, and later anatomically modern humans, originated in Africa and subsequently dispersed into Eurasia and beyond across multiple episodes.
Interglacial
A warmer climatic phase occurring between glacial periods within the Pleistocene, generally associated with expanded habitable zones.
Glacial period
A colder climatic phase within the Pleistocene, generally associated with contracted habitable zones and, in higher latitudes, extensive ice sheets.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period of peak global cold and aridity, roughly twenty-six thousand to nineteen thousand years ago, associated with contracted habitable zones across Southwest Asia.
Faunal assemblage
The collection of animal bones recovered from an archaeological site, used to reconstruct diet, hunting strategy, and environmental conditions.
Radiocarbon dating
A dating method measuring the decay of radioactive carbon in organic remains, generally reliable for material up to about fifty thousand years old.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered sediment deposits at an archaeological site, used to establish the relative sequence of occupation and deposition.
Symbolic behavior
Behavior, such as deliberate burial, personal ornamentation, or pigment use, interpreted by some archaeologists as evidence for abstract thought, communication, or ritual, though such interpretation requires caution.
Foraging economy
A subsistence system relying principally on hunting, gathering, fishing, and scavenging rather than crop cultivation or livestock husbandry; the dominant economic pattern throughout this chapter's period, notwithstanding the presence of the domesticated dog and possible early Natufian cereal cultivation.
Broad-spectrum foraging
A foraging pattern emphasizing a wide range of smaller and more labor-intensive resources, increasingly documented in the later Upper Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic.
Kebaran culture
An early Epipaleolithic archaeological culture of the Levant associated with microlithic tool technology and increasingly intensive, though still largely mobile, foraging.
Natufian culture
A late Epipaleolithic culture of the Levant associated with semi-sedentary settlement, intensive wild-cereal processing, and elaborate burial practices, examined fully in Chapter 5.
Microlith
A very small, standardized stone tool component, often hafted in combination with other materials, characteristic of Epipaleolithic technology.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from fossil remains or archaeological sediment, used to study population relationships, migration, and interbreeding among ancient hominin and human populations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has extended the physical foundation established in Chapter 2 into the realm of human experience, tracing more than a million years during which hominins, and eventually anatomically modern humans, occupied the landscapes of Southwest Asia long before any of the institutions examined in later chapters existed. The habits of mind this chapter has modeled — careful attention to the limits and uncertainties of evidence, resistance to overconfident interpretation, and recognition that current scientific understanding remains provisional and subject to revision — are not unique to prehistory. They are habits this textbook will continue to apply, in different forms appropriate to different kinds of evidence, throughout its treatment of every subsequent period.

The prehistoric populations surveyed in this chapter belong to no later nation, ethnicity, or religious tradition. Their significance lies instead in what they reveal about the shared, deep human past of a region that later peoples would inhabit, name, and transform in an extraordinary variety of ways. The technologies, environmental adaptations, and social patterns developed across the Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic periods were not separate from, but were important antecedents to, the transition toward food production examined across Chapters 5 and 6, the first cities examined in Chapter 8, and, ultimately, every civilization, empire, and state that this nine-volume textbook will go on to describe.

Students who understand that human history in this region did not begin with writing, agriculture, or cities, but extends back well over a million years into a period of mobile foraging bands responding to shifting climates and landscapes, are better equipped to appreciate both the remarkable durability of certain human adaptations and the genuine transformation represented by the agricultural and urban revolutions that follow. Understanding the Levantine corridor's role as a route of connection between Africa and the rest of the world, moreover, reinforces this textbook's recurring argument that Southwest Asia's history has been shaped by movement and exchange from the very beginning of the human story in the region.

Chapter 4 builds directly on this foundation by examining, in systematic detail, the methods historians and archaeologists use to reconstruct the ancient Middle East, including archaeology, inscriptions, oral tradition, religious texts, linguistics, genetics, and the inherent limits of historical reconstruction — methods this chapter has already applied, in preliminary form, to the specific problem of deep human prehistory. The human beings who first walked the landscapes surveyed in Chapter 2 have now been introduced; the following chapter turns to the evidence and methods by which their much later descendants' civilizations can be studied with confidence.
