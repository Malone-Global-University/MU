Middle East History

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century traces the history of the region from human prehistory, early agriculture, and the first cities through Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Anatolian, Levantine, Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, Turkic, Mongol, Ottoman, Safavid, and modern state systems, continuing through the political, social, economic, cultural, and geopolitical transformations of the Middle East through 2026.

Overview

Middle Eastern history is treated here as the history of a changing regional system rather than as a synonym for Arab, Islamic, or modern nation-state history. Because “Middle East” is itself a modern analytical term, the textbook uses historically appropriate names for earlier peoples, states, empires, religions, and regions while tracing the long interaction of Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Levant, Arabia, Anatolia, Iran, the Gulf, the Red Sea, and their neighboring worlds.

The nine-volume work begins with geography, human prehistory, archaeology, agriculture, villages, cities, and the first states. It then follows the civilizations and empires of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Anatolia, the Levant, and Iran; the Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Parthian, and Sasanian worlds; the rise of Islam and the caliphates; Persianate, Turkic, Crusader, Mongol, Mamluk, Ottoman, and Safavid systems; and the social, economic, religious, intellectual, and cultural histories that connected the region across centuries.

The modern volumes examine reform, European imperialism, nationalism, Ottoman transformation and collapse, mandates and nation-making, Turkey and Iran, Jewish and Palestinian history, Kurdish history, Israel and the Arab-Israeli conflict, oil and the Gulf states, the Cold War, political Islam, revolutions, civil wars, regional rivalries, migration, economic change, water and climate pressures, and the still-developing regional order through 2026. Throughout, the textbook keeps Arab, Iranian, Turkish, Kurdish, Jewish, Palestinian, Armenian, Assyrian/Syriac, Coptic, Greek, Druze, Yazidi, and other regional histories visible where they are historically relevant.

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain the major chronological transformations of Middle Eastern history from human prehistory and the first civilizations through the twenty-first century without projecting modern regional labels, borders, or national identities backward onto earlier periods.
  • Analyze how geography, rivers, mountains, deserts, climate, agriculture, pastoralism, cities, trade routes, maritime networks, migration, technology, warfare, institutions, and environmental change shaped societies across the region.
  • Trace the development and interaction of Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Anatolian, Levantine, Iranian, Arabian, Jewish, Christian, Islamic, Turkic, Kurdish, Armenian, and other regional peoples and traditions across changing political systems.
  • Compare major state and imperial formations, including ancient kingdoms and empires, the caliphates, Persianate and Turkic states, the Ottoman and Safavid empires, colonial and mandate systems, and modern states, while recognizing regional variation and historical contingency.
  • Evaluate the historical development of Judaism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Sunni and Shia Islam, Sufism, and other religious traditions without treating religion or sectarian identity as a complete explanation for political and social change.
  • Assess the transformations produced by imperialism, reform, nationalism, world war, state formation, Zionism, Palestinian nationalism, Kurdish movements, oil, the Cold War, political Islam, revolution, regional wars, globalization, and twenty-first-century geopolitical change through 2026.
