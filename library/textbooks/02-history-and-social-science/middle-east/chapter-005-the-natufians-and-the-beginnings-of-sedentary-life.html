The Natufians and the Beginnings of Sedentary Life

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

At ʿAin Mallaha, in the Hula Valley of northern Israel, archaeologists uncovered the remains of round, semi-subterranean houses with stone foundations, hearths worn smooth by repeated use, and storage pits cut into the earth beside them. Nearby lay a cemetery in which more than one hundred individuals had been buried over generations, some accompanied by shell ornaments, bone pins, and, in one particularly discussed case, the skeleton of an elderly person laid to rest with a hand resting on a young dog. None of these people left a name for themselves, a word for their community, or a written account of their lives. What they left was stone, bone, ash, seed, and soil — and from that material record, archaeologists have reconstructed a way of living that does not fit comfortably into either of the two categories, "hunter-gatherer" and "farmer," that popular imagination often uses to divide the human past.

Chapter 4 established that reconstructing the ancient Middle East depends on multiple, imperfect, and continually reinterpreted forms of evidence — stratigraphy, material culture, archaeobotany, faunal analysis, and, for later periods, texts — and that historians and archaeologists must be candid about what that evidence can and cannot show. This chapter puts those methods to immediate use. Its subject is a group of communities that lived across the Levant during the final millennia of the Pleistocene and the very beginning of the Holocene, communities that modern archaeologists group together under the label "Natufian," after Wadi an-Natuf in the highlands west of Jerusalem, where the first such remains were formally identified in the 1920s. The label is a modern scholarly convention. No Natufian person ever called themselves by that name, and the term describes a recurring pattern of stone tools, architecture, and other material traces rather than a nation, a tribe with a known name, or a single political community.

What makes the Natufian record so important to the history that follows in this textbook is not that these communities practiced agriculture — they did not, in the sense of cultivating domesticated crops or herding domesticated animals — but that many of them lived in ways that look, in important respects, like settled village life centuries or millennia before the domestication of food crops and livestock is securely documented anywhere in the region. They built substantial round houses that were reused and rebuilt across multiple generations. They ground and pounded wild cereals and other plant foods with a sustained intensity previously unknown in the region. They buried their dead in cemeteries associated with particular places, sometimes returning to the same location for burial across many generations. They created ornaments from shell, bone, and stone that moved across considerable distances through exchange networks. And, at least in some regions and periods, they appear to have lived in one place, or a small number of places, for much or all of the year, rather than moving continually across the landscape in search of food.

This chapter's central historical problem, developed most fully in its final sections, is the relationship between settlement and food production. For much of the twentieth century, many archaeologists assumed that farming came first and that settled village life was simply agriculture's natural and inevitable consequence — people supposedly began growing crops, and this in turn required them to stay in one place to tend their fields. The Natufian evidence overturned this assumption. Here were communities that, in some cases, appear to have become substantially settled — building durable houses, maintaining cemeteries, storing food in quantity — without possessing domesticated food crops or herded livestock, notwithstanding the separate, more qualified evidence for an early or proto-domestic dog discussed in Section 5.7. This raises a question this chapter will treat as genuinely open rather than settled: did increasing sedentism create conditions that favored intensified food production, eventually including domestication, examined fully in Chapter 6? Or did prior changes in how communities gathered, processed, and stored wild foods make increased sedentism possible, with domestication arriving only later and for partly separate reasons? Neither answer should be assumed in advance.

It is important to state plainly, before this chapter begins its detailed treatment, what the Natufians were not. They were not proto-farmers unconsciously building toward an agricultural revolution they could not have anticipated. They were not a single unified people with one language, one government, or one shared identity spanning the length and breadth of the Levant. They did not live in identical ways from the Galilee to the Negev, or across the roughly three and a half millennia that archaeologists now believe the Natufian period encompassed. Treating the Natufians as a preliminary, incomplete version of the Neolithic societies discussed in Chapter 6 would misrepresent both periods: it would make Natufian communities seem like a rough draft of something else, rather than societies who were solving problems specific to their own environments, technologies, and social relationships, on their own terms, without any foreknowledge of what would come after them.

This chapter therefore occupies careful, well-defined ground between Chapter 3's survey of human prehistory in Southwest Asia and Chapter 6's account of the Neolithic Revolution and the origins of agriculture. It examines a genuine transition — from more mobile late Pleistocene hunter-gatherer lifeways toward more settled patterns of life — without assuming that the transition's eventual outcome, farming, was already underway. Readers should come away from this chapter understanding sedentism and food production as related but analytically separate historical developments, each requiring its own evidence and its own explanation, a distinction that will matter throughout this textbook's treatment of early Middle Eastern history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Natufian as an archaeological horizon rather than a known ethnic, linguistic, or political identity, and explain what this distinction means for historical interpretation.
  2. Describe the geographic setting and chronological span of Natufian communities within the Late Epipaleolithic Levant.
  3. Identify the principal categories of material evidence — stone tools, ground-stone implements, architecture, ornaments, and botanical and faunal remains — used to define Natufian assemblages.
  4. Explain the evidence for intensified wild-plant exploitation among Natufian communities and distinguish this practice clearly from domesticated agriculture.
  5. Describe the diversified Natufian subsistence economy, including hunting, fishing, fowling, and plant gathering, and explain why hunting remained central rather than marginal.
  6. Analyze the evidence for settlement and mobility among Natufian communities, distinguishing permanent settlement, seasonal aggregation, repeated reoccupation, and partial or intermittent sedentism.
  7. Compare major Natufian sites, including ʿAin Mallaha/Eynan, Hayonim, Wadi Hammeh 27, el-Wad, and Shubayqa, and explain what regional variation among them reveals about the limits of generalization.
  8. Evaluate Natufian burial practices and ritual evidence, including cemetery use and the human-animal burial evidence, while distinguishing secure inference from speculation about social structure.
  9. Assess evidence for exchange networks and social differentiation among Natufian communities without assuming the existence of fixed social classes or centralized political institutions.
  10. Explain the significance of the Younger Dryas climatic event for Late Natufian communities while avoiding simple climate-determinist explanations of cultural change.
  11. Articulate the central historical debate over the relationship between sedentism and food production, and explain why this textbook treats the question as open rather than settled.
  12. Distinguish the Natufian period's historical significance from that of the Neolithic Revolution examined in Chapter 6, avoiding teleological narratives that treat Natufian communities as an incomplete version of later Neolithic society.

Historical Context

Chapter 3 traced the deep prehistory of human presence in Southwest Asia, from early hominin dispersals through the long span of the Middle and Upper Paleolithic, during which fully modern human populations occupied the Levant and neighboring regions as highly mobile hunter-gatherers, moving across the landscape in small groups in pursuit of seasonally available game and plant resources. Chapter 4 then examined the methods — archaeological, botanical, faunal, genetic, and linguistic — by which historians and archaeologists reconstruct societies that left no written record, and it cautioned against treating any single line of evidence as sufficient on its own. This chapter applies those methods directly to a specific and unusually well-documented archaeological horizon: the Natufian culture of the Levant, dated by most current radiocarbon evidence to approximately 15,000 to 11,500 years ago, spanning the very end of the Pleistocene epoch and the threshold of the Holocene.

The Natufian belongs to the broader period archaeologists call the Epipaleolithic, a term used in Southwest Asian archaeology for the final phase of hunter-gatherer prehistory following the Upper Paleolithic and preceding the Neolithic. The Epipaleolithic itself is typically divided into an earlier phase, often called the Kebaran and related industries, characterized by small, highly mobile groups using distinctive microlithic stone tools, and a later phase in which the Natufian emerges as a regionally specific and, in the view of most specialists, historically significant departure from earlier patterns of mobility and settlement. Students should understand this chapter as picking up the human story precisely where Chapter 3 left off geographically — in the same Levantine landscapes whose physical geography Chapter 2 introduced — while narrowing the focus to a particular window of time in which the material evidence for changing ways of life becomes unusually rich.

It is essential to state clearly what this chapter does not yet address. It does not address the domestication of plants and animals, which Chapter 6 treats as its central subject; it does not address the origins of pottery, which arrives considerably later in most of the region; and it does not address the large agricultural villages and early towns, such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük, examined in Chapter 7. Some scholars have historically referred to Natufian communities as practicing a "pre-agricultural" or "broad-spectrum" economy, sometimes calling the entire Epipaleolithic-to-Neolithic transition a single continuous process. This textbook uses such language cautiously, since describing the Natufian primarily through what it lacks, or through what came after it, risks exactly the teleological error this chapter's opening warned against. The Natufians should be examined on the evidence of what they actually did, not primarily as a preface to Chapter 6.

Section 5.1

The Late Epipaleolithic Levant

The environment in which Natufian communities lived was, in broad outline, the Levantine landscape introduced in Chapter 2: a narrow, geologically varied corridor bounded by the Mediterranean to the west and by increasingly arid steppe and desert to the east, crossed by the Jordan Rift Valley and framed by highlands rising abruptly from coastal plains. During the final millennia of the Pleistocene, this region supported a mosaic of vegetation zones broadly similar to, though not identical with, those of later periods: Mediterranean woodland and parkland containing wild cereals, legumes, almonds, and pistachios in the hill country and along its margins; more open steppe vegetation toward the interior; and riverine and lakeside environments, such as the Hula Valley and the margins of the Sea of Galilee, offering fish, waterfowl, and other aquatic resources. This mosaic was not static. Climatic fluctuations across the terminal Pleistocene shifted the boundaries between these zones over time, a point this chapter returns to in Section 5.9 when discussing the Younger Dryas.

Human communities had occupied this landscape for many thousands of years before the Natufian period, as Chapter 3 discussed, but the archaeological record of the preceding Kebaran and related Epipaleolithic industries points to small, highly mobile groups whose camps typically show thinner and less repeated occupation than what later Natufian sites reveal. Populations moved across the landscape in patterns adapted to the seasonal availability of game, plants, and water, likely aggregating and dispersing at different points in the year, but leaving relatively ephemeral traces at most individual locations. This mobile pattern forms the essential baseline against which the Natufian period represents a genuine and well-documented archaeological change — a change in the intensity, repetition, and, at some sites, apparent permanence of occupation at particular places.

The label "Natufian" is a modern archaeological convention, not a term any ancient person would have recognized or used. It originates from Wadi an-Natuf, a valley west of Jerusalem where the British archaeologist Dorothy Garrod first uncovered the distinctive stone-tool assemblage associated with this horizon during excavations at Shukbah Cave in 1928, before formally defining and naming the "Natufian" culture in a 1932 publication, discussed further in the Primary Source Window below. Archaeologists subsequently identified similar assemblages at sites distributed across the Levant, from the Euphrates Valley in the north to the Negev and Sinai in the south, and from the Mediterranean coast to the edges of the eastern desert — a wide but not uniform geographic distribution that later sections of this chapter will show corresponds to considerable regional variation in how Natufian communities actually lived.

Historical Vocabulary
Epipaleolithic and Archaeological Culture

Archaeologists use the term Epipaleolithic specifically within Southwest Asian prehistory to describe the final phase of hunter-gatherer adaptation following the Upper Paleolithic and preceding the Neolithic, roughly spanning the last glacial-to-interglacial transition. Within this broader period, archaeologists further distinguish a series of named industries and horizons — including the Kebaran, the Geometric Kebaran, and, at the period's end, the Natufian — based on recurring, statistically distinctive patterns in stone-tool types, manufacturing techniques, and associated material culture.

An archaeological culture, such as "the Natufian," is a scholarly construct: a label applied to a recurring pattern of material remains found across multiple sites, used because the pattern is useful for organizing evidence and comparing sites to one another. It is not automatically evidence of a shared language, a shared political identity, a shared sense of collective belonging, or a fixed ethnic group in a modern sense. Some archaeologists have cautioned that treating an archaeological culture as though it were a single "people" with uniform beliefs and practices risks projecting modern assumptions about nationhood and ethnicity onto societies for which no such direct evidence exists. This textbook uses "Natufian" throughout this chapter as shorthand for a recognizable and historically significant pattern of material evidence, not as a claim about ancient self-identity.

Section 5.2

Defining the Natufian Archaeological Horizon

Archaeologists identify Natufian assemblages primarily through a distinctive combination of chipped-stone and ground-stone technology, supplemented by architecture, burial practice, and ornamentation. The chipped-stone component is dominated by microliths — small, carefully shaped stone tools, often no larger than a fingernail, produced from blades struck from prepared cores. Among the most diagnostic Natufian microlith forms is the lunate, a crescent-shaped microlith whose consistent, recurring shape across many sites allows archaeologists to recognize Natufian assemblages even where organic preservation is poor. Lunates and related microliths were likely hafted in combination, set into wood or bone shafts to form composite cutting or projectile edges, a technology requiring considerable planning and skill even though each individual microlith is small and, on its own, unremarkable in appearance.

Equally important, and in some ways more historically significant, is the Natufian ground-stone tool assemblage: mortars, pestles, grinding slabs, and handstones, often manufactured from basalt or limestone and, in some cases, substantial enough to have remained fixed in place at a single location rather than being carried from camp to camp. The appearance of heavy, immobile ground-stone equipment in quantity is one of the pieces of evidence archaeologists use to argue for increased site permanence, discussed further in Section 5.5, since transporting heavy stone tools repeatedly across a landscape makes far less practical sense than leaving them at a location to which a group intends to return.

Natufian sites also frequently preserve architectural remains unusual for earlier Epipaleolithic contexts: round or oval structures, often partly excavated into the ground (semi-subterranean), with stone wall foundations, internal hearths, postholes suggesting superstructures of wood, brush, or hide, and, at some sites, evidence of repair, rebuilding, and reuse across multiple occupation phases. Bone tools, including awls, needles, and points, along with worked antler and, at coastal and near-coastal sites, marine shell ornaments, round out the characteristic Natufian material assemblage. Faunal remains overwhelmingly dominated by gazelle, supplemented by a wide range of smaller game, birds, and fish depending on local environment, complete the picture that archaeologists use to define and compare Natufian sites, a subject developed further in Section 5.4.

It is important to note that not every Natufian site contains every one of these features in equal measure, and that the horizon is now generally divided by specialists into an Early Natufian phase, associated with the most substantial architecture and largest cemeteries at sites concentrated in the Mediterranean woodland zone, and a Late Natufian phase, associated with greater regional diversity, some apparent reduction in site permanence in certain areas, and continued or even intensified plant processing in others. This internal chronological and geographic variation is itself an important piece of evidence, cautioning against treating "the Natufian" as a single unchanging way of life across its full three-and-a-half-millennium span.

Section 5.3

Intensive Wild-Plant Use and Its Limits

Among the most historically consequential features of the Natufian record is evidence for markedly intensified processing and, in all likelihood, intensified harvesting of wild plant foods, particularly wild cereals such as wild barley and wild wheat, along with legumes, nuts (especially almonds and pistachios), and various fruits. This evidence takes several forms. The proliferation of ground-stone mortars and pestles, discussed in Section 5.2, points to increased processing of plant foods requiring pounding or grinding, such as removing husks from cereal grains or preparing nuts and other foods for consumption. Sickle blades — small stone blades set into a haft to form a composite cutting tool — appear at many Natufian sites bearing a distinctive polish, often called "sickle sheen" or "silica gloss," produced by repeated contact with the siliceous stems of grasses, including wild cereals, during harvesting. Archaeobotanical remains recovered through systematic flotation of excavated sediment, a technique introduced in Chapter 4's discussion of archaeological method, have in several cases directly recovered charred cereal grains and other plant remains from Natufian and closely related contexts, most notably at Shubayqa 1 in northeastern Jordan, where researchers identified charred remains interpreted as evidence of bread-like preparations made from wild, not domesticated, cereals and other plants — among the earliest such evidence yet documented anywhere in the world.

This evidence is genuinely significant, but students must interpret it with precision. Intensive gathering, processing, and even the deliberate preparation of foods such as bread-like products from wild plants is not the same thing as domesticated agriculture. Domestication, examined fully in Chapter 6, refers to a set of biological and behavioral changes in plants and animals produced through generations of human selection — for instance, cereal plants whose seed heads no longer shatter and scatter naturally, requiring human harvesting for the plant's reproduction, or animals whose size, behavior, or reproductive patterns changed measurably under human management. The wild cereals processed by Natufian communities remained, as far as current archaeobotanical evidence indicates, genetically and morphologically wild: their seed-dispersal mechanisms had not yet been altered by generations of human selection. Whether Natufian communities were also cultivating these plants — that is, deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting them across successive growing seasons in prepared plots, as opposed to gathering them only from naturally occurring stands — has become a more actively contested question following new evidence published in 2026, discussed below, and should no longer be treated as settled in either direction.

Some archaeologists have proposed that certain late Epipaleolithic and Natufian communities may have engaged in low-level forms of plant management short of full cultivation — practices such as selectively harvesting particular stands, encouraging the growth of preferred plants by clearing competing vegetation, or returning to the same wild stands across multiple seasons in ways that could, over time, have begun to exert selective pressure on plant populations. This possibility remains an active area of research and is not uniformly accepted. What can be said with more confidence is that Natufian communities across much of the Levant developed a suite of technologies — grinding equipment, sickles, and, in some cases, storage facilities discussed in Section 5.5 — that reflect a substantially intensified relationship with wild plant resources compared with earlier Epipaleolithic communities, and that this intensified relationship likely formed part of the broader context within which domestication eventually occurred in the millennia examined in Chapter 6, without itself constituting agriculture.

A May 2026 study published in Scientific Reports has pushed this discussion further. Examining sickle blades from el-Wad Terrace and Salibiya I through use-wear analysis and experimental replication, the study's authors argue that the intensity, consistency, and scale of cereal harvesting implied by the wear patterns on these tools are difficult to explain through opportunistic gathering of wild stands alone, and they interpret the evidence as pointing toward an early, still pre-domestication form of cultivation — including the deliberate retention of seed reserves for sowing in subsequent seasons. This is a significant and still interpretive scholarly argument rather than an established consensus, and other specialists have urged caution about inferring deliberate sowing from use-wear evidence alone, since intensive harvesting of dense, naturally occurring wild stands could in principle produce similar wear patterns. Students should note carefully that even if this 2026 interpretation is broadly accepted by other researchers, it would establish cultivation — a human behavior involving the deliberate planting and tending of a crop — without by itself establishing morphological domestication, the biological changes in seed-dispersal mechanisms and related traits that mark the eventual, later transformation examined in Chapter 6. Cultivation and domestication, in other words, remain distinct concepts even as the evidentiary case for early Natufian cultivation grows more substantial.

Section 5.4

Hunting, Fishing, and a Diversified Economy

Despite the emphasis that popular accounts sometimes place on Natufian plant processing as a harbinger of agriculture, faunal evidence makes clear that hunting remained a central component of Natufian subsistence throughout the period, and gazelle commonly dominates identified faunal assemblages. Gazelle bones make up the great majority of identified animal remains at many excavated Natufian sites, and analysis of gazelle mortality patterns — the ages at which animals were killed, reconstructed from tooth eruption and wear — has been used by some researchers to argue for deliberately managed, selective hunting strategies at certain sites, though this interpretation remains debated and should not be equated with livestock domestication — the domestication of herd animals for food production, which lies later and is examined in Chapter 6.

Beyond gazelle, Natufian faunal assemblages typically include a wide range of smaller game — hare, fox, and various rodents — along with birds, reptiles, and, at sites located near lakes, rivers, or the coast, fish and aquatic resources. The site of ʿAin Mallaha, situated beside a lake in the Hula Valley, has yielded substantial evidence of fishing and waterfowl exploitation alongside its terrestrial game, illustrating how local environmental conditions shaped the specific balance of resources any given Natufian community relied upon. Coastal sites, similarly, show evidence of exploitation of shellfish and marine resources unavailable to inland communities. This diversity underlines an essential point for students: there was no single "Natufian diet." Natufian communities practiced what archaeologists sometimes call a broad-spectrum economy, drawing on a wide range of locally available plant and animal resources rather than specializing narrowly in any one food source, and the specific composition of that diet varied considerably by region, season, and period.

This diversified economic pattern matters for the chapter's larger argument. If Natufian communities had relied on a single, narrow resource base, sudden environmental change could have proven catastrophic; the evidence instead suggests considerable resilience and flexibility, likely one of the underlying conditions that allowed at least some Natufian communities to sustain more permanent settlement, discussed next in Section 5.5, across multiple seasons and, in some cases, multiple generations. At the same time, this flexibility should not be mistaken for economic security equivalent to later agricultural surplus; wild resource availability still fluctuated significantly from year to year and season to season, a vulnerability that becomes especially relevant to the discussion of the Younger Dryas in Section 5.9.

Section 5.5

Settlement and the Mobility Spectrum

This section addresses the intellectual center of this chapter: what the Natufian archaeological record can and cannot tell historians about sedentism, the condition of remaining in one place, or a small set of places, for most or all of the year. Sedentism should not be understood as a simple binary between "mobile" and "settled." Archaeologists increasingly describe it as a spectrum, encompassing fully mobile groups that relocate frequently throughout the year; groups practicing seasonal aggregation, gathering at particular locations for part of the year and dispersing for the remainder; groups occupying "base camps" that serve as a primary residential location for much of the year while smaller task-specific groups range outward for hunting, gathering, or other activities; and, at the most settled end of the spectrum, communities occupying a single location on a genuinely year-round, multi-generational basis.

Several categories of evidence allow archaeologists to situate particular Natufian sites along this spectrum, though no single category is conclusive on its own. Architectural evidence is central: substantial, well-built structures — with prepared stone foundations, internal features such as hearths and storage pits, and clear evidence of maintenance, repair, and rebuilding over time — suggest an investment of labor that mobile groups moving frequently would be less likely to make. The sheer number of superimposed building phases at sites such as ʿAin Mallaha, where structures were rebuilt repeatedly on the same footprint across generations, points toward sustained attachment to particular locations even if not necessarily unbroken, year-round habitation by the same group without interruption.

Faunal seasonality evidence provides a second, independent line of inference. By analyzing animal remains for indicators of the season in which particular animals were killed — for example, using evidence from bird migration patterns, fish spawning behavior, or the growth patterns of certain shells and bones that record seasonal variation — researchers can sometimes determine whether a site was occupied during a single season or across multiple seasons within the same year. At several major Natufian sites, faunal seasonality studies have produced evidence consistent with occupation across multiple seasons, supporting an interpretation of substantial, if not necessarily uninterrupted, permanence at those specific locations. Cemetery evidence, discussed further in Section 5.7, points in a similar direction: a location used repeatedly for burial across multiple generations suggests a durable relationship between a community and that specific place, whether or not every member of that community was physically present at the site throughout every season of every year.

At the same time, archaeologists have identified considerable variation among Natufian sites in the degree of apparent permanence, and this variation increases in the Late Natufian period. Some sites show thinner, less elaborate occupation more consistent with seasonal or intermittent use; some regions show evidence of settlements that appear to have been used intensively for a period and then abandoned or used more sporadically thereafter; and reconstructing the actual, lived pattern of movement for any specific community — how many people lived at a site, for how much of the year, and where the rest of the community might have gone during other seasons — remains difficult even at the best-documented sites. This textbook's position, consistent with the balanced approach to evidence introduced in Chapter 4, is that many Natufian communities, especially in the Early Natufian period and especially within the Mediterranean woodland zone of the central and northern Levant, achieved a substantially greater degree of sedentism than any documented earlier population in the region, without necessarily achieving the fully year-round, uninterrupted, multi-generational village permanence that later Neolithic communities, examined in Chapter 7, would eventually establish.

Historical Vocabulary
Sedentism and Its Gradations

Sedentism describes the degree to which a human community remains settled in one place, or a small set of places, rather than moving frequently across a landscape. Archaeologists working on the Epipaleolithic-to-Neolithic transition generally reject a simple binary distinction between "nomadic" and "sedentary" populations, instead describing a spectrum that includes fully mobile groups, groups practicing seasonal rounds between predictable locations, groups organized around a stable "base camp" supplemented by task-specific outings, and fully sedentary, year-round, multi-generational settlement.

Determining where a specific ancient community falls along this spectrum requires combining multiple, independent lines of evidence — architecture, faunal seasonality, botanical seasonality, cemetery use, and the density and duration of artifact deposition — because no single type of evidence reliably distinguishes, on its own, between a location that was occupied briefly but intensively and one that was occupied continuously over a long period. This methodological caution, introduced in Chapter 4, is essential to interpreting Natufian settlement evidence responsibly.

Section 5.6

Key Sites as Evidence

Rather than cataloging every excavated Natufian site, this section examines a small number of well-documented locations to illustrate how archaeologists build interpretations from specific bodies of evidence, and to demonstrate the regional variation this chapter has repeatedly emphasized. ʿAin Mallaha, also known as Eynan, situated beside a former lake in the Hula Valley of northern Israel, has produced some of the most extensive evidence for Early Natufian architecture anywhere in the Levant: multiple superimposed phases of round, semi-subterranean stone-founded houses, some quite large, along with hearths, storage installations, and an extensive cemetery containing more than one hundred burials accumulated across generations. The site's excavators have argued that the scale of architectural investment, the repeated rebuilding on the same locations, and the size of the associated cemetery together support an interpretation of substantial, long-term settlement, making ʿAin Mallaha one of the most frequently cited examples of Natufian sedentism.

Hayonim Cave and its adjacent open-air terrace, in the western Galilee, present a complementary body of evidence, combining cave deposits with outdoor architectural remains and burials, and documenting occupation across both the Early and Late Natufian periods. The site's long excavation history and detailed stratigraphy, of the kind Chapter 4 described as fundamental to archaeological interpretation, have allowed researchers to track changes in tool technology, subsistence, and mortuary practice across the Natufian sequence at a single location, providing an unusually clear diachronic (time-sequenced) perspective rather than a single snapshot.

Wadi Hammeh 27, located in the Jordan Valley, has yielded evidence of substantial Early Natufian architecture and a rich assemblage of decorated ground-stone objects and other artifacts, including finely worked items that some researchers interpret as evidence of artistic or symbolic activity extending beyond strictly utilitarian tool production. El-Wad, on Mount Carmel, possesses one of the longest and most continuous excavation histories of any Natufian site, beginning with Dorothy Garrod's own fieldwork in the years surrounding her definition of the Natufian horizon, and has provided crucial evidence for ground-stone technology, ornamentation using marine shell (indicating exchange or direct access to the nearby Mediterranean coast), and burial practice across the full span of the Natufian sequence.

Shubayqa 1, in the semi-arid steppe of northeastern Jordan, offers an instructive contrast. Located in an environment considerably drier than the Mediterranean woodland sites discussed above, Shubayqa 1 has nonetheless produced exceptionally well-preserved charred botanical remains, including evidence interpreted by its excavators as bread-like preparations made from wild cereals and club-rush tubers, radiocarbon dated to a period several millennia before the earliest secure evidence for domesticated cereals discussed in Chapter 6. This site demonstrates that intensive plant processing was not confined to the woodland heartland where Natufian architecture is most elaborate, and it illustrates how new excavations continue to reshape scholarly understanding of the geographic and ecological range of Natufian-period plant use. Taken together, these sites should not be read as a single unified story but as a set of regionally specific case studies, each shaped by local environment, each excavated using different methods across different decades, and each contributing a different piece to a still-developing scholarly picture.

Section 5.7

Burial, Ritual, and Attachment to Place

Natufian sites have produced some of the richest and most extensively studied burial evidence from any prehistoric context in Southwest Asia, and this evidence is central to arguments about sedentism, community, and ritual life. Cemeteries at sites such as ʿAin Mallaha, Hayonim, and el-Wad contain dozens to over a hundred individuals, buried over what appears, based on stratigraphy and radiocarbon dating, to have been multiple generations. The existence of a bounded, repeatedly used burial location — rather than isolated, scattered burials — suggests that these communities maintained a durable, intentional relationship with specific places, returning to bury their dead in locations already associated with earlier burials, whether or not the entire community lived at that specific location year-round.

Burial position and treatment varied across sites and individuals: some burials are flexed (with limbs drawn toward the body), some extended; some individuals were buried singly, others in multiple or successive burials within the same location; and grave goods, where present, include perforated shell ornaments, bone pins and pendants, and, at some sites, items suggestive of headdresses or body ornamentation made from dozens of carefully selected and pierced shells. One of the most widely discussed individual burials, from ʿAin Mallaha, involves an elderly individual interred with a young canid, generally interpreted as an early domestic or proto-domestic dog, positioned with a hand resting on the animal — evidence that has been widely cited in discussions of the deep history of human-canine relationships, though the exact nature of that relationship, and the degree of the animal's domestication at this early date, remains a subject of specialist debate rather than settled certainty.

Students should exercise particular caution when interpreting this evidence. The presence of a shared cemetery does not by itself establish the existence of a centralized political authority, a formal religious institution, or a rigid social hierarchy; it establishes that the community engaged in structured, repeated mortuary practice at particular locations, which is itself historically significant without requiring further, unsupported elaboration. Differences in grave goods among individuals — some burials richly ornamented, others without any accompanying objects — have prompted some archaeologists to suggest emerging forms of social differentiation, a possibility examined further in Section 5.8, but such differences can equally reflect age, individual biography, family relationships, ritual role, or historical accident of preservation, and a single burial pattern rarely permits a confident, singular explanation. The most defensible historical conclusion is that Natufian communities in several regions developed formal, place-based mortuary traditions substantial enough to leave a durable, multi-generational archaeological signature — a genuinely significant development in its own right, whatever its precise social meaning.

Section 5.8

Exchange, Ornament, and Social Differentiation

Marine shell ornaments recovered from inland Natufian sites — locations located many tens of kilometers from the Mediterranean coast or the Red Sea — provide direct evidence of exchange networks or, at minimum, long-distance movement of materials and objects, whether through direct travel, down-the-line trade between neighboring communities, or some combination of both. Similarly, certain stone materials used for tools and ornaments at some sites derive from sources located well beyond the immediate vicinity, indicating that Natufian communities were not isolated from one another but participated in wider regional networks of contact, even if the precise mechanisms of that contact — visiting kin, periodic gatherings, structured trade relationships, or something else entirely — cannot be reconstructed with certainty from the material evidence alone.

The presence of these networks, together with the burial variation discussed in Section 5.7, has led some archaeologists to propose that Natufian society, at least in its more settled Early Natufian form, exhibited incipient social differentiation — the beginnings of distinctions in status, wealth, or social role among individuals or families, distinct from the relatively unranked social organization more commonly associated with highly mobile hunter-gatherer bands. Evidence sometimes cited in support of this view includes variation in the elaborateness of burials, possible differences in house size at some sites, and the sheer scale of labor invested in features such as substantial architecture and large communal cemeteries, which some researchers argue would have required a degree of coordinated effort beyond what a purely egalitarian, unranked society might organize.

This textbook presents these arguments as genuine scholarly hypotheses rather than established fact. Other archaeologists caution that variation in burial treatment, ornamentation, or architecture can arise from many causes unrelated to formal social ranking, and that the scale of Natufian communal projects, while impressive relative to earlier Epipaleolithic sites, remains modest compared with the clearly stratified societies examined in later chapters, such as the temple-centered cities of Sumer in Chapter 9. The honest historical position is that Natufian evidence permits — but does not conclusively demonstrate — some degree of social differentiation beyond the least formalized hunter-gatherer societies, and that this question remains an active area of archaeological research rather than a settled conclusion. Students should resist the temptation to read fully formed social classes, formal leadership positions, or centralized authority into evidence that, at most, suggests the beginnings of social distinctions whose exact nature remains genuinely uncertain.

Section 5.9

Climate and the Younger Dryas

Roughly midway through the Natufian sequence, the Northern Hemisphere experienced an abrupt climatic reversal known as the Younger Dryas, beginning approximately thirteen thousand years ago and lasting roughly a thousand years, during which temperatures fell sharply and many regions, including much of the Levant, appear to have experienced increased aridity, following several preceding millennia of generally warmer and wetter conditions associated with the end of the last glacial period. This climatic episode is documented through multiple independent lines of paleoclimatic evidence, including ice cores from Greenland, lake sediment records, and pollen sequences from the eastern Mediterranean region, and its approximate chronological overlap with the transition from the Early to the Late Natufian period has made it one of the most discussed climatic events in the archaeology of Southwest Asian prehistory.

Some influential explanations, developed especially from the 1980s onward, proposed a relatively direct causal chain: that the increased aridity and vegetation stress associated with the Younger Dryas placed pressure on wild cereal stands and other plant resources upon which increasingly settled Late Natufian populations had come to depend, and that this environmental stress in turn incentivized some communities to begin actively cultivating plants they had previously only gathered, providing an important part of the immediate trigger for the domestication process examined in Chapter 6. This explanation remains influential and continues to shape scholarly discussion, but it should not be presented to students as an uncontested, settled account.

Several complications and counter-arguments deserve attention. First, the archaeological evidence for Late Natufian responses to the Younger Dryas is not uniform: some regions show evidence consistent with reduced site permanence, smaller settlements, or a partial return toward greater residential mobility, while other areas, including Shubayqa 1 discussed in Section 5.6, continued to show evidence of intensive plant processing during and after this period. Second, the timing of secure domesticated-plant evidence, examined in Chapter 6, postdates the end of the Younger Dryas by a further interval whose length remains debated, meaning that a simple, immediate "climate crisis caused agriculture" narrative oversimplifies a more gradual and geographically uneven process. Third, historians and archaeologists increasingly caution against environmental-determinist explanations of the kind Chapter 2 warned against in a different context: climatic change did not mechanically force any predetermined outcome, but instead altered the conditions, risks, and opportunities within which human communities, drawing on the technologies, knowledge, and social relationships already discussed in this chapter, made their own historically contingent choices.

The most defensible position, and the one this textbook adopts, is that the Younger Dryas represents an important environmental context for understanding changes in Late Natufian subsistence and settlement, and quite possibly an important part of the broader background against which domestication eventually occurred, without functioning as a sufficient, standalone explanation for either phenomenon. Students should treat the relationship between Natufian sedentism, the Younger Dryas, and the eventual emergence of agriculture as a set of related historical developments requiring careful, evidence-based reconstruction, not as a simple causal chain running neatly from climate to crisis to farming.

Section 5.10

The Sedentism Debate and Review

This chapter closes by returning directly to the question posed in its opening: did increasing sedentism create the conditions that eventually favored intensified food production and, ultimately, domestication, examined fully in Chapter 6? Or did prior changes in how communities gathered, processed, and stored wild resources make greater sedentism possible in the first place, with domestication arriving later for reasons that were only partly related to settlement itself? This textbook does not resolve this question in favor of either position, because the archaeological evidence, honestly assessed, does not currently permit a confident resolution, and presenting a false certainty would violate the standards of evidence-based historical reconstruction established in Chapter 4.

What can be said with confidence is this: across the roughly three and a half millennia of the Natufian period, at least some communities in the Levant, especially within the Mediterranean woodland zone during the Early Natufian, developed patterns of architecture, burial, tool use, and plant and animal exploitation that reflect a substantially more settled way of life than anything documented among earlier Epipaleolithic populations, without those same communities possessing domesticated food crops or herded livestock. The one partial exception, discussed in Section 5.7, is the possible early or proto-domestic status of dogs suggested by burial evidence such as the ʿAin Mallaha human-canid interment — a special case involving a companion species rather than a food-producing plant or animal, and one whose precise domestic status at this early date remains subject to some interpretive qualification among specialists. This finding, established through decades of careful excavation and reanalysis at sites such as ʿAin Mallaha, Hayonim, el-Wad, Wadi Hammeh 27, and Shubayqa 1, fundamentally reshaped twentieth-century understanding of the relationship between settlement and farming, and it stands as one of the most historically important contributions Levantine archaeology has made to the broader study of human prehistory worldwide.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a specific methodological lesson connecting directly back to Chapter 4: nearly every major claim this chapter has made — about settlement permanence, diet, seasonality, burial practice, social differentiation, and the impact of climatic change — depends on the careful integration of multiple, independent lines of evidence rather than any single decisive proof. Architecture alone cannot establish year-round occupation; faunal remains alone cannot establish subsistence strategy; a single burial cannot establish social structure; and a coincidence in timing between a climatic event and a cultural change cannot, by itself, establish causation. This chapter has modeled, at every stage, the kind of cautious, evidence-weighing historical reasoning that this textbook will continue to apply as it turns, in Chapter 6, to the origins of agriculture itself — a process that unfolded across the same Levantine landscapes this chapter has surveyed, building upon, but not simply continuing, the distinctive Natufian way of life examined here.

Primary Source Window

Dorothy Garrod's Definition of the Natufian, Shukbah Cave Excavations and "A New Mesolithic Industry," 1928 and 1932

Creator: Dorothy Garrod, British archaeologist · Date: excavations at Shukbah Cave in 1928; formal publication of "A New Mesolithic Industry: the Natufian of Palestine" in 1932 · Type: Archaeological excavation report and artifact analysis

Context: While excavating Shukbah Cave in Wadi an-Natuf, west of Jerusalem, in 1928, Garrod identified a stratified layer containing a distinctive stone-tool assemblage — including small crescent-shaped microliths, or lunates — that did not match previously known Paleolithic or Neolithic industries in the region. Over the following years she developed and refined this identification, and in a 1932 publication she formally proposed that the assemblage represented a previously unrecognized cultural horizon situated chronologically between the Upper Paleolithic and the Neolithic, naming it "Natufian" after the wadi in which it was first identified.

Paraphrase: Garrod described the Shukbah assemblage as characterized by a distinctive small, crescent-shaped flint industry unlike anything previously catalogued in Palestinian prehistory, associated with ground-stone tools and burials, and she argued in her 1932 publication that its stratigraphic position indicated it represented a genuine, previously unrecognized transitional culture rather than a variant of already-known Paleolithic or Neolithic material.

Analysis: Garrod's identification of the Natufian as a distinct horizon was methodologically significant because it depended on careful stratigraphic observation and systematic artifact typology of the kind Chapter 4 identified as fundamental to archaeological reconstruction, rather than on any written record, which does not exist for this period. Her original interpretation of Natufian society, developed with the more limited excavation methods and comparative evidence available between her 1928 fieldwork and 1932 publication, has since been substantially expanded and in some respects revised by nearly a century of subsequent excavation, radiocarbon dating (a technique unavailable in Garrod's time), and archaeobotanical and zooarchaeological analysis — including, as recently as 2026, new use-wear evidence bearing on the question of early cultivation discussed in Section 5.3. Her foundational contribution was defining the horizon's existence and its basic material signature; understanding its full significance for the history of sedentism and food production, as this chapter has shown, required the cumulative work of many subsequent generations of researchers.

Historical Debate

Did Sedentism Cause Farming, or Farming Cause Sedentism?

The question: Did the increased sedentism documented among Natufian communities create social, demographic, and economic conditions that favored the eventual development of domesticated agriculture, or did earlier and largely separate changes in food-procurement technology and practice make greater sedentism possible, with domestication emerging afterward for its own distinct set of reasons? Archaeologists remain divided, and new evidence continues to complicate both positions.

Interpretation A — Sedentism came first and encouraged food production: On this view, intensified wild-resource use, storage capacity, and the social and demographic pressures created by living in larger, more settled communities — including population growth, more complex social organization, and eventually resource stress intensified by the Younger Dryas — created conditions in which some communities began actively cultivating plants they had previously only gathered, as a means of stabilizing food supply for a population no longer able or willing to relocate as freely as earlier mobile groups had done.
Interpretation B — Changes in food procurement made sedentism possible, and cultivation developed independently: On this view, the intensified plant-processing technologies, storage practices, and broad-spectrum resource use documented among Natufian communities, discussed in Sections 5.3 and 5.4, were themselves the product of earlier changes in knowledge, technology, and environmental opportunity that permitted greater settlement, and cultivation, when it eventually developed, resulted from a partly separate set of causes — including, on some accounts, deliberate human decisions to secure predictable yields, population dynamics, or localized environmental pressures — rather than being a straightforward consequence of prior sedentism.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying Natufian settlement, subsistence, and paleoclimatic evidence surveyed throughout this chapter, but they differ in how they sequence cause and effect, and in how much explanatory weight they assign to sedentism itself as opposed to prior technological and ecological developments.

Why it matters: Resolving, or failing to resolve, this debate shapes how historians understand one of the most consequential transformations in human history, examined fully in Chapter 6. This textbook presents both interpretations as live scholarly positions rather than adjudicating between them, consistent with its broader commitment to distinguishing established evidence from contested interpretation.

Student task: Using the evidence presented across Sections 5.3 through 5.9, construct the strongest possible argument for each interpretation, and then explain what kind of additional evidence might help future archaeologists move closer to resolving this debate.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Natufian Settlement: Perspectives on Community and Subsistence

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many kinds of evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical individual, since no Natufian written record exists.

From the perspective of a resident of a substantially settled Early Natufian community such as ʿAin Mallaha: Daily life is organized around a home structure maintained and periodically rebuilt across a lifetime, seasonal cycles of gazelle hunting and wild-cereal harvesting within reach of the settlement, communal labor invested in food processing and storage, and a shared cemetery connecting the living community to generations of ancestors buried in the same place.
From the perspective of a member of a more mobile Late Natufian group in a drier, marginal environment: Subsistence still depends on detailed knowledge of scattered water sources, seasonal game movement, and the timing of wild-plant availability, and settlement patterns remain flexible in ways that resemble earlier Epipaleolithic mobility more closely than the substantial permanence documented at woodland sites such as ʿAin Mallaha.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist excavating a Natufian site: Interpreting the site requires patiently distinguishing separate architectural phases, analyzing faunal remains for seasonal indicators, submitting charred botanical remains for radiocarbon dating and species identification, and resisting the temptation to read a single, tidy narrative of "the origins of farming" into evidence that, examined carefully, resists easy simplification.
From the perspective of a specialist debating the causes of Late Natufian change: Explaining why some communities appear to have become more mobile again during the Younger Dryas, while others, such as the community at Shubayqa 1, continued intensive plant processing, requires weighing environmental, technological, demographic, and locally specific factors against one another rather than assuming a single climatic cause applies uniformly across the entire Levant.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kebaran Mobility to Natufian Settlement

What Changed

  • Site architecture developed from ephemeral camps toward substantial, repeatedly rebuilt round structures at many Early Natufian locations
  • Ground-stone tool investment increased dramatically, reflecting more intensive processing of wild plant foods
  • Formal, repeatedly used cemeteries appeared at several sites, indicating durable, multi-generational attachment to particular places
  • Some Late Natufian communities, in certain regions, show evidence of reduced settlement permanence compared with the Early Natufian, coinciding with the Younger Dryas

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting, especially of gazelle, remained a central component of subsistence throughout the Natufian period
  • No Natufian community is securely documented as possessing domesticated food crops or herded livestock, although the possible domestic or proto-domestic status of dogs is discussed separately in Section 5.7
  • Considerable regional and local variation in settlement, subsistence, and mortuary practice persisted across the entire span of the period
  • Communities continued to rely on detailed, place-specific ecological knowledge of seasonal resource availability

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change across the Natufian sequence helps students avoid treating the period as either a static "hunter-gatherer" baseline or a fully formed early version of Neolithic village life; it was neither, but a historically specific and internally variable adaptation in its own right.

Cause and Effect

Explaining Increased Sedentism

Long-term causes

Gradual post-glacial environmental amelioration expanded the range and density of wild cereals, nuts, and gazelle populations in the Mediterranean woodland zone, creating conditions in which a broad-spectrum diet could support larger, less mobile groups.

Contributing technological factors

The development of ground-stone processing tools, sickle technology, and storage capacity allowed communities to harvest, process, and preserve wild plant foods at a scale not previously achieved, reducing the necessity of constant relocation.

Effect

At least some Levantine communities, especially in the Early Natufian, built substantial architecture, maintained formal cemeteries, and participated in exchange networks — evidence of a way of life considerably more settled than that of earlier Epipaleolithic populations.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats increased sedentism as arising from an interacting set of environmental, technological, and social factors rather than any single cause, and it declines to assert a settled answer to whether sedentism caused food intensification or resulted from it, given the current state of the evidence.

Historical complexity: The Younger Dryas complicates any simple causal chain, since its effects on settlement and subsistence were not uniform across the Levant, a point developed fully in Section 5.9 and the Historical Debate feature above.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a Natufian Community

For the people who actually lived within Natufian settlements, daily life would have been organized around tasks this chapter's evidence allows historians to partially reconstruct: gathering and processing wild cereals, nuts, and legumes using grinding stones that, at more settled sites, may have remained fixed in one location for use across many years; hunting gazelle and smaller game, likely organized through cooperative strategies whose details remain only partly recoverable from faunal evidence alone; maintaining and periodically rebuilding houses whose stone foundations survived to be excavated millennia later; and participating in the burial of community members at locations that had already received earlier generations of the dead, creating a lived, physical connection between the living and their ancestors within the same settlement.

Because no Natufian community left any written record, historians and archaeologists must reconstruct these everyday experiences entirely from material remains — architecture, tools, food remains, ornaments, and burials — supplemented cautiously by ethnographic comparison with more recent hunter-gatherer and semi-sedentary societies studied by anthropologists, while remaining alert to the real differences between any modern comparative case and the specific, irrecoverable lived experience of Natufian communities themselves. This method, introduced in Chapter 4 and applied throughout this chapter, will remain essential as this textbook turns, in Chapter 6, to the origins of agriculture and, in Chapter 7, to the first large village communities that eventually replaced the Natufian way of life across much of the Levant.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Natufian archaeological horizon of the Late Epipaleolithic Levant, dated to approximately fifteen thousand to eleven and a half thousand years ago, as a case study in reconstructing a genuinely transitional period in human history without projecting later developments backward onto it. Section 5.1 situated the Natufian within its geographic and chronological context, emphasizing that "Natufian" is a modern archaeological label describing a recurring pattern of material remains rather than a known ethnic or political identity. Section 5.2 defined the horizon through its characteristic microlithic and ground-stone tool technologies, distinctive architecture, and associated material culture, while noting important internal variation between the Early and Late Natufian phases.

Sections 5.3 and 5.4 examined Natufian subsistence in detail, establishing that communities intensively processed wild plant foods, including wild cereals, using ground-stone equipment and sickle technology, while continuing to rely heavily on hunting, particularly of gazelle, alongside fishing, fowling, and gathering across a genuinely diversified broad-spectrum economy. Crucially, this chapter has repeatedly distinguished intensive wild-resource exploitation from domesticated agriculture, a distinction Chapter 6 will develop fully.

Section 5.5 addressed this chapter's central historical problem: the evidence for increased sedentism among Natufian communities, drawn from substantial architecture, faunal seasonality studies, and cemetery use, while cautioning that sedentism exists along a spectrum and that considerable regional variation complicates any uniform generalization about "Natufian settlement." Section 5.6 examined specific, well-documented sites — ʿAin Mallaha, Hayonim, Wadi Hammeh 27, el-Wad, and Shubayqa 1 — as case studies illustrating both shared patterns and significant regional difference.

Sections 5.7 and 5.8 examined burial practice, ritual, exchange, and possible social differentiation, presenting the evidence for formal, place-based cemeteries and long-distance exchange networks while cautioning against overinterpreting this evidence as proof of centralized political institutions or rigid social classes. Section 5.9 introduced the Younger Dryas climatic event and evaluated, with appropriate caution, its proposed relationship to changes in Late Natufian settlement and subsistence, rejecting any simple deterministic account of climate directly causing cultural change.

Section 5.10 returned to this chapter's central debate — whether sedentism encouraged intensified food production or resulted from prior changes that made such production possible — and explained why this textbook treats the question as genuinely open rather than resolved, consistent with the current state of archaeological evidence and ongoing scholarly disagreement.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled the evidentiary caution introduced in Chapter 4: virtually every conclusion about Natufian life depends on the careful integration of architectural, faunal, botanical, mortuary, and paleoclimatic evidence, and no single line of evidence is sufficient on its own. The Natufians were not proto-farmers or an incomplete version of the Neolithic societies examined in Chapter 6; they were communities who developed distinctive, historically specific solutions to the challenges and opportunities of their own environment, leaving behind a material record whose careful interpretation has fundamentally reshaped modern understanding of how sedentism, food intensification, and eventually farming relate to one another.

Chapter 6 builds directly on this foundation, examining how, across the following millennia and largely after the end of the Younger Dryas, communities across the wider Fertile Crescent began the process of plant and animal domestication — a process shaped by, but analytically distinct from, the Natufian achievements this chapter has surveyed.

Archaeological culture
A modern scholarly label describing a recurring pattern of material remains across sites, used for organizing evidence rather than establishing ancient ethnic or political identity.
Natufian
The archaeological horizon of the Late Epipaleolithic Levant, dated to approximately 15,000–11,500 years ago, named after Wadi an-Natuf; first identified by Dorothy Garrod during excavations at Shukbah Cave in 1928 and formally defined and named by her in a 1932 publication.
Microlith
A small, carefully shaped stone tool, often hafted in combination with others to form composite cutting or projectile edges, characteristic of Epipaleolithic technology.
Lunate
A crescent-shaped microlith form considered diagnostic of Natufian stone-tool assemblages.
Ground-stone tool
A stone tool, such as a mortar, pestle, or grinding slab, shaped and used through grinding and pounding rather than flaking, associated with intensified plant processing.
Mortar and pestle
Paired ground-stone implements used for pounding and processing plant foods such as cereals and nuts, found in quantity at many Natufian sites.
Sickle blade
A small stone blade hafted to form a composite cutting tool used for harvesting cereals and other grasses.
Sickle sheen
A distinctive polish or silica gloss on stone tool edges produced by repeated contact with siliceous plant stems during harvesting, used as evidence of cereal processing.
Broad-spectrum economy
A subsistence strategy drawing on a wide range of plant and animal resources rather than specializing narrowly, characteristic of Natufian communities.
Sedentism
The degree to which a community remains settled in one place or a small set of places, understood as a spectrum rather than a simple binary condition.
Seasonal aggregation
A settlement pattern in which groups gather at a particular location for part of the year and disperse for the remainder.
Base camp
A primary residential location occupied for much of the year, supplemented by smaller task-specific outings, representing a middle point on the mobility spectrum.
Semi-subterranean architecture
Building construction partly excavated into the ground, characteristic of many Natufian round structures.
Cemetery
A bounded location used repeatedly for burial across multiple individuals and, at several Natufian sites, multiple generations.
Grave goods
Objects, such as ornaments or tools, placed with a burial, whose presence or absence has been used, with appropriate caution, to discuss possible social differentiation.
Personal ornament
Items such as perforated shell beads, bone pins, or pendants worn on the body, providing evidence of both craft production and exchange networks.
Exchange network
A system of contact, whether direct or indirect, through which materials and objects, such as marine shell, moved across considerable distances between communities.
Social differentiation
Distinctions in status, wealth, or social role among individuals or families, a subject of ongoing debate for Natufian society rather than an established fact.
Younger Dryas
A sharp climatic cooling and drying episode affecting much of the Northern Hemisphere beginning roughly 13,000 years ago, coinciding with changes documented among Late Natufian communities.
Archaeobotany
The study of plant remains recovered from archaeological sites, essential to reconstructing Natufian diet and plant use.
Zooarchaeology
The study of animal remains recovered from archaeological sites, essential to reconstructing Natufian subsistence and seasonality.
Taphonomy
The study of processes affecting organic remains after death and burial, relevant to interpreting the preservation and condition of Natufian faunal and botanical evidence.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific dating technique measuring the decay of carbon-14 in organic remains, essential to establishing Natufian chronology and unavailable to early researchers such as Dorothy Garrod.
Kebaran
An earlier Epipaleolithic industry preceding the Natufian, associated with smaller, more mobile groups and less elaborate architecture.
Domestication
A set of biological and behavioral changes in plants or animals produced through generations of human selection, distinct from intensive wild-resource gathering and examined fully in Chapter 6.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a period of human history that left behind no names, no words, and no written testimony, yet whose material remains have transformed modern understanding of one of the most consequential transitions in the human past. The habits of mind this chapter has modeled — distinguishing intensive gathering from domestication, situating sedentism along a spectrum rather than a binary, weighing multiple independent lines of evidence against one another, and resisting the temptation to read a tidy, inevitable story into a genuinely uncertain and regionally varied record — are not specific to Natufian archaeology alone. They are the same habits of careful, evidence-based reasoning that this textbook will continue to apply across every subsequent period, region, and society it examines.

The Natufian record also carries a broader lesson about historical explanation itself. For much of the twentieth century, historians and archaeologists assumed that settled village life was simply agriculture's natural consequence. The Natufian evidence overturned that assumption by demonstrating that substantial settlement, intensive food processing, formal cemeteries, and long-distance exchange could all develop among societies that had not yet domesticated any food crop or herded livestock species, even as debate continues, especially following the 2026 sickle-blade evidence discussed in Section 5.3, over how early some form of cereal cultivation itself may have begun. This finding should encourage a broader historical humility: major transformations in human society do not always unfold in the order, or for the reasons, that later observers might assume from hindsight.

Understanding the Natufian period on its own terms — as a genuine, historically specific way of life shaped by particular environments, technologies, and social relationships, rather than as an incomplete preview of the Neolithic Revolution examined in Chapter 6 — also models a form of historical respect that this textbook will extend to every society it studies, however far removed from later, more familiar historical actors. The Natufians were not failing to become farmers. They were living full human lives, solving the problems particular to their own time and place, within landscapes this textbook's readers have already encountered in Chapter 2 and will encounter again and again across the volumes ahead.

Chapter 6 builds directly on the foundation this chapter has established, turning to the process by which, across the wider Fertile Crescent and in the millennia following the period surveyed here, human communities began to domesticate plants and animals — transforming, gradually and unevenly, the broad-spectrum, intensively harvested wild economy examined in this chapter into the agricultural economies that would eventually support the first villages, the first cities, and the first states examined throughout the remainder of this nine-volume history.
