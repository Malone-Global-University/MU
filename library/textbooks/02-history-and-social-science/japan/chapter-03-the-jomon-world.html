Part I — The Archipelago Before the Japanese State
3

The Jōmon World

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

For more than ten thousand years — longer than the span separating the present day from the first cities of Mesopotamia — communities living across the Japanese archipelago made and used pottery, built substantial settlements, harvested the sea and forest with remarkable skill, and produced some of the most technically accomplished hunter-gatherer material culture known anywhere in the world, for most of that long sequence without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture. Historians and archaeologists call this long, regionally varied world the Jōmon world, and it is the direct namesake of the "Jōmon Japan" announced in this textbook's title.

Consider a household on the Tōhoku coast roughly five thousand years ago, at a settlement archaeologists now call Sannai Maruyama. Its residents live in a large, long-occupied village of pit dwellings clustered along a low rise above a bay, with refuse heaps, burial areas, raised-floor storage structures, and a monumental arrangement of enormous chestnut-wood posts at its center. They eat chestnuts from managed groves nearby, salmon and other fish taken from the coast and rivers, deer and wild boar from the surrounding forest, and food stored in deep pits against the winter. They make and decorate pottery in styles shared, with local variation, across a wide swath of northeastern Japan, and they possess jade ornaments, obsidian tools, and asphalt-sealed containers made from materials that traveled, by exchange rather than direct extraction, from sources hundreds of kilometers away. They are not "primitive" by any honest historical standard; they are the makers of a sophisticated, long-lived, and regionally distinct way of life fully adapted to a rich temperate and subarctic environment.

This chapter and Chapter 4 together examine that Jōmon world in full. This chapter establishes the foundations: chronology, pottery, settlement, subsistence, exchange, population, and the evidentiary methods archaeologists use to reconstruct a period with no writing of its own. Chapter 4 turns more directly to ritual, material culture, social organization, and the interpretive debates surrounding Jōmon belief and society. The division is a matter of instructional convenience rather than a claim that technology and belief can be neatly separated in Jōmon life itself; readers should expect the two chapters to refer back and forth to one another.

It is essential, as with the Paleolithic material examined in Chapter 2, to avoid two opposite errors. The first error is to imagine "the Jōmon people" as a single, unified population sharing one culture, one language, and one way of life across the entire archipelago for ten thousand years. In fact, the term "Jōmon" describes a broad and long-lived pattern — pottery-using, generally non-agricultural, often semi-sedentary forager societies — that took strikingly different regional forms in Hokkaido, northeastern Honshu, the Kantō region, western Japan, and the Ryukyu Islands, and that changed considerably over its own many centuries. The second error is the opposite one: to treat "Jōmon" merely as a technical archaeological label with no connection to later Japanese history. Jōmon communities are not simply a prelude to "real" Japanese history beginning with the Yayoi period examined in Chapter 5; they represent one of the longest-lived and most successful non-agricultural cultural traditions documented anywhere in the archaeological record, and their genetic, cultural, and, in some interpretations, linguistic legacies continued to shape later populations of the archipelago, including but not limited to the Ainu of Hokkaido, in ways that remain the subject of active scholarly research.

This chapter also continues the methodological themes introduced in Chapters 1 and 2. Almost everything known about Jōmon life comes from archaeology — pottery typology, radiocarbon dating, faunal and botanical analysis, settlement excavation, skeletal analysis, and, increasingly, ancient DNA research — rather than from any contemporary written record, since writing does not appear in the archipelago for many centuries after the Jōmon world had already ended in most regions. Historians must therefore be especially careful to distinguish secure archaeological inference from popular assumption, particularly regarding subjects — social organization, gender roles, religious belief, and political authority — for which the evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold in mind a question that will recur throughout the book: what should count as a meaningful marker of civilization, complexity, or historical significance? Jōmon communities lacked writing and metal tools, depended for most of their long sequence on hunting, fishing, and gathering rather than full-scale cereal agriculture, and lacked, for most of the period, large-scale political hierarchy, yet they built substantial permanent settlements, produced technically sophisticated craft goods, participated in exchange networks spanning much of the archipelago, and, in some regions, sustained village life for a thousand years or more at a single location. Studying the Jōmon world carefully complicates any simple, linear story in which "civilization" begins only with farming, cities, or writing, and it prepares students for a more nuanced understanding of the transformations examined in Chapter 5's treatment of the Yayoi transition.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the term "Jōmon" and explain both its usefulness and its limitations as a label for over ten thousand years of regionally diverse history.
  2. Trace the conventional chronological subdivisions of the Jōmon sequence and explain why these subdivisions vary by region.
  3. Describe the development, technology, and stylistic diversity of Jōmon pottery, and explain its significance as some of the world's earliest ceramic technology.
  4. Explain Jōmon settlement patterns, including pit dwellings, village layout, and the debate over sedentism and semi-sedentism.
  5. Analyze Jōmon subsistence strategies, including hunting, fishing, gathering, and the management of plant resources such as chestnut groves.
  6. Assess the evidence for long-distance exchange networks connecting different Jōmon regions and, in some cases, the wider East Asian mainland.
  7. Evaluate what skeletal, dietary, and demographic evidence can and cannot reveal about Jōmon population, health, and daily life.
  8. Compare regional Jōmon traditions, using Sannai Maruyama and other major sites as case studies.
  9. Identify the archaeological methods — pottery typology, radiocarbon dating, faunal and botanical analysis, and skeletal and genetic analysis — used to reconstruct Jōmon life.
  10. Evaluate the scholarly debate over whether Jōmon societies should be classified as "complex hunter-gatherers" and what is at stake in that classification.
  11. Avoid treating Jōmon communities as a single, uniform, or "primitive" population, or as merely a prelude to later Japanese history.
  12. Contextualize the Jōmon world within global comparative discussions of pottery-using, non-agricultural societies.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does the archaeological evidence actually show about how Jōmon communities lived, and how confident can historians be about different aspects of that evidence?
  2. Why did Jōmon communities develop pottery, semi-permanent settlement, and complex exchange networks for most of their history without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture?
  3. How and why did Jōmon ways of life vary across different regions of the archipelago?
  4. What can Jōmon settlement sites such as Sannai Maruyama reveal about the scale and organization of non-agricultural village life?
  5. How did Jōmon communities manage and modify their environment, and how does this complicate a simple hunter-gatherer/farmer distinction?
  6. What does the evidence of exchange in obsidian, jade, asphalt, and other materials suggest about connections between different Jōmon communities?
  7. Why is the classification of Jōmon societies as "complex hunter-gatherers" scholarly useful, and why is it also debated?
  8. Why should historians avoid describing Jōmon communities as ancestors of a single, unified "Japanese people"?

Historical Context

Chapter 2 traced the Japanese Paleolithic from the earliest confirmed human presence in the archipelago, around 38,000 years ago, through the gradual technological transition marked by the appearance of some of the world's earliest pottery, associated with what many archaeologists call the "Incipient Jōmon" phase, beginning roughly 16,000 to 15,500 years ago within the Late Pleistocene. This chapter picks up that story as the broader environmental transformation from the Pleistocene to the Holocene — retreating glacial conditions, rising sea levels, warming temperatures, and expanding forest cover, as described in Chapters 1 and 2 — created the environmental conditions within which the fuller, developed Jōmon cultural pattern took shape.

Students should understand the relationship between "Incipient Jōmon" and the developed Jōmon world examined in this chapter as continuous rather than as a sharp break. The earliest pottery-bearing communities of the Late Pleistocene already shared important technological ground with later Jōmon populations, even though their stone-tool traditions, settlement patterns, and subsistence strategies still closely resembled the preceding Paleolithic in important respects. The "Initial Jōmon" phase, beginning within the early Holocene, is generally understood by archaeologists as the point at which fuller sedentary or semi-sedentary village life, more standardized and widespread pottery production, and more clearly Jōmon-style stone and bone tool assemblages become well established across much of the archipelago.

Because the Jōmon period spans, on the most inclusive chronological schemes, well over ten thousand years — considerably longer than the entire span of recorded Japanese political history examined in the rest of this textbook — this chapter cannot describe Jōmon life as though it were a single, static condition. Chronological subdivisions, discussed in Section 3.2, are essential tools for organizing this vast span, even though archaeologists disagree about precise transition dates and regional patterns.

Section 3.1

Naming a World: What "Jōmon" Means and Does Not Mean

The word "Jōmon" (縄文) means "cord-marked" or "rope-patterned," and refers originally to a decorative technique in which cords were pressed or rolled into wet clay before firing, leaving distinctive impressed patterns on the finished pottery. Edward S. Morse's 1877 excavation of the Ōmori shell mound, discussed further in Section 3.9, described this pottery in his 1879 report using the English term "cord-marked," and the Japanese term jōmon subsequently developed from that description as the accepted label for the pottery style; archaeologists later extended jōmon from a description of a pottery style to a label for the entire cultural period and set of societies associated with that pottery tradition, spanning roughly the beginning of the Holocene (and, on inclusive definitions, extending back into the Late Pleistocene "Incipient" phase discussed in Chapter 2) to the gradual, regionally uneven adoption of Yayoi-style agriculture, beginning in northern Kyushu by around the tenth century BCE and spreading, at markedly different rates and dates, across much of the rest of the archipelago over the following centuries.

This extension of meaning — from a pottery decoration technique to an entire cultural-historical period spanning thousands of years and enormous regional diversity — is useful for organizing archaeological evidence, but it carries real risks if used carelessly. "Jōmon" does not name a single ethnic group, a single language community, or a single unified culture. It names a broad pattern: societies across the archipelago that made and used cord-marked and related pottery styles, generally did not practice full-scale cereal agriculture (with some late and regionally limited exceptions discussed in Section 3.5), and organized their subsistence around hunting, fishing, gathering, and, in many cases, active management of wild plant resources. Within that broad pattern, Jōmon communities in Hokkaido, northeastern Honshu, the Kantō Plain, western Honshu, and the Ryukyu Islands developed distinctive regional pottery styles, settlement patterns, tool traditions, and, quite likely, languages and social customs that differed substantially from one another.

It is equally important not to treat the Jōmon world as a static "prehistoric" backdrop that remained essentially unchanged for ten thousand years before the "real" history of the Yayoi period began. As the chronology in Section 3.2 shows, Jōmon societies changed considerably over the course of their long history — in population, settlement scale, technology, exchange, and, in some interpretations, social organization — and experienced real transformation, expansion, and, in some periods and regions, contraction and crisis. Students should approach the Jōmon world as a genuine, dynamic, and internally diverse subject of historical inquiry, not as an undifferentiated prologue.

Section 3.2

Chronology and Regional Diversity of the Jōmon Sequence

Archaeologists conventionally divide the Jōmon period into several sub-phases — commonly Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final Jōmon — based primarily on changes in pottery style, supplemented by settlement, tool, and environmental evidence. The exact calendar dates assigned to each phase vary somewhat between scholars and regions, and students should treat the date ranges given in the Chronology Preview above as reasonable, widely used approximations rather than as fixed, universally agreed figures. The transition from one phase to the next was gradual and did not occur simultaneously across the entire archipelago; a pottery style associated with the "Middle Jōmon" in northeastern Honshu might persist somewhat later, or arrive somewhat earlier, in other regions.

The Incipient Jōmon phase, discussed in Chapter 2, begins within the Late Pleistocene with the appearance of some of the world's earliest pottery, roughly 16,000 to 15,500 years ago, alongside stone-tool traditions that still closely resemble the preceding Paleolithic. The Initial Jōmon phase, beginning in the early Holocene, is generally associated with more standardized and widespread pottery production, the appearance of more clearly Jōmon-style stone and bone tools, and the beginnings of the shell-mound (kaizuka) record described in Section 3.9's vocabulary feature.

The Early and Middle Jōmon phases, spanning roughly the mid-Holocene warm period discussed further in the Cause and Effect feature below, saw substantial population growth and settlement expansion in many regions, particularly in central and northeastern Honshu, where large, long-occupied villages such as Sannai Maruyama (Section 3.8) developed. This period is often treated by archaeologists as something close to a demographic and, in some interpretations, cultural high point of the Jōmon world, though this generalization applies unevenly across different regions; western Japan, for instance, generally supported lower Jōmon population densities than the resource-rich temperate and cool-temperate forest zones of central and northeastern Honshu.

The Late and Final Jōmon phases coincide with a period of climatic cooling following the mid-Holocene warm peak. In several regions, especially northeastern Honshu, this cooling appears to correspond with population contraction and the abandonment or reduction of some large settlements, even as other developments — including elaborate ritual monuments such as large stone circles, discussed further in Chapter 4 — became more prominent in the archaeological record. By around the tenth century BCE, under the revised AMS radiocarbon chronology now widely used by Japanese archaeologists (older conventional chronologies placed the transition several centuries later, a debate examined further in Chapter 5), evidence for wet-field rice cultivation and other continental-derived agricultural practices appears at sites in northern Kyushu, marking the local beginning of the Yayoi cultural pattern examined fully in Chapter 5, even while communities in central and eastern Honshu continued to live according to Late and Final Jōmon patterns for centuries afterward. The Initial/Early/Middle/Late/Final phase sequence outlined above should therefore be understood as an approximate ceramic chronology developed mainly from eastern and northeastern Japanese evidence, not as a single nationwide timetable; "Final Jōmon" in eastern Japan was, in real calendar time, contemporary with "Early Yayoi" in northern Kyushu.

Students should note that this transition did not occur simultaneously or completely across the archipelago. While Yayoi-style agriculture and material culture spread relatively quickly through much of Kyushu, Shikoku, and Honshu over the following centuries, Hokkaido followed a substantially different trajectory, developing its own distinct post-Jōmon cultural sequence (examined further in later chapters) that did not adopt wet-rice agriculture on the Honshu model, while the Ryukyu Islands followed yet another distinct regional trajectory, discussed further in Chapter 32. This regional unevenness is a recurring theme of this textbook's treatment of the archipelago's deep history.

Section 3.3

Pottery: Technology, Style, and Regional Variation

Jōmon pottery is, alongside stone tools, the primary basis on which archaeologists date and classify sites, and it is also a remarkable technical and artistic achievement in its own right. Jōmon potters built vessels by hand, generally using coiling techniques rather than a potter's wheel (which does not appear in the archipelago until much later), and fired them at relatively low temperatures, generally below about 800–900°C, using open bonfire firing; this differed from the covered open-firing methods characteristic of much Yayoi pottery, discussed in Chapter 5, and from the permanent enclosed kilns introduced in still later East Asian ceramic traditions. Despite these comparatively simple technical means, Jōmon pottery displays an extraordinary range of decorative techniques and regional styles across its long history, from the simple, often undecorated or lightly cord-marked vessels of the Incipient and Initial phases to the elaborate, sculptural, and sometimes flame-like rim decoration associated with certain Middle Jōmon styles in central and northeastern Honshu, most famously the dramatic "flame-style" (kaen-shiki) vessels of the Shinano River region.

Pottery served practical functions central to Jōmon subsistence: cooking, particularly the boiling and processing of foods that would otherwise be difficult to prepare, storage of foods and other materials, and, in some cases, ritual or mortuary use, discussed further in Chapter 4. The ability to boil food efficiently is thought by many archaeologists to have expanded the range of plant and animal foods Jōmon communities could exploit, including foods that required prolonged cooking to be edible or nutritionally useful, complementing the subsistence strategies examined in Section 3.5.

Regional pottery styles varied considerably, and archaeologists use these stylistic differences, cross-referenced with stratigraphy and radiocarbon dating, to construct detailed relative chronologies and to trace contact and exchange between different regions when pottery of one region's style is found at a site in another region. This method of using pottery typology as a chronological and geographical tool closely parallels the stone-tool typology introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of Paleolithic archaeology, illustrating a broader continuity in archaeological method even as the material evidence itself changes across the Paleolithic-to-Jōmon transition.

Section 3.4

Settlements, Pit Dwellings, and the Question of Sedentism

One of the most historically significant features of the Jōmon world is the appearance of substantial, often long-occupied settlements without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture for most of the sequence. The characteristic Jōmon dwelling was the pit dwelling (tateana jūkyo), a semi-subterranean structure built by digging a shallow pit, erecting a wooden post frame, and covering the frame with thatch, bark, or earth. Pit dwellings provided effective insulation against both summer heat and winter cold and appear, with regional and chronological variation in size and construction detail, at Jōmon sites across the archipelago.

Jōmon settlements ranged enormously in scale and duration, from small, seasonally used camps to large villages occupied, with some interruption, for centuries or, in exceptional cases such as Sannai Maruyama (Section 3.8), well over a thousand years. This range complicates any simple binary distinction between "mobile hunter-gatherers" and "sedentary farmers." Many Jōmon communities, particularly in resource-rich coastal and riverine environments in central and northeastern Honshu, appear to have practiced substantial sedentism or semi-sedentism — remaining at a single settlement for much or all of the year, across many years — while relying on hunting, fishing, and gathering rather than agriculture for their subsistence base. This pattern, sometimes described by archaeologists internationally as characteristic of "complex hunter-gatherers," is examined further in the Historical Debate feature below.

Settlement layout at larger Jōmon sites often shows evidence of planning and long-term spatial organization: dwellings arranged around open central areas, separate zones for refuse disposal (including the shell mounds discussed in Section 3.9), distinct areas associated with burial, and, at some sites, structures interpreted as having ritual or communal rather than purely domestic functions. This organization suggests that at least some Jōmon communities maintained stable, intergenerational settlement patterns and shared spatial conventions passed down over long periods, even in the absence of the political and administrative institutions associated with later, agriculturally based states.

Section 3.5

Subsistence: Hunting, Fishing, Gathering, and Managed Landscapes

Jōmon subsistence rested on a broad-spectrum strategy that combined hunting, fishing, shellfish gathering, and the collection and, increasingly, active management of wild plant resources. Deer and wild boar were the most important hunted mammals across much of the archipelago, taken with bows and arrows tipped with stone points, as well as, in some contexts, pit traps and other techniques. Coastal and riverine communities relied heavily on fish — including salmon, particularly important in northeastern Honshu and Hokkaido — along with shellfish, whose remains, discarded over generations, formed the shell mounds that provide much of the archaeological evidence for Jōmon diet and daily life.

Plant foods were central to Jōmon subsistence and, in some inland or forest-rich communities, probably supplied a large share of dietary energy, although their importance relative to marine and terrestrial animal foods varied considerably by region, locality, and season, as stable-isotope and residue studies discussed in Section 3.7 indicate. Nuts — including chestnut, walnut, horse chestnut, and acorn — required processing to remove tannins and other compounds before they could be eaten safely and in quantity, and archaeological evidence, including specialized processing tools and, at waterlogged sites such as Torihama, actual preserved plant remains, indicates that Jōmon communities developed effective techniques for leaching, roasting, and storing nuts on a substantial scale.

A particularly important body of evidence, especially clear at Sannai Maruyama (Section 3.8), indicates that some Jōmon communities actively managed, and in some interpretations effectively cultivated, chestnut trees and certain other plant resources, selecting for desirable traits and maintaining groves near settlements over many generations. Some archaeologists describe this practice as a form of low-intensity plant management or incipient cultivation that blurs the conventional line between "foraging" and "farming," though most researchers stop short of describing it as full agriculture in the sense associated with the Yayoi period's wet-rice cultivation, since Jōmon communities did not depend on a small number of intensively cultivated staple crops in the way later agricultural societies did. Toward the very end of the Jōmon sequence, limited evidence for rice, foxtail millet, and other cultivated plants appears at some western Japanese sites, foreshadowing, but not yet constituting, the fuller agricultural transformation examined in Chapter 5.

This combination of hunting, fishing, gathering, and plant management allowed Jōmon communities to sustain substantial, often long-term settlements without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture for most of the sequence, a pattern that depended heavily on the exceptional biological productivity of the archipelago's temperate and cool-temperate forest and coastal environments, discussed in Chapter 1, and one that would have been considerably more difficult to sustain in a less resource-rich environment.

Section 3.6

Exchange Networks and Long-Distance Contact

Archaeological evidence indicates that Jōmon communities participated in exchange networks moving certain valued materials over considerable distances, complicating any picture of Jōmon life as isolated or confined to a single local territory. Obsidian, a volcanic glass prized for its ability to be flaked into extremely sharp cutting edges, was quarried at a limited number of geologically distinctive sources — including sites on the Izu Peninsula, in Nagano Prefecture, and on Hokkaido — and, through chemical sourcing techniques that identify a given obsidian artifact's geological origin, has been found at sites many tens or even hundreds of kilometers from its source, indicating either direct long-distance travel or, more likely in many cases, exchange relationships passing material from community to community.

Jade (hisui), particularly jadeite jade sourced from the Itoigawa region on the Sea of Japan coast of central Honshu — among the world's oldest known exploited sources of jadeite, worked from at least 6,500 years ago — was worked into ornaments that have been found at Jōmon sites across a wide area of the archipelago, again indicating exchange networks connecting distant communities. Natural asphalt, used as an adhesive for hafting stone tools and for other purposes, was likewise moved from a limited number of natural seep sources to more distant sites of use. Salt, produced through the evaporation of seawater at some coastal sites, may also have moved through similar exchange relationships to inland communities, though the archaeological evidence for salt exchange is less direct than for durable materials such as obsidian and jade.

Some archaeologists and geneticists have also proposed connections, at various points across the long Jōmon sequence, between Jōmon populations and communities on the Asian mainland and in the Russian Far East, based on similarities in certain pottery and tool traditions and on genetic evidence discussed further in Section 3.7. These connections should be understood as evidence of periodic contact and exchange across the straits discussed in Chapters 1 and 2, rather than as evidence that Jōmon Japan was simply an extension of a mainland culture; the archipelago's Jōmon tradition developed its own distinctive regional character even as it maintained these external connections.

Section 3.7

Population, Health, and the Rhythms of Jōmon Life

Estimating Jōmon population is necessarily an inexact undertaking, based on the number and estimated size of known settlements, extrapolated across regions and periods with very uneven archaeological investigation. Widely cited estimates suggest that Jōmon population in the archipelago rose from a relatively small base in the Initial Jōmon phase to a peak, plausibly in the range of a few hundred thousand people, during the Middle Jōmon climatic optimum, before contracting somewhat during the cooler Late and Final Jōmon phases. Students should treat any specific numerical estimate as a rough order-of-magnitude figure rather than a precise census, since the underlying data — site counts, estimated dwelling occupancy, and assumptions about how many sites remain undiscovered or destroyed — carry substantial uncertainty.

Skeletal remains recovered from Jōmon burial contexts, particularly shell-mound sites where bone preservation is comparatively good, provide direct evidence about health, diet, and physical activity. Studies of Jōmon skeletal material indicate a population with generally robust bone structure consistent with an active, physically demanding way of life, evidence of dental wear patterns consistent with a diet including substantial plant fiber and shellfish, and, at some sites, evidence of deliberate tooth ablation (the removal of certain teeth, likely for ritual or social reasons) discussed further in Chapter 4's treatment of Jōmon ritual and social practice. Isotopic analysis of bone collagen, which reflects long-term dietary intake, indicates considerable regional and individual variation in the relative importance of marine versus terrestrial food sources, consistent with the archipelago's varied coastal and inland environments described in Chapter 1.

Ancient DNA research, a comparatively recent and rapidly developing field, has begun to clarify the genetic relationship between Jōmon populations and later populations of the archipelago, including modern Japanese, Ainu, and Ryukyuan populations. Current genetic evidence indicates that Jōmon ancestry forms one significant component of the ancestry of later populations across the archipelago, generally in combination with substantial genetic contributions associated with Yayoi-period and later migration from the Asian mainland, discussed further in Chapter 5; the Ainu of Hokkaido appear, on current evidence, to retain a comparatively higher proportion of Jōmon-derived ancestry than populations in central and southern Honshu, though this remains an active area of ongoing genetic research rather than a fully settled question, and students should be cautious about treating any current genetic finding as a final or complete account of these complex population histories.

Section 3.8

Regional Worlds: Sannai Maruyama and Beyond

Sannai Maruyama, in present-day Aomori Prefecture in northeastern Honshu, is among the most extensively excavated and historically significant Jōmon sites, occupied across much of the Early and Middle Jōmon phases, roughly 5,900 to 4,200 years ago, for a span of about 1,700 years. Excavations beginning in the 1990s, prompted initially by construction work for a baseball stadium that was subsequently relocated to preserve the site, revealed a settlement of remarkable scale: numerous pit dwellings, large raised-floor structures interpreted as storage buildings or communal structures, extensive refuse areas containing enormous quantities of pottery, tools, and food remains, formal burial areas, and a set of six massive postholes, roughly two meters in diameter and arranged in two parallel rows, that once held enormous chestnut-wood posts as part of a monumental structure whose precise function — communal, ceremonial, astronomical, or some combination — remains debated among archaeologists.

Evidence from Sannai Maruyama indicates a subsistence base drawing on a wide range of resources: managed chestnut groves supplying a dietary staple, salmon and other fish from nearby rivers and the coast, and further evidence, discussed in Section 3.6, of participation in long-distance exchange networks bringing obsidian, jade, and asphalt to the settlement from distant sources. Pollen records, preserved wood and nut remains, increases in nut size over time, and some genetic studies have all been interpreted as evidence of active chestnut management or a form of pre-domestication cultivation at the site, supporting the broader argument, discussed in Section 3.5, that some Jōmon communities practiced a form of plant management that blurs any sharp foraging/farming distinction. Students should note that the genetic evidence in particular remains limited: at least one modern genetic study has found no direct genetic link connecting excavated Sannai Maruyama chestnut remains to modern cultivated varieties, so the extent of intentional selection or true domestication at the site remains an open and actively debated question rather than a settled conclusion.

Sannai Maruyama's scale and duration have made it central to scholarly debates about Jōmon social complexity, discussed further in the Historical Debate feature below, since a settlement of this size and longevity, if it maintained anything like a stable resident population over its full occupation span, would represent a substantially larger and more socially organized community than the small, mobile bands sometimes assumed to characterize hunter-gatherer societies generally. At the same time, archaeologists caution against assuming that Sannai Maruyama was continuously and fully occupied at maximum scale throughout its roughly 1,700-year history; like many long-lived sites, its population and built environment likely expanded and contracted considerably across different sub-phases of its occupation.

Other regions of the archipelago developed their own distinct Jōmon traditions during the same broad period. Western Japan generally supported lower population densities and somewhat different subsistence emphases than resource-rich northeastern Honshu. The Ryukyu Islands developed a broadly related but regionally distinct shell-midden tradition, discussed further in Chapter 32, that continued, in modified form, well after Jōmon lifeways had given way to Yayoi agriculture in most of Kyushu, Honshu, and Shikoku. Hokkaido likewise developed regionally distinct Jōmon traditions that, as noted in Section 3.2, did not transition directly into Yayoi-style agriculture but instead developed their own subsequent cultural sequence, examined further in later chapters treating Ainu history.

Section 3.9

Evidence, Excavation, and the Limits of Knowledge

The Jōmon archaeological record is unusually rich by the standards of most non-agricultural, non-literate societies studied anywhere in the world, owing to the sheer number of excavated sites, the durability of pottery and stone tools, and, at certain waterlogged or shell-rich sites, exceptional preservation of organic material that would otherwise decay. Shell mounds (kaizuka) are especially valuable, since the alkaline environment created by large accumulations of shell helps preserve bone, including human skeletal remains, animal bone, and, at some sites, wooden and other organic artifacts that provide direct evidence for diet, health, and material culture rarely available for the acidic-soil sites discussed in Chapter 2's treatment of the Paleolithic.

Archaeobotany — the recovery and analysis of ancient plant remains, including seeds, nutshells, and, increasingly, phytoliths and starch residues recovered from tools and pottery — has substantially advanced understanding of Jōmon plant use and management in recent decades, complementing the faunal analysis of animal bone that has long been central to reconstructing Jōmon diet. Radiocarbon dating, cross-checked against pottery typology and, where relevant, tephrochronology (introduced in Chapters 1 and 2), remains the primary method for establishing absolute chronology across the long Jōmon sequence.

Historical Vocabulary
Kaizuka (Shell Mound)

A kaizuka, or shell mound, is an accumulation of shell, bone, pottery fragments, tools, and other discarded material built up over years or generations at or near a settlement, typically in coastal or estuarine locations where shellfish gathering was an important subsistence activity. Shell mounds are among the richest and most extensively studied categories of Jōmon archaeological sites because the calcium carbonate in accumulated shell creates an alkaline soil environment that preserves bone and other organic material far better than the acidic soils typical of much of the Japanese archipelago.

The Ōmori shell mound, excavated by Edward S. Morse in 1877 and discussed in the Primary Source Window below, is the most historically significant kaizuka in the history of Japanese archaeology, since it was the first site systematically excavated using recognizably modern archaeological method in Japan, and its "cord-marked" pottery, as Morse described it in his 1879 report, supplied the terminology from which the later Japanese label Jōmon developed.

Section 3.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced the Jōmon world as a long, regionally diverse, and technologically and socially sophisticated pattern of non-agricultural life that developed across the Japanese archipelago over more than ten thousand years. Before proceeding to Chapter 4's treatment of Jōmon ritual, material culture, and social organization, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: "Jōmon" describes a broad, long-lived cultural-historical pattern identified primarily through pottery, not a single unified ethnic group or culture, and it varied considerably by region and changed considerably over its own long history, conventionally divided into Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final phases. Jōmon pottery represents remarkable technical and artistic achievement and provides the primary basis for dating and classifying Jōmon sites. Jōmon settlements, including substantial and long-occupied villages such as Sannai Maruyama, demonstrate that considerable sedentism and social organization could develop without dependence on full-scale cereal agriculture for most of the sequence. Jōmon subsistence combined hunting, fishing, gathering, and active management of plant resources such as chestnut groves, complicating any sharp distinction between foraging and farming. Long-distance exchange networks moved obsidian, jade, asphalt, and likely other materials across substantial distances, connecting Jōmon communities to one another and, at points, to the wider region beyond the archipelago. Skeletal and genetic evidence indicates a population adapted to an active, varied subsistence base and provides an important, though still developing, foundation for understanding the ancestry of later populations of the archipelago, including the Ainu. And the rich Jōmon archaeological record, especially at shell-mound sites, allows historians and archaeologists considerable insight into this long non-literate period, even as real limits remain on what can be known with confidence.

Primary Source Window

Edward Morse and the Ōmori Shell Mound, 1877

Creator: Edward Sylvester Morse, American zoologist · Date: Excavation and initial publication, 1877–1879 · Type: Archaeological excavation report

Context: Morse, traveling by train near Tokyo shortly after his arrival in Japan to study marine invertebrates, observed shell deposits exposed in a railway cutting near Ōmori and recognized them as an ancient midden. His subsequent excavation, conducted with early Japanese students who would go on to help found Japanese archaeology as a professional discipline, recovered pottery decorated with cord-marked patterns, along with animal bone, stone tools, and other artifacts.

Paraphrase of Morse's 1879 report (not a verbatim quotation): Morse's excavation report described the pottery's cord-impressed surface decoration in systematic detail, compared the assemblage to shell-mound sites he had studied elsewhere, and proposed that the deposit represented the discarded refuse of an ancient population that had subsisted substantially on shellfish and other locally available foods.

Analysis: Morse's excavation is significant less for any single artifact it produced than for its method and its consequences. It introduced systematic, stratigraphically informed excavation technique to Japan, trained some of the first Japanese archaeologists, and, through Morse's description of the pottery's cord-marked decoration, supplied the terminology from which the later Japanese term Jōmon developed as the name of the period. Students should note, however, that Morse's late-nineteenth-century interpretations of the site's makers reflected the racial and evolutionary assumptions common in the anthropology of his era, and later generations of Japanese and international archaeologists have substantially revised his original interpretations using the many additional lines of evidence discussed throughout this chapter.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the method of an excavation, and not only its findings, historically significant?
  2. How might a nineteenth-century excavator's assumptions have shaped his interpretation of the evidence?
  3. Why does the naming of an entire historical period sometimes derive from a single site or artifact type?
  4. What does the involvement of early Japanese students in Morse's excavation suggest about the origins of Japanese archaeology as a professional field?
Historical Debate

Were the Jōmon People "Complex Hunter-Gatherers"?

The question: Archaeologists worldwide use the term "complex hunter-gatherers" for societies that combine substantial sedentism, social differentiation, and, in some cases, large settlements or monumental construction with a subsistence base that remains rooted in hunting, fishing, and gathering rather than agriculture. Does this label fit Jōmon societies, and if so, how far does it extend across the Jōmon world's long history and many regions?

Interpretation A — Jōmon societies, at least in their Middle-phase peak, were genuinely "complex": Some archaeologists point to the scale and longevity of sites such as Sannai Maruyama, evidence of chestnut management approaching low-intensity cultivation, long-distance exchange networks, and elaborate later ritual monuments as evidence that at least some Jōmon communities, in at least some periods and regions, developed a level of settlement stability and social organization comparable to complex hunter-gatherer societies documented elsewhere in the world, such as on the Northwest Coast of North America.
Interpretation B — Caution against overstating Jōmon "complexity": Other archaeologists caution that evidence for social hierarchy, specialized labor, and centralized authority remains limited and unevenly distributed across the Jōmon sequence, that most Jōmon settlements were considerably smaller and less permanent than Sannai Maruyama, and that applying a term developed for other world regions risks imposing external analytical categories on Jōmon evidence rather than letting the archipelago's own archaeological record define its own patterns.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of settlement, subsistence, and exchange evidence but differ in how much weight to give exceptional sites such as Sannai Maruyama relative to the broader, more variable Jōmon settlement record, and in how directly to apply comparative frameworks developed in other world archaeological traditions.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how the Jōmon world is compared to other non-agricultural societies globally, and shapes how sharply or gradually later chapters should present the transition to Yayoi agricultural society in Chapter 5.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain whether you think a single label should apply to the entire ten-thousand-year, multi-regional Jōmon sequence, or whether different sub-phases and regions require different characterizations.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Jōmon World

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical categories drawn from archaeological evidence across the Jōmon world, not quotations or biographies of named individuals.

A resident of a large, long-settled village such as Sannai Maruyama: Daily life is organized around a stable home settlement occupied across generations, managed chestnut groves, salmon fishing tied to seasonal runs, and participation in exchange relationships that bring obsidian, jade, and asphalt from distant sources in return for locally available goods.
A member of a smaller, more mobile Jōmon community in a less resource-rich region: Subsistence requires more frequent movement between seasonal resource areas, a lighter and more portable material culture, and close attention to the annual cycle of plant and animal availability, without the scale of storage and settlement infrastructure visible at the largest Jōmon sites.
A Jōmon potter developing regional decorative traditions: Pottery-making combines practical technique — coiling, tempering, firing — with the maintenance of shared regional stylistic conventions, whose gradual change over generations later allows archaeologists to construct relative chronologies.
A modern archaeologist reconstructing Jōmon life from material evidence: Interpretation depends on carefully cross-checking pottery typology, radiocarbon dates, faunal and botanical remains, and skeletal and genetic evidence, while remaining alert to the danger of imposing later or externally derived cultural assumptions onto a society that left no writing of its own.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Paleolithic Foundations to Jōmon Diversity

What Changed

  • Pottery became standardized, widespread, and stylistically diverse across regions, in contrast to its rare and experimental Incipient-phase beginnings
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward greater sedentism and, at some sites, very large and long-lived villages
  • Subsistence expanded to include active management of plant resources such as chestnut groves, alongside continued hunting, fishing, and gathering
  • Population rose substantially through the Early and Middle Jōmon phases before contracting in some regions during the cooler Late and Final phases
  • Exchange networks moving obsidian, jade, and asphalt expanded across greater distances

What Stayed the Same

  • Continued absence of full-scale cereal agriculture across most of the sequence and most regions
  • Continued reliance on hunting, fishing, and gathering as the core subsistence strategy
  • Continued regional diversity rather than a single unified culture across the archipelago
  • Continued technological and demographic connection, at various points, to the broader Northeast Asian mainland

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity across the Jōmon sequence prevents students from treating "Jōmon" as a single static condition and prepares them to understand the transition to Yayoi society in Chapter 5 as a further stage of ongoing change rather than a sudden break with an unchanging past.

Cause and Effect

Postglacial Warming and the Jōmon Way of Life

Long-term causes

The retreat of Pleistocene glacial conditions and the broader postglacial warming trend transformed the archipelago's vegetation, sea levels, and coastline over several thousand years, as introduced in Chapters 1 and 2.

Short-term causes

The mid-Holocene climatic optimum, together with the associated Jōmon marine transgression — a relative sea-level highstand whose local timing and magnitude varied by region because of uplift, subsidence, and sedimentation — expanded estuarine and coastal environments particularly rich in fish, shellfish, and nut-bearing forest resources in many, though not all, parts of the archipelago.

Effect

Jōmon communities in resource-rich regions, especially central and northeastern Honshu, developed larger, more sedentary settlements, more elaborate pottery traditions, and more extensive exchange networks during the Early and Middle Jōmon phases.

Long-term consequence

Subsequent cooling during the Late and Final Jōmon phases coincided with population contraction and settlement change in some regions, illustrating that Jōmon prosperity was linked to, though not simply determined by, favorable environmental conditions.

Historical complexity: Climatic change affected different regions of the Jōmon world differently, and communities responded through a range of strategies — settlement relocation, subsistence diversification, and, in some interpretations, increased ritual activity — rather than a single uniform reaction.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Rhythms of Jōmon Daily Life

Daily life in a Jōmon settlement was organized around the seasonal availability of food resources: salmon runs in autumn in many northeastern rivers, nut harvests in autumn requiring extensive processing and storage for the winter months, and hunting and coastal gathering activities distributed across the rest of the year according to local conditions. Pit dwellings provided shelter suited to the archipelago's climatic extremes described in Chapter 1, and stored food, particularly processed nuts kept in storage pits, provided a buffer against seasonal scarcity, especially through the winter.

Craft production — pottery-making, stone-tool knapping, basketry and cordage production (evidenced indirectly through cord-marked pottery decoration and, at exceptionally preserved sites such as Torihama, directly through surviving organic fragments), and the working of bone, antler, and shell into tools and ornaments — occupied significant time and skill within Jōmon households and communities. Understanding these everyday dimensions of Jōmon life helps students appreciate that the "Jōmon world" was not merely an archaeological abstraction but a lived reality of considerable material richness and technical accomplishment, populated by communities who built durable homes, managed their landscapes with real skill, and sustained cultural traditions across many centuries.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced the Jōmon world, a long, regionally diverse pattern of pottery-using, generally non-agricultural societies that developed across the Japanese archipelago over more than ten thousand years, from the Incipient Jōmon pottery of the Late Pleistocene described in Chapter 2 through the gradual, regionally uneven transition toward Yayoi agricultural society examined in Chapter 5. The term "Jōmon," derived from the "cord-marked" pottery decoration Edward S. Morse described at the Ōmori shell mound in his 1879 report on the 1877 excavation, describes a broad cultural-historical pattern rather than a single unified ethnic group or culture, and it encompasses substantial regional and chronological diversity.

Jōmon pottery represents remarkable technical and artistic achievement, providing both practical benefits for food processing and storage and the primary evidentiary basis on which archaeologists date and classify Jōmon sites. Jōmon settlement patterns, including substantial and long-occupied villages such as Sannai Maruyama, demonstrate that considerable sedentism, settlement planning, and social organization could develop without full-scale cereal agriculture, complicating any simple, linear narrative in which settled village life begins only with farming.

Jōmon subsistence combined hunting, fishing, shellfish gathering, and the increasingly active management of plant resources such as chestnut groves, sustained by the exceptional biological productivity of the archipelago's temperate and cool-temperate environments described in Chapter 1. Long-distance exchange networks moved obsidian, jade, asphalt, and likely other materials across substantial distances, connecting Jōmon communities to one another in ways that complicate any picture of Jōmon life as isolated or narrowly local.

The chapter has also addressed what skeletal, dietary, and genetic evidence can and cannot reveal about Jōmon population and health, and has introduced the ongoing scholarly debate over whether Jōmon societies should be classified as "complex hunter-gatherers," a debate that illustrates the broader interpretive challenges historians face when applying comparative categories developed elsewhere in the world to the Japanese archipelago's own distinctive archaeological record.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized regional diversity — the differences among Jōmon communities in northeastern Honshu, western Japan, Hokkaido, and the Ryukyu Islands — and has cautioned against treating the Jōmon world as a single, static, or "primitive" backdrop to later Japanese history. The Jōmon world's long duration, technical sophistication, and demonstrated capacity for sustained settlement and exchange establish it as a subject of serious historical inquiry in its own right, not merely a prelude to the agricultural, political, and cultural transformations examined in later chapters.

These foundations prepare students directly for Chapter 4's fuller examination of Jōmon ritual, material culture such as dogū figurines, mortuary practice, and social organization, and for Chapter 5's account of the Yayoi transformation, in which wet-rice agriculture, metallurgy, and new social and political patterns began to reshape much of the archipelago while Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands continued along their own distinct historical trajectories.

Key Terms

Jōmon
"Cord-marked"; the pottery style and, by extension, the entire long cultural-historical period and regionally diverse societies associated with it.
Cord-marked pottery
Pottery decorated by pressing or rolling cords into wet clay before firing, the namesake decorative technique of the Jōmon period.
Kaizuka (shell mound)
An accumulation of shell, bone, pottery, and other refuse built up over time, whose alkaline soil environment preserves organic material unusually well.
Pit dwelling (tateana jūkyo)
A semi-subterranean dwelling built over a shallow dug pit with a wooden post frame and thatch, bark, or earth covering; the characteristic Jōmon house form.
Sedentism
The practice of remaining at a single settlement location for extended periods rather than moving frequently.
Semi-sedentism
A settlement pattern combining a stable home base with periodic seasonal movement or use of secondary sites.
Complex hunter-gatherers
A comparative archaeological category describing non-agricultural societies exhibiting substantial sedentism, social differentiation, or monumental construction; debated as applied to Jōmon Japan.
Midden
A deposit of domestic waste, such as a shell mound, used by archaeologists to reconstruct diet and daily life.
Obsidian
A volcanic glass flaked into sharp-edged tools, quarried at specific sources and moved through Jōmon exchange networks over long distances.
Jade (hisui)
Jadeite jade, notably sourced from the Itoigawa region — among the world's oldest known exploited jadeite sources — worked into ornaments found widely across Jōmon exchange networks.
Asphalt
Natural bitumen used as an adhesive for hafting tools, moved from limited natural sources through exchange.
Jōmon Transgression
The mid-Holocene marine transgression, a relative sea-level highstand whose precise timing and height varied regionally, distinct from but roughly contemporary with the broader mid-Holocene climatic optimum; associated with Jōmon coastal settlement and population expansion in many regions.
Faunal analysis
The study of animal bone remains to reconstruct diet, hunting practices, and environment.
Archaeobotany
The recovery and study of ancient plant remains, used to reconstruct Jōmon plant use and management.
Radiocarbon dating
A method estimating the age of organic material by measuring radioactive carbon decay; central to Jōmon chronology.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered soil deposits used to establish relative chronology at excavated sites.
Dogū
Clay figurines associated with Jōmon ritual or symbolic practice, examined fully in Chapter 4.
Stone circle
A large Late Jōmon monumental arrangement of stones, associated with ritual or funerary purposes and examined in Chapter 4.
Managed landscape
An environment actively modified or tended by human communities, such as Jōmon chestnut groves, without constituting full agriculture.
Chestnut cultivation
The apparent active management of chestnut trees by some Jōmon communities, evidenced especially at Sannai Maruyama.
Regional diversity
The recognition that Jōmon societies varied significantly by region rather than forming a single uniform culture.
Anachronism
The error of projecting a later concept or identity backward onto an earlier period; relevant to describing Jōmon populations as a unified "Japanese people."
Sannai Maruyama
A very large, long-occupied Early-to-Middle Jōmon settlement in Aomori Prefecture, central to debates over Jōmon settlement scale and social complexity.
Incipient Jōmon
The transitional phase, beginning within the Late Pleistocene, in which early pottery appears alongside continuing Paleolithic-style tools (Chapter 2).
Ainu
The indigenous people of Hokkaido, whose ancestry retains a comparatively higher proportion of Jōmon-derived genetic heritage on current evidence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has introduced the Jōmon world as one of the longest-lived and most distinctive non-agricultural cultural traditions documented anywhere in the archaeological record — a world that gives this textbook's title, "From Jōmon Japan," its deep historical anchor. Understanding the Jōmon world matters not because it represents a simple point of origin for a timeless Japanese nation, but because it demonstrates that substantial settlement, technical sophistication, environmental management, and long-distance exchange developed across the archipelago many thousands of years before anything resembling a Japanese state existed.

The Jōmon world also matters because of what it reveals about the archipelago's regional diversity from the very outset. The differences among Jōmon communities in northeastern Honshu, western Japan, Hokkaido, and the Ryukyu Islands anticipate a pattern this textbook will trace throughout its remaining chapters: political, cultural, and economic developments radiating from any single center rarely applied evenly across the whole archipelago, and regions such as Hokkaido and the Ryukyus frequently followed their own distinct historical trajectories.

Students should also carry forward this chapter's methodological lessons: the careful use of pottery typology, radiocarbon dating, faunal and botanical analysis, and increasingly, ancient DNA research to reconstruct a society that left no writing of its own, and the discipline required to distinguish secure archaeological inference from speculation, particularly regarding social organization and belief. These lessons will recur directly in Chapter 4's fuller treatment of Jōmon ritual and society, and they anticipate the evidentiary challenges historians face throughout the early chapters of this textbook.

Students who understand the Jōmon world as a genuine, dynamic, and regionally diverse historical subject — rather than a static prehistoric backdrop — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 4's deeper examination of Jōmon ritual, material culture, and social life, and from there into Chapter 5's account of the Yayoi transformation that would, over the following centuries, reshape much of the archipelago's economy, society, and material culture.
