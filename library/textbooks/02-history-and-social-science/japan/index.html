Japanese History

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century traces the history of the Japanese archipelago from prehistoric settlement and Jōmon communities through the Yayoi transformation, Yamato state formation, imperial court civilization, warrior government, Tokugawa society, the Meiji Revolution, empire and total war, Allied occupation, postwar reconstruction, high-growth Japan, and the social, political, economic, and cultural transformations of the twenty-first century.

Overview

Japanese history examines the peoples, communities, states, institutions, economies, religions, cultures, and political systems that developed across the Japanese archipelago and its surrounding maritime world from prehistory to the present. The textbook does not project a timeless or homogeneous national identity backward into the past. Jōmon, Yayoi, Emishi, Ainu, Ryukyuan, immigrant, provincial, colonial, minority, and other populations remain visible within the larger historical narrative.

The textbook begins with the archipelago's environment, Paleolithic settlement, Jōmon societies, the Yayoi transformation, and the emergence of increasingly complex political communities. It then follows Yamato state formation, Buddhism and continental exchange, the Nara and Heian courts, the rise of warriors, Kamakura and Ashikaga government, Ryukyu and East Asian maritime networks, Sengoku warfare, reunification, and the Tokugawa order. The modern sections trace the collapse of the shogunate, the Meiji Revolution, industrialization, constitutional government, imperial expansion, mass society, militarism, total war, defeat, occupation, democratization, economic reconstruction, the postwar political order, social movements, cultural globalization, demographic change, natural disaster, and debates over identity, memory, and Japan's future.

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain the major chronological transformations of Japanese history from prehistoric settlement through the twenty-first century without projecting modern national identity backward onto earlier societies.
  • Analyze how geography, migration, agriculture, maritime exchange, religion, political institutions, warfare, commerce, technology, and cultural adaptation shaped historical change across the archipelago.
  • Trace the changing relationship among imperial legitimacy, court authority, warrior government, domain power, centralized state institutions, and representative politics across different historical periods.
  • Compare the historical experiences of regional and minority communities, including Emishi, Ainu, Ryukyuan and Okinawan peoples, Burakumin, Zainichi Koreans, colonial subjects, women, laborers, and other populations whose experiences complicate a homogeneous narrative of Japan.
  • Evaluate Japan's changing relationships with Korea, China, Russia, Southeast Asia, Europe, the United States, and the wider Pacific world, including migration, diplomacy, trade, cultural exchange, imperialism, colonialism, and war.
  • Assess the transformations produced by the Meiji Revolution, industrialization, empire, total war, Allied occupation, postwar reconstruction, high economic growth, the asset bubble and its collapse, demographic change, social movements, and contemporary cultural globalization.
