Part I — The Archipelago Before the Japanese State
5

The Yayoi Transformation

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Somewhere along a small river valley in what is now northern Kyushu, in a century historians once dated to roughly the fourth or fifth century BCE but now, on the basis of refined radiocarbon evidence, place closer to the tenth century BCE, a community began doing something new with the land. They diverted water from a stream into a leveled, bunded field. They planted rice, a plant already long domesticated on the Asian mainland, in standing water rather than relying solely on the dry-land gathering, hunting, and small-scale cultivation that had sustained Jōmon communities in the region for thousands of years. They were not the first people to eat rice in the archipelago — some evidence suggests rice grains reached parts of Japan earlier, in small quantities, without wet-field cultivation — but they were among the first to reorganize their labor, their landscape, and eventually their society around the demands of paddy agriculture.

This chapter is about what historians and archaeologists call the Yayoi transformation: the centuries-long process by which wet-rice agriculture, new craft technologies, new burial customs, new forms of exchange, and new patterns of migration and admixture reshaped much of the Japanese archipelago between roughly the tenth century BCE and the third century CE. The word "Yayoi" itself comes not from any ancient community's name for itself but from a Tokyo neighborhood, Yayoi-chō, where a distinctive style of reddish, wheel-less pottery was first identified by modern archaeologists in the late nineteenth century. Like "Jōmon" before it, "Yayoi" is a label invented by later scholars to describe an archaeological pattern — a package of material culture and subsistence practices — not a people, a race, an ethnicity, or a nation. No one living through this transformation called themselves "Yayoi people."

It is tempting, and historically dangerous, to tell this story as a simple, single event: rice arrives, iron arrives, bronze arrives, immigrants arrive, and Japan is suddenly transformed from a "Stone Age" Jōmon world into an agricultural, metal-using "Yayoi" one, as though a switch were flipped across the whole archipelago at once. The archaeological and genetic evidence gathered over the past several decades, and especially the revised chronology developed through accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon dating in the early twenty-first century, tells a considerably more complicated and more interesting story. Wet-rice cultivation began in a small corner of northern Kyushu centuries before it reached central Honshu. Bronze and iron objects appeared centuries after the earliest paddies on partly overlapping but distinct chronologies; under the long chronology developed through this dating program, iron implements appear roughly six hundred years after wet-rice cultivation began, at approximately the same broad horizon in which bronze implements also become well established in the archipelago, rather than one metal simply arriving long after the other. Northern Kyushu itself eventually became a center of bronze production rather than remaining solely a recipient of continental imports. Migrants from populations of the Korean Peninsula intermarried with descendants of local Jōmon communities in patterns that varied considerably by region and period, producing a genetically and culturally heterogeneous population rather than a single new "Yayoi race" replacing an older one.

Just as importantly, this transformation did not occur everywhere in the archipelago, or on the same timetable. Hokkaido did not adopt wet-rice agriculture during this period at all; its Jōmon-descended communities instead continued and gradually developed distinct hunting, fishing, and gathering traditions adapted to a colder climate unsuited to paddy rice, traditions that archaeologists distinguish from the earlier Jōmon pattern as "post-Jōmon culture" beginning around the fourth century BCE. The Ryukyu Islands followed yet another trajectory, associated with what specialists call Kaizuka-period shell-midden cultures, only loosely and partially connected to developments unfolding in Kyushu and Honshu. Even within Honshu and Kyushu, communities in different regions adopted rice agriculture, bronze and iron tools, and new pottery styles at markedly different speeds, and some communities resisted or delayed adoption for generations. The word "Yayoi" describes an archaeological horizon that swept across parts of the archipelago unevenly, like weather moving across a landscape of hills and valleys, touching some places early and others late, and leaving some places largely untouched.

This chapter also asks a harder and more careful question than older textbooks once posed. Earlier generations of scholars sometimes asked simply, "Were the Yayoi people immigrants from the continent, or the descendants of Jōmon inhabitants?" — as though the answer had to be one or the other. Ancient DNA research from sites such as Doigahama, in Yamaguchi Prefecture, now shows that individual Yayoi-period people carried varying combinations of Jōmon-related and continental ancestry, with some individuals showing especially close genetic affinity to populations of the Korean Peninsula and others showing much stronger continuity with local Jōmon populations. The genetic record, in other words, does not support a simple replacement narrative in which one population wholly displaced another, nor does it support treating "Yayoi" as a purely cultural phenomenon adopted without any accompanying migration. It supports something more historically realistic: migration and intermarriage occurring alongside cultural transmission, at varying intensities, in different places and times, producing a population whose ancestry was already mixed and regionally variable well over two thousand years ago.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Yayoi transformation not as the moment "Japan" was born, nor as a simple story of foreign conquest or peaceful cultural diffusion, but as a long, uneven, and genuinely transformative process — one whose social consequences, including the growth of larger settlements, more differentiated burials, competition over productive land, and the accumulation of prestige goods through long-distance exchange, laid the groundwork for the political communities that Chinese observers, several centuries later, would describe as the polities of Wa, and among them a queen named Himiko ruling a place called Yamatai. Those political developments belong to Chapter 6. This chapter's task is to explain how the underlying social and economic conditions that made such political communities possible came into being in the first place.

As in earlier chapters, students should keep firmly in mind the difference between an archaeological label and a living identity. The people discussed in this chapter did not think of themselves as ancestors of a future nation-state, and their descendants — genetic, cultural, and otherwise — would go on to participate in a long history of continued migration, intermarriage, regional diversity, and cultural change that this textbook will trace across the chapters that follow. The Yayoi transformation is a beginning of certain important historical patterns, not a finished national origin story.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "Yayoi" as an archaeological-cultural category rather than an ethnic or national identity, and explain the term's origin.
  2. Explain the revised AMS radiocarbon chronology for the beginning of wet-rice agriculture in northern Kyushu and how it differs from the older conventional chronology.
  3. Trace the technology, labor, and social organization associated with early wet-rice paddy agriculture.
  4. Evaluate ancient-DNA and skeletal evidence for migration and admixture between continental migrants and Jōmon-descended populations, avoiding a simple replacement or diffusion model.
  5. Describe the slow, regionally uneven spread of rice agriculture from northern Kyushu through western and central Honshu.
  6. Analyze everyday Yayoi village life, including fields, storage, settlement planning, and craft production.
  7. Compare Yayoi pottery and firing techniques with Jōmon traditions and continental antecedents.
  8. Explain the roughly six-hundred-year interval between the beginning of paddy agriculture and the appearance of iron and bronze metallurgy on their own overlapping chronologies.
  9. Assess the evidence for growing social differentiation, property, and conflict without endorsing a mechanical "agriculture causes hierarchy" model.
  10. Identify regional variation across Hokkaido, the Ryukyu Islands, and different parts of Honshu and Kyushu during this period.
  11. Use Yoshinogari as a case study illustrating a later, more developed phase of Yayoi settlement rather than a representative "typical" Yayoi village.
  12. Connect the social changes described in this chapter to the emergence of larger political communities examined in Chapter 6.

Historical Context

Chapters 3 and 4 examined the Jōmon world on its own terms: a long-lived pattern of hunting, fishing, gathering, and increasingly sophisticated pottery-based material culture that developed across the archipelago beginning within the Late Pleistocene and continuing for well over ten thousand years. Jōmon communities were not static or unchanging over this immense span of time; they diversified regionally, developed increasingly elaborate ritual and material culture, and in some regions engaged in small-scale cultivation of certain plants long before the developments discussed in this chapter. It is essential, however, not to treat "Jōmon" and "Yayoi" as two neatly sequential boxes, one following the other on a single calendar date across the whole archipelago. As this chapter will show, Jōmon-descended lifeways continued for centuries in some regions after Yayoi-style agriculture had already taken root elsewhere, and in Hokkaido, Jōmon and later post-Jōmon traditions continued for a much longer period still.

The developments examined in this chapter are inseparable from northern Kyushu's geographic position. The Korea Strait separating Kyushu from the Korean Peninsula, discussed in Chapter 2 as a persistent water barrier even at the lowest Pleistocene sea levels, had by this later period become a route of regular, if not effortless, maritime contact — a channel for people, plants, technologies, and ideas moving in both directions between the archipelago and the continent. Northern Kyushu's relationship to southern Korea, rather than to central Honshu, is therefore the essential starting point for understanding how and why the Yayoi transformation began where and when it did.

Archaeological evidence for this period comes from an increasingly rich combination of sources: rice paddy remains preserved in waterlogged soil, wooden agricultural tools, stone reaping knives, storage-pit and raised-floor building remains, pottery and evidence from firing locations, bronze and iron artifacts, burial mounds and grave goods, and, increasingly over the past two decades, ancient DNA extracted from skeletal remains at sites such as Doigahama. Chinese textual sources describing the peoples of Wa become available only toward the very end of the period covered by this chapter and are examined in depth in Chapter 6; this chapter relies primarily on archaeological and genetic evidence rather than documentary sources, since sustained written description of Yayoi-period society did not yet exist from within the archipelago itself.

Section 5.1

What Was the Yayoi Transformation?

The term "Yayoi" originates not from antiquity but from the late nineteenth century, when archaeologists identified a distinctive style of reddish, relatively thin-walled, wheel-less pottery at a site in the Yayoi-chō neighborhood of Tokyo. As with "Jōmon," a term coined from the "cord-marked" decoration typical of earlier pottery, "Yayoi" began as a purely ceramic classification before archaeologists extended it to describe an entire cultural-historical period characterized by wet-rice agriculture, new pottery styles, bronze and iron metallurgy, and new patterns of settlement and burial. Students should hold onto this origin firmly: "Yayoi" names an archaeological pattern identified by modern scholars studying material remains, not a self-identified people, ethnicity, or nation. No community living through this period called itself "Yayoi."

For much of the twentieth century, Japanese archaeology relied on what this chapter calls the conventional chronology, which placed the beginning of the Yayoi period — defined by the onset of wet-rice paddy agriculture in northern Kyushu — at roughly the fifth or fourth century BCE. Beginning in the early twenty-first century, however, researchers associated with the National Museum of Japanese History applied refined accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon dating to carbonized residues on early Yayoi pottery and other organic material, producing a revised chronology that places the beginning of wet-field rice cultivation in northern Kyushu roughly five centuries earlier, in approximately the tenth century BCE. This textbook adopts the revised AMS chronology as its working framework while noting, wherever relevant, that many earlier and some current publications continue to use the older, later conventional dates. Students encountering other sources — including some museum materials, popular histories, and older textbooks — should expect to see the more conservative fourth- or fifth-century-BCE starting point and should understand why the dates differ rather than assuming one source is simply mistaken.

Perhaps the most important conceptual correction this chapter makes to older narratives concerns simultaneity. Older popular accounts sometimes described the Yayoi period as though rice cultivation, bronze technology, iron tools, and new burial customs arrived together, as a single coherent "package," transforming Japan all at once from a Jōmon world into a Yayoi one. The evidence, examined in detail in the sections that follow, shows something quite different: wet-rice cultivation began several centuries before iron and bronze metallurgy became well established, and different regions of the archipelago adopted these various elements at markedly different times, if they adopted them at all. The National Museum of Japanese History's own framework for this period explicitly recognizes multiple, simultaneously existing cultural patterns across the archipelago during the centuries covered by this chapter: Yayoi agricultural culture spreading through parts of Kyushu and Honshu, continuing Jōmon-descended traditions in eastern and northern regions not yet reached by rice agriculture, Hokkaido's distinct post-Jōmon culture emerging from roughly the fourth century BCE onward, and the Ryukyu Islands' Kaizuka shell-midden culture. "Yayoi Japan," in other words, never described the whole archipelago at any single moment; it described one regional and temporal pattern among several coexisting ones.

Historical Vocabulary
Yayoi

The term Yayoi refers to an archaeological-cultural category, named after a Tokyo neighborhood where a distinctive pottery style was first identified, used by modern scholars to describe the constellation of wet-rice agriculture, new ceramic styles, bronze and iron metallurgy, and related social changes that developed unevenly across parts of the Japanese archipelago from roughly the tenth century BCE (under the revised AMS chronology used in this textbook) to the third century CE. It is not the name of an ethnic group, a nation, or a self-identified people, and it did not describe the entire archipelago at any single point in time.

Students should treat "Yayoi period" as shorthand for a regionally and chronologically variable historical process, comparable to how "Jōmon" describes a long-lived and internally diverse pattern rather than a single uniform culture.

Section 5.2

Wet-Rice Agriculture Arrives in Northern Kyushu

Rice (Oryza sativa) had already been domesticated on the Asian mainland for thousands of years before the earliest evidence of wet-field cultivation appears in the Japanese archipelago, and scattered evidence suggests that rice grains, and perhaps small-scale dry-field or garden cultivation, reached parts of Japan even before the fuller wet-paddy system examined in this section took hold. The technological and social threshold that defines the beginning of the Yayoi period in the strictest sense, however, is the appearance of wet-field paddy agriculture: leveled, bunded fields flooded with controlled water, into which rice was transplanted or sown, and from which water could be drained at appropriate points in the growing cycle.

Sites such as Itazuke and Nabatake, in what is now Fukuoka and Saga prefectures respectively, preserve some of the earliest known evidence of this system in the archipelago: paddy field surfaces, associated irrigation channels designed to bring water from nearby streams into the fields, and wooden agricultural implements including hoes, spades, and rakes, alongside polished stone reaping knives used to harvest rice by cutting individual ear-bearing stalks rather than mowing entire plants at ground level. Under the revised AMS chronology, these earliest paddies date to approximately the tenth century BCE, considerably earlier than the fourth- or fifth-century-BCE date long given in the conventional chronology.

Wet-field rice cultivation required a degree of coordinated labor and environmental modification well beyond what most earlier Jōmon subsistence practices had demanded. Constructing and maintaining bunded fields, digging and clearing irrigation channels, managing the timing of flooding and draining, and coordinating planting and harvest labor across a community all required forms of social cooperation, planning, and, in many cases, shared or communal decision-making about land and water use. This does not mean that Jōmon communities lacked social organization or cooperative labor — Chapters 3 and 4 showed otherwise — but wet-rice agriculture introduced a qualitatively different relationship between a community and a fixed piece of land, one in which years of invested labor in leveling and improving a particular field made that field, and the water rights associated with it, a form of durable property in a way that mobile hunting and gathering territories generally were not.

It is important not to reduce the entire early Yayoi economy to rice alone. Fishing, shellfish gathering, hunting, and the collection of nuts and other wild plant foods continued to matter a great deal, particularly in the earliest phases of the transformation and in areas where rice cultivation was only beginning or had not yet been adopted. Millet and other crops also appear in the archaeological record of this period in some regions. Archaeologists increasingly describe early Yayoi subsistence as a diversified system in which wet-rice agriculture was added to, rather than immediately replaced, a broader and still-important set of subsistence practices inherited from the Jōmon world.

Section 5.3

Migration, Local Communities, and Population Admixture

For much of the twentieth century, scholars debated the origins of Yayoi-period populations largely as a binary question: were the people associated with the new agricultural technology immigrants from the Korean Peninsula who displaced or absorbed the existing Jōmon population, or were they primarily the descendants of local Jōmon communities who adopted new technologies and practices through cultural contact without significant population movement? Ancient-DNA research conducted over the past two decades has made clear that this binary framing does not fit the evidence. The genetic record instead shows a more historically realistic pattern: migration and intermarriage between continental migrants and Jōmon-descended populations occurring at varying intensities in different places and periods, producing a genetically and culturally heterogeneous population rather than either a clean replacement or a purely internal cultural transformation.

A genomic study of an individual from the Doigahama site in Yamaguchi Prefecture, a well-known Yayoi-period cemetery, published online in October 2024 and appearing in the 2025 volume of the Journal of Human Genetics, found that the sampled individual carried both Jōmon-related ancestry and substantial continental ancestry, showing especially close genetic affinity to populations of the Korean Peninsula. This finding supports the conclusion that meaningful migration from the continent did occur and that at least some Yayoi-period individuals had recent, direct continental ancestors or close continental relatives. At the same time, other Yayoi-period skeletal populations studied genetically show much stronger continuity with local Jōmon ancestry, with comparatively little continental admixture. Taken together, this heterogeneity indicates that the intensity of migration and intermarriage varied by region, by community, and quite possibly within single communities over successive generations, rather than following a single archipelago-wide pattern.

This chapter deliberately avoids describing the continental migrants discussed here as "Koreans," since the modern national and ethnic category of "Korean" did not exist in anything like its present form during the first millennium BCE, any more than "Japanese" did. Instead, this textbook uses the phrase migrants from populations of the Korean Peninsula, a formulation intended to acknowledge the geographic origin of migration without projecting a modern national identity backward onto communities separated from the present by well over two thousand years of subsequent historical change on both sides of the strait.

Students should also understand that genetic research on this period remains an active and evolving field. Scholars continue to debate more refined models of later Japanese population history, including proposals involving two-way or even three-way (tripartite) admixture models incorporating Jōmon, Yayoi-associated continental, and later continental contributions to the ancestry of populations in subsequent historical periods. This textbook presents ancient DNA evidence as a valuable and rapidly developing body of evidence rather than as a settled, final population model, and students should expect refinement as additional ancient genomes are sequenced and analyzed in the years ahead.

Historical Vocabulary
Admixture

Admixture refers to the genetic mixing that occurs when two or more previously more distinct populations interbreed over time, producing descendant populations carrying ancestry from multiple sources in varying proportions. In the context of the Yayoi transformation, admixture describes the process by which migrants from populations of the Korean Peninsula and descendants of local Jōmon communities intermarried across the centuries covered by this chapter, producing genetically heterogeneous Yayoi-period populations rather than two sharply separate groups living side by side without interbreeding, or a single population that had wholly replaced another.

Admixture is a technical, demographic concept and should not be confused with cultural assimilation, political unification, or the emergence of a shared identity; genetic mixing, cultural change, and political community formation are related but distinct historical processes, each requiring its own evidence.

Section 5.4

How Rice Agriculture Spread

From its point of origin in northern Kyushu, wet-rice agriculture spread outward across the following centuries, but this spread was neither rapid nor uniform. Under the revised AMS chronology, the process of eastward expansion through western Honshu, into the Kinki region, and eventually toward central and, much later, parts of eastern Honshu occurred over a considerably longer span of centuries than the older conventional chronology had assumed, since the earlier starting date in northern Kyushu leaves more total time for the same eventual eastward extent to be reached at the same later dates in Kinki and central Honshu.

Several factors help explain why this expansion took so long and proceeded so unevenly. Ecological suitability mattered considerably: wet-rice agriculture, as practiced with the technology available in this period, depended on suitable water sources, appropriate terrain for constructing and irrigating paddies, and a climate warm enough to support the growing season rice required, all of which varied significantly across the archipelago's diverse regional geography introduced in Chapter 1. Social and cultural factors mattered as well: communities that had developed successful and well-adapted Jōmon-descended subsistence strategies, particularly in resource-rich coastal or riverine environments, may have seen comparatively less immediate advantage in adopting a labor-intensive new technology, especially in its earlier, less-refined forms. Some communities appear to have selectively adopted certain elements of the new material culture — new pottery styles, for instance — without immediately adopting wet-rice cultivation itself, or adopted rice cultivation as a supplement to existing subsistence practices rather than a wholesale replacement.

The result was a long transitional period during which communities practicing full wet-rice agriculture, communities practicing partial or supplementary rice cultivation alongside continued hunting, fishing, and gathering, and communities continuing essentially Jōmon-descended lifeways without rice cultivation at all coexisted within relatively short distances of one another, sometimes for centuries. This pattern should further discourage students from imagining a single moving "frontier line" advancing steadily eastward across a map; the actual process resembled a slow, patchy diffusion in which some areas were reached early and others very late, and in which advancement was sometimes followed by pauses, local variation, or reversals depending on local conditions.

Section 5.5

Villages, Fields, Storage, and Everyday Life

Life in an early or middle Yayoi agricultural community centered on the annual rhythm of paddy cultivation: field preparation, irrigation management, planting, weeding, and harvest, interspersed with continued fishing, hunting, and gathering activities that remained economically significant throughout the period. Settlements were typically organized around clusters of pit-dwellings, similar in basic construction to earlier Jōmon dwellings but often arranged with greater attention to spatial planning in relation to nearby fields, water sources, and storage facilities.

Raised-floor storehouses, built on wooden posts to lift stored grain above ground level, appear at numerous Yayoi sites and represent an important technological and social development: they protected harvested rice from moisture, pests, and flooding, and their construction and use imply a community-level concern with protecting a stored surplus that could sustain a community through the following year or serve as a resource for exchange, ritual use, or, as Section 5.8 will discuss, competitive accumulation. The presence of stored surplus, made possible by successful rice harvests, marks an important departure from the more immediate, less storage-dependent subsistence patterns of much of the Jōmon world, though Jōmon communities in some regions had already developed their own storage practices for nuts and other foods.

Craft production continued and diversified during this period. Pottery, discussed in more depth in Section 5.6, remained an essential technology for cooking, storage, and ritual use. Textile production, evidenced by spindle whorls and, at some sites, fragments of woven material, provided clothing and other woven goods. Woodworking, supported by increasingly sophisticated tools, produced agricultural implements, structural timber, and household objects. Households and communities likely balanced agricultural labor with these forms of craft production according to season and need, a pattern of mixed subsistence and craft activity that would remain characteristic of much of Japanese village life for centuries to come.

It is worth emphasizing that most Yayoi settlements throughout most of the period were considerably smaller and less elaborately fortified than the large, later-phase sites, such as Yoshinogari, that receive the most attention in popular accounts and museum reconstructions. The moats, palisades, and specialized elite spaces associated with such prominent sites represent a later development within the broader Yayoi period, discussed further in Sections 5.8 through 5.10 and in the case study below, rather than typical conditions throughout the several centuries this chapter covers.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Rhythm of an Agricultural Year

Archaeologists reconstruct the agricultural calendar of an early Yayoi village from a combination of paddy-field evidence, tool assemblages, and comparison with better-documented later rice-farming societies. Fields required preparation in spring, involving the repair of bunds and irrigation channels damaged over the winter and the flooding of fields in advance of planting. Rice was transplanted or sown depending on local practice and period, and fields required ongoing management of water levels and weeding through the growing season. Harvest, conducted with polished stone reaping knives that cut individual grain-bearing stalks, would have been a labor-intensive communal undertaking requiring the coordinated effort of many households, followed by drying, threshing, and storage in raised-floor structures.

Outside the agricultural calendar itself, fishing, shellfish gathering, and the hunting of deer and wild boar continued to supplement the diet and provide materials for tools, clothing, and other goods, much as they had during the Jōmon period. Understanding Yayoi daily life as a blend of new agricultural labor and continuing older subsistence practices, rather than a wholesale replacement of one way of life by another, helps prevent students from imagining an abrupt, total transformation of everyday experience.

Section 5.6

Pottery and Material-Culture Change

Yayoi pottery, first identified at the Tokyo site that gave the period its name, is generally thinner-walled, less heavily decorated, and more standardized in form than much of the elaborately decorated cord-marked pottery associated with the Jōmon tradition discussed in Chapter 4, though regional Yayoi pottery traditions varied considerably and some vessels retained decorative elements with recognizable roots in earlier Jōmon practice. Common Yayoi vessel forms include jars for storage and cooking, bowls, and specialized vessels associated with ritual or mortuary use, reflecting both continuity with earlier ceramic functions and new demands associated with agricultural storage and communal ritual.

One especially instructive technical development concerns firing method. Jōmon pottery, as Chapter 4 explained, was generally fired in open bonfires without a fully enclosed kiln structure. Archaeological research on early Yayoi pottery from northern Kyushu has identified evidence of a distinctive covered open-firing technique — using some form of partial covering over an open fire rather than either a fully open bonfire or a fully enclosed kiln, producing more consistent and often higher firing temperatures — that closely parallels firing techniques documented at contemporary sites in southern Korea. This connection provides direct material evidence for the transmission of ceramic technology across the Korea Strait alongside the transmission of agricultural technology and, in at least some cases, migrating people themselves.

At the same time, Yayoi potters in the archipelago did not simply copy continental techniques wholesale. Vessel forms, surface treatments, and regional stylistic variations developed that were distinctly their own, reflecting a broader pattern this textbook will return to repeatedly: technological transmission across the Korea Strait and, in later periods, more broadly across East Asia typically involved selective adoption and local adaptation rather than simple, unmodified copying. This pattern of adaptive borrowing, visible here in Yayoi ceramics, recurs throughout later Japanese history in contexts ranging from Buddhist architecture to Chinese writing to, many centuries later, Western industrial technology.

Section 5.7

Bronze, Iron, and Continental Exchange

Few aspects of the older, conventional narrative of the Yayoi period require more careful correction than the assumption that rice, bronze, and iron arrived together as a single technological package transforming the archipelago all at once. The revised AMS chronology places the beginning of wet-rice paddy agriculture in northern Kyushu at roughly the tenth century BCE, while current estimates from the same long-chronology research place the appearance of iron implements in the archipelago considerably later — on the order of some six hundred years after the earliest paddies. Bronze objects become well established in the archipelago on a broadly overlapping timeline with iron rather than a strictly later one, developing over the following centuries into a more substantial local production tradition. Students should therefore understand rice agriculture as preceding metallurgy by several centuries, and bronze and iron as arriving within a similar broad horizon of one another rather than in a strict, uniform sequence of rice, then iron, then bronze, or rice, then bronze, then iron.

Early bronze objects reaching the archipelago, and eventually produced within it, included weapons such as swords, spearheads, and halberds — often larger and more ceremonially elaborate than would be practical for actual combat use, suggesting a shift over time toward ritual and prestige functions — together with mirrors and, distinctively in parts of the archipelago, large bronze bells known as dōtaku, whose specific ritual function remains a subject of scholarly discussion but which appear to have served ceremonial or communal purposes rather than everyday practical ones. Bronze mirrors in particular carried significant value as prestige goods and continued to matter greatly in the political and ceremonial life of the polities examined in Chapter 6.

Iron, though appearing on a broadly similar timeline to bronze rather than centuries later, gradually proved more significant for everyday practical use, since iron tools — including agricultural implements, woodworking tools, and weapons — could be manufactured with greater durability and, once local production capacity developed, more economically than equivalent bronze tools. Raw iron and, for a considerable period, finished iron objects continued to depend heavily on continental sources, particularly from the Korean Peninsula, meaning that access to iron functioned as an important marker of a community's or a leader's continental connections and exchange relationships throughout much of the Yayoi period.

Importantly, northern Kyushu did not remain merely a recipient of finished continental bronze objects throughout this period. Archaeological evidence, including molds and production debris, indicates that northern Kyushu developed local capacity to manufacture bronze mirrors and other bronze goods, including specific types of small mirrors associated with continental prototypes but manufactured domestically using locally adapted techniques. This local production capacity illustrates, once again, the broader pattern of adaptive technological transmission rather than passive dependency, while also underscoring northern Kyushu's continuing central position within networks of continental exchange throughout the period.

Section 5.8

Social Differentiation, Property, and Conflict

As wet-rice agriculture, storage capacity, and continental exchange networks developed across the centuries covered by this chapter, archaeological evidence increasingly documents growing social differentiation within many Yayoi communities. Burials from the middle and later Yayoi period show considerably more variation in grave construction, grave goods, and apparent social investment than earlier Yayoi or Jōmon burials typically display: some individuals were buried with bronze mirrors, weapons, or other prestige goods, in more elaborately constructed graves, while others received far simpler burial treatment. Some settlements developed defensive features, including surrounding ditches or moats and wooden palisades, together with evidence of interpersonal or inter-community violence, including skeletal remains showing weapon injuries and the accumulation of weapons at some sites in quantities suggesting organized conflict rather than only individual disputes.

It is tempting to explain this differentiation with a simple causal formula: agriculture produces surplus, surplus produces inequality, and inequality produces chiefs. This chapter deliberately resists that formula as too mechanical and too deterministic. Agricultural surplus alone does not automatically or inevitably produce social hierarchy; many agricultural societies throughout world history have maintained comparatively egalitarian social structures for extended periods, and archaeological evidence suggests considerable regional variation in the degree and timing of social differentiation even among Yayoi communities practicing similar agricultural technologies. A more historically adequate explanation must consider multiple interacting factors together: the productive potential and reliability of particular agricultural environments; unequal access to the most productive land and to irrigation infrastructure; differential access to continental exchange networks and the prestige goods, including bronze and iron objects, that such access could provide; population growth and its pressure on available land; the social and political choices made by specific communities and their emerging leaders; and, in some cases, organized conflict over land, water rights, or exchange relationships.

Different regions of the archipelago, even within the areas where wet-rice agriculture had taken hold, appear to have followed different trajectories of social differentiation, with some regions developing more visibly stratified societies earlier than others. Northern Kyushu, with its especially favorable position for continental exchange and its correspondingly greater access to bronze and iron prestige goods, appears prominently among the regions where the archaeological evidence for social differentiation, defensive settlement construction, and organized conflict becomes clearest earliest, a pattern directly relevant to the political developments Chapter 6 will examine.

Cause and Effect

Agriculture and Social Differentiation

Long-term causes

The centuries-long establishment and gradual spread of wet-rice agriculture, together with the associated development of storage capacity and durable investment in particular parcels of land, created new possibilities for accumulating and defending surplus.

Short-term causes

Unequal access to especially productive land and irrigation infrastructure, differential participation in continental exchange networks providing bronze and iron prestige goods, population growth in some regions, and specific political and social choices made by particular communities and emerging leaders all contributed to differentiation at particular times and places.

Effect

Middle and late Yayoi communities in some regions, especially northern Kyushu, developed increasingly differentiated burials, defensive settlement features, concentrations of prestige goods, and evidence of organized conflict.

Long-term consequence

These regionally uneven processes of social differentiation and competition created the social and political preconditions for the larger political communities — chiefdoms and, eventually, the polities of Wa described in later Chinese sources — examined in Chapter 6.

Historical complexity: Wet-rice agriculture did not mechanically or automatically produce social hierarchy. It created new possibilities and new stakes around land, storage, and exchange, which different communities then acted upon in different ways depending on local conditions, opportunities, and choices.

Section 5.9

A Patchwork Archipelago

It bears repeating, because it is so easily lost in a narrative organized around the spread of a single technological package, that the Yayoi transformation never encompassed the whole Japanese archipelago. In Hokkaido, communities descended from Jōmon populations continued Jōmon-descended lifeways through the earlier centuries covered by this chapter, gradually developing what archaeologists distinguish as post-Jōmon culture from roughly the fourth century BCE onward: a continuing hunting, fishing, and gathering economy, adapted to Hokkaido's colder climate and different ecological conditions, that did not incorporate wet-rice paddy agriculture during this period. This should not be understood as Hokkaido's communities simply failing to "catch up" with developments to the south; rather, it reflects a rational and successful adaptation to an environment in which the wet-rice technology developed in northern Kyushu offered little practical advantage, given the region's shorter growing season and generally unsuitable conditions for the rice varieties and techniques available at the time.

The Ryukyu Islands followed yet another distinct trajectory during this period, associated with what specialists term Kaizuka-period shell-midden culture, continuing patterns of subsistence and material culture only loosely connected to the wet-rice agricultural developments unfolding in Kyushu and Honshu. Ryukyuan communities maintained their own regional networks of exchange and cultural development, a pattern of independent regional history that this textbook will return to at length in Chapter 32's treatment of the Ryukyu Kingdom several centuries later.

Even within Honshu and Kyushu, where wet-rice agriculture eventually did spread, the timing, intensity, and social consequences of adoption varied enormously by region, as Section 5.4 explained. Some communities in regions well suited to rice cultivation adopted it relatively early and developed increasingly differentiated, competitive societies; others, even in reasonably suitable environments, appear to have adopted rice agriculture more slowly, partially, or with less accompanying social transformation. Northeastern Honshu, in particular, saw wet-rice agriculture arrive considerably later than in western Japan, and some communities there and elsewhere continued substantially Jōmon-descended lifeways for a much longer period.

This regional and temporal patchwork carries an important methodological lesson that this textbook will apply throughout its later chapters as well: describing an entire historical period with a single label — "the Yayoi period," "the Jōmon period," and, in far later chapters, "the Tokugawa period" or "wartime Japan" — always risks implying a false uniformity across the archipelago's genuinely diverse regional histories. Students should approach broad period labels as useful organizing tools while remaining alert to the regional variation such labels can obscure if applied too casually.

Section 5.10

From Transformation to Political Competition

By the later centuries of the Yayoi period, the cumulative effects of the developments traced throughout this chapter had become clearly visible in the archaeological record of certain regions, especially northern Kyushu and parts of the Kinki region. Settlements grew larger and, in some cases, more elaborately defended, with surrounding ditches, moats, and palisades suggesting concern with organized defense against rival communities. Burials became more differentiated, with some individuals interred with substantial quantities of bronze mirrors, weapons, and other prestige goods obtained through continental exchange, while the majority of burials remained comparatively modest. Bronze ritual objects, including dōtaku bells and ceremonial weapons, appear to have played an increasing role in communal or regional ritual life, quite possibly connected to the religious or political authority of emerging local leaders. Intensifying regional exchange networks connected communities across considerable distances, moving not only everyday goods but also prestige items, ideas, and, in all likelihood, people.

Taken together, these developments point toward the emergence of larger, more centralized, and more competitive political communities than had existed earlier in the Yayoi period, or during the Jōmon period before it. This chapter deliberately stops short of examining those political communities in detail. The chiefdoms, and eventually the named polities described in Chinese historical sources — including the peoples of Wa and a queen named Himiko ruling a polity called Yamatai — belong to Chapter 6, which will take up the political, diplomatic, and historiographical questions surrounding these developments directly, informed by the social and economic foundation this chapter has established.

What this chapter has shown, and what Chapter 6 will build directly upon, is that the conditions making such political communities possible — durable investment in agricultural land, storage and accumulation of surplus, differentiated access to continental prestige goods, growing populations in favorable regions, and increasing competition and, at times, conflict between communities — developed gradually and unevenly over some seven or eight centuries of the transformation this chapter has traced. The political history of Yamatai and its neighbors was not a sudden new development imposed from outside the Yayoi world; it grew directly out of processes already well underway within it.

Primary Source Window

The Doigahama Skeletal Population and Ancient DNA

Primary evidence: Yayoi-period human skeletal remains, including preserved ancient DNA, recovered from the Doigahama cemetery · Scholarly analysis: Genomic study of a sampled Doigahama individual, published online in October 2024 and appearing in the 2025 volume of the Journal of Human Genetics · Type: Archaeological/biological primary evidence (skeletal remains and ancient DNA) interpreted through modern genomic analysis

Context: The Doigahama site, in present-day Yamaguchi Prefecture, is a well-documented Yayoi-period cemetery whose skeletal population has long been studied by physical anthropologists for its distinctive cranial and skeletal features, often cited in earlier scholarship as evidence of continental affinities among some Yayoi-period individuals. Advances in ancient-DNA extraction and sequencing techniques over the past decade have allowed researchers to move beyond skeletal measurement alone and examine the genetic ancestry preserved in these remains directly. The skeletal remains and the DNA they preserve are the primary evidence from this period; the modern research team's genomic study is a scholarly analysis of that evidence, not the evidence itself.

Paraphrase of the study's findings (not a verbatim quotation): Genomic analysis of a sampled Doigahama individual identified a combination of Jōmon-related ancestry and substantial ancestry connected to continental populations, with the closest genetic affinities found among populations of the Korean Peninsula, supporting the conclusion that meaningful migration and intermarriage, rather than a single uniform Yayoi gene pool, characterized this period.

Analysis: This finding is significant because it moves the historical debate over Yayoi origins beyond skeletal measurement and material culture alone, providing direct genetic evidence for continental migration into the archipelago during this period. At the same time, researchers emphasize that other Yayoi-period skeletal populations studied genetically show much greater continuity with local Jōmon ancestry, meaning that no single sampled individual or site can be taken as representative of the entire Yayoi population. Historians and archaeologists must therefore resist generalizing from a single genetic study to the whole archipelago, even as such studies provide valuable, concrete evidence unavailable to earlier generations of scholars.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Doigahama genetic evidence suggest about migration and admixture during the Yayoi period?
  2. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe the sampled individual's ancestry as making them simply "Korean" or simply "Japanese"?
  3. Why must historians be cautious about generalizing from a single sampled individual or site to the entire Yayoi-period population?
  4. Why is it useful to distinguish the skeletal remains and their preserved DNA, as primary evidence, from the published genomic study that analyzes them?
Historical Debate

When Did the Yayoi Period Begin?

The question: How early did wet-rice paddy agriculture begin in northern Kyushu, and how should historians and archaeologists weigh a significant chronological revision against decades of prior scholarship built on the older framework?

Interpretation A — The conventional chronology: For much of the twentieth century, Japanese archaeology dated the beginning of wet-rice paddy agriculture in northern Kyushu to roughly the fifth or fourth century BCE, based on typological comparison of pottery styles and earlier radiocarbon methods. Many textbooks, museum displays, and general reference works, including some still in wide use, continue to present this later starting date.
Interpretation B — The revised AMS chronology: Beginning in the early twenty-first century, researchers associated with the National Museum of Japanese History applied accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon dating directly to carbonized organic residues on early Yayoi pottery, producing a revised chronology that places the same technological threshold roughly five centuries earlier, in approximately the tenth century BCE. This textbook adopts this revised chronology as its working framework.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on physical archaeological evidence from the same body of northern Kyushu sites, but differ in the radiocarbon dating methodology applied and in how directly the dated material relates to the actual paddy features being dated.

Why it matters: The choice between these chronologies affects how much total time historians believe elapsed between the beginning of rice agriculture and later developments such as the appearance of iron and bronze metallurgy, the eastward spread of agriculture, and the emergence of the political communities discussed in Chapter 6. It also illustrates how apparently well-established historical chronologies can be substantially revised by improved scientific dating methods, a theme this textbook has already introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of the Japanese Paleolithic.

Student task: Explain why a five-century difference in the starting date of the Yayoi period might or might not significantly change how historians interpret the pace of the transformations discussed in this chapter.

Historical Debate

Migration, Replacement, or Admixture?

The question: How should historians characterize the relationship between continental migrants and Jōmon-descended populations during the Yayoi period, given genetic evidence that does not support either a simple replacement model or a purely cultural-diffusion model?

Interpretation A — Emphasis on substantial continental migration: Some researchers emphasize genetic findings, such as those from Doigahama, showing individuals with substantial continental ancestry and close affinity to populations of the Korean Peninsula, arguing that meaningful and sustained migration, not merely the diffusion of ideas and technology without accompanying population movement, drove the Yayoi transformation in at least some regions, especially northern Kyushu.
Interpretation B — Emphasis on regional heterogeneity and Jōmon continuity: Other researchers point to Yayoi-period skeletal populations showing much greater genetic continuity with local Jōmon ancestry, arguing that the pace and intensity of migration and admixture varied enormously by region, and that historians should resist generalizing any single site's genetic profile, however striking, to the Yayoi population as a whole.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same expanding body of ancient-DNA research, together with skeletal, archaeological, and linguistic evidence, but differ in how much weight to give a small number of especially well-studied sites relative to the still-incomplete overall genetic sampling of the Yayoi-period archipelago.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how confidently later chapters can describe the population history underlying later Japanese, Ainu, and other archipelago populations, and shapes how cautiously students should treat popular claims about "pure" ancestry of any kind. Scholars continue to debate more refined models, including proposals for tripartite or multi-stage admixture extending well beyond the Yayoi period itself.

Student task: Using evidence from the Doigahama Primary Source Window and this Historical Debate feature, explain why "migration versus diffusion" is a less useful question than "how did migration and admixture vary by region and period?"

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Transformation

Note: as with Chapter 2, because no named individuals or first-person testimony survive from most of this period, the perspectives below describe categories of experience reconstructed from archaeological and genetic evidence, not quotations or biographies of specific people.

A household in an early northern Kyushu paddy-farming community: Daily and seasonal life increasingly centered on the coordinated labor of field preparation, irrigation, planting, and harvest, alongside continued fishing and gathering, and on the responsibility of protecting stored surplus in raised-floor storehouses against the coming year.
A community farther east, still practicing largely Jōmon-descended subsistence: For such a community, continental agricultural technology may have arrived first as occasional trade goods, new pottery styles, or secondhand knowledge rather than as an immediate, wholesale change in subsistence practice, with full adoption of wet-rice agriculture, where it eventually occurred, coming only generations later.
A migrant family or individual arriving from the Korean Peninsula: Genetic evidence indicates that some individuals with substantial continental ancestry lived, married, and were buried within communities in the archipelago, suggesting lived experiences of migration, intermarriage, and, in many cases, integration into local communities over the course of a lifetime or across generations.
An emerging local leader in a middle or late Yayoi settlement: Increasing access to bronze and iron prestige goods through continental exchange networks, together with control over agricultural land and stored surplus, may have provided the material basis for the kind of local authority and inter-community competition that the archaeological record increasingly documents in this period.
A community in Hokkaido continuing Jōmon-descended, and later post-Jōmon, lifeways: For communities in Hokkaido, the Yayoi transformation occurring far to the south had little direct bearing on daily subsistence, which continued to rely on hunting, fishing, and gathering strategies well adapted to a colder regional environment unsuited to the wet-rice technology developing elsewhere.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Jōmon to Yayoi

What Changed

  • Wet-rice paddy agriculture introduced durable, labor-intensive investment in specific parcels of land, in contrast to more mobile Jōmon subsistence territories
  • Raised-floor storage facilities enabled the accumulation of surplus grain across years
  • New pottery styles, firing techniques, and, later, bronze and iron metallurgy diversified the material-culture toolkit available to communities
  • Burials and settlement architecture became considerably more differentiated in many regions by the middle and late Yayoi period
  • Continental exchange networks, particularly with populations of the Korean Peninsula, intensified and became a significant source of prestige goods and political authority

What Stayed the Same

  • Fishing, hunting, and gathering remained economically important in most regions throughout the period
  • Pit-dwelling construction techniques continued in broadly Jōmon-descended forms in many settlements
  • Regional diversity across the archipelago's varied geography continued to shape distinct local historical trajectories, exactly as it had during the Jōmon period
  • Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands continued largely independent cultural trajectories, unconnected to the agricultural transformation unfolding in Kyushu and Honshu

Why the pattern matters: Understanding the Jōmon-to-Yayoi transition as a gradual, regionally uneven layering of new practices onto continuing older ones, rather than a sudden and total replacement, prepares students to recognize similar patterns of selective adoption and continuity that recur throughout later Japanese history.

Case Study

Yoshinogari: A Later-Phase Settlement, Not a Typical Village

The Yoshinogari site, in present-day Saga Prefecture in northern Kyushu, is among the most extensively excavated and widely visited Yayoi-period archaeological sites in Japan, and its reconstructed moats, palisades, watchtower-like elevated structures, and specialized elite precincts have become, for many students and members of the public, an image standing in for "the Yayoi village" in general. This chapter treats Yoshinogari instead as a valuable case study of a later, more developed phase of Yayoi settlement, illustrating the kinds of social differentiation and organized defense discussed in Section 5.8, rather than as a representative example of typical Yayoi settlement throughout the several centuries this chapter has covered.

Excavation has shown that Yoshinogari developed over a long span of time, beginning as a smaller settlement and growing, through successive phases of construction and expansion, into a large, moated regional center with differentiated internal spaces, including areas associated with elite residence, ritual activity, and specialized storage, by the later Yayoi period. This developmental trajectory itself illustrates the chapter's central argument: the elaborate, defended, socially differentiated settlement visible in Yoshinogari's later phases was the outcome of centuries of accumulated change, not evidence of how Yayoi villages generally looked from the very beginning of the period.

Using Yoshinogari responsibly as a teaching tool therefore requires distinguishing its early village phase from its later, more elaborate phase, and resisting the temptation to project its impressive later fortifications and elite spaces backward onto the entire Yayoi period or onto smaller, less thoroughly excavated settlements elsewhere in the archipelago that likely never developed comparable scale or complexity.

Case Study Questions:

  1. Why might a single, exceptionally well-preserved and well-excavated site risk distorting students' understanding of a broader historical period?
  2. What specific features of Yoshinogari's later phase connect directly to the social-differentiation evidence discussed in Section 5.8?
  3. How does Yoshinogari's own internal developmental sequence, from smaller village to large regional center, mirror the chapter's broader argument about gradual transformation?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Yayoi transformation, the centuries-long process by which wet-rice paddy agriculture, new craft technologies, bronze and iron metallurgy, new burial customs, and new patterns of migration and exchange reshaped parts of the Japanese archipelago between roughly the tenth century BCE, under the revised AMS radiocarbon chronology adopted in this textbook, and the third century CE. "Yayoi" is an archaeological-cultural category named after a Tokyo neighborhood where a distinctive pottery style was first identified, not the name of an ethnic group or a nation, and it never described the entire archipelago at any single moment.

Wet-rice agriculture began in northern Kyushu, at sites such as Itazuke and Nabatake, where communities constructed bunded, irrigated paddy fields and developed wooden and stone tools suited to planting and harvesting rice. This technology required a form of coordinated labor and durable investment in specific parcels of land considerably different from most earlier Jōmon subsistence practices, though fishing, hunting, and gathering remained economically important throughout the period rather than disappearing.

Ancient-DNA evidence, including a genomic study of an individual from the Doigahama site published online in October 2024 and appearing in the 2025 volume of the Journal of Human Genetics, demonstrates that meaningful migration from populations of the Korean Peninsula occurred alongside intermarriage with descendants of local Jōmon communities, producing a genetically heterogeneous population whose composition varied by region and period rather than following a single archipelago-wide pattern of either replacement or purely cultural diffusion. This chapter has deliberately replaced the older question "Were the Yayoi immigrants or Jōmon descendants?" with the more historically adequate question of how migration and admixture varied regionally and over time.

Rice agriculture spread only slowly and unevenly from northern Kyushu through western Honshu and eventually toward the Kinki region and beyond, shaped by ecological suitability, existing subsistence success, and local social choices. Everyday Yayoi village life combined new agricultural labor, storage practices, and craft production with continuing older subsistence patterns, and most settlements throughout most of the period were considerably smaller and less elaborately defended than famous later-phase sites such as Yoshinogari, examined in this chapter's case study as an example of later, more developed settlement rather than a representative typical village.

Yayoi pottery developed distinctive forms and a covered open-firing technique closely paralleling contemporary southern Korean practice, illustrating a broader pattern of adaptive technological transmission across the Korea Strait rather than simple, unmodified copying. Bronze and iron appeared on partly overlapping chronologies several centuries after the earliest wet-rice paddies, with iron implements appearing roughly six hundred years after cultivation began, correcting the older assumption that rice, bronze, and iron arrived together as a single technological package, or that one metal simply preceded the other by a long interval. Northern Kyushu eventually developed local capacity for bronze production rather than remaining solely dependent on continental imports.

By the middle and late Yayoi period, growing social differentiation, evidenced through increasingly varied burials, defended settlements, concentrations of bronze and iron prestige goods, and organized conflict, developed unevenly across the archipelago, most prominently in northern Kyushu. This chapter has deliberately rejected a mechanical formula linking agricultural surplus directly and automatically to social hierarchy, emphasizing instead the interaction of unequal land access, differential participation in continental exchange, population pressure, and local political choice.

The chapter has also insisted throughout on the patchwork character of the Yayoi transformation: Hokkaido continued Jōmon-descended lifeways that only gradually, from roughly the fourth century BCE onward, came to be distinguished as a separate post-Jōmon cultural pattern, without adopting wet-rice agriculture during this period; the Ryukyu Islands followed a distinct Kaizuka shell-midden trajectory; and even within Honshu and Kyushu, adoption of the new agricultural package proceeded at markedly different speeds in different regions. "Yayoi Japan" never described a unified whole.

Finally, this chapter has deliberately stopped short of examining the political communities whose emergence its final section anticipated. The growing social differentiation, competition, and accumulation of prestige goods documented especially in northern Kyushu created the social and economic preconditions for the chiefdoms and, eventually, the polities of Wa described in later Chinese textual sources, including Himiko and Yamatai. Those developments belong to Chapter 6, which will examine them directly, building on the foundation this chapter has established.

Students who understand the Yayoi transformation as a long, regionally uneven, and genuinely complex process of migration, admixture, agricultural change, technological adoption, and gradual social differentiation — rather than a sudden, uniform, or inevitable "arrival" of a finished new civilization — are well prepared to approach the political history of chiefdoms and early polities examined next.

Key Terms

Yayoi
An archaeological-cultural category, named after a Tokyo neighborhood, describing the constellation of wet-rice agriculture, new pottery, and metallurgy that spread unevenly across parts of the archipelago from roughly the tenth century BCE (revised chronology) to the third century CE.
Wet-rice agriculture
Rice cultivation conducted in flooded, bunded, irrigated fields (paddies), requiring water control and coordinated community labor.
Paddy field
A leveled, bunded agricultural field designed to be flooded and drained for wet-rice cultivation.
Irrigation channel
A constructed watercourse bringing controlled water from a stream or other source into agricultural fields.
AMS radiocarbon dating
Accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon dating, a refined dating method applied to organic residues that produced the revised, earlier chronology for the beginning of Japanese wet-rice agriculture used in this textbook.
Conventional chronology
The older, twentieth-century dating framework placing the beginning of Japanese wet-rice agriculture at roughly the fifth or fourth century BCE, still found in many sources.
Admixture
The genetic mixing that occurs when previously distinct populations interbreed, producing descendant populations with mixed ancestry in varying proportions.
Ancient DNA (aDNA)
Genetic material recovered from ancient skeletal or other biological remains, used to study population history and migration.
Continental migration
The movement of people from the Asian mainland, particularly populations of the Korean Peninsula, into the Japanese archipelago during the Yayoi period.
Reaping knife
A polished stone tool used to harvest rice by cutting individual grain-bearing stalks rather than mowing entire plants.
Raised-floor storehouse
A storage structure built on elevated wooden posts to protect harvested grain from moisture, pests, and flooding.
Moated settlement
A settlement surrounded by a constructed ditch or moat, often alongside a palisade, associated with defense and social differentiation in the middle and late Yayoi period.
Covered open firing
A pottery-firing technique using some form of partial covering over an open fire, distinct from both fully open bonfire firing and a fully enclosed kiln, producing more consistent results and paralleling techniques documented in southern Korea.
Bronze mirror
A cast bronze mirror, often obtained through or modeled on continental prototypes, serving as a significant prestige good and, in later periods, a ritual and political object.
Dōtaku
Large, elaborately decorated bronze bells produced in the Japanese archipelago during the Yayoi period, apparently used for ceremonial or communal ritual purposes rather than everyday function.
Iron implement
Tools and weapons made of iron, appearing in the archipelago roughly six hundred years after the earliest wet-rice paddies and remaining dependent on continental sources for much of the period.
Prestige goods
Valued objects, such as bronze mirrors and weapons, obtained through exchange and used to signal or reinforce social status and authority.
Differentiated burial
A grave whose construction, size, or accompanying grave goods differ markedly from typical contemporary burials, used by archaeologists as evidence of social differentiation.
Social stratification
The organization of a society into unequal tiers of status, wealth, or authority, evidenced archaeologically through burials, settlement architecture, and prestige-goods distribution.
Post-Jōmon culture
The distinct hunting-fishing-gathering cultural tradition into which Hokkaido's continuing Jōmon-descended communities developed from roughly the fourth century BCE onward, during the period when wet-rice agriculture spread through Kyushu and Honshu.
Kaizuka culture
The shell-midden-based cultural tradition of the Ryukyu Islands during the Yayoi period, following its own distinct regional trajectory.
Wa
The name used in later Chinese textual sources for peoples and polities of the Japanese archipelago, introduced here and examined fully in Chapter 6.
Migrants from populations of the Korean Peninsula
This textbook's preferred terminology for continental migrants associated with the beginning of wet-rice agriculture, chosen to avoid projecting modern national categories onto first-millennium-BCE populations.
Craft specialization
The development of dedicated, skilled production of particular goods, such as bronze objects, by specific individuals or communities rather than general household production.
Itazuke Site
An early northern Kyushu wet-rice paddy site providing key evidence for the beginning of the Yayoi agricultural transformation.
Nabatake Site
An early northern Kyushu site yielding well-preserved paddy field remains, irrigation features, and agricultural tools.
Doigahama Site
A Yayoi-period cemetery in Yamaguchi Prefecture central to ancient-DNA research on migration and admixture.
Yoshinogari Site
A large northern Kyushu settlement that developed over centuries into a moated, palisaded regional center, used in this chapter as a case study of later-phase Yayoi settlement.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the archipelago's human story from the long, largely anonymous world of the Jōmon into a period of accelerating and increasingly regionally differentiated change. It has shown that even a transformation as significant as the introduction of wet-rice agriculture did not sweep across the Japanese archipelago as a single event, but unfolded over some seven or eight centuries, through processes of migration, admixture, technological adaptation, and social change that varied enormously by region and period.

The chapter's careful treatment of chronology — distinguishing the revised AMS dating from the older conventional framework, and carefully sequencing rice, iron, and bronze rather than treating them as a single package — and its careful treatment of population history — replacing a simple migration-versus-diffusion binary with a more historically adequate emphasis on regionally variable admixture — both model a broader lesson this textbook will return to repeatedly: apparently settled historical narratives can be substantially revised by new scientific evidence, and historians must remain willing to update their frameworks accordingly rather than defending an older consensus for its own sake.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the chapters that follow. The growing social differentiation, competition over land and prestige goods, and intensifying continental exchange documented especially in northern Kyushu by the middle and late Yayoi period did not develop in a vacuum; they grew directly out of the agricultural, technological, and demographic transformations traced across this chapter's ten sections. Chapter 6 will pick up this story precisely where this chapter leaves it, examining the chiefdoms, the peoples of Wa, and the queen Himiko's polity of Yamatai as the political consequences of a transformation whose social and economic foundations this chapter has now fully established.

Students who understand the Yayoi transformation as a genuinely complex, regionally uneven, and still-actively-researched historical process — rather than a single tidy story of rice, bronze, iron, and a new people arriving together to found a new civilization — are well prepared to move forward into the political history of early Japanese chiefdoms and their world.
