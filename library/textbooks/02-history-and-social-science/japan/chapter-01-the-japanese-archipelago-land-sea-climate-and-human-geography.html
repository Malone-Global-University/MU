Part I — The Archipelago Before the Japanese State
1

The Japanese Archipelago: Land, Sea, Climate, and Human Geography

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Japan is not one island but thousands; not one climate but several; not one kind of land but a narrow, mountainous, earthquake-prone, monsoon-drenched arc stretched across more than three thousand kilometers of the western Pacific. Before there was a Japanese state, a Japanese language community, or a "Japanese people" in any settled sense, there was this archipelago — and the archipelago's mountains, coastlines, rivers, currents, and hazards shaped, and continue to shape, nearly everything that later chapters of this book will describe.

Stand today on the deck of a ferry crossing the Tsugaru Strait between Honshu and Hokkaido, or on a fishing boat working the waters off the Sanriku coast, or in a rice paddy on the Kantō Plain in early summer heat, and you are standing inside a set of physical conditions that have governed human life in this archipelago for tens of thousands of years. The land is mountainous — roughly three-quarters of the Japanese archipelago consists of hills and mountains, leaving only a fraction of the terrain suitable for large-scale settlement or intensive agriculture. The coastline is extraordinarily long relative to the archipelago's land area, laced with bays, inlets, and offshore islands that have made fishing, coastal trade, and maritime travel central to human life since the earliest known settlement. The climate ranges from the subarctic winters of Hokkaido to the subtropical warmth of the Ryukyu Islands, more than two thousand kilometers to the south, with most of the main islands governed by a monsoon rhythm that brings a wet early-summer rainy season, typhoons in late summer and early autumn, and, in many regions, heavy winter snowfall. And beneath all of this lies a landscape built by, and still shaped by, some of the most active tectonic and volcanic processes on Earth.

This chapter does not tell the story of a nation. It has no emperor, no shogun, no capital city, no written language, and, for most of the time span it covers, no population that a modern person would recognize as "Japanese" in any cultural or political sense. It instead introduces the physical stage — islands, mountains, rivers, coasts, currents, climate zones, and natural hazards — on which the human dramas of the following ninety-nine chapters will unfold. Understanding this stage is not a decorative preface to "real" history. Geography shaped where people could settle and farm, what they could eat, how they moved goods and information, which communities remained isolated and which stayed in continuous contact with the Asian mainland and the wider maritime world, and how repeatedly, across thousands of years, whole societies had to rebuild after earthquakes, tsunami, volcanic eruptions, typhoons, and famine.

It is important, from this very first chapter, to resist a temptation that runs through a great deal of popular writing about Japan: the temptation to treat the archipelago's geography as though it produced a single, timeless "Japanese" way of life — a supposedly innate love of nature, a supposedly innate capacity for disciplined endurance, or a supposedly innate cultural unity born of island isolation. Geography does not determine culture in so direct or mechanical a way. What geography does is create recurring conditions, constraints, and opportunities with which many different human communities, across many different periods, have had to reckon in their own ways. The Jōmon-period communities described in Chapters 3 and 4, the Yayoi agricultural societies of Chapter 5, the Ainu of the northern islands, the Ryukyuan societies of the southern islands examined at several points later in this book, and the modern residents of Tokyo, Osaka, and rural Tōhoku have all inhabited this same physical archipelago while building very different societies upon it. This chapter's task is to describe the physical conditions honestly, without projecting later cultural or political identities backward onto a landscape that long predates any of them.

The archipelago's geography also connects Japan outward rather than sealing it off. Later chapters will show repeatedly that the sea has functioned historically as much as a highway as a barrier: it carried Jōmon-era exchange networks, Yayoi-period migrants and rice agriculture from the Korean Peninsula and beyond, Buddhist monks, Chinese court culture, Portuguese and Dutch traders, and, in the twentieth century, empires, occupying armies, and global commerce. At the same time, the archipelago's mountainous interior repeatedly divided regions from one another internally, producing strong regional identities, dialects, and political fragmentation that recur throughout Japanese history, from the contending provincial warrior houses of the medieval centuries to the strong regional character of contemporary Japan.

This chapter also introduces a geographic reality easily lost in narratives centered on the Yamato court, Kyoto, or Edo/Tokyo: "Japan" as understood in this textbook is not simply Honshu, and Honshu's central regions are not simply representative of the whole archipelago. Hokkaido, home to the Ainu and their distinct history and culture, was not formally absorbed into a Japanese state until the modern era, a story developed in Chapter 16, in the later chapters on Meiji-era state-building, and in Chapter 94. The Ryukyu Islands, home to a distinct set of societies and eventually to the independent Ryukyu Kingdom examined in Chapter 32, lie geographically and historically apart from the main islands' narrative for much of this book's chronology. Both regions will recur throughout this textbook rather than appearing only at the moments they were absorbed into an expanding Japanese state, and this chapter's geographic survey accordingly covers the full archipelago rather than only its central islands.

Finally, this chapter introduces the kinds of evidence that allow historians, geographers, archaeologists, seismologists, and climatologists to reconstruct the physical history of the archipelago: sediment cores, tree rings, volcanic ash layers (tephra), coastal landform studies, historical disaster records, and the written and archaeological record of human settlement itself. Much of what we know about ancient climate, coastlines, and disaster history in Japan comes not from written chronicles — which do not exist for the vast majority of the period this chapter addresses — but from the physical record embedded in the land itself. Learning to read that physical record, and to distinguish firm evidence from later inference, is itself a historical skill, and one this chapter begins to develop.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the physical structure of the Japanese archipelago, including its major islands, mountain ranges, and regional divisions.
  2. Explain how the archipelago's mountainous terrain limited and shaped the location of settlement, agriculture, and political power.
  3. Analyze the historical role of the surrounding seas, straits, and currents as both a connective and a dividing force.
  4. Explain the monsoon climate system and its regional variation from Hokkaido to the Ryukyu Islands.
  5. Describe the tectonic and volcanic processes that produce Japan's earthquakes, volcanoes, and tsunami, and explain their long-term historical significance.
  6. Identify the major natural resources historically available within the archipelago and their long-term economic significance.
  7. Distinguish the geographic and historical experience of Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu, and the Ryukyu Islands from one another.
  8. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of environmental or geographic explanations for historical development.
  9. Identify the types of scientific and historical evidence used to reconstruct ancient climate, coastlines, and disaster history.
  10. Avoid anachronistic assumptions that project a unified, modern "Japanese" identity onto the archipelago's geography before such an identity existed.
  11. Contextualize the Japanese archipelago within the wider geography of East Asia and the western Pacific.
  12. Apply careful geographic vocabulary in preparation for the chapters on prehistoric settlement that follow.

Historical Context

The Japanese archipelago lies along the eastern edge of the Asian continent, separated from the Korean Peninsula by the Korea Strait and the Sea of Japan (known in Korea as the East Sea), and from mainland China and the Russian Far East by open water and, at its narrowest points in the far north and far south, relatively short sea crossings. During periods of lower sea level in the Pleistocene epoch — the long Ice Age era discussed in Chapter 2 — parts of the archipelago were connected, or nearly connected, to the Asian mainland by land bridges or narrow straits, notably in the north where Hokkaido was joined at times to Sakhalin and the continent; other routes onto the archipelago, however, appear to have required deliberate sea crossings even at low sea levels, and current archaeological evidence indicates that early Homo sapiens possessed the maritime capability to cross stretches of open water, including the Korea Strait and the sea passages leading into the Ryukyu Islands, well before the end of the Pleistocene. As global sea levels rose after the end of the last glacial period, roughly between about 15,000 and 8,000 years ago according to current geological reconstructions, the archipelago gradually took on a form closer to its present shape, separated from the continent by open water while remaining within reach of maritime crossing.

Geologically, the archipelago sits at the meeting point of several tectonic plates — commonly identified as the Pacific, Philippine Sea, North American (or Okhotsk), and Eurasian (or Amurian) plates, depending on the geological model used — whose collision and subduction have, over tens of millions of years — including a particularly important multi-stage rifting and arc-rotation phase during the Miocene, roughly 21 to 15 million years ago, that opened the Sea of Japan — produced the mountainous, volcanically active landscape that later chapters will show shaping settlement, agriculture, warfare, and disaster experience throughout Japanese history. This chapter introduces these physical processes at a general level; more specialized geological and archaeological detail relevant to early human settlement follows in Chapter 2.

Because the physical geography described in this chapter long predates any human society that could be called Yamato, Jōmon, Ainu, or Ryukyuan in the specific historical senses those terms carry, students should understand this chapter as establishing an environmental baseline rather than a cultural or political history. The chapters that follow will introduce, in turn, the earliest human settlement of this landscape (Chapter 2), the long, regionally diverse Jōmon world that developed within it (Chapters 3 and 4), and the transformative agricultural and social changes of the Yayoi period (Chapter 5) — each of which will return to the geographic patterns introduced here as a continuing framework for understanding where and how people lived.

Section 1.1

An Archipelago, Not a Continent

The Japanese archipelago consists of four large islands — Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu — together with several thousand smaller islands, including the Ryukyu chain stretching southwest toward Taiwan and the Izu and Ogasawara (Bonin) island groups extending south into the Pacific. Honshu, the largest island, is itself long and narrow, stretching more than 1,300 kilometers from its northern to southern ends, and its shape has historically produced significant internal regional variation in climate, agriculture, and culture even within what modern maps present as a single island.

This basic physical fact — that "Japan" is an archipelago rather than a single landmass — has consequences that recur throughout this textbook. Political power radiating from a single capital, whether Nara, Kyoto, or Edo, has never automatically or immediately extended its authority uniformly across the entire archipelago. Regions separated by mountains, straits, and open water developed distinct economies, dialects, and, in the cases of Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands, entirely separate political histories for extended periods. At the same time, the archipelago's many harbors, straits, and coastal routes allowed maritime connection between regions that mountainous terrain made difficult to reach by land, a pattern discussed further in Section 1.3.

Total land area of the modern Japanese archipelago is approximately 378,000 square kilometers, roughly comparable in size to Germany or the U.S. state of Montana, but distributed across a much longer and more fragmented shape, with a coastline disproportionately long relative to that land area. This elongated shape means that residents of the far north and residents of the far south experience markedly different climates, growing seasons, and natural hazards, even though both are typically described in modern usage as living in "Japan."

Historical Vocabulary
Archipelago

An archipelago is a group or chain of islands, typically formed by related geological processes such as volcanic activity or tectonic uplift. Describing Japan as an archipelago rather than as a single island or landmass emphasizes an important historical reality: political, cultural, and economic unity across the Japanese islands was never simply given by geography, but had to be built, negotiated, and at times imposed by human institutions across many centuries.

Historians and geographers sometimes use the Japanese term Nihon rettō (日本列島, "the Japan archipelago" or "Japanese island chain") for this same physical grouping. Using "archipelago" throughout this chapter, rather than simply "Japan," helps keep visible the fact that the physical islands existed — and were already home to diverse human communities — long before any political entity resembling a unified Japanese state existed to claim them.

Section 1.2

Mountains, Plains, and the Shape of Settlement

Approximately three-quarters of the Japanese archipelago's land area is mountainous or hilly, a proportion that has profoundly shaped where human communities could build permanent settlements, cultivate crops, and construct the roads, castles, and cities examined in later chapters. Central Honshu contains a series of high mountain ranges — the Hida, Kiso, and Akaishi ranges, often collectively called the Japanese Alps in modern usage — whose peaks exceed 3,000 meters and whose valleys and passes historically constrained overland travel between the archipelago's eastern and western regions.

Because mountainous terrain limited the amount of land available for agriculture and dense settlement, the archipelago's plains took on outsized historical importance relative to their share of total land area. The Kantō Plain, the largest lowland region in the archipelago, later became the demographic and political center of eastern Japan, home to Edo and then Tokyo, discussed extensively in Parts VI through XIV. The Kinai region around Nara and Kyoto, though smaller, combined fertile lowland with strategic access to the Inland Sea and overland routes, helping explain why early Yamato and later imperial court authority concentrated there, as Chapters 7 through 18 will examine. The Nōbi Plain around modern Nagoya, the Osaka Plain, and smaller pockets of lowland throughout Kyushu, Shikoku, and Tōhoku each supported regional population centers whose relative size and agricultural capacity shaped patterns of political power for centuries.

Mountainous terrain also created durable regional distinctiveness. Provinces and, later, domains separated by mountain ranges frequently developed distinct dialects, agricultural practices, craft traditions, and political loyalties, a pattern visible from the provincial diversity of the Nara and Heian periods (Part III) through the intensely regional political landscape of the Sengoku period (Part VI) and into the persistence of strong regional identity in contemporary Japan. Mountains were not merely obstacles; they also functioned as sites of religious significance, examined further in later chapters through traditions such as Shugendō mountain asceticism, and as sources of timber, minerals, and other resources discussed in Section 1.8.

Section 1.3

The Sea: Coastlines, Currents, and Connection

If mountains divided the archipelago internally, the surrounding and internal seas connected it — to itself and to the wider world. The archipelago's coastline, when measured in its full complexity of bays, inlets, and offshore islands, is extraordinarily long relative to its land area, and few places in Japan lie very far from the coast. This geography made fishing, salt production, coastal shipping, and maritime trade central to the economy and diet of communities across the archipelago from the earliest known settlement onward, a pattern already visible in the shell-mound and coastal-settlement evidence associated with the Jōmon period discussed in Chapters 3 and 4.

The Seto Inland Sea, the body of water separating Honshu from Shikoku and Kyushu, functioned historically as one of the archipelago's most important corridors, sheltered from the open ocean and studded with islands that made it navigable by the relatively small vessels available before the modern era. Its ports linked the Kinai region to Kyushu and, from there, to the Korean Peninsula and the Asian mainland — a route of central importance to the diplomatic and cultural contacts discussed in Chapters 9 through 12.

The straits separating the archipelago from the Asian mainland and from its own outlying regions were narrow enough to cross by boat under favorable conditions but wide and often rough enough to make crossing a serious undertaking requiring skill, favorable season, and, in many periods, considerable risk. The Korea/Tsushima Strait, a broader marine passage roughly 160–200 kilometers across, is divided by the intervening island of Tsushima into two channels, of which the western channel narrows to only about 40–50 kilometers, providing Tsushima as a stepping stone that connected Kyushu to the Korean Peninsula and, through it, to the broader currents of continental East Asian history — a route used for migration, trade, diplomacy, Buddhist transmission, and, at times, warfare, as later chapters will show in detail. The Tsugaru Strait, separating Honshu from Hokkaido, was narrower still but marked a persistent boundary zone between Wajin (Japanese-speaking settlers and communities from the main islands, in this northern-frontier context) and Ainu spheres of life, discussed further in Section 1.7 and in Chapters 16 and 48, and in the later chapters treating Meiji-era Hokkaido colonization and Ainu policy.

Two major ocean currents further shaped the archipelago's relationship to the sea. The warm Kuroshio Current flows northeastward along Japan's Pacific coast, moderating temperatures and supporting fisheries and, historically, facilitating maritime travel along the southern and eastern coasts. The cold Oyashio Current flows southward along the northeastern coast from the Sea of Okhotsk, and its interaction with the Kuroshio east of northern Honshu creates a dynamic Kuroshio–Oyashio transition zone rather than a fixed line where the two currents simply collide; this variable, nutrient-rich confluence region has historically supported some of the richest fishing grounds off the Sanriku coast — a resource examined further in Section 1.8. These currents also affect regional climate, contributing to the often foggy, cooler summers of the Sanriku coast relative to areas further south along the same general latitude.

The historical significance of the sea as a connective rather than purely isolating force will recur throughout this textbook: in Yayoi-period migration and the arrival of wet-rice agriculture (Chapter 5), in the maritime kingdom of Ryukyu (Chapter 32), in the Nanban trade with Portuguese merchants and Jesuit missionaries (Chapter 37), and in the carefully regulated but genuine international contacts maintained even during the Tokugawa period's restrictive foreign-relations policy, examined in Chapter 48. At the same time, the sea's role as a barrier should not be dismissed either: the difficulty and danger of long ocean crossings, particularly before the development of more advanced maritime technology, meant that contact with the mainland, while real and historically significant, was neither constant nor unmediated. Section 1.9 and the Historical Debate feature later in this chapter return to this tension directly.

Section 1.4

Rivers, Water, and the Making of Agricultural Land

Japanese rivers are, by the standards of continental river systems, generally short and steep, descending rapidly from mountainous headwaters to the sea across a comparatively narrow landmass. Rivers such as the Shinano, the longest river in Japan, and the Tone, which drains much of the Kantō Plain, nonetheless played a disproportionately important historical role: they carried sediment that built the alluvial plains on which wet-rice agriculture depended from the Yayoi period onward, they supplied water for irrigation, and they served as transport routes for people and goods, particularly before the construction of extensive road networks.

Because Japanese rivers descend quickly from steep terrain, they are also prone to sudden flooding, particularly during the early-summer rainy season and the typhoon season described in Section 1.5. Historical communities and, later, political authorities invested heavily in river management, embankment construction, and irrigation engineering — efforts examined in later chapters in the context of Tokugawa-period village agriculture (Chapter 44) and Meiji-era infrastructure development (Chapter 56), among others. Flood control and water management should be understood as a recurring historical challenge and a recurring arena of both cooperation and conflict among villages, domains, and, later, the modern state, rather than as a solved problem belonging only to engineering history.

The alluvial plains built by these rivers — the Kantō, Kinai, Nōbi, and Osaka plains chief among them — became, over the long span of Japanese history, the archipelago's most agriculturally productive and densely populated regions, a pattern with direct consequences for where political power concentrated in later centuries, discussed throughout Parts II through VII of this textbook.

Section 1.5

Climate and Monsoon Rhythms

The Japanese archipelago's great north-south extent produces significant climatic variation. Hokkaido experiences a humid continental climate with long, cold, snowy winters and relatively short, mild summers. Much of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu lies within a humid subtropical climate zone, with hot, humid summers and generally mild winters, though regional variation is considerable: the Sea of Japan coast receives exceptionally heavy winter snowfall as moist air crosses the sea and rises over the mountains, while the Pacific coast tends toward drier winters and wetter summers. The Ryukyu Islands, far to the southwest, experience a subtropical to tropical climate with warm temperatures year-round and their own distinct exposure to typhoons.

Across most of the archipelago, the climate follows a monsoon-influenced annual rhythm long central to agricultural life. An early-summer rainy season, known in Japanese as tsuyu or baiu, typically brings weeks of persistent rain in June and early July across most of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu (though notably not Hokkaido, which lacks a pronounced rainy season of this kind). Late summer and early autumn bring typhoon season, when tropical cyclones moving north from the western Pacific frequently strike the archipelago, bringing intense rainfall, flooding, and wind damage. This annual rhythm structured the traditional agricultural calendar, particularly for the wet-rice cultivation that became central to the archipelago's economy from the Yayoi period onward, discussed in Chapter 5 and throughout the chapters on agrarian society in later periods.

Climate historians and paleoclimatologists have also reconstructed longer-term climatic fluctuations affecting the archipelago across the centuries — including periods of relative warming and cooling that some scholars connect to agricultural expansion, famine, and social stress in particular periods, discussed at relevant points in later chapters such as the famines and agrarian crises of the Tokugawa period (Chapter 44). Such reconstructions rely on evidence including tree-ring analysis, sediment cores, and historical crop and disaster records, and their precise causal relationship to specific historical events remains, in many cases, a subject of ongoing scholarly investigation rather than settled fact.

Section 1.6

Living on the Ring of Fire: Earthquakes, Volcanoes, and Tsunami

The Japanese archipelago sits within the Pacific "Ring of Fire," the horseshoe-shaped zone of intense seismic and volcanic activity that circles the Pacific Ocean basin, produced by the subduction of oceanic tectonic plates beneath continental and other oceanic plates. Japan lies at a particularly complex junction of several plates, commonly identified as the Pacific, Philippine Sea, and Eurasian/Amurian and North American/Okhotsk plates, whose ongoing collision and subduction produce the archipelago's mountainous terrain, its 111 active volcanoes as currently recognized by the Japan Meteorological Agency, and its extremely high frequency of earthquakes, some of which generate destructive tsunami.

This geological reality has been a constant, recurring feature of human life in the archipelago for as long as it has been inhabited, though the documentary record of specific disasters becomes substantially fuller from the Nara and Heian periods onward, when court chronicles and other written sources began recording major earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and their consequences. Before the availability of written records, geologists and archaeologists reconstruct earthquake and eruption history through methods including the study of tsunami deposits — layers of sand and marine sediment left inland by major tsunami and identifiable in soil profiles centuries or millennia later — and the study of tephra, layers of volcanic ash whose chemical composition and thickness can often be matched to specific eruptions and used to date associated archaeological layers.

Volcanic activity has shaped the archipelago's landscape directly, producing dramatic and often fertile terrain such as the Mount Fuji volcanic complex, the calderas and hot springs of Kyushu, and numerous other volcanic features that later became sites of religious significance, resource extraction, and, in the modern era, geothermal energy production and tourism. At the same time, volcanic eruptions have periodically caused severe, sometimes catastrophic damage to agriculture and settlement, including instances of ashfall disrupting crops across wide regions, a pattern examined further where relevant in later chapters, including the discussion of Tokugawa-period famine in Chapter 44.

Earthquakes and the tsunami they can generate along Japan's Pacific margin — which contains several major subduction zones, including the Japan Trench and the Nankai Trough, where the Pacific and Philippine Sea plates, respectively, descend beneath the overriding plates in configurations prone to producing very large "megathrust" earthquakes — have repeatedly devastated coastal communities across the archipelago's documented history, a pattern this textbook returns to directly in its treatment of the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 (Chapter 71), the Great Hanshin (Kobe) earthquake of 1995 (Chapter 98), and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami and the resulting Fukushima nuclear disaster (Chapter 99). This chapter establishes the underlying physical mechanism and long historical pattern; those later chapters examine specific disasters and their social, political, and economic consequences in full depth.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinsai and Saigai

Japanese historical and contemporary usage includes several distinct terms for disaster that are useful for students to recognize. Saigai (災害) is a general term for disaster of any kind, natural or otherwise. Shinsai (震災) refers specifically to earthquake disaster, and appears in the historical names given to major earthquakes discussed later in this textbook, such as the Great Kantō Shinsai of 1923 and the Great Hanshin-Awaji Shinsai of 1995. Related terms include tsunami (津波), a Japanese-origin term now used internationally to describe the long, powerful ocean waves generated by undersea earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or landslides, and funka (噴火), meaning volcanic eruption.

Recognizing this vocabulary matters because it signals how deeply disaster recurrence is embedded in the archipelago's historical experience and language, rather than being an occasional or exceptional interruption to an otherwise stable historical narrative.

Section 1.7

Regional Diversity: Hokkaido to the Ryukyus

Because this textbook is committed to keeping the archipelago's full regional diversity visible rather than allowing the history of the central islands to stand for the whole, this section surveys the major regional divisions students will encounter throughout the book.

Hokkaido, the northernmost main island, has a cooler climate, later and shorter growing seasons, and a distinct ecology relative to the rest of the archipelago. For most of the historical period covered by Parts I through XI of this textbook, most of Hokkaido/Ezochi — the territory known to the expanding Japanese state as Ezo — remained outside direct Japanese administration, home to the Ainu people, whose distinct language, mixed economy of fishing, hunting, gathering, some cultivation, and extensive regional trade, and social and religious institutions developed in continuous contact and exchange with Wajin society to the south. Wajin settlement and the Matsumae domain, however, established political and commercial footholds in southern Hokkaido from the early modern period onward, and the Tokugawa shogunate at times imposed direct administration over parts of Ezo in response to Russian pressure, exercising growing and increasingly coercive influence over Ainu communities well before the formal nineteenth-century incorporation discussed in Chapters 16 and 48 and in the later chapters treating Meiji-era state-building. Hokkaido's formal political incorporation into the modern Japanese state, its renaming, and the profound consequences for Ainu society under the Kaitakushi (Hokkaido Development Commission) and subsequent assimilation policy are examined in the chapters covering Meiji-era domestic transformation, and Ainu activism and cultural recognition in the modern era are discussed in Chapters 94 and 100.

Honshu, as discussed in Section 1.1, is internally diverse, encompassing the Tōhoku region in the northeast (historically more thinly populated, agriculturally marginal in its northern reaches, and the site of significant historical contact and conflict between Wajin and the Emishi peoples discussed in Chapter 16), the Kantō region centered on the plain of the same name, the Chūbu region including the central mountains, the Kansai/Kinki region historically home to Nara, Kyoto, and Osaka, and the Chūgoku region in the southwest.

Shikoku and Kyushu each developed distinct regional histories: Shikoku's relative smallness and mountainous terrain shaped its political history as a secondary rather than central region for most of the periods covered by this textbook, while Kyushu's proximity to the Korean Peninsula made it historically the archipelago's primary point of contact with continental Asia, a role discussed in Chapters 2, 5, 9, and 37, among others, and later a center of nineteenth-century domains central to the Meiji Restoration, discussed in Chapters 52 and 53.

The Ryukyu Islands, extending southwest from Kyushu toward Taiwan, developed a distinct maritime society and, following unification around 1429, an independent kingdom with close relations to Ming and Qing China as well as to Japan, examined fully in Chapter 32. After Satsuma domain's invasion in 1609, the Ryukyu Kingdom retained its monarchy, distinct institutions, and tributary relationship with China, but its sovereignty became increasingly constrained under Satsuma and Tokugawa domination, a dual status recurring throughout this textbook's treatment of the early modern, modern, and contemporary periods, including Satsuma's intervention (Chapter 48) and the kingdom's final abolition and the formal establishment of Okinawa Prefecture in 1879, the catastrophic Battle of Okinawa in 1945 (Chapter 80), the prolonged period of American administration after the war, the 1972 reversion to Japanese sovereignty (Chapter 90), and contemporary politics surrounding U.S. military bases (Chapters 99 and 100).

Holding Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands in view from this very first chapter, rather than introducing them only at the moments of their incorporation into an expanding Japanese state, reflects this textbook's explicit commitment, stated in the Authoritative Chapter Outline governing the whole book, to treat these regions' histories as recurring and independently significant rather than as footnotes to a mainland-centered narrative.

Section 1.8

Natural Resources and the Long-Term Economy

The archipelago's natural endowments shaped the long-term economic possibilities available to the societies that developed within it. Extensive forest cover across much of the mountainous terrain supplied timber for construction, shipbuilding, and fuel throughout Japanese history, and later chapters will discuss both the economic importance of forestry and the periodic problems of deforestation and resource management that accompanied population growth and urban construction, particularly in the early modern period examined in Part VII.

Mineral resources, though not uniformly abundant, included regionally significant deposits of gold, silver, copper, and iron sand, which supported craft production, currency, and, from the sixteenth century onward, substantial foreign trade, discussed in Chapters 37 and 48. Volcanic activity, while hazardous, also produced hot springs (onsen) that became significant sites of bathing culture, medicine, and later tourism, and geothermal features that remain economically significant today.

The surrounding seas provided a major source of dietary protein in many regions and periods, through fish and other seafood harvested using techniques ranging from Jōmon-period shell-gathering and early fishing described in Chapters 3 and 4 to the large-scale commercial fisheries of the early modern and modern periods, though the importance of fish and seafood relative to rice, other grains, vegetables, and soybean products varied considerably by region, social status, and historical period, as isotopic and dietary evidence discussed in later chapters shows. The rich fishing grounds off the Sanriku coast, produced by the dynamic interaction of the Kuroshio and Oyashio currents within their transition/confluence zone discussed in Section 1.3, remain among the most productive in the world and have supported coastal communities for millennia.

Agriculturally, the archipelago's limited plains, discussed in Section 1.2, constrained the total area available for cultivation, but the combination of adequate rainfall, warm summers across most of the main islands, and river-built alluvial soil made intensive wet-rice agriculture, once introduced during the Yayoi period, highly productive per unit of cultivated land relative to many other agricultural systems, a pattern with consequences for population density, social organization, and taxation examined throughout this textbook.

Section 1.9

Geography, Evidence, and the Historian's Task

Reconstructing the archipelago's physical history draws on evidence quite different from the documentary sources historians rely on for political or cultural history. Geologists and paleoclimatologists study sediment cores from lakes, bogs, and the seafloor, tree-ring records (dendrochronology), coral growth bands, pollen records, speleothems (cave mineral deposits), and other paleoclimate proxies to reconstruct ancient climate and sea-level change. Archaeologists studying early settlement, discussed in Chapter 2, work closely with these physical scientists to correlate human occupation sites with past coastlines, vegetation, and climate conditions. Volcanologists and seismologists reconstruct the eruption and earthquake history of the archipelago through tephra layers, fault-trenching studies, and, for more recent centuries, written historical records.

From at least the eighth century CE, written sources begin to supplement this physical evidence directly. The fudoki, provincial gazetteers compiled at imperial request beginning in 713 CE, recorded local geography, place names, natural products, and regional traditions for many provinces of the emerging Japanese state, and portions of several fudoki, including the well-preserved Izumo Fudoki, survive today as valuable, though not unproblematic, sources for early historical geography, examined further in the Primary Source Window below. Later court chronicles, temple records, and provincial administrative documents added further detail on natural disasters, land use, and regional conditions across the centuries that followed.

Students should approach geographic history with the same critical attention historians bring to any other evidence. Physical reconstructions carry their own margins of uncertainty and are subject to revision as new techniques and data become available. Written sources such as the fudoki reflect the perspectives, political purposes, and limitations of the court and provincial officials who compiled them, and should not be read as neutral, comprehensive surveys of the actual conditions and populations of the provinces they describe. Above all, students should resist the temptation to treat geography as a simple, deterministic explanation for later historical outcomes — a caution developed further in the Historical Debate feature below.

Section 1.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has introduced the physical landscape, seas, climate, and natural hazards of the Japanese archipelago as a foundation for the historical narrative that begins in Chapter 2. Before moving into the specific content of subsequent chapters, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational geographic concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: Japan is an archipelago whose mountainous terrain historically limited settlement and agriculture to a relatively small proportion of total land area, concentrated on scattered plains. The surrounding and internal seas functioned historically as much as connective routes as barriers, linking the archipelago to the Asian mainland and linking its own regions to one another. The monsoon climate produced a recurring agricultural rhythm across most of the main islands, while considerable regional climatic variation distinguished Hokkaido, the central islands, and the Ryukyus from one another. The archipelago's position on the Pacific Ring of Fire has made earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and tsunami a recurring feature of its history rather than an occasional interruption. Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands developed distinct historical trajectories that this textbook treats as integral rather than peripheral. Reconstructing this physical history draws on a combination of scientific and documentary evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations.

Primary Source Window

The Izumo Fudoki, completed 733 CE

Creator: Compiled by provincial officials of Izumo Province under imperial order · Date: completed 733 CE · Type: Provincial gazetteer describing geography, place names, local products, and regional traditions

Context: In 713 CE the imperial court ordered each province of the emerging Japanese state to compile a fudoki, a gazetteer recording local geography, the origins of place names, natural resources and products, and notable local traditions, partly for administrative purposes and partly to document the diversity of the provinces the court claimed to govern. Most provincial fudoki survive today only in fragments or later quotations, but the Izumo Fudoki, covering a province on the Sea of Japan coast of western Honshu, survives in a comparatively complete form and is considered one of the most valuable sources for early eighth-century regional geography and local tradition.

The Izumo Fudoki describes the province's coastline, mountains, rivers, and shrines in systematic detail, records local explanations for place names, and includes local legendary traditions, such as an account describing the land of Izumo as having been enlarged by pulling additional land toward it with ropes, alongside straightforward geographic and administrative description.

Analysis: The Izumo Fudoki is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates that geographic knowledge in early Japan was not purely descriptive; it blended practical administrative geography with local religious tradition, place-name legend, and provincial identity. Historians must therefore read the fudoki critically, distinguishing between passages that likely reflect direct observation of physical geography and passages that record legendary or origin traditions valuable for understanding local belief and identity but not intended, even by their compilers, as literal geographic fact. The fudoki also reflects the perspective of provincial officials answerable to the imperial court, and its selection of what to record was shaped by that administrative purpose.

Questions for students:

  1. Why would the eighth-century imperial court have wanted systematic gazetteers describing the geography and products of each province?
  2. How should historians distinguish between the fudoki's descriptive geographic content and its inclusion of legendary place-name traditions?
  3. What does the survival of only a few relatively complete fudoki, out of many originally compiled, suggest about the limitations of the documentary record for early Japanese historical geography?
  4. What can a source like the Izumo Fudoki tell historians about regional identity in the eighth century, and what can it not tell them about earlier periods lacking similar documentation?
Historical Debate

Was Japan's Geography an Isolating or a Connecting Force?

The question: Popular narratives about Japanese history have long emphasized the archipelago's maritime separation from the Asian mainland as a source of relative isolation, cultural distinctiveness, and, in some older scholarship, an explanation for a supposedly unique or homogeneous national character. Other historians and geographers emphasize that the surrounding seas functioned historically as active routes of migration, trade, diplomacy, and cultural exchange, making the archipelago a participant in, rather than an outlier from, broader East Asian and Pacific historical currents. How should historians weigh these two emphases against one another?

Interpretation A — Geography as a source of relative isolation and distinctiveness: Some historians and geographers argue that the archipelago's separation from the mainland by open water, however crossable, meaningfully slowed and filtered the flow of people, technology, and ideas relative to societies connected by open land frontiers, contributing to distinctive linguistic, cultural, and political developments, and providing a degree of protection from large-scale invasion for much of Japanese history, most notably during the failed Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century discussed in Chapter 27.
Interpretation B — Geography as an active connective medium: Other historians emphasize that the same seas carried Yayoi-period migration and agricultural technology from the Korean Peninsula, Buddhism and Chinese court culture through Korea and directly from China, continuous diplomatic and commercial contact with Korea, China, and later Ryukyu and Europe, and that treating the archipelago as fundamentally isolated risks minimizing the depth of its historical integration into continental and maritime East Asian networks, a risk this textbook's treatment of Tokugawa "seclusion" in Chapter 48 addresses directly.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same physical geography and the same historical record of migration, trade, and diplomacy, but differ in how much relative weight to give the difficulty of sea crossing versus the demonstrated frequency and historical significance of the contact that did occur across those same seas.

Why it matters: How a historian resolves this question shapes how they narrate nearly every subsequent period in this textbook, from the interpretation of Yayoi migration and Buddhist transmission to the interpretation of Tokugawa-era foreign relations and the shock of Perry's arrival examined in Chapter 51.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain whether you think "isolating" or "connecting" is the more historically accurate single-word description of the sea's role — or whether the question itself may rest on a false choice.

Multiple Perspectives

Living With the Land: Perspectives on the Archipelago's Geography

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians, archaeologists, and geographers have identified across many sources and periods. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a coastal fishing community, any period: The sea is a primary source of food and livelihood and a familiar, navigable space rather than an empty barrier, but it is also unpredictable and, at times, catastrophically dangerous, as sudden storms and tsunami could destroy boats, homes, and lives with little warning.
From the perspective of an inland mountain-valley farming community, any period: Steep terrain limits the amount of land available for cultivation and can isolate a community from neighboring valleys for much of the year, encouraging local self-sufficiency, distinct local traditions, and strong attachment to a specific home valley or watershed.
From the perspective of an Ainu community of Hokkaido/Ezo, premodern period: The northern environment supports a mixed economy centered on hunting, river and coastal fishing (including salmon), gathering, some cultivation, and extensive regional trade, organized around different seasonal rhythms and land-use practices than the wet-rice agriculture of Wajin society to the south, and the boundary zone across the Tsugaru Strait is a site of both exchange and, at times, conflict.
From the perspective of a Ryukyuan mariner or merchant, premodern period: The southern islands' geography places them within reach of maritime trade routes connecting Japan, China, Korea, and Southeast Asia, making the sea a source of prosperity and cosmopolitan contact rather than primarily a hazard or a barrier.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Geography Across the Centuries

What Changed

  • The precise shoreline of the archipelago shifted with post-glacial sea-level rise and, in some regions, later land reclamation projects
  • Human technology for managing rivers, flood control, land reclamation, and maritime travel improved substantially across the centuries, altering how communities could respond to the same underlying physical conditions
  • The political incorporation of Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands into a unified Japanese state occurred only in the modern era, changing their administrative relationship to the central islands without changing their underlying geography
  • The scale, density, and location of human settlement changed dramatically with population growth, urbanization, and industrialization

What Stayed the Same

  • The archipelago's fundamental mountainous terrain and the limited proportion of land suitable for intensive settlement
  • The monsoon climate rhythm and its regional variation from Hokkaido to the Ryukyus
  • The archipelago's position on the Pacific Ring of Fire and its resulting exposure to earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and tsunami
  • The sea's dual role as both a connective route to the wider world and an internal and external boundary

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: assuming that Japan's geography has produced a single unchanging historical pattern, or assuming that human societies have simply overridden their physical environment without lasting constraint.

Cause and Effect

Disaster, Adaptation, and Institutions

Long-term causes

The archipelago's position at the junction of several tectonic plates produces chronic exposure to earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and tsunami, while its monsoon climate produces recurring flooding and typhoon risk.

Short-term causes

A specific earthquake, eruption, flood, or typhoon strikes a particular region in a particular year, often with limited warning under premodern conditions.

Effect

Loss of life, destruction of settlements and agricultural land, disruption of food supply, and, in severe cases, broader social and political stress, examined for specific disasters in later chapters such as 44, 71, 98, and 99.

Long-term institutional response

Communities and political authorities across Japanese history developed recurring institutional responses to disaster risk, including irrigation and flood-control engineering, granaries and famine relief measures, and, in the modern era, building codes, early-warning systems, and disaster-response agencies.

Historical complexity: The relationship between environmental hazard and social or political outcome is rarely simple or automatic; the same kind of disaster produced different consequences depending on existing political institutions, economic conditions, and social organization at the time it occurred.

Everyday Life and Environment

Why Geography Belongs in the History of Ordinary Life

The physical conditions described in this chapter shaped ordinary daily experience across Japanese history in ways that recur throughout this textbook's treatment of social and everyday life. Diet depended heavily on locally available resources: coastal communities relied heavily on fish and other seafood, while inland and mountain communities depended more on grains, foraged foods, and, from the Yayoi period onward, wet-rice cultivation wherever suitable plains and water supply existed. Housing construction responded to climate and disaster risk, with building techniques adapted, over the centuries, to snow loads in the north, humidity and typhoon winds in the south, and, in many regions, the recurring threat of earthquake damage.

Seasonal rhythms tied to the monsoon climate and the agricultural calendar structured village life, festivals, and religious observance across many centuries, a pattern examined further in later chapters covering Jōmon and Yayoi society, Tokugawa village life, and beyond. Disaster recovery itself became, over the long span of Japanese history, a recurring form of communal and, later, institutional experience, shaping village cooperation, religious ritual addressing natural forces, and eventually formal state disaster policy. Understanding these everyday dimensions of the physical environment helps prevent this textbook's later chapters on political, cultural, and economic history from losing sight of the ordinary material conditions within which most people in Japanese history actually lived.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced the Japanese archipelago's physical geography as the environmental foundation for the historical narrative that follows across the remaining ninety-nine chapters of this textbook. The archipelago consists of four large islands — Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu — together with thousands of smaller islands, including the Ryukyu chain, spread across a long, narrow arc of the western Pacific. Its terrain is overwhelmingly mountainous, concentrating settlement and agriculture onto a comparatively small number of plains whose relative size and location shaped where political and economic power historically concentrated.

The surrounding and internal seas have functioned throughout Japanese history as both connective routes and dividing boundaries. The Seto Inland Sea linked the Kinai region to Kyushu and, through the Korea Strait, to the Asian continent, while the Tsugaru Strait marked a persistent boundary between Wajin and Ainu societies. The warm Kuroshio and cold Oyashio currents shaped regional climate and supported some of the richest fisheries in the region. The archipelago's monsoon climate produced a recurring agricultural rhythm across most of the main islands while also producing substantial regional variation, from Hokkaido's cold winters to the Ryukyu Islands' subtropical warmth.

The chapter has given sustained attention to the archipelago's position on the Pacific Ring of Fire, and to the earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and tsunami that have recurred throughout its documented and undocumented history alike. Rather than treating natural disaster as an occasional interruption to Japanese history, this chapter has established disaster and recovery as a structural, recurring theme that later chapters will revisit directly in the contexts of the Great Kantō earthquake, the Kobe earthquake, and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami and Fukushima disaster.

The chapter has also insisted on the archipelago's regional diversity, giving Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands independent attention from this very first chapter rather than introducing them only when they were incorporated into an expanding Japanese state. This choice reflects a commitment that recurs throughout the textbook: Ainu and Ryukyuan histories are treated as integral parts of the archipelago's story, with their own distinct environmental and historical trajectories, rather than as peripheral footnotes to a mainland-centered narrative.

Finally, the chapter has addressed the kinds of evidence — geological, climatological, archaeological, and documentary — that allow historians and scientists to reconstruct the archipelago's physical history, and has cautioned against treating geography as a simple, deterministic explanation for later historical, cultural, or political outcomes. The Historical Debate feature on whether the sea functioned primarily as an isolating or a connecting force illustrated this challenge concretely, previewing a tension this textbook will revisit throughout its treatment of Japan's relationship with Korea, China, Europe, and the wider world.

These geographic foundations are not incidental background. They recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of prehistoric settlement, ancient state formation, medieval warrior government, early modern social and economic life, modern industrialization and empire, and the disasters and demographic transformations of contemporary Japan.

Key Terms

Archipelago
A group or chain of islands, typically formed by related geological processes; used to describe the Japanese islands as a physical rather than political unit.
Tectonic plate
A large section of Earth's crust whose movement and interaction with neighboring plates produces mountain-building, earthquakes, and volcanic activity.
Subduction zone
A boundary where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another, producing intense seismic and volcanic activity; central to the Japanese archipelago's geological formation.
Ring of Fire
The horseshoe-shaped zone of intense seismic and volcanic activity encircling the Pacific Ocean basin, within which Japan lies.
Tsunami
A series of long, powerful ocean waves generated by undersea earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or landslides; a Japanese-origin term now used internationally.
Shinsai
Earthquake disaster, as used in the historical names of major Japanese earthquakes discussed later in this textbook.
Saigai
A general Japanese term for disaster of any kind.
Monsoon
A seasonal change in atmospheric circulation and prevailing winds that affects temperature and precipitation; shapes the annual climate rhythm across most of the Japanese archipelago, though its specific seasonal expression varies by region.
Tsuyu / Baiu
The early-summer rainy season affecting most of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu.
Taifū (Typhoon)
A tropical cyclone; typhoon season affects the archipelago in late summer and early autumn.
Kuroshio Current
A warm ocean current flowing northeastward along Japan's Pacific coast, moderating climate and supporting fisheries.
Oyashio Current
A cold ocean current flowing southward along Japan's northeastern Pacific coast; its interaction with the Kuroshio within a dynamic transition/confluence zone supports especially rich and variable fishing grounds.
Tephra
Layers of volcanic ash and rock fragments deposited by eruptions, used by geologists and archaeologists to date and correlate historical events.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch, roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated glacial cycles; the period during which early humans first reached the archipelago (Chapter 2).
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago, during which the archipelago's modern coastline and climate patterns took shape.
Land bridge
A temporary land connection between landmasses exposed during periods of lower sea level, relevant to early human and animal movement onto the archipelago.
Alluvial plain
Flat lowland built up by sediment deposited by rivers; the archipelago's alluvial plains became its most agriculturally productive regions.
Ezo / Ezochi
Ezo historically referred both to the peoples of Hokkaido and surrounding lands and, more broadly, to those northern territories themselves; Ezochi specifically denotes the territory as administered or claimed by the Japanese state before Hokkaido's formal incorporation in the Meiji period.
Wajin
A term whose meaning shifted across history; in the context of the northern frontier, it commonly identifies Japanese-speaking settlers and traders from the main islands in contrast with the Ainu, rather than serving as a fixed, timeless ethnic label.
Ainu
The indigenous people of Hokkaido and surrounding regions, with a distinct language, a mixed economy of fishing, hunting, gathering, some cultivation, and extensive regional trade, and a distinct culture, discussed further in later chapters.
Ryukyu
The island chain extending southwest from Kyushu, home to a distinct society and, from unification around 1429, an independent kingdom that fell under constrained Satsuma/Tokugawa domination after 1609 while retaining its monarchy and tributary ties to China until its 1879 abolition, examined in Chapter 32.
Fudoki
Provincial gazetteers compiled from 713 CE recording local geography, place names, products, and traditions.
Environmental determinism
The theory that physical geography directly and predictably determines cultural or historical outcomes; a framework this textbook treats with caution.
Regional diversity
The recognition that the Japanese archipelago varies significantly by region rather than forming a single uniform physical or cultural space.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Dendrochronology
The scientific method of dating events and environmental conditions using patterns of tree rings.
Sediment core
A cylindrical sample of layered sediment extracted from a lake, bog, or seafloor, used to reconstruct past climate and environmental conditions.

Research or Library Extension

MGU Library Research Project

Mapping a Region of the Japanese Archipelago

This project asks you to investigate the historical geography of one region of the Japanese archipelago in greater depth, using library and reference resources to connect physical geography to later historical development.

  1. Choose a region. Select one region discussed in this chapter, such as the Kantō Plain, the Kinai region, Tōhoku, Hokkaido, or the Ryukyu Islands.
  2. Research physical geography. Using library resources, atlases, and reliable academic sources, describe your chosen region's terrain, climate, rivers or coastline, and natural hazards.
  3. Research historical settlement. Identify how and when human communities historically settled and made use of this region, drawing on later chapters as they become relevant.
  4. Connect geography to history. Explain at least two ways in which your chosen region's physical geography shaped its historical political, economic, or cultural development.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay (or equivalent multimedia presentation, including an original map), explain your chosen region's geography and historical development, using careful language such as "historians generally agree" or "this remains debated" rather than overstating certainty.

Library resources to use: Physical and historical atlases of Japan, academic encyclopedias, geological and seismological survey publications, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Japanese historical geography (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the physical groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces — distinguishing geography from culture, recognizing the sea as both connector and boundary, treating disaster as a recurring structural theme, and keeping Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands visible from the very beginning — are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

The Japanese archipelago's history is, in a fundamental sense, a history of human communities repeatedly adapting to, working with, and occasionally being overwhelmed by a demanding physical environment: mountainous terrain that limited and concentrated settlement, seas that connected the archipelago to a wider East Asian and Pacific world while also dividing its own regions from one another, a monsoon climate that structured agricultural life, and a restless geological foundation that has produced some of the most destructive natural disasters in recorded history. None of this determined what kind of societies, states, or cultures would emerge from the archipelago — that story, beginning with the earliest human settlement in Chapter 2, is the subject of the following ninety-nine chapters.

Students who understand that geography creates conditions rather than destinies, that the sea has been a highway as often as a wall, and that Hokkaido and the Ryukyu Islands possess histories of their own rather than existing merely as later additions to a mainland story, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook — from the Paleolithic settlement of Chapter 2 through the Jōmon and Yayoi worlds of Part I, the ancient and medieval states of Parts II through V, the early modern order of Part VII, the modern transformations of Parts VIII through XI, and the postwar and contemporary Japan of Parts XII through XIV — with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians and geographers bring to the physical world in which human history unfolds.

The story this textbook tells extends from the ancient tectonic processes that opened the Sea of Japan through the unresolved questions of twenty-first-century Japan examined in Chapter 100. It is a story shaped, at every stage, by mountains, coastlines, rivers, and the restless earth beneath them — not a predetermined outcome of that geography, but a long history of human societies making choices, building institutions, and rebuilding after disaster within the physical world this chapter has introduced.
