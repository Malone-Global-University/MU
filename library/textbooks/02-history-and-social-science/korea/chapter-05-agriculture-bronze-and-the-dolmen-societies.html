5

Agriculture, Bronze, and the Dolmen Societies

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Scattered across the Korean peninsula, from the hills above the Han River to the coastal terraces of the southwest, stand tens of thousands of massive stone monuments that predate any Korean state, any Korean writing system, and any king whose name has survived. Someone organized the labor to quarry, move, and raise these stones. Understanding who, and why, requires looking past the monuments themselves to the agricultural, social, and technological transformations that made such labor possible.

By the time the last Neolithic fishing and gathering communities described in Chapter 4 had given way to new forms of settlement, sometime in the centuries around 1500 to 1000 BCE, the peninsula's human landscape was changing in ways that would eventually make organized states possible. Villages grew larger and stayed in one place longer. Fields expanded. Storage pits multiplied. A new, comparatively plain style of pottery, conventionally called Mumun, replaced the elaborately decorated Jeulmun wares that had dominated the peninsula for millennia. Somewhere in this same broad span of centuries, communities on the peninsula began working a new material, bronze, whose blue-green weathered surface and sharp cutting edge marked out its owners as different from their neighbors.

None of these changes happened everywhere at once, and none of them by itself created a Korean state. Millet cultivation had already been practiced for centuries before the Bronze Age began, and stone tools continued to do most of the peninsula's daily work long after bronze objects first appeared in graves and hoards. Rice, when it did spread widely, did so unevenly, taking hold first in some river valleys and coastal plains while remaining marginal or absent elsewhere for generations. Bronze itself was never abundant enough, or evenly enough distributed, to replace stone and wood as the everyday materials of Korean life; it functioned instead as a marker of status, ritual authority, and long-distance connection, controlled by a comparatively small number of people even where it appeared.

What did change, cumulatively and unevenly across the peninsula, was the scale and permanence of human communities and the degree of inequality within them. Villages that had once housed a few dozen people in scattered pit-houses grew into settlements of hundreds, organized around cleared agricultural fields, storage facilities, and, in some places, ditches or palisades that suggest organized defense. Graves that had once held broadly similar assortments of goods began to show sharp differences in size, construction, and contents, some containing bronze daggers, polished jade ornaments, or elaborate stone chambers, while most contained little or nothing at all. And across the landscape, in numbers found almost nowhere else in the world at comparable density, communities began raising dolmens, megalithic tombs whose construction required the coordinated labor of dozens or, for exceptionally massive monuments, hundreds of people, while smaller monuments required considerably less labor.

This chapter examines these interlocking transformations, agricultural intensification, more permanent settlement, bronze technology and the exchange networks that carried it, monumental tomb construction, and growing social inequality, as a connected process rather than as separate curiosities. It resists two temptations that make this period easy to misunderstand. The first temptation is to treat the appearance of bronze as though it single-handedly created a new age, as if metal alone explains social change; in fact, stone tools, agricultural labor, and settlement organization mattered more to most people's daily lives than bronze ever did. The second temptation is to read the scale of dolmen construction, or the presence of bronze daggers in a handful of graves, as proof that centralized kingdoms already existed on the peninsula by the second or first millennium BCE. The evidence supports a more modest and, in some ways, more interesting conclusion: increasingly complex, increasingly unequal, and increasingly interconnected communities, capable of mobilizing substantial labor and controlling access to prestige goods. For most of the period this chapter covers, the evidence does not securely demonstrate the kind of centralized state institutions this textbook begins to describe in Chapter 6, although, as this chapter's final section explains, the later part of this sequence overlaps chronologically with the contested early development of Gojoseon.

Archaeology carries almost the entire evidentiary weight of this chapter. No contemporary written text describes these societies in their own words; the peoples who built the dolmens, worked the fields, and cast the bronze daggers left no inscriptions explaining their political organization, their beliefs, or their names for themselves. What survives are settlement remains, agricultural tools and their traces, pottery, bronze objects, and, above all, tens of thousands of megalithic burial monuments, along with the skeletal remains, grave goods, and construction techniques associated with them. Interpreting this evidence requires the same discipline introduced in earlier chapters: attention to regional variation, caution about projecting later political categories backward, and honesty about what the material record can and cannot establish.

By the chapter's end, students should understand how agricultural surplus, more permanent settlement, controlled access to bronze and other prestige materials, monumental labor mobilization, and sharpening social differentiation combined to produce communities capable, eventually, of supporting the early states examined in Chapter 6. They should also understand why the path from these Bronze Age communities to Gojoseon and the peninsula's other early states was gradual, regionally uneven, and shaped by processes that remain only partly visible in the surviving archaeological record.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the transition from Jeulmun to Mumun pottery traditions and explain why this change should not be read as evidence of sudden ethnic replacement.
  2. Explain the growing importance of intensive agriculture, including millet cultivation and the uneven spread of rice, and its effects on settlement and labor.
  3. Describe the Mumun settlement world, including village size, household organization, and settlement hierarchy.
  4. Explain the social significance of bronze technology as a prestige and ritual material rather than an everyday replacement for stone tools.
  5. Analyze dolmen construction as evidence of labor mobilization, social differentiation, and ritual practice, while avoiding the assumption that dolmens prove the existence of kings or states.
  6. Assess evidence for emerging inequality, including differences in house size, grave goods, and access to prestige materials.
  7. Explain the appearance of fortified settlements and evidence of conflict without treating warfare as an inevitable consequence of farming.
  8. Trace regional exchange networks connecting the peninsula to Liaodong, Manchuria, the Yellow Sea zone, and the Japanese archipelago.
  9. Describe everyday agricultural, household, and craft labor during the Mumun period.
  10. Evaluate how archaeologists distinguish increasing social complexity from the existence of centralized states.
  11. Identify major regional variations in Bronze Age Korea rather than treating the peninsula as historically uniform.
  12. Explain why this chapter stops short of narrating Gojoseon and the peninsula's earliest attested state-level polities.

  3. Why should bronze objects on the peninsula be understood chiefly as prestige and ritual goods rather than as everyday tools?

Historical Context

Chapter 4 traced the peninsula's Neolithic communities from roughly the eighth millennium BCE onward: pottery-using, fishing, gathering, and increasingly cultivating populations settled along the coasts and river valleys, connected to neighboring regions through maritime exchange across the Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait. Toward the end of that Neolithic sequence, communities in several regions of the peninsula had already begun cultivating millet and other crops on a modest scale, supplementing rather than replacing fishing, shellfish gathering, and hunting.

The centuries between roughly 1500 and 1000 BCE, with considerable regional variation in exact timing, mark a broad transition to the archaeological period this textbook, following standard convention, calls the Mumun period, named for the largely undecorated ("mumun," or "patternless") pottery that replaced the elaborately incised Jeulmun wares of the preceding millennia. This transition unfolded unevenly: some regions show a comparatively abrupt shift in material culture, while others show gradual overlap between Jeulmun and Mumun traditions over an extended period. Archaeologists debate how much of this change reflects the movement of new populations into the peninsula, the spread of new technologies and practices among existing populations, or some combination of the two, and this textbook treats that question as genuinely open rather than settled.

This chapter picks up directly where Chapter 4 left off, examining how communities already practicing limited cultivation moved toward more intensive agriculture, larger and more permanent settlements, and new forms of social differentiation, while a new metal technology, bronze, began to circulate through exchange networks linking the peninsula to Liaodong and Manchuria. Most of the sequence examined here precedes secure evidence of centralized state-level political organization, and this textbook resists describing these communities as a state merely because a later state eventually emerged from broadly the same territory. By its terminal centuries, however, this sequence overlaps chronologically with the contested early emergence of Gojoseon, examined in Chapter 6, so this chapter's ending should be understood as a threshold shared with, rather than cleanly preceding, the developments that follow.

Section 5.1

From Jeulmun to Mumun: A Major and Regionally Variable Transition

The archaeological boundary between the Jeulmun and Mumun periods is defined primarily by pottery, but the transition it marks involved more than a change in ceramic style. Recent archaeological synthesis describes the onset of the Mumun agricultural economy, conventionally placed around 1500 BCE, as a pronounced transformation associated with the appearance of rice alongside millet, new house forms, new pottery, and an expanded polished stone tool kit, in some regions occurring with notable speed rather than as a slow, imperceptible drift. This chapter therefore treats the transition as major and regionally variable rather than assuming in advance that it must have been gradual everywhere. Jeulmun wares, examined in Chapter 4, were typically decorated with combed or incised patterns and associated with communities that combined fishing, shellfish gathering, hunting, and increasingly, limited cultivation. Mumun pottery, by contrast, is largely plain, more standardized in shape, and associated with communities relying more heavily on field agriculture. The label "Mumun," meaning roughly "patternless" or "undecorated," describes a ceramic style rather than a people, a language, or a political community, and students should resist the temptation to treat the pottery change as though it announced the arrival of a new nation.

Archaeologists have long debated what caused this ceramic and economic shift. One line of interpretation emphasizes population movement, suggesting that new groups, possibly connected to agricultural communities in Liaodong and Manchuria, moved into parts of the peninsula and introduced new pottery styles, crops, and settlement practices. Another line of interpretation emphasizes continuity, suggesting that existing Jeulmun populations gradually adopted new agricultural techniques, pottery styles, and settlement patterns already present in neighboring regions through exchange and cultural transmission rather than large-scale migration. A third position treats the transition as a combination of both processes, varying by region: some areas show evidence consistent with new settlement, while others show more continuous occupation with gradually changing material culture.

This textbook does not adopt a single resolution to this debate, because the evidence does not currently support one. What can be said with more confidence is that the transition was not uniform across the peninsula, and that it should not be assumed, as a matter of course, to have been either gradual everywhere or abrupt everywhere. In some regions, particularly parts of the northern and central peninsula, Mumun pottery, house forms, and settlement patterns appear relatively early and show a comparatively marked break from preceding Jeulmun material culture, developing in ways that suggest closer connections to continental Manchurian traditions. In other regions, especially parts of the south, the transition appears somewhat later and shows more evidence of continuity and overlap with preceding Jeulmun communities. Chronological overlap between the two pottery traditions has been documented at several sites, further complicating any single narrative that would describe the transition as either a uniform slow drift or a uniform sudden replacement across the whole peninsula.

Students should also resist reading this transition backward through the lens of later Korean ethnic or national identity. Whatever combination of migration and internal change produced the Mumun world, the people living through it did not understand themselves as ancestors of a future Korean nation, and archaeologists cannot responsibly use pottery styles alone to reconstruct language, ethnicity, or political identity for this period. The Mumun transition matters historically because of what it made possible, more intensive agriculture, larger settlements, and new forms of social organization, not because it can be equated with the beginning of "Korean" identity in any modern sense.

Section 5.2

Agricultural Intensification: Millet, Rice, and the Changing Countryside

Millet, principally foxtail millet and broomcorn millet, had already been cultivated on the peninsula in modest quantities before the Mumun period began, as Chapter 4 discussed. During the Mumun period, millet cultivation intensified and expanded, becoming a staple crop across much of the peninsula and supporting larger and more sedentary populations than the mixed foraging-and-cultivation economies of the preceding Neolithic era.

Alongside millet, archaeological evidence points to the appearance and, over time, growing importance of rice as part of the emerging Mumun agricultural complex from around 1500 BCE onward. Carbonized rice remains and rice-processing tools appear at Mumun sites across a broad span of this period, but the earlier centuries of the sequence generally show rice cultivated as one crop among several rather than as the basis of large, engineered paddy-field systems. This distinction matters: the presence of rice grains at a site is not equivalent to evidence of organized, irrigated wet-rice agriculture, and the two kinds of evidence should not be treated as automatically occurring together.

Clearer evidence for intensified, irrigated paddy-field agriculture becomes archaeologically visible somewhat later, especially during the Middle Mumun period, roughly 800 to 400 BCE, in favorable southern and south-central river valleys and lowlands where excavated field systems, bunded plots, and water-management features have been documented. Even at this later stage, rice-paddy intensification did not spread evenly across the peninsula. Drier northern and interior regions continued to rely more heavily on millet and other dry-field crops throughout the Mumun sequence, and fishing, shellfish gathering, and hunting continued to supplement agricultural production in many communities regardless of region. Students should therefore keep separate, in their own minds, the earlier and more modest appearance of rice cultivation and the later, more clearly documented development of intensified paddy agriculture in particular favored localities.

Distinguishing cultivation from fully intensive agriculture matters here. Cultivation, in the sense of planting and tending selected crops, had existed on the peninsula for centuries before the Mumun period. What changed across the Mumun sequence, and especially by its middle centuries in favored regions, was the scale of land clearance, the permanence of fields, the labor invested in irrigation and field preparation where paddy systems developed, and the development of storage infrastructure capable of holding surplus grain across multiple seasons. These changes together constitute agricultural intensification: not simply growing crops, but organizing labor, land, and storage around agriculture as the central economic activity of a community.

Agricultural intensification carried significant social consequences, though these should be stated as possibilities the evidence supports rather than as automatic outcomes. Clearing and maintaining fields, particularly paddy systems requiring irrigation management, demanded sustained, often cooperative labor extending across multiple growing seasons. Storage facilities, discussed further in Section 5.3, allowed communities to accumulate surplus grain, providing a buffer against poor harvests and also creating a resource that could, in principle, be unequally distributed, controlled, or contested. Research specifically examining storage and surplus at Mumun-period settlements cautions that clear evidence of centralized storage and routine elite appropriation of surplus is largely confined to a smaller number of larger, more central settlements, rather than demonstrated across most Mumun communities. The archaeological record from this period is therefore best read as showing larger and more permanent settlements built around agricultural production, together with growing opportunities for differentiation in wealth and status, which the following sections examine through more direct evidence such as grave goods and bronze access, rather than assuming that surplus by itself automatically produced hierarchy everywhere it appeared.

Section 5.3

The Mumun Settlement World

Mumun-period settlements differ from their Neolithic predecessors in scale, permanence, and internal organization. Whereas Jeulmun communities typically consisted of a modest number of pit-houses occupied for varying lengths of time, Mumun settlements in many regions grew into villages of dozens of pit-houses, sometimes arranged in rows or clusters that suggest planned or at least habitually reproduced spatial organization. Individual houses also changed in form during this period, in some regions becoming larger and more rectangular, with internal features such as hearths, storage pits, and postholes indicating roof-support structures.

Storage facilities multiplied alongside residential structures. Raised-floor storage buildings, storage pits dug into the ground, and large ceramic storage vessels appear with increasing frequency at Mumun sites, reflecting the growing importance of accumulated agricultural surplus discussed in the previous section. Some settlements also developed specialized structures that may have served communal or ritual functions, distinct from ordinary domestic pit-houses, though their precise use often remains uncertain.

Settlement hierarchy becomes visible in the archaeological record during this period, though it should not be overstated. Most Mumun settlements were modest agricultural villages of broadly similar size and organization. A smaller number of sites, however, show evidence of larger scale, more elaborate architecture, greater quantities of prestige goods, or strategically advantageous locations along rivers, coastal routes, or overland paths connecting agricultural regions to exchange networks. Archaeologists interpret these larger or more elaborately equipped sites as evidence of emerging settlement hierarchy, in which certain communities may have exercised greater influence, controlled more resources, or served as nodes in regional exchange, without necessarily implying that such settlements governed a centralized territorial state.

Regional variation remained pronounced throughout the Mumun period. Settlements in the central river valleys, where wet-rice cultivation was most developed, tended toward larger scale and more elaborate storage infrastructure than settlements in more mountainous or coastal regions relying more heavily on millet cultivation, fishing, or a mixed subsistence strategy. Northern settlements, closer to the Liaodong and Manchurian bronze-working networks discussed in Section 5.4, sometimes show earlier or more abundant access to bronze objects than settlements further south. Island and coastal communities maintained distinct economic profiles shaped by continued reliance on maritime resources alongside agriculture. This regional diversity should caution students against treating "the Mumun village" as a single uniform settlement type reproduced identically across the entire peninsula.

Section 5.4

Bronze Technology and Prestige Networks

Bronze metallurgy on the Korean peninsula produced copper alloys normally combining copper with tin, and in some objects with added lead, following alloying practices connected technologically and stylistically to bronze-working traditions already established in Liaodong and neighboring parts of Manchuria and northeastern China. This technological and stylistic connection should be distinguished from the separate question of where the raw metal itself originated: provenance studies of prehistoric Korean bronzes indicate that source materials could derive from both northern Chinese sources and from ore deposits within the southern Korean peninsula, so bronze production on the peninsula should not be assumed to have depended entirely on imported metal. Bronze objects begin appearing in the peninsula's archaeological record only partway through the broad Mumun sequence, well after Mumun pottery itself becomes established, and their precise dating remains subject to ongoing revision as new sites are excavated and analyzed.

It is important for students to understand what bronze did not do on the Korean peninsula during this period: it did not replace stone as the primary material for everyday tools. Stone remains overwhelmingly the dominant material for agricultural implements, woodworking tools, and household equipment throughout the Mumun period. Bronze was scarce, requiring specialized alloying and casting knowledge and control over the furnaces and molds needed for production, whatever the specific source of its raw materials. Where bronze objects appear in the archaeological record, they are concentrated disproportionately in graves, hoards, and contexts suggesting ritual or ceremonial use rather than ordinary daily labor.

The most distinctive bronze objects of this period are daggers, often described using the term "Liaoning-style" because closely related forms appear across Liaodong, southern Manchuria, and the Korean peninsula. These daggers, along with associated bronze mirrors, bells, and ornamental fittings found at some sites, indicate that peninsula communities participated in a broader northeastern Asian bronze-working and exchange network rather than developing bronze technology in isolation. The daggers' distribution and stylistic variation allow archaeologists to trace connections and, to some extent, regional workshops or production centers, though the exact mechanisms of exchange, whether through trade, gift exchange, marriage alliances, or the movement of skilled artisans, remain difficult to reconstruct with precision from archaeological evidence alone.

The social function of bronze during this period appears to have centered on status display, ritual practice, and, in some contexts, symbols of military or ceremonial authority. Bronze daggers recovered from elaborate graves, sometimes associated with dolmens or other substantial burial monuments discussed in Section 5.5, suggest that access to bronze correlated with elevated social status. This does not mean that everyone buried with bronze objects held political authority over a defined territory; it means that bronze functioned as one marker, among several, of differential access to prestige materials within increasingly stratified communities.

Students should also note a labeling complication. Korean archaeology has conventionally correlated the beginning of the Mumun period, around 1500 BCE, with the start of the peninsula's "Bronze Age," but the earliest secure bronze artifacts on the peninsula date from considerably later within that sequence, and bronze itself remained rare for centuries afterward. The period label therefore reflects a broad conventional correlation rather than a claim that bronze technology appeared at the same moment as Mumun pottery.

This chapter's account of bronze technology should not be read as implying that a "Bronze Age" on the Korean peninsula matched developments elsewhere in the world in either timing or character. Bronze arrived on the peninsula later than in parts of the Near East and considerably later than in parts of China, and it never displaced stone tools as completely as in some other Bronze Age societies. The Korean Bronze Age is therefore better understood as a regionally specific process, shaped by connections to Liaodong and Manchuria and by the particular agricultural and settlement transformations already underway on the peninsula, than as a direct parallel to Bronze Age developments elsewhere.

Historical Vocabulary
Mumun and Periodization

Definition. "Mumun" (literally "no pattern" or "patternless") describes a pottery tradition characterized by largely undecorated surfaces, used by archaeologists to name the broad period, roughly 1500 to 300 BCE with considerable regional variation, during which this pottery predominated on the Korean peninsula.

Historical context. The Mumun period corresponds roughly to what is sometimes called the Korean Bronze Age, though bronze itself appears only partway through the period and never as an everyday material, as Section 5.4 explains. Archaeologists sometimes subdivide the Mumun period into earlier, middle, and later phases based on pottery style, settlement pattern, and the presence or absence of bronze and, eventually, early iron objects.

Why it matters. Using "Mumun" as a period label, rather than a more loaded term such as "early Korean," allows historians to describe this era's material culture without prematurely assigning it a national or ethnic identity that the evidence cannot support.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should not assume that "Mumun" refers to a single people, language, or political community. It is a pottery-based archaeological label, applicable across what were almost certainly multiple distinct communities with varying degrees of contact and common ancestry.

Section 5.5

Dolmens and Mortuary Monumentality

No single body of evidence illustrates the social transformations of this period as vividly as the peninsula's dolmens, megalithic burial monuments constructed from large stone slabs. The Korean peninsula contains one of the highest concentrations of dolmens found anywhere in the world, with tens of thousands of examples surviving into the modern period, distributed unevenly but extensively from the northern peninsula through the southern coast and offshore islands.

Archaeologists commonly distinguish, with acknowledged regional overlap and ongoing refinement, several broad dolmen forms, of which two traditional major groupings are most often cited. "Table-type" dolmens, more common in the northern and central peninsula, consist of a large flat capstone raised above ground level on upright supporting slabs, forming a visible chamber. "Go-board-type" dolmens, more common in the southern peninsula, consist of a capstone resting on smaller support stones above a burial chamber constructed at or below ground level, so that the capstone sits closer to the ground, resembling, in the descriptive term long used by observers, the board used for the game of go. Korean archaeology also recognizes a third major grouping, sometimes called the "capstone type" or "unsupported-capstone type," in which the capstone rests directly on, or very close to, a buried burial chamber with minimal or no visible above-ground support stones. Further structural variants, mixed forms, and locally distinct burial arrangements have also been documented, and archaeologists continue to refine this typology as more sites are surveyed. The broad association of table-type forms with the north and go-board-type forms with the south should therefore be understood as a documented tendency rather than an absolute or exhaustive division, since all three major types, along with local variants, appear in varying proportions across different regions.

The construction of even a modest dolmen required substantial coordinated labor: quarrying or transporting a capstone that could weigh from several hundred kilograms to, in the case of the largest known examples, many tons, and positioning it precisely atop supporting stones or a prepared burial chamber. How builders moved the largest stones is not directly documented. Archaeologists and experimental reconstructions have proposed methods involving combinations of ropes, wooden rollers or sledges, earthen ramps, levering, and coordinated human labor, since the peninsula's Mumun communities lacked wheeled vehicles or draft animals of the kind used for comparable tasks in some other regions. These reconstructions demonstrate plausible means of transport and placement but should not be treated as surviving instructions for how every Mumun community actually built its dolmens. Large dolmens plausibly required the coordinated labor of dozens of people, and exceptionally massive monuments potentially many more, sustained over an extended period, while smaller monuments required considerably less labor and organization.

This labor investment is central to how historians interpret dolmens. A dolmen's construction required someone, whether a family, a lineage group, or a broader community, to mobilize significant labor, and the ability to command such labor implies some degree of social status or authority on the part of whoever the monument commemorated, or whoever organized its construction. Excavated dolmen burials sometimes contain bronze daggers, polished stone tools, jade or other ornamental beads, and pottery, while many other dolmens contain few or no surviving grave goods, either because they were originally modest burials or because looting, decay, or acidic soil conditions have destroyed perishable and even some durable materials over the intervening millennia.

It is essential, however, that students avoid overinterpreting dolmens as proof of centralized kingship or full state formation. Dolmen construction appears across a wide range of settlement sizes and regions, including areas that show little other evidence of settlement hierarchy or long-distance exchange, suggesting that the capacity to organize monumental labor was not confined to a small number of powerful, state-like centers. Communal or lineage-based labor mobilization, rather than command by a centralized ruler governing a defined territory, offers a more cautious and, in the view of many archaeologists, better-supported explanation for how most dolmens were built. Dolmens are best understood as strong evidence for social differentiation, ritual practice surrounding death, and the capacity for organized communal labor, not as direct evidence for kings, capitals, or bureaucratic states.

Section 5.6

Emerging Inequality and Ranked Communities

Multiple, independent lines of archaeological evidence point toward growing social differentiation during the Mumun period. House size varies noticeably at some settlements, with a small number of unusually large structures standing among more numerous smaller dwellings, suggesting differences in household wealth, labor access, or social standing. Grave treatment varies even more dramatically: alongside simple pit burials with few or no accompanying goods, the archaeological record includes elaborate dolmen burials, some containing bronze weapons, ornaments, and substantial quantities of pottery, indicating that some individuals, families, or lineages commanded significantly greater resources and labor than others.

Differential access to bronze provides another indicator of emerging inequality. Because bronze required specialized metallurgical knowledge, access to suitable copper-alloy raw materials, and participation in production or exchange networks connected technologically to Liaodong and Manchuria, the ability to acquire and display bronze objects marked a household or lineage as unusually well connected, skilled, or prestigious. The concentration of bronze daggers and related objects in a comparatively small number of graves, often though not exclusively associated with dolmens, is consistent with the emergence of distinguishable elite or high-status groups within Mumun communities.

Archaeologists and historians sometimes describe societies showing this pattern of differentiation, without full state institutions, as "ranked" or "chiefdom-like." These terms describe societies in which status, and sometimes access to resources or labor, is inherited or otherwise unequally distributed, and in which certain individuals or lineages may exercise informal or semi-formal authority over labor mobilization, ritual practice, or exchange relationships, but in which formal government institutions, bureaucracies, standing military forces, and centralized territorial administration, the hallmarks of a state examined more fully in Chapter 6, are not yet clearly documented.

This textbook uses "ranked" and "chiefdom-like" cautiously and provisionally, as analytical terms describing a pattern of evidence, rather than as a settled classification applicable uniformly across the entire peninsula. Some regions and periods within the broader Mumun sequence show stronger evidence of social ranking, including larger settlements, more elaborate mortuary monuments, and greater concentrations of prestige goods, than others. Students should therefore think of "increasing inequality" as a general trend documented with varying strength across different regions and time periods, rather than as a single, uniform social order that had been achieved everywhere on the peninsula by a particular date.

Section 5.7

Fortified Settlements and the Archaeology of Conflict

Alongside evidence of growing agricultural surplus, settlement scale, and social differentiation, archaeologists have identified a number of Mumun-period sites showing features associated with defense: encircling ditches, earthen or timber palisades, and settlement locations chosen for defensible terrain such as hilltops or promontories overlooking agricultural land. Weapon finds, including stone and, later, bronze projectile points and daggers, also appear with increasing frequency at some sites during the later Mumun period, and skeletal remains showing evidence of traumatic injury have been documented at several locations, though such finds remain limited in number and should not be generalized into a picture of constant, widespread warfare.

Historians and archaeologists offer several, not mutually exclusive, explanations for this evidence of fortification and conflict. Agricultural surplus and accumulated stores of grain, discussed in Section 5.2, represented a resource worth defending or, from another community's perspective, worth raiding, particularly during years of poor harvest elsewhere. Population growth and the expansion of cultivated land may have intensified competition over the most productive agricultural terrain, particularly wet-rice paddies requiring specific water and soil conditions. Control over bronze production, exchange routes, or prestige goods, discussed in Sections 5.4 and 5.6, may also have generated competition among emerging elite groups or communities seeking to establish or defend advantageous positions within regional exchange networks.

Students should resist the assumption that agricultural intensification made warfare an automatic or inevitable feature of Mumun society. Most Mumun settlements show no clear evidence of fortification, and many regions and periods within the broader sequence show little indication of large-scale conflict. Fortified or defensively situated sites appear to represent a subset of communities, likely responding to locally specific pressures, competition, or perceived threats, rather than a peninsula-wide condition of endemic war. The evidence supports a more modest conclusion: that increased surplus, settlement permanence, and social stratification created new stakes and new occasions for conflict in some places and periods, without transforming Mumun society as a whole into a uniformly militarized world.

Section 5.8

Regional Networks and Mobility

The connections across the Yellow Sea, the Korea Strait, and the Manchurian frontier that Chapter 4 identified as central to Neolithic life continued, and in some respects intensified, during the Mumun period. Bronze technology and its associated stylistic traditions, most visibly the Liaoning-style dagger discussed in Section 5.4, moved across the Liaodong-peninsula frontier through mechanisms that likely combined trade, gift exchange, the movement of skilled artisans, and, in some interpretations, the movement of small groups of people. Pottery styles, agricultural techniques, and ornamental traditions likewise show patterns of similarity and gradual transformation across this same broad zone, consistent with sustained contact rather than isolated parallel development.

To the south and east, evidence for contact between the peninsula and the Japanese archipelago becomes increasingly visible during this period. Pottery styles, stone tool forms, and, in later phases, agricultural techniques show connections across the Korea Strait. The evidence supports sustained interaction across the strait and, especially during the later Mumun, significant movement of agricultural populations and technologies from the southern Korean peninsula toward northern Kyushu during the emergence of Yayoi farming. Other forms of exchange and cultural interaction operated across the strait, but population movement should not automatically be assumed to have been symmetrical in both directions. These connections would become considerably more developed in the following centuries, examined in later chapters, but their roots extend back into the Mumun-period exchange networks described here.

Students should maintain careful geographic distinctions when discussing these connections. The Korean peninsula, Liaodong and southern Manchuria, the Yellow Sea maritime zone, and the Japanese archipelago were not single unified political or cultural blocks during this period; each contained multiple distinct communities with varying degrees of contact with their neighbors. Describing bronze-working traditions as shared across the peninsula and Liaodong does not imply that these regions formed a single people or state, any more than shared pottery traditions across the Korea Strait imply that the peninsula and the archipelago formed a single political community. These were connected but politically and culturally distinct regions, linked through exchange, mobility, and technological transmission rather than unified governance.

Section 5.9

Everyday Life and Labor in an Intensifying Agricultural World

Behind the dolmens, bronze daggers, and fortified settlements that dominate much of the archaeological record for this period lay the daily labor of ordinary households whose lives are far less visible in the surviving evidence but whose work made every other development in this chapter possible. Most Mumun-period people spent the greater part of their lives engaged in agricultural labor: clearing land, preparing fields, planting and tending millet and, where conditions allowed, rice, harvesting crops, and processing and storing grain for the months between harvests.

Seasonal labor demands shaped household and community rhythms. Field preparation and planting required concentrated effort in spring, while harvest demanded similarly intensive labor in autumn; wet-rice cultivation, where practiced, added the further seasonal burden of irrigation management. Storage, discussed in Section 5.3, required constructing and maintaining pits or raised structures capable of protecting grain from moisture, pests, and spoilage across the agricultural off-season, work that itself represented a significant investment of household or community labor.

Craft production continued alongside agricultural work. Stone tool manufacture, though gradually supplemented in prestige contexts by bronze, remained the dominant technology for agricultural implements, woodworking, and household tasks throughout this period. Pottery production, textile working using plant fibers, and, in coastal and riverine communities, continued fishing and shellfish gathering all contributed to household subsistence and material culture. Archaeological evidence for textile production, including spindle whorls and loom weights at some sites, indicates that spinning and weaving occupied meaningful portions of household labor, though the surviving evidence does not allow confident, generalizable conclusions about how such labor was divided by gender across all Mumun communities.

The relationship between household labor and communal labor deserves particular attention. Field clearance and irrigation construction, storage-facility building, and monumental dolmen construction, examined in Section 5.5, all required labor that extended beyond the capacity of a single household, implying some degree of cooperative organization at the level of extended family, lineage group, or village community. Historians cannot determine with precision how such cooperative labor was organized, whether through reciprocal obligation, kinship duty, communal custom, or emerging forms of directed authority, but its existence is clear from the scale of the resulting agricultural infrastructure and monuments alike.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reading Ordinary Life from an Unwritten Record

Because no Mumun-period community left written records, historians must reconstruct everyday life almost entirely from material remains: the size and layout of houses, the contents of storage pits, the wear patterns on stone tools, the residues found in cooking vessels, and, where preserved, plant and animal remains recovered through careful excavation and flotation techniques. This kind of evidence cannot recover individual names, personal stories, or specific historical events, but it can recover patterns: how large households typically were, what crops and other foods people relied upon, how labor was likely organized across a farming year, and how wealth and status differences were expressed in daily material surroundings as well as in death.

Much of what survives disproportionately reflects the more durable and more elaborately treated end of Mumun society, since dolmens and bronze objects are more likely to attract archaeological attention and more likely to survive intact than the modest pit-houses and simple burials of most of the population. Historians must therefore make a deliberate effort to study ordinary settlement remains, storage facilities, and simple burials alongside more spectacular monuments, in order to avoid a picture of Mumun society weighted too heavily toward its wealthiest and most visible members.

Section 5.10

Toward Political Complexity

By the later centuries of the Mumun period, several interlocking developments had transformed the peninsula's communities well beyond the scale and organization of the Neolithic societies described in Chapter 4. Agricultural intensification, particularly the spread of wet-rice cultivation in favored regions, had increased surplus production and supported larger, more permanent settlements. Settlement hierarchy had begun to emerge, with a smaller number of larger or more strategically situated communities occupying positions of greater influence within regional networks. Bronze technology, though never displacing stone as an everyday material, had introduced a scarce and prestigious resource whose control marked out emerging elites. Dolmen construction, on a scale and density found almost nowhere else in the world, demonstrated the capacity of Mumun communities to mobilize substantial coordinated labor for monumental, likely lineage-based or communal, purposes. Grave goods and settlement remains together indicate growing, though regionally uneven, social differentiation. And, in some regions, fortified settlements and weapon finds point to intensifying local competition, without suggesting peninsula-wide warfare.

Taken together, these developments describe communities with the organizational capacity, surplus production, and social differentiation that historians generally regard as preconditions for the emergence of more centralized political institutions. For most of the Mumun sequence, the archaeological evidence reviewed in this chapter does not securely demonstrate centralized state institutions across most of the peninsula: standing bureaucratic administration, systematic taxation extending across multiple communities, a centralized military organization capable of sustained campaigns beyond a local area, and written record-keeping are not clearly documented at Mumun-period sites for most of this span. By the fourth to third centuries BCE, however, terminal Mumun and Early Iron Age developments overlap chronologically with political developments associated with Gojoseon. The chronology, geography, and archaeological identification of that emerging polity remain debated and are examined in Chapter 6. The archaeological record for most of this chapter's period instead shows increasingly complex, increasingly unequal, but still largely locally organized communities, connected to one another and to neighboring regions through exchange, shared technology, and, in some cases, shared ritual practice, rather than unified under a single governing authority.

The transition from these Bronze Age communities to the documented early states examined in Chapter 6, beginning with Gojoseon, remains only partly visible in the archaeological record and almost certainly proceeded gradually and unevenly across different regions of the peninsula and neighboring Manchuria. Chapter 6 takes up this transition directly, examining both the later Bronze Age and early Iron Age developments that preceded organized statehood and the traditional and documentary evidence historians use to reconstruct Gojoseon's emergence. This chapter's task ends here, at the threshold of that story, having established the agricultural, technological, and social foundations upon which the peninsula's first states would eventually be built.

Historical Vocabulary
Dolmen, Megalith, and Mortuary Monumentality

Definition. A dolmen is a type of megalithic (large-stone) burial monument consisting of a heavy capstone supported above or over a burial chamber by smaller upright or supporting stones. "Mortuary monumentality" refers more broadly to the practice of marking death and burial with substantial, labor-intensive architecture.

Historical context. Dolmens appear across many parts of the world in different periods, but the Korean peninsula's concentration and diversity of dolmen forms, discussed in Section 5.5, is exceptional by global standards, making dolmen studies a central and distinctive part of Korean prehistoric archaeology.

Why it matters. Dolmens provide historians with rare, tangible evidence of labor organization, ritual practice, and social differentiation from a period that left no written record.

Potential misunderstanding. A dolmen's scale should not automatically be read as evidence of kingship. As Section 5.5 explains, communal or lineage-based labor mobilization offers a better-supported explanation for most dolmen construction than command by a centralized ruler.

Primary Source Window

Gochang Jukrim-ri Dolmen Group (Area II, Group 6) Excavation Report

Institution/Creator: National Wanju Cultural Heritage Research Institute · Publication date: 2025 · Location: Jukrim-ri dolmen group, Gochang County, part of the wider Gochang, Hwasun, and Ganghwa Dolmen Sites recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage property · Type: Official archaeological excavation report

Context: Because Mumun-period communities left no writing, the primary documentary evidence historians rely on for this chapter's subject is not an ancient text but a modern archaeological excavation report: a formal record produced by a professional research institute documenting the systematic investigation of a specific dolmen group. This 2025 report by the National Wanju Cultural Heritage Research Institute documents excavation of Area II, Group 6, within the Jukrim-ri dolmen cluster at Gochang, one of the densest and most thoroughly studied dolmen concentrations on the peninsula.

What the report records: A report of this kind documents each excavated dolmen's precise location and orientation, the dimensions and estimated weight of its capstone, the construction and dimensions of any surviving stone-lined or pit burial chamber, the arrangement of supporting stones, and an inventory of associated finds, such as pottery sherds, stone tools, and any surviving bronze or ornamental objects, along with soil stratigraphy and site-formation observations that help establish relative chronology and later disturbance.

Analysis: Excavation reports of this kind provide the primary evidentiary basis for nearly everything this chapter states about dolmen construction, mortuary treatment, and associated grave goods. A single report such as this one documents one excavated group within a much larger dolmen landscape; its value for the broader arguments made in this chapter comes from being combined with comparable reports from other groups and other regions, allowing archaeologists to identify patterns in monument scale, construction technique, and grave-good distribution of the kind discussed in Sections 5.5 and 5.6.

Limitations: A single excavation report describes a specific, limited set of monuments and cannot by itself establish peninsula-wide patterns. Like most dolmen sites, portions of the Jukrim-ri group show evidence of long-term disturbance from agricultural use, erosion, and earlier unsystematic investigation, meaning that recovered grave goods likely understate what these burials originally contained. As with all modern archaeological reports, this source documents prehistoric material remains as interpreted by present-day researchers; it is not a written source produced by the Mumun-period people who built and used these monuments, and its interpretive conclusions, such as proposed construction sequences or chronological placement, remain subject to revision as further evidence is recovered.

  2. Why can a single excavation report not, by itself, support conclusions about dolmen-building practices across the whole peninsula?
  3. Why might disturbance from agriculture, erosion, or earlier unsystematic digging cause historians to understate the original contents of dolmen burials?

Historical Debate

What Do Dolmens Prove About Political Organization?

The question: Does the scale and density of dolmen construction on the Korean peninsula indicate the presence of powerful chiefs or proto-kings capable of commanding large labor forces, or does it instead reflect widespread communal or lineage-based labor practices that did not depend on centralized political authority?

Interpretation A — Dolmens as evidence of concentrated elite authority: Some archaeologists emphasize that the largest dolmens, particularly those requiring the movement of multi-ton capstones, could only have been built through the organized command of substantial labor, and that the association of some dolmens with bronze weapons and other prestige goods suggests the emergence of powerful individual leaders or lineages capable of directing such projects.
Interpretation B — Dolmens as evidence of widespread communal practice: Other archaeologists point out that dolmens of varying, often modest, scale are found across a very wide range of settlement types and regions, including areas with little other evidence of settlement hierarchy, suggesting that the capacity to organize dolmen-building labor was broadly distributed among many communities through kinship or communal obligation, rather than concentrated in the hands of a small number of powerful rulers.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, dolmen scale, distribution, associated grave goods, and settlement context, but differ in how much political centralization they infer from labor mobilization capacity alone.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and scale: how much labor organization implies political centralization, and whether the largest dolmens represent a meaningfully different phenomenon from the much more numerous smaller and mid-sized examples.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how early, and how centralized, they believe Korean political authority became, and it directly affects how confidently this textbook, or any other, can describe Mumun-period societies as "chiefdoms" rather than simply "ranked communities."

Student task: Using the evidence summarized above, evaluate both interpretations and explain what additional evidence, such as evidence of labor specialization, long-distance resource control, or settlement hierarchy, would help historians decide between them.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading Mumun Society Through Different Bodies of Evidence

Note: because no Mumun-period text or first-person account survives, the categories below describe what different bodies of evidence allow historians to infer, rather than reconstructing the thoughts, attitudes, or experiences of specific prehistoric individuals.

Ordinary agricultural households — evidence from dwellings, agricultural tools, storage features, and food remains: The great majority of excavated Mumun-period pit-houses, stone tool assemblages, and modest storage pits indicate that most households organized their labor around field preparation, planting, harvest, and grain storage, with little or no access to bronze or elaborate burial treatment.
High-status mortuary contexts — evidence from monument scale, bronze objects, ornaments, and differential burial treatment: A smaller number of burials, including some but not all dolmens, contain bronze daggers, polished ornaments, or unusually elaborate construction, indicating that some households or lineages commanded significantly greater resources, labor, and prestige-goods access than the majority of the population.
Archaeologists interpreting Mumun social organization: Professional interpretation of settlement remains, mortuary variation, and prestige-goods distribution allows historians to identify patterns of social ranking and labor mobilization, while requiring caution against projecting later political categories, such as kingship or centralized statehood, onto evidence that may not support them.
Modern heritage communities managing dolmen landscapes: Present-day residents, local governments, and heritage authorities responsible for dolmen sites, including UNESCO-recognized groups such as those at Gochang, Hwasun, and Ganghwa, must balance ongoing archaeological research and public education against continued agricultural use, development pressure, and tourism in the surrounding landscape.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Neolithic World to the Threshold of Statehood

What Changed

  • Pottery style shifted from decorated Jeulmun wares to largely undecorated Mumun wares
  • Subsistence shifted from a mixed foraging-and-limited-cultivation economy toward more intensive agriculture, including millet and, regionally, wet-rice cultivation
  • Settlements grew larger, more permanent, and increasingly organized around storage infrastructure
  • A new prestige material, bronze, entered circulation through exchange with Liaodong and Manchuria
  • Mortuary practice diversified sharply, from broadly similar Neolithic burials toward dramatically unequal Mumun-period graves, including monumental dolmens
  • Some settlements developed defensive features, indicating new forms and stakes of local competition

What Stayed the Same

  • Fishing, shellfish gathering, and hunting continued to supplement agriculture in many communities
  • Stone remained the dominant material for everyday tools throughout the period
  • Maritime and overland connections to neighboring regions, established in the Neolithic period, continued and in some respects intensified
  • For most of the sequence examined in this chapter, the communities studied here are not securely documented as centralized states with standing bureaucracy or written record-keeping; by the terminal Mumun and Early Iron Age transition, however, this chronology begins to overlap with the contested emergence of Gojoseon discussed in Chapter 6

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the substantial changes and the important continuities prevents the twin errors of treating the Mumun period as a sudden revolution unconnected to what came before, or as simply "early Korea" already possessing fully formed state institutions throughout its span, rather than a long sequence whose final centuries approach, without narrating, the documented statehood examined in the next chapter.

Cause and Effect

Surplus, Labor, and the Roots of Social Hierarchy

Long-term causes

Centuries of gradually intensifying cultivation inherited from the late Neolithic period, combined with the peninsula's varied river valleys, coastal plains, and soils suited to different crops and degrees of agricultural investment.

Short-term causes

The spread of Mumun agricultural techniques, including wet-rice cultivation in favored regions, and the introduction of bronze through exchange networks with Liaodong, both concentrated within the broad Mumun sequence.

Effect

Agricultural surplus and storage capacity, combined with scarce and unevenly distributed prestige goods such as bronze, created opportunities for resources to be accumulated, displayed, exchanged, or contested unequally. These processes may have contributed to sharper differences in house size, grave wealth, and settlement scale, but the archaeological evidence does not demonstrate that surplus automatically produced hierarchy in every Mumun community.

Longer-term consequence

Increasingly ranked, labor-capable communities, exemplified by dolmen construction, whose organizational capacity contributed to conditions favorable to more centralized political institutions; by the terminal Mumun and Early Iron Age transition, this chronology overlaps with the contested early emergence of Gojoseon examined in Chapter 6.

Historical complexity: No single cause, neither bronze nor rice nor dolmen construction alone, explains the growth of social inequality during this period; historians instead point to the interaction of agricultural surplus, labor organization, prestige-goods exchange, and mortuary competition as a mutually reinforcing, but not strictly deterministic, set of processes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of peninsula communities from the mixed foraging-and-cultivation societies of the late Neolithic period, examined in Chapter 4, into the more agriculturally intensive, more permanently settled, and increasingly unequal communities of the Mumun period. It began by explaining the shift from decorated Jeulmun pottery to largely undecorated Mumun wares, cautioning against reading this ceramic change as evidence of sudden population replacement or the arrival of a new ethnic identity, and emphasizing instead the genuinely unresolved scholarly debate over how much this transition reflects migration, internal cultural change, or some combination of both.

The chapter then examined agricultural intensification, distinguishing the long-established practice of limited cultivation from the more thoroughgoing transformation represented by expanded millet farming and, in favorable river valleys and coastal plains, the uneven spread of wet-rice cultivation. It explained how these agricultural changes supported larger, more permanent Mumun settlements, organized increasingly around storage infrastructure and, in a smaller number of cases, showing evidence of settlement hierarchy.

Bronze technology received sustained attention as a socially significant but never everyday material, arriving through exchange networks connecting the peninsula to Liaodong and Manchuria and appearing disproportionately in graves and ritual contexts rather than ordinary domestic tool assemblages. The chapter used Liaoning-style bronze daggers as a case study in how shared material culture reflects regional exchange networks without implying unified political identity across the peninsula and neighboring Manchuria.

Dolmens occupied a central place in the chapter's argument, treated as this period's clearest surviving evidence of coordinated labor mobilization, ritual practice, and social differentiation. The chapter distinguished table-type, go-board-type, and capstone/unsupported-capstone dolmen forms, while emphasizing that these are broad archaeological groupings with regional overlap, local variants, and ongoing typological refinement, and while insisting, through both the main narrative and the Historical Debate feature, that dolmen construction should be read as evidence of communal or lineage-based labor capacity and social ranking rather than as proof of centralized kingship or full state formation.

The chapter examined emerging inequality through multiple independent lines of evidence, including variation in house size, sharply differentiated grave treatment, and unequal access to bronze and other prestige goods, using cautious analytical language such as "ranked" or "chiefdom-like" to describe this pattern without overstating the degree of political centralization the evidence can support. It likewise examined fortified and defensively situated settlements and increasing weapon finds in some regions, explaining these as evidence of localized competition over agricultural surplus, land, or prestige goods rather than as proof of peninsula-wide, constant warfare.

Regional networks connecting the peninsula to Liaodong, Manchuria, the Yellow Sea maritime zone, and the Japanese archipelago received continued attention, extending the connective themes introduced in Chapter 4 into the Bronze Age and underscoring that peninsula communities developed through ongoing interaction with their neighbors rather than in isolation. The chapter's treatment of everyday life emphasized that most Mumun-period people spent their lives in agricultural and household labor, that stone tools continued to dominate daily material culture, and that the labor underlying storage facilities, field systems, and dolmens alike depended on cooperative organization extending beyond individual households.

Finally, the chapter's concluding section drew these threads together to explain how agricultural surplus, settlement growth, bronze-based prestige competition, monumental labor mobilization, and sharpening inequality combined to create communities with the organizational capacity that historians generally regard as preconditions for the emergence of more centralized political institutions. For most of the sequence, this chapter has deliberately stopped short of describing these Mumun-period communities as states, while acknowledging that its terminal centuries overlap chronologically with the contested early emergence of Gojoseon, the earliest historically attested polity conventionally identified as an early Korean state. That transition, and the documentary and archaeological evidence historians use to reconstruct Gojoseon's emergence, is the subject of Chapter 6.

Throughout, the chapter has modeled the historical caution this textbook asks of its students: distinguishing what the archaeological evidence clearly supports from what remains uncertain, resisting the temptation to read modern political categories backward into a nonliterate Bronze Age world, and treating regional variation, rather than uniform development, as the norm across the peninsula during this critical period of transformation.

Differences in size, elaboration, or strategic importance among settlements within a region, used by archaeologists as evidence of emerging social or economic complexity.
Prestige goods
Scarce or symbolically valuable objects, such as bronze daggers or ornamental beads, whose possession marked elevated social status.
Grave goods
Objects placed with the dead at burial, used by archaeologists to infer social status, wealth, and ritual practice.
Mortuary differentiation
Variation in the scale, construction, and furnishing of burials within a society, used as evidence of social inequality.
Chiefdom
An analytical term for a society with hereditary or otherwise unequal social ranking and some capacity for centralized labor mobilization, but without the full institutional apparatus of a state.

Differences in material culture, settlement pattern, or social organization across different parts of the peninsula during the same broad period.

A grouping of sites sharing similar material culture, used as an analytical convenience and not equivalent to a single people, language, or political community.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how the ordinary, often invisible labor of farming, storing, and building combined with a scarce new metal and an extraordinary tradition of monumental burial to transform peninsula society well beyond anything the Neolithic world of Chapter 4 had produced. None of these transformations happened suddenly, and none of them by itself created a Korean state. What they created instead were communities capable of mobilizing substantial labor, sustaining meaningful surplus, and organizing themselves around real, if still largely informal, differences in status and authority.

Holding this conclusion firmly in mind matters for everything that follows in this textbook. Chapter 6 will introduce Gojoseon, an early historically attested polity whose chronology, political development, and relationship to the archaeological record remain debated, along with the Dangun tradition that later Koreans came to associate with its founding. Readers who have absorbed this chapter's caution, that increasing complexity is not the same thing as statehood, and that a society capable of building dolmens is not automatically a society ruled by kings, will be far better equipped to evaluate Gojoseon's own emergence carefully, distinguishing documented political development from later tradition and legend.

The dolmens that still stand across the Korean peninsula today are, in this sense, a fitting bridge between prehistory and history. They are silent monuments, built by people who left no names, no laws, and no chronicles, yet whose organized labor, social competition, and ritual practice laid part of the human and social groundwork from which the peninsula's first states would eventually emerge. Understanding that groundwork, without mistaking it for the states themselves, is the task this chapter has completed and the foundation on which Chapter 6 now builds.
