The Paleolithic Peninsula

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Long before any word existed for "Korea," and tens of thousands of years before the traditional founding date associated with Dangun, small groups of people were already making their way across the river terraces, basalt plateaus, and coastal margins of the Korean peninsula, leaving behind stone tools that remain, in many cases, the only surviving trace of their presence.

In 1978, along a bend of the Hantan River in what is now Yeoncheon County, not far from the modern Demilitarized Zone, an American serviceman named Greg L. Bowen, who had studied archaeology, surface-collected an unusual dark stone tool at a riverside recreation area. The stone turned out to be a hand axe, a distinctive stone tool type, found among unconsolidated sediment layers, including ancient loess, paleosol, and river- and lake-deposited material, lying above a thick layer of basalt that had itself formed from earlier volcanic activity along the river valley. Formal excavation began the following year, in 1979. The site, known today as Jeongok-ri, became one of the most important Paleolithic discoveries in East Asia, prompting decades of subsequent excavation and a still-unresolved scholarly debate about the tools' age, their makers, and their place within a much larger story of early human technology across Africa, Europe, and Asia. This chapter begins with such a discovery not because Jeongok-ri single-handedly explains the peninsula's earliest human history, but because it illustrates, concretely, the kind of evidence this chapter must rely upon: no written words, no named individuals, no securely dated royal chronicles, only stone, sediment, bone where it survives, and the patient, often contested, work of archaeological interpretation.

The Paleolithic period, meaning the "Old Stone Age," refers to the long span of human history identified archaeologically through chipped stone tool traditions, generally understood on the Korean peninsula as preceding the appearance of pottery-bearing Neolithic communities examined in Chapter 4. As Chapter 4 will explain, this shorthand cannot be reduced to a simple rule that pottery marks the beginning of agriculture, since in other parts of East Asia pottery was made by mobile hunter-gatherer populations many thousands of years before farming developed; the Paleolithic-to-Neolithic boundary is defined here through regional archaeological sequences rather than through any single universal marker. On the Korean peninsula and in adjoining Manchuria, the Paleolithic extends back, according to current archaeological estimates, at least two hundred thousand years, and by some more contested interpretations of early sites considerably further, encompassing a period more than an order of magnitude longer than the entire span of history, documented and prehistoric alike, examined in the remaining ninety-three chapters of this textbook. For nearly all of that immense span, the human beings who inhabited the peninsula lived in small, mobile groups, relied on stone, bone, wood, and other perishable materials for their tools, and adapted repeatedly to dramatic environmental changes driven by the advance and retreat of glacial ice sheets across the Northern Hemisphere.

Studying this period requires a different historical posture than the chapters that follow. There are no royal genealogies to trace, no foundation myths to weigh against archaeological evidence, and no state institutions to describe, because none of these existed yet. What this chapter can offer instead is an account of how archaeologists have reconstructed, from an extremely fragmentary and unevenly preserved record, the basic outlines of how people lived on this landscape: what tools they made and how those tools changed over time, what animals and plants they depended upon, how the peninsula's climate and coastline looked radically different from today during the coldest phases of the last Ice Age, and where the surviving physical traces of their presence have been found.

This chapter also introduces, for the first time in this textbook, a methodological problem that will recur throughout: the relationship between deep-time human presence and any later concept of "Korean" identity. The Paleolithic inhabitants of this peninsula were not "ancient Koreans" in any meaningful national sense. They belonged to a much broader pattern of human dispersal across Northeast Asia that did not respect, because it could not have anticipated, boundaries that would only take political shape tens of thousands of years later. At the same time, understanding this deep human presence matters for its own sake, and it establishes an essential baseline against which the following chapters, on Neolithic communities, Bronze Age societies, and the eventual emergence of Gojoseon, must be understood.

Korean and international archaeologists have worked for decades to build this record, often under difficult conditions. Many important discoveries, including Jeongok-ri itself, occurred by accident, through construction, riverbank erosion, or military activity along a heavily fortified and only partially accessible modern border region. Excavation north of the Demilitarized Zone has followed its own separate scholarly trajectory, shaped by North Korean state institutions and, for outside researchers, by limited direct access, a circumstance this chapter addresses directly rather than ignoring. As a result, the archaeological record for the Korean Paleolithic is genuinely incomplete, unevenly distributed, and, on several important points, actively debated among specialists, a condition students should expect to encounter throughout this chapter rather than treat as an unusual exception.

None of this uncertainty diminishes the chapter's importance. On the contrary, learning to reason carefully about fragmentary, contested evidence is itself one of the central historical skills this textbook aims to develop, and the Paleolithic peninsula offers an unusually clear laboratory for practicing it. Every claim in the pages that follow, about dates, tool types, sites, and population history, should be read with the same disciplined attention to evidence, uncertainty, and interpretation that professional archaeologists themselves bring to this remarkably deep, and remarkably quiet, chapter of human history.

By the chapter's end, students should have a working understanding of how the peninsula's earliest inhabitants lived, how archaeologists know what they know, and why the transition from this long Paleolithic world to the Neolithic communities examined in Chapter 4 represents one of the most significant transformations in the entire span of human history on this landscape.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the term "Paleolithic" means and how archaeologists subdivide it into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases.
  2. Describe the earliest currently accepted evidence for human occupation of the Korean peninsula and explain why some claimed early dates remain disputed.
  3. Trace the diversification of stone tool technologies across the Paleolithic period, from early core and chopping tools through large bifacial hand axes to the overlapping blade, tanged-point, and microblade technologies of the Upper Paleolithic.
  4. Analyze how Ice Age climate change, including glacial and interglacial cycles, reshaped the peninsula's coastline, vegetation, and animal populations.
  5. Describe the subsistence strategies, including hunting, gathering, and scavenging, that sustained Paleolithic communities.
  6. Identify major Paleolithic sites on the peninsula, including cave and open-air locations, and explain what each contributes to the overall record.
  7. Evaluate the ongoing scholarly debate concerning the Jeongok-ri hand axes and their significance for understanding Paleolithic East Asia.
  8. Assess competing interpretations of population continuity between the Paleolithic and Neolithic periods on the peninsula.
  9. Distinguish archaeological evidence from later nationalist or popular narratives that project modern identity backward onto prehistoric populations.
  10. Explain why the North-South division of the modern peninsula complicates access to and interpretation of Paleolithic sites.
  11. Contextualize the Korean Paleolithic record within wider Northeast Asian and global patterns of early human dispersal and technological change.
  12. Apply careful reasoning about archaeological uncertainty, dating methods, and interpretive debate to the study of prehistoric evidence generally.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established that this textbook studies "Korea" as an evolving geographic space and set of political and cultural communities, not as a timeless national category, and Chapter 2 introduced the peninsula's physical geography, including its mountain ranges, river systems, and maritime surroundings, in their present-day form. This chapter now turns to the deepest layer of that geographic story: the Pleistocene epoch, commonly called the Ice Age, during which the peninsula's landscape looked in many respects unrecognizable compared to today, and during which the earliest known human communities established themselves on it.

The Pleistocene epoch extended from roughly 2.6 million years ago until approximately 11,700 years ago, and it was characterized by repeated cycles of glacial advance and retreat across much of the Northern Hemisphere. Although continental Northeast Asia, including the Korean peninsula, was never covered by ice sheets in the way that northern Europe and parts of North America were, glacial cycles nonetheless produced dramatic changes in sea level, coastline position, vegetation zones, and the distribution of animal species across the region. During the coldest glacial maxima, global sea levels fell so far that much of the Yellow Sea shelf became dry land or shallow marsh, substantially narrowing the sea gap with the Chinese mainland, while the Korea Strait separating the peninsula from the Japanese archipelago was greatly narrowed but most likely retained a marine channel rather than becoming dry land. Understanding this radically different physical setting is essential to understanding how, when, and by what routes early human populations could have reached and moved across the peninsula.

Human evolution itself unfolded primarily in Africa over several million years, with successive hominin populations, including various species of the genus Homo, dispersing into Asia and elsewhere at different times beginning roughly two million years ago. The Korean peninsula's place within this larger story of hominin dispersal into East Asia remains only partially understood, both because the surviving archaeological record is sparse and unevenly distributed and because the peninsula's acidic soils generally preserve stone tools far better than they preserve human or animal bone, leaving archaeologists more evidence about what people made than about the physical people themselves.

This chapter should also be read alongside an important methodological caution introduced in Chapter 1: the peoples discussed here were not "Korean" in any modern national, ethnic, or political sense. They were small, mobile human communities responding to the same evolutionary, technological, and environmental pressures that shaped early human populations across Northeast Asia more broadly. Their descendants, if traceable at all through such an immense span of time, eventually became part of the much later populations examined in Chapters 4 through 6, but the connection between Paleolithic populations and later historical Korean societies is a matter of ongoing scholarly investigation rather than settled fact, a point examined in detail later in this chapter.

Section 3.1

Studying a Peninsula Without Written Sources

Many chapters of this textbook, beginning in Chapter 6 with the earliest Korean states, will draw, in varying degrees, on written evidence: inscriptions, chronicles, administrative records, diplomatic correspondence, and eventually newspapers, memoirs, and government archives. This chapter, and the two that follow it, must proceed almost entirely without any of that. No population on the Korean peninsula during the Paleolithic period left behind writing, since writing itself first developed in Mesopotamia only a few thousand years before the earliest Korean states examined later in this volume, many millennia after the Pleistocene period this chapter examines had already ended. Historians and archaeologists studying the Paleolithic peninsula therefore rely almost entirely on material evidence: stone tools, the sediment layers in which those tools are found, occasional animal and human skeletal remains, and, for later phases, indirect traces such as hearths and modified landscapes.

This absence of written sources does not mean the period is unknowable, but it does mean that the questions historians can answer, and the confidence with which they can answer them, differ substantially from later chapters. Archaeologists can often establish, with reasonable confidence, what kinds of tools a population made and how those tools changed over time. They can sometimes establish, through careful excavation of undisturbed sediment layers, a general chronological sequence at a given site. They can occasionally recover evidence of diet, through animal bone remains found alongside tools, or evidence of environment, through pollen and other botanical remains preserved in ancient soils. What they generally cannot do is identify named individuals, specific events, spoken languages, political organization, or, in most cases, precise population size, biological ancestry, or defined territorial boundaries. Historians must therefore resist projecting later categories, including any notion of "Korea" or "Korean" identity, onto evidence that simply cannot support such claims.

This chapter proceeds site by site and technology by technology rather than through a continuous narrative of events, because that is the form the surviving evidence actually takes. Students should not expect the same kind of connected political story that later chapters, beginning with Gojoseon in Chapter 6, will be able to offer. What this chapter can offer instead is an honest, evidence-based account of how people on this landscape adapted, over an immense span of time, to a changing physical world using an evolving toolkit of stone technology.

Section 3.2

Earliest Evidence of Human Occupation

Determining precisely when human or hominin populations first occupied the Korean peninsula remains one of the most contested questions in the region's archaeology. Several sites have produced stone tool assemblages that some researchers have argued date to several hundred thousand years ago or more, placing early hominin presence on the peninsula within the broader pattern of hominin dispersal across Northeast Asia documented at various sites in China and Siberia. More recent, technically refined dating work at Jeongok-ri, using optically stimulated luminescence methods applied directly to the quartz grains within the artifact-bearing sediment, has established well-supported dates for the site's oldest artifact-bearing deposits of roughly two hundred thousand years ago, with the surrounding unconsolidated sediment sequence spanning approximately two hundred thousand to one hundred twenty thousand years ago. Claims for substantially older occupation, sometimes citing dates of three hundred thousand, five hundred thousand, or even seven hundred thousand years or more, remain considerably more controversial and rest on methods, or on sediment contexts, that carry wider margins of uncertainty than the more recent, technically refined work at Jeongok-ri.

This disagreement is not simply a matter of scholarly caution versus scholarly boldness. It reflects genuine, unresolved technical problems in dating very old sediment layers, particularly at open-air sites where geological processes such as river erosion, redeposition, and soil formation can complicate the relationship between a stone tool's original manufacture and the age of the sediment layer in which it is eventually found. Establishing a secure date requires not simply finding an old-looking stone tool but demonstrating, through careful geological and stratigraphic analysis, that the tool has not moved significantly from its original depositional context since it was made and discarded. The most defensible current synthesis is that well-dated Middle Pleistocene occupation of the peninsula is established by roughly two hundred thousand years ago, while claims for substantially earlier occupation remain disputed and await further technical confirmation.

Whatever the precise date of earliest occupation, most researchers agree that human populations reached the peninsula from the Asian mainland, most plausibly through Manchuria, rather than representing an isolated or independent center of human development. This pattern matches the broader scholarly understanding of hominin dispersal across Eurasia, in which populations originating ultimately in Africa spread gradually across the continent over hundreds of thousands of years, adapting their technology and subsistence strategies to a wide range of different regional environments along the way.

Skeletal evidence for the peninsula's earliest inhabitants remains extremely limited. The acidic soils common across much of the peninsula tend to dissolve bone relatively quickly compared to the more alkaline conditions found at some well-preserved cave sites elsewhere in East Asia, meaning that archaeologists working on the Korean Paleolithic generally have far more stone tools to study than human remains. This imbalance shapes much of what this chapter, and the broader scholarly literature, can and cannot say with confidence: technological development can often be traced in reasonable detail, while questions about the specific biological populations who made and used these tools remain considerably more speculative.

Section 3.3

Stone Technologies and Their Development

Archaeologists conventionally divide the Paleolithic period into three broad phases, Lower, Middle, and Upper, based primarily on changes in stone tool technology, distinguishing this period from the subsequent Neolithic primarily through regional archaeological sequences of stone-tool and, eventually, pottery-bearing assemblages rather than through any single universal marker. It is worth noting that this common shorthand, that the Paleolithic ended when pottery and agriculture began, does not hold up well in East Asia specifically: in parts of China, pottery was produced by mobile hunter-gatherer populations many thousands of years before agriculture developed, with some assemblages dated to roughly twenty thousand years ago or earlier. For Korea, as Chapter 4 will explain, pottery is an important marker of the archaeological Neolithic sequence, but its appearance did not correspond to the immediate or simultaneous adoption of agriculture. These three broad technological phases, developed originally in European archaeology, are therefore applied to the Korean peninsula with some caution, since the pace and character of technological change did not necessarily match European or even neighboring East Asian patterns precisely, and regional chronologies across Northeast Asia continue to be refined.

The Korean early Paleolithic, corresponding to the Middle Pleistocene, is associated with core tools, chopping tools, and, at some sites, large bifacially worked stone tools known as handaxes, shaped through the systematic removal of flakes from a stone core. The handaxes recovered at Jeongok-ri, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window, belong to this broad technological category. Because the best current dating places the site's oldest artifact-bearing deposits at roughly two hundred thousand years ago, a date that falls within the conventional global Middle Pleistocene and thus within the range often labeled Middle Paleolithic elsewhere in the world, many Korean archaeologists prefer to describe Jeongok-ri simply as a Korean early Paleolithic or Middle Pleistocene site with large bifacial, Acheulean-like tools, rather than forcing its distinctive assemblage into the European-derived Lower Paleolithic category. This labeling difficulty itself illustrates why the three-part European framework must be applied to Korea with real caution rather than treated as a universal, automatically transferable scheme.

For much of the twentieth century, many Western archaeologists working within a framework proposed by the American archaeologist Hallam Movius argued that large bifacial handaxe technology, associated with the so-called Acheulean tradition first defined in Africa and Europe, was largely absent from East Asia, where early populations were instead thought to rely primarily on a more limited chopping-tool tradition. The handaxes recovered at Jeongok-ri, along with related finds elsewhere in East Asia, substantially challenged this proposed geographical division, sometimes called the Movius Line, by demonstrating that large bifacial tools did, in fact, appear in East Asia. This finding is best understood as evidence of greater technological diversity and a wider geographic distribution of handaxe-making traditions than the Movius framework had proposed, rather than as evidence that East Asian hominin populations possessed superior cognitive capability compared with populations elsewhere; debate over the tools' precise age, cultural affiliation, and relationship to African and European handaxe traditions continues among specialists.

Later Korean Paleolithic assemblages, corresponding to the conventional global Middle Paleolithic framework of roughly three hundred thousand to forty thousand years ago, are generally associated with continued core and flake tool production, without a single, sharply defined technological break separating this phase clearly from the preceding one. Some archaeologists studying Northeast Asia have questioned whether the three-part Lower-Middle-Upper Paleolithic framework, developed for European contexts, maps cleanly onto the East Asian record at all, since technological change in this region often appears more gradual, more regionally variable, and less tied to sharp chronological boundaries than the transitions sometimes described in parts of Europe and Africa.

The Korean Upper Paleolithic, beginning by roughly forty-three thousand years ago according to recent chronological work, is associated with the appearance of blade technology, involving long, parallel-sided flakes struck from a specially prepared core, alongside continued use of core and flake tools. Tanged points, a distinctive stone tool form with a shaped haft element for attachment to a shaft, also appear during this period at several sites. Microblade technology, involving extremely small and finely worked blade tools likely hafted into composite tools made from wood, bone, or antler, is well established at Korean sites by roughly twenty-five thousand years ago, with possible earlier examples proposed at some localities. Rather than describing this sequence as a simple, linear progression from cruder to more refined tools, current research emphasizes technological diversification: core and flake tool traditions persisted alongside newer blade, tanged-point, and microblade technologies, and different sites, shaped by local raw-material availability and immediate needs, sometimes combined these approaches in varying ways rather than replacing older methods wholesale. Microblade technology in particular is generally understood as part of a wider Northeast Asian and Siberian tradition, reflecting continued connection between the peninsula's inhabitants and populations across the broader region.

Across all phases, stone tool development reflects considerable technical skill and a growing repertoire of production strategies, but it does not support a simple narrative of continuous, uninterrupted progress from simple to sophisticated forms. Different sites, even within the same broad period, sometimes show markedly different tool assemblages, reflecting local resource availability, immediate subsistence needs, and possibly distinct traditions of craft knowledge passed between generations.

Section 3.4

Environment, Climate, and the Ice Age Peninsula

The Pleistocene peninsula that Paleolithic communities inhabited looked, in important respects, quite different from the peninsula described in Chapter 2. Repeated glacial cycles, driven by long-term changes in the earth's orbit and resulting shifts in solar radiation, caused global temperatures, sea levels, and vegetation zones to fluctuate dramatically over hundreds of thousands of years. During glacial maxima, when enormous volumes of the earth's water were locked in continental ice sheets, particularly in North America and northern Europe, global sea levels fell by well over one hundred meters compared to today, exposing large areas of the continental shelf that are now submerged beneath the Yellow Sea and parts of the Korea Strait.

During the most recent glacial maximum, occurring roughly between twenty-six thousand and nineteen thousand years ago, much of what is today the Yellow Sea became a broad, low-lying plain, substantially extending the peninsula's western coastline and narrowing, though probably not eliminating, the maritime gap separating the peninsula from the Chinese mainland. The Korea Strait separating the peninsula from the Japanese archipelago behaved somewhat differently: detailed reconstructions of the strait's Pleistocene geography suggest that even at the height of the last glacial maximum, a narrow marine channel, estimated at only some ten to fifteen kilometers wide and ten to thirty meters deep in one influential reconstruction, likely remained open, meaning the strait was substantially narrowed rather than converted into a continuous, walkable land bridge. These changing low-sea-level landscapes mattered enormously for the movement of animal populations, and plausibly influenced the movement of human populations as well, though the specific routes, timing, and means, whether by short water crossings or through areas of exposed or seasonally exposed land, by which human populations may have moved across this changing landscape remain, like much of this chapter's subject matter, an area of active research rather than settled fact.

Vegetation and animal life also shifted substantially between glacial and interglacial periods, though not in a uniformly cold direction. Faunal studies of cave deposits spanning the later Pleistocene indicate a more complex mosaic than a single cold steppe environment: evidence for substantial temperate forest continuity appears alongside evidence for cold-adapted species, such as bison and woolly rhinoceros, whose presence or absence varies across different phases and sites. During colder intervals, more open woodland and grassland environments likely expanded at the expense of denser forest, while cold-adapted large mammals appear more prominently in the faunal record at certain sites and periods. During warmer interglacial phases, including the period immediately before the current Holocene epoch, denser forest cover expanded and animal populations shifted further toward species more typical of temperate environments. This alternation between temperate forest, open woodland or grassland, and, at times, colder conditions, rather than a single uniform cold-steppe environment, characterized the Pleistocene peninsula as a whole.

Human communities living through these long climatic cycles had no choice but to adapt continuously: to changing availability of game animals, changing plant resources, changing access to raw stone material for toolmaking, and changing coastal geography that opened and closed different areas to settlement and movement. This chapter's account of technological change in Section 3.3 should be understood against this backdrop of continuous environmental adaptation rather than treated as a purely internal story of toolmaking skill developing in isolation from the physical world in which it occurred.

Section 3.5

Hunting, Gathering, and Daily Subsistence

Paleolithic communities on the peninsula, like Paleolithic communities elsewhere in the world, depended on a combination of hunting, gathering, scavenging, and, in some periods and locations, fishing, rather than on cultivated agriculture, which would not develop until the much later Neolithic period examined in Chapter 4. Reconstructing the specific balance among these subsistence strategies at any given time or place is difficult, given the limited and unevenly preserved evidence, but several general patterns can be identified with reasonable confidence.

Stone tools themselves provide indirect evidence of subsistence activity. Cutting and scraping tools suggest the processing of animal hides, meat, and plant materials. Some tool forms appear specifically suited to woodworking, suggesting the production of wooden spears, digging implements, or other perishable tools that have not survived in the archaeological record. Where faunal remains have survived, generally at sites with more favorable preservation conditions, they indicate that Paleolithic groups hunted a range of animals whose specific composition shifted along with the broader climatic and vegetational changes described in Section 3.4.

Gathering of wild plant foods, including seeds, nuts, roots, and seasonal fruits, almost certainly formed an important part of the diet throughout the Paleolithic period, though plant remains preserve far less readily than stone or bone and are therefore substantially underrepresented in the surviving archaeological record relative to their likely dietary importance. Archaeologists studying comparable Paleolithic societies elsewhere in the world have increasingly emphasized that gathered plant foods, rather than hunted meat alone, likely provided a substantial share of caloric intake for many mobile foraging populations, a pattern plausibly applicable to the Korean peninsula as well, even though direct botanical evidence from this period remains limited.

Mobility characterized Paleolithic subsistence more broadly. Small groups likely moved seasonally or opportunistically across the landscape, following game animals, seeking areas of plant abundance, and accessing sources of workable stone for toolmaking. This mobile pattern of life stands in significant contrast to the more settled communities that begin to appear, in some regions, toward the very end of the period covered by this chapter and more fully during the Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 4.

Section 3.6

Sites, Caves, and Open-Air Settlements

The archaeological record of the Korean Paleolithic derives from two broad categories of sites: open-air locations, typically along river terraces or ancient lakeshores, and cave or rock-shelter sites, which are concentrated in limestone regions found in both the northern and central peninsula, notably around Danyang in the central peninsula as well as in parts of the north, where natural cave formation is more common; open-air sites, by contrast, are especially numerous in the southern peninsula.

Open-air sites, including Jeongok-ri, have generally yielded large numbers of stone tools within layers of river-deposited sediment, but they less frequently preserve organic remains such as bone or plant material, since such materials tend to decompose more readily when exposed to fluctuating moisture and temperature conditions typical of river terrace environments. These sites are therefore especially valuable for tracing long-term technological change but offer less direct evidence about diet, environment, or the specific animals hunted at a given location.

Cave and rock-shelter sites, by contrast, sometimes preserve a richer range of evidence, including animal bone, occasional human skeletal remains, and, at some locations, evidence of hearths and other traces of prolonged or repeated occupation. Several important cave sites are located in the northern peninsula, a circumstance that has significant consequences for the completeness of the modern scholarly record. Since the division of the peninsula in 1948, examined in detail in Volume IV, and especially since the Korean War examined in Part XII, outside researchers have generally been unable to directly inspect northern sites, collections, stratigraphy, or samples, and information about these sites often derives from North Korean state publications. This lack of direct access means outside researchers often cannot independently replicate or reassess many published claims about northern sites, a limitation of access and verification rather than a claim about the inherent quality of North Korean archaeological work. This chapter treats claims originating from sites that outside researchers cannot directly examine with appropriate caution, distinguishing them from more thoroughly independently verified sites such as Jeongok-ri, without dismissing northern evidence altogether.

Later Upper Paleolithic sites in the southern peninsula provide two especially important, and distinct, kinds of evidence. Suyanggae, a major open-air locality, has yielded an extensive sequence of blade, tanged-point, and microblade technology, along with hearths, and remains one of the most thoroughly studied Upper Paleolithic sites in Korea. Sangsi, a rock-shelter site, is particularly significant for its Late Pleistocene human skeletal remains found in direct association with archaeological deposits, making it an important source of evidence about the region's Paleolithic populations themselves rather than only their tools. These two sites should not be treated as interchangeable examples of the same kind of evidence; each contributes something the other does not. Separately, Geomeunmoru, a northern site, has drawn attention for claimed stone artifacts and faunal remains of considerable proposed antiquity, but both the extreme age claimed for the site and whether some of the fractured stones recovered there are genuinely the product of human activity, rather than natural fracturing processes, remain contested among specialists. Human skeletal remains from the northern peninsula have instead come primarily from other cave sites, such as Ryonggok, illustrating why claims about artifacts, fauna, and human remains from different northern sites should not be casually merged into a single undifferentiated category.

Taken together, the growing number of documented sites across the peninsula, though still shaped by uneven research access, particularly regarding the north, allows archaeologists to sketch a general, if incomplete, picture of settlement patterns changing over hundreds of thousands of years, from scattered evidence of early hominin activity to a somewhat denser and more technologically varied record by the close of the Pleistocene.

Section 3.7

Population, Migration, and Continuity Debates

One of the most historically significant, and most frequently misunderstood, questions concerning the Korean Paleolithic involves the relationship between its inhabitants and the much later populations who developed the Neolithic, Bronze Age, and eventually historically documented societies examined in subsequent chapters. Popular historical writing sometimes assumes, implicitly or explicitly, an unbroken line of biological and cultural continuity stretching from the earliest Paleolithic inhabitants of the peninsula to the present-day Korean population. The archaeological and genetic evidence available at present does not straightforwardly support such a simple claim, nor does it straightforwardly refute the possibility of some degree of continuity; the honest scholarly position is considerably more cautious.

Several distinct questions are often conflated in popular discussion but should be kept separate. First, there is the question of whether the same general geographic region, the peninsula and its Manchurian hinterland, remained continuously inhabited by some human population across the transition from the Pleistocene to the Holocene; on this point, the archaeological record does not clearly demonstrate wholesale depopulation of the region at any point, but the record's substantial gaps also prevent continuous occupation by a single, biologically continuous population from being established with confidence. Second, there is the considerably more difficult question of whether the specific populations present at the end of the Paleolithic period were biologically and culturally ancestral, in any close or exclusive sense, to the Neolithic populations examined in Chapter 4, or whether Neolithic populations instead arrived through substantial new migration, replacing or significantly mixing with earlier inhabitants. Modern population genetics has begun to offer some evidence relevant to this second question for other regions of the world, but ancient DNA evidence directly relevant to the Korean peninsula's Paleolithic-to-Neolithic transition remains extremely limited, in part because of the poor bone preservation discussed in Section 3.2 and in part because systematic ancient DNA research in the region remains a comparatively recent and still-developing field.

This chapter therefore treats the question of Paleolithic-to-Neolithic population continuity as a genuinely open scholarly problem, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, rather than as a settled matter that can be resolved through appeals to national tradition or cultural continuity alone. Students should understand that uncertainty on this specific point does not diminish the historical value of studying the Paleolithic period; rather, it reflects the current limits of the available evidence and should encourage appropriately cautious, source-based reasoning of the kind this textbook emphasizes throughout.

What can be said with greater confidence is that the peninsula's Paleolithic populations were connected, through technology, subsistence strategies, and very likely through some degree of movement and contact, to broader populations across Manchuria, Siberia, and other parts of Northeast Asia. The peninsula was never a sealed or isolated environment even at this earliest stage of human presence, a pattern of connection rather than isolation that this textbook will trace across every subsequent period, from the Neolithic maritime networks of Chapter 4 through the modern global connections examined in Volume IV.

Section 3.8

Toward the Neolithic Transition

As the Pleistocene epoch drew to a close, roughly 11,700 years ago by conventional geological reckoning, the peninsula's climate began a sustained, if uneven, process of warming that had already been underway, with significant interruptions, since the end of the last glacial maximum around nineteen thousand years ago. Glacial ice retreated at higher latitudes across the globe, sea levels began rising toward their modern position, narrowing the low-sea-level landscapes described in Section 3.4, and vegetation zones shifted as forests expanded across areas that had previously supported more open, colder environments.

It is important to be precise about what this geological boundary does, and does not, mark for Korean history specifically. The transition from the Pleistocene to the Holocene epoch is a global geological boundary, dated with reasonable precision to approximately 11,700 years ago. It is not the same as the archaeological transition from Korea's Paleolithic to its Neolithic period, examined fully in Chapter 4. The earliest securely dated Korean Neolithic pottery, of the Gosan-ri type found on Jeju Island, dates to only about 9,600 years ago, roughly two thousand years after the start of the Holocene. Chapter 4 accordingly begins not at the Pleistocene-Holocene boundary itself but with the subsequent Early Holocene period, during which the environmental changes described in this chapter continued to unfold and during which the region's first recognizably Neolithic, pottery-bearing communities eventually emerged.

These environmental changes nonetheless set the stage for one of the most significant transformations examined anywhere in this textbook: the gradual shift from mobile Paleolithic hunting and gathering toward the more settled, pottery-using, and eventually food-producing communities of the Neolithic period. This transition did not occur suddenly or uniformly, and, as Chapter 4 will explain in detail, cannot be reduced to a single cause such as climate change alone, since social, technological, and demographic factors likely also played important roles.

This chapter closes, then, not with a resolution but with a transition: from a very long period of relatively slow technological change, measured in hundreds of thousands of years, toward a period of more rapid and, in important respects, more archaeologically visible transformation. The stone toolmaking traditions, subsistence strategies, and mobile settlement patterns described in this chapter did not disappear overnight, and elements of hunting and gathering continued to play a role in Korean subsistence for a long time even after the Neolithic transition began. But the fundamental patterns of daily life were, over the following millennia, about to change in ways that would eventually make possible the more complex, socially stratified, and eventually state-organized societies examined in the remaining chapters of Part I and throughout the rest of this textbook.

Historical Vocabulary
Paleolithic and Its Subdivisions

Definition: "Paleolithic" derives from Greek roots meaning "old stone" and refers to the earliest and by far the longest phase of human technological history, characterized by the use of chipped, rather than polished or ground, stone tools. Archaeologists conventionally subdivide it into the Lower Paleolithic, associated with early core and hand axe technology; the Middle Paleolithic, associated with continued core and flake tool traditions; and the Upper Paleolithic, associated with more standardized blade and microblade technology.

Historical context: This three-part division was developed originally through the study of European archaeological sequences during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and has since been applied, with varying degrees of fit, to archaeological records across the world, including Northeast Asia and the Korean peninsula.

Why it matters: Understanding these subdivisions allows students to follow the gradual technological changes described throughout this chapter and to compare the Korean peninsula's Paleolithic sequence with patterns documented elsewhere in East Asia and beyond.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that these three phases represent sudden, sharply defined transformations. On the peninsula, as in many regions, technological change across these phases appears gradual and regionally variable rather than marked by abrupt, uniform breaks.

Historical Vocabulary
Dating Methods in Paleolithic Archaeology

Definition: Archaeologists use several scientific dating methods to estimate the age of Paleolithic sites and artifacts, since no written or otherwise directly datable record exists for this period. Stratigraphy establishes relative age based on the position of sediment layers, with deeper layers generally, though not always, older than layers above them. Radiocarbon dating measures the decay of radioactive carbon isotopes in organic materials such as charcoal or bone and is useful for dating material up to roughly fifty thousand years old. Optically stimulated luminescence dating measures the time elapsed since certain minerals in sediment were last exposed to sunlight, and is particularly useful for dating much older sediment layers where organic material for radiocarbon dating is unavailable.

Historical context: Different dating methods carry different margins of error and different underlying assumptions, and disagreements over site chronology, including the ongoing debate over Jeongok-ri discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, often reflect genuine scientific disagreement over which method, or combination of methods, most reliably dates a given context.

Why it matters: Understanding these methods helps students evaluate competing chronological claims throughout this chapter with appropriate scientific literacy rather than treating all dating claims as equally certain.

Primary Source Window

The Excavation Reports from Jeongok-ri, Hantan River Basin

Creator: multiple Korean and international archaeological teams conducting excavation since 1979 · Date: initial surface discovery in 1978, with formal excavation beginning in 1979 and continuing across subsequent decades · Type: archaeological site reports and stone tool analyses

Context: The Jeongok-ri site, situated on a terrace capped by ancient basalt along the Hantan River, was discovered in 1978 when Greg L. Bowen, an American serviceman who had studied archaeology, surface-collected a hand axe at a riverside recreation area. Formal excavation began the following year and has continued across subsequent decades. Because Jeongok-ri lies close to a heavily fortified modern border region, research access has at times required coordination with military authorities, a circumstance that shaped the pace of early research.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of general findings reported across multiple excavation seasons.

Successive excavation seasons at Jeongok-ri recovered large bifacial hand axes and other core tools from unconsolidated sediment layers, including ancient loess, paleosol, and river- and lake-deposited material, lying above the site's basalt bedrock. Researchers noted that the tools' morphology closely resembled Acheulean-like hand axe traditions previously described primarily from Africa and parts of western Eurasia, a similarity that directly challenged an influential mid-twentieth-century proposal, associated with the archaeologist Hallam Movius, that such large bifacial tools were geographically confined to those regions and were largely absent from East Asia.

Analysis: This body of excavation reporting is significant because it directly challenged the so-called Movius Line, an earlier scholarly proposal that divided the Old World into a western zone with sophisticated bifacial hand axe technology and an eastern zone supposedly limited to simpler chopping tools. The Jeongok-ri finds, along with comparable discoveries elsewhere in East Asia, are best understood as evidence of greater technological diversity and a wider geographic distribution of hand axe traditions than the Movius framework had proposed, rather than as evidence bearing on the relative cognitive capability of different early human populations, a framing this textbook explicitly avoids.

Limitations: Despite decades of subsequent research, the precise age of the Jeongok-ri hand axes remains actively studied, with different dating studies, using different methods and sampling different sediment contexts, producing different age estimates; the best-supported recent work places the site's oldest artifact-bearing deposits at roughly two hundred thousand years ago, though this figure may be refined by future research. The site's excavation reports therefore cannot, by themselves, resolve every scholarly question about the tools' age or their makers' identity, and readers should treat any single date offered for Jeongok-ri as the current best estimate rather than a permanently fixed fact.

Perspective: These reports represent the perspective of professional archaeologists working within an evolving international scientific framework; they do not, and cannot, represent the perspective of the tools' original makers, about whom essentially nothing beyond their technological choices can be directly known.

Questions for students:

  1. Why did the discovery of hand axes at Jeongok-ri challenge the Movius Line, an earlier scholarly proposal about the geographic distribution of hand axe technology?
  2. Why does dating a stone tool found within river-terrace sediment present particular scientific challenges?
  3. What does the persistence of scholarly disagreement over Jeongok-ri's age suggest about the limits of current archaeological dating methods?
  4. Why is it important to distinguish what excavation reports can establish about ancient toolmaking from what they cannot establish about the toolmakers themselves?
Historical Debate

Population Continuity and the Paleolithic-to-Neolithic Question

The question: Were the Neolithic populations of the Korean peninsula, examined in Chapter 4, descended primarily from the peninsula's own Paleolithic inhabitants, or did the Neolithic transition involve substantial new population movement into the region, with limited direct continuity from earlier Paleolithic groups?

Interpretation A — Substantial local continuity: Some researchers emphasize that the peninsula shows no clear evidence of complete depopulation between the Paleolithic and Neolithic periods, and argue that at least some technological and settlement patterns suggest gradual local adaptation rather than wholesale population replacement.
Interpretation B — Significant new population movement: Other researchers point to the substantial technological, and in some analyses possible demographic, differences between late Paleolithic and early Neolithic assemblages, and argue that patterns observed elsewhere in Northeast Asia, where Neolithic subsistence strategies and pottery traditions appear to spread partly through population movement rather than purely local invention, may also apply, at least in part, to the Korean peninsula.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited body of stone tool, settlement, and, where available, skeletal evidence, since ancient DNA evidence directly addressing this specific transition on the peninsula remains very limited at present.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily the interpretation of indirect evidence and the appropriate weight to give to regional comparative patterns from Manchuria, Siberia, and China, rather than a dispute over directly conflicting primary data specific to the peninsula itself.

Why it matters: This question bears directly on how historians should describe the relationship between the peninsula's deep prehistoric past and its later historically documented populations, a relationship this textbook treats with careful qualification rather than as a settled matter of continuous national ancestry.

Student task: Considering the evidentiary limitations described in Section 3.7, explain what kind of new evidence, if discovered, would help resolve this debate, and evaluate why definitive resolution currently remains out of reach.

Multiple Perspectives

Interpreting Deep Time: Who Studies the Paleolithic Peninsula, and Why?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns among researchers and interested parties, not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a field archaeologist excavating an open-air river terrace site: Establishing a secure chronology depends on painstaking attention to sediment layers and depositional history; a single displaced tool or misread stratigraphic boundary can distort an entire site's interpretation, making patient, technically rigorous excavation more valuable than dramatic individual discoveries.
From the perspective of a specialist in East Asian Paleolithic technology working within an international comparative framework: Sites such as Jeongok-ri matter primarily for what they reveal about the geographic reach of early hominin technological capability across Eurasia, a question that transcends any single national historical narrative and requires comparison with contemporaneous sites in China, Siberia, and beyond.
From the perspective of a popular writer or educator emphasizing national historical continuity: Deep prehistoric occupation of the peninsula can be presented as evidence of an exceptionally long-standing connection between a people and a place, a narrative that carries real cultural resonance even though, as this chapter has emphasized, it requires far more caution regarding claims of unbroken biological or cultural continuity than popular accounts sometimes provide.
From the perspective of a researcher working with limited access to sites north of the Demilitarized Zone: Restricted access to potentially significant cave sites in the northern peninsula leaves meaningful gaps in the overall regional record, since outside researchers generally cannot directly inspect northern collections, stratigraphy, or samples and must instead rely on published North Korean reports that are difficult to independently verify or replicate.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Stone Technology over Tens of Thousands of Years

What Changed

  • Stone tool traditions diversified over time, from early core and chopping tools toward large bifacial handaxes, and eventually toward blade, tanged-point, and microblade technologies that appeared alongside, rather than simply replacing, older core and flake traditions
  • Toolmaking strategies became more varied and, in some contexts, more standardized, suggesting an expanding repertoire of technical knowledge and possibly more structured methods of skill transmission between individuals
  • Site types diversified over time, from scattered early evidence concentrated at a small number of open-air locations toward a somewhat wider and more varied range of open-air and cave sites by the close of the Pleistocene
  • Environmental conditions shifted repeatedly among temperate forest, open woodland or grassland, and colder intervals, rather than alternating simply between uniformly cold and uniformly warm phases, each requiring different subsistence adaptations

What Stayed the Same

  • Reliance on hunting, gathering, and, where possible, fishing persisted as the basic subsistence pattern throughout the entire period covered by this chapter
  • Mobile, small-scale group living remained the norm, without evidence of large, permanently settled populations
  • Connection to broader Northeast Asian populations and technological traditions, rather than isolated local development, characterized the peninsula throughout the Paleolithic
  • Stone remained the primary durable material for toolmaking throughout the period, even as specific tool forms changed considerably

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the remarkable technological change and the underlying continuity of mobile foraging life across this immense span of time helps students appreciate both the achievements and the limitations of Paleolithic societies, without either exaggerating their sophistication or dismissing their adaptability.

Cause and Effect

Why Paleolithic Sites Cluster Where They Do

Long-term causes

River terraces and cave-forming limestone regions provided reliable water access, shelter, and, in the case of river terraces, exposed raw stone material suitable for toolmaking, making these locations attractive across many thousands of years of repeated use.

Short-term causes

Modern discovery of individual sites has depended heavily on chance factors such as riverbank erosion, construction activity, and military infrastructure projects that happened to expose ancient sediment layers, as in the case of Jeongok-ri.

Effect

The known distribution of Paleolithic sites reflects a combination of genuine ancient settlement patterns and modern research access and discovery conditions, meaning the map of known sites is not a perfectly accurate map of ancient population distribution.

This chapter's response

Consistent acknowledgment throughout this chapter that the known archaeological record is shaped by preservation conditions and research access as much as by ancient reality, requiring cautious interpretation rather than treating the current distribution of known sites as a complete picture.

Historical complexity: The political division of the peninsula since 1948 has itself become a modern cause shaping which ancient sites can be studied and verified, a rare case in this textbook where twentieth-century political history directly affects the completeness of evidence for events tens of thousands of years earlier.

Everyday Life and Social History

Imagining Daily Life Without Overreaching the Evidence

Paleolithic daily life on the peninsula almost certainly centered on small mobile groups connected by social and probably kin ties, whose survival depended on cooperative hunting, gathering, and toolmaking. The technical traditions evident in stone tool assemblages demonstrate genuine learning and knowledge transmission between individuals, whether achieved through observation, practice, demonstration, or direct instruction, though archaeologists cannot recover the specific mechanism, family structure, spoken language, or belief systems from stone tools alone, and this textbook resists the temptation, common in some popular treatments of prehistory, to fill these gaps with confident but unsupported speculation.

What can be said, based on comparative evidence from other well-studied Paleolithic societies and on the direct evidence of tool standardization discussed in Section 3.3, is that these communities possessed genuine technical expertise, adapted continuously to a changing environment, and maintained some degree of contact and shared technological tradition with populations across a wide surrounding region. Recognizing the skill and adaptability embedded in something as seemingly simple as a well-made stone tool is itself an important corrective to any assumption that technological simplicity implies a lack of intelligence, social organization, or cultural sophistication among Paleolithic communities.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced the Korean peninsula's Paleolithic record, the earliest and by far the longest phase of human presence examined anywhere in this textbook. Working without any written evidence, archaeologists have reconstructed this period primarily through stone tools, sediment layers, and the sparse skeletal and faunal remains that have survived the peninsula's generally unfavorable preservation conditions.

The chapter began with the 1978 discovery of hand axes at Jeongok-ri, a find that substantially reshaped international scholarly understanding of East Asian Paleolithic technology and that continues to generate active debate over precise dating methods and interpretation. This debate illustrated a broader theme running throughout the chapter: dating very old, often disturbed sediment layers involves genuine scientific uncertainty, and students should treat confident-sounding dates for early prehistoric evidence with appropriate caution rather than unquestioned acceptance.

The chapter traced the conventional Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic technological framework as applied, with real caution, to the Korean peninsula, describing the shift from early core and large bifacial handaxe tools, best exemplified by the Jeongok-ri assemblage, now understood as a Korean early Paleolithic or Middle Pleistocene site rather than a strict "Lower Paleolithic" one under the conventional global framework, through continued flake tool traditions to the more diversified blade, tanged-point, and microblade technologies of the Upper Paleolithic. It emphasized that this technological development involved diversification and regional variation rather than a simple, linear march from crude to sophisticated forms.

Environmental change formed a second major theme. Repeated glacial and interglacial cycles reshaped the peninsula's coastline, vegetation, and animal populations throughout the Pleistocene, at times connecting the peninsula more closely to the Japanese archipelago and Chinese mainland through exposed continental shelf areas during periods of lowered sea level. Human communities adapted continuously to these changing conditions through mobile hunting, gathering, and, in some contexts, fishing.

The chapter examined the peninsula's Paleolithic sites in two broad categories, open-air river terrace locations and cave or rock-shelter sites, noting that each preserves different kinds of evidence and that the modern political division of the peninsula has significantly limited direct international access to and verification of many important northern sites, a genuine gap in the current scholarly record that this textbook has not attempted to minimize.

A central methodological theme of the chapter concerned the relationship between Paleolithic populations and later historically documented Korean societies. The chapter explained why applying the modern national label "Korean" to Paleolithic populations would be historically inaccurate, and it examined the genuinely unresolved scholarly debate over the degree of population continuity between the peninsula's Paleolithic and Neolithic inhabitants, a debate that current archaeological and genetic evidence cannot yet fully resolve.

The chapter also addressed the limits of what stone tool evidence alone can reveal about daily life, deliberately avoiding unsupported speculation about family structure, belief, or language while still recognizing the genuine technical skill, adaptability, and likely social cooperation reflected in the archaeological record.

Finally, the chapter closed by tracing the environmental changes that accompanied the end of the Pleistocene roughly 11,700 years ago, while carefully distinguishing this geological boundary from the later archaeological beginning of Korea's Neolithic period, marked by securely dated pottery only around 9,600 years ago. This distinction sets the stage for the Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 4, a transformation that would bring pottery, more settled communities, and eventually agriculture to the peninsula over the following millennia.

Understanding this deep, largely silent, and evidence-constrained period establishes an essential foundation for the rest of Part I. Only by first appreciating how long, how gradual, and how genuinely uncertain much of this early record remains can students properly evaluate the later, more rapid, and more richly documented developments examined in Chapters 4 through 6 and beyond.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The "Old Stone Age," the earliest and longest phase of human technological history, characterized by chipped stone tool traditions.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch, roughly 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, encompassing repeated glacial and interglacial cycles and the entire period examined in this chapter.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago; this geological boundary predates Korea's archaeological Neolithic transition, examined in Chapter 4, by roughly two thousand years.
Hominin
A term referring to modern humans and their close evolutionary relatives and ancestors, used when the specific species identity of an early human population is uncertain or not the primary focus.
Lower Paleolithic
The earliest Paleolithic phase in the conventional global framework, associated with core, chopping, and hand axe tool traditions; Korean sites such as Jeongok-ri, now dated to the Middle Pleistocene, are often described instead as "Korean early Paleolithic" to avoid forcing them into this European-derived category.
Middle Paleolithic
The Paleolithic phase, roughly 300,000–40,000 years ago in the conventional global framework, associated with continued core and flake tool traditions, without a single sharply defined technological break from the preceding period.
Upper Paleolithic
The final Paleolithic phase, beginning by roughly 43,000 years ago in Korea, associated with blade, tanged-point, and, from roughly 25,000 years ago, microblade tool technologies.
Hand axe
A large, bifacially worked stone tool shaped through systematic flake removal, notably recovered at Jeongok-ri, where such finds challenged the Movius Line proposal about the geographic distribution of hand axe technology.
Core tool
A stone tool made from the core piece of stone itself, as distinct from a tool made from a removed flake.
Flake tool
A stone tool made from a flake struck off a larger stone core.
Blade technology
A stone toolmaking method producing long, parallel-sided flakes from a specially prepared core, associated with the Upper Paleolithic.
Microblade
An extremely small, finely worked blade tool, likely hafted into composite tools, well established at Korean sites by roughly 25,000 years ago.
Glacial maximum
A period of peak cold and maximum ice extent during a glacial cycle, associated with significantly lowered global sea levels.
Interglacial
A warmer period between glacial maxima, associated with higher sea levels and expanded forest cover.
Land bridge / low-sea-level landscape
An area of seabed exposed, or a strait substantially narrowed, by lowered sea levels during glacial maxima; the Yellow Sea shelf was extensively exposed, while the Korea Strait likely remained a narrow marine channel rather than dry land.
Stratigraphy
The study of sediment layering used to establish relative chronological sequence at an archaeological site.
Radiocarbon dating
A dating method measuring radioactive carbon decay in organic material, useful for material up to roughly fifty thousand years old.
Optically stimulated luminescence
A dating method measuring time elapsed since certain sediment minerals were last exposed to sunlight, useful for older contexts than radiocarbon dating.
Faunal assemblage
The collection of animal bone remains recovered from an archaeological site, used to reconstruct diet and environment.
Open-air site
An archaeological site located outside a cave or rock shelter, typically along a river terrace or ancient lakeshore.
Cave site
An archaeological site located within a natural cave or rock shelter, often preserving a richer range of organic evidence than open-air sites.
Population continuity
The hypothesis that a later population is substantially descended from an earlier population inhabiting the same region, a contested question for the Korean Paleolithic-to-Neolithic transition.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept or identity, such as "Korean," to an earlier period in which it did not exist or apply in the same way.
Deep time
A term describing the vast timescales of geological and prehistoric history, often difficult to grasp intuitively relative to the much shorter timescale of documented human history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to spend serious attention on a period of history that offers no named individuals, no political events, and no written words, yet that spans an incomprehensibly longer span of time than every other chapter in this textbook combined. That imbalance is itself instructive. It reminds students that the history explored in the remaining ninety-three chapters, including the Neolithic and Bronze Age chapters immediately ahead as well as the documented political history that follows them, however rich and consequential, represents only the most recent, and in a strictly chronological sense the smallest, portion of the human story on this peninsula.

The chapter has also modeled a historical method that will recur throughout this textbook: careful attention to what evidence can and cannot establish, honest acknowledgment of scholarly disagreement, and firm resistance to the temptation to fill evidentiary gaps with comforting but unsupported narratives, whether nationalist, romantic, or simply convenient. The debate over Jeongok-ri's age and the unresolved question of Paleolithic-to-Neolithic population continuity are not signs of a poorly understood subject; they are honest reflections of how difficult, and how genuinely interesting, the study of deep human prehistory actually is.

At the same time, this chapter has shown that the peninsula's Paleolithic populations were neither isolated nor static. They participated in broader Northeast Asian patterns of technological development, adapted continuously to dramatic environmental change, and, over hundreds of thousands of years, developed increasingly varied technological repertoires that reflect genuine skill, knowledge, and, very plausibly, methods of teaching and cultural transmission between generations. These patterns of adaptation, connection, and gradual change will recur, in different forms, throughout the rest of this textbook.

As the next chapter turns to the Neolithic communities that emerged as the Early Holocene unfolded, students should carry forward this chapter's central lesson: that even the most fundamental transformations in human history, from mobile foraging toward more settled, food-producing communities, unfolded gradually, unevenly, and for reasons that historians and archaeologists continue to investigate rather than fully resolve. Understanding the Paleolithic peninsula on its own terms, without projecting later identities or assuming inevitable progress, is the necessary foundation for everything that follows.
