South Asia as a Historical Region

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

South Asia is not simply "ancient India" wearing a more careful name, and it is not a backdrop waiting for the arrival of the modern nation-states that now occupy it. It is a historically formed geographic and human region — bounded by mountains, shaped by two great river systems and a rhythm of seasonal winds, and inhabited across more than five thousand years by societies whose political boundaries, languages, and identities have shifted again and again. Before this textbook can narrate the Indus cities, the Vedic cattle-herders, the Mauryan emperors, the Delhi sultans, the Mughal court, the colonial state, or the modern republics of India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives, it must first define, carefully, the region all of that history occupies.

Stand today at the edge of the Thar Desert at dusk, where the sand gives way in the distance to the irrigated green of the Indus plain, and it is possible to sense something that written history confirms again and again: this landmass has always been a place of sharp environmental contrast held together by a handful of great connecting features — rivers, mountain passes, monsoon winds, and long-traveled roads and sea lanes. A farmer working a rice paddy in the Bengal delta, a herder moving flocks across the high pastures of Ladakh, a fisherman working the Malabar coast, and a trader crossing the Khyber Pass with pack animals have, across many centuries, inhabited a single connected historical world without ever occupying identical environments or speaking mutually intelligible languages. Understanding how such a varied landmass came to be studied as one coherent historical region — while never becoming culturally, religiously, linguistically, or politically uniform — is the task of this opening chapter.

This textbook uses the term "South Asia" as its primary geographic frame, while treating India — because of its central place in the title, its geographic scale, and its demographic weight — as the narrative's central thread rather than its only subject. The lands now called Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives are not peripheral footnotes to an Indian story; for much of the period this textbook covers, the very idea of "India" as a single political or even cultural unit did not exist, and the borders that today separate these modern states are, on the timescale of this book, extremely recent. A history that ignored this would misrepresent almost every period before the twentieth century.

Consider a single illustrative complication. An archaeologist excavating a Mature Harappan city in Sindh, in present-day Pakistan, a philologist studying hymns composed in early Vedic Sanskrit somewhere in the Punjab, a Buddhist monk copying a Pali manuscript in medieval Sri Lanka, and a Bengali peasant farming rice under Mughal revenue administration in the delta that today straddles India and Bangladesh are all, in a meaningful historical sense, subjects of this textbook — yet none of them belonged to a state called "India" in the way the word is used today, and projecting that modern label backward onto their worlds would distort rather than clarify the history this book sets out to tell.

This chapter does not begin with the Indus cities, the Vedic hymns, or the Mauryan Empire. It begins with something more basic: an honest account of what "South Asia" means as a historical region, what physical and environmental features have repeatedly shaped human life across it, what kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct its past, and what dangers arise from studying that past through the lens of borders and identities that did not yet exist. Getting this foundation right shapes everything that follows across the following one hundred and ten chapters.

The geographic frame adopted here is deliberately broad. It includes the entire Indian subcontinent — the great landmass bounded by the Himalayan arc to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west, and the Bay of Bengal to the east — together with Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and the Himalayan kingdoms of Nepal and Bhutan, and it remains attentive to the subcontinent's long-standing connections with Afghanistan, Central Asia, Tibet, China, Southeast Asia, the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Peninsula, and East Africa. India forms the central narrative of this textbook because of its geographic scale, demographic weight, and the title of the work itself, but the histories of the other South Asian lands are woven throughout, not appended as an afterthought.

Understanding South Asia as a historical region requires the same disciplined tools historians bring to any large, ancient, and diverse civilizational area: careful attention to geography and environment, to chronology, to the varied kinds of evidence that survive from different periods, to the languages and scripts in which that evidence is recorded, and to the dangers of reading later political and religious categories backward into periods that did not recognize them. It also requires a certain humility, because much of the earliest evidence — above all the still-undeciphered Indus script — resists confident interpretation, and because even well-documented later periods remain subjects of genuine, continuing scholarly debate.

This chapter establishes the geographic scope, evidentiary foundations, linguistic landscape, and periodization principles that will govern the next one hundred and ten chapters. It explains why this textbook is titled a history of South Asia with India as its central narrative, rather than simply "a history of India," and why that distinction — often blurred in casual usage — matters for writing this history honestly, completely, and without erasing the many peoples and polities whose pasts belong within it.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook — the Indus Civilization of Chapters 2 through 4, the Vedic and early Iron Age worlds of Chapters 5 through 8, the Second Urbanization and the rise of Buddhism and Jainism in Chapters 9 through 14, the Mauryan Empire of Chapters 15 through 18, the early historic and classical worlds of Chapters 19 through 37, the Islamic frontiers and the Delhi Sultanate of Chapters 38 through 49, the Mughal Empire and the eighteenth-century transformation of Chapters 50 through 61, Company rule and colonial conquest of Chapters 62 through 75, nationalism and Partition of Chapters 76 through 87, and postcolonial South Asia of Chapters 88 through 111 — they need a shared, carefully defined geographic and evidentiary foundation.

The lands this textbook studies span an extraordinary range of environments: the high, glaciated peaks of the Himalaya, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush; the fertile alluvial plains built by the Indus and Ganges-Brahmaputra river systems; the arid expanse of the Thar Desert straddling the modern India-Pakistan border; the ancient, eroded uplands of the Deccan Plateau; the narrow, monsoon-drenched coastal strips of the Konkan, Malabar, and Coromandel coasts; the vast delta of Bengal, one of the most densely populated and agriculturally productive regions on earth; the terraced hill kingdoms of Nepal and Bhutan; and the low coral atolls of the Maldives and the island of Sri Lanka, oriented toward the surrounding Indian Ocean. No single environment, climate, or economic system unified all of these regions in antiquity, and none does so today.

Historians studying South Asia must resist two opposite errors, familiar from the study of any large and long-inhabited world region. The first error treats "India," or "South Asia," as an essentially timeless civilizational unity stretching back to remote antiquity, projecting modern national or religious identity onto Harappan city-dwellers, Vedic cattle-herders, or medieval sultans who would not have recognized such categories. The second error, a reaction against the first, minimizes the genuine historical connections — linguistic, religious, commercial, artistic, and political — that have linked the peoples of this subcontinent across thousands of years, treating South Asian history as nothing more than an arbitrary sequence of unrelated regional episodes. This textbook adopts neither error. It treats the deep environmental, linguistic, religious, and commercial connections across the subcontinent as historically real and significant, while insisting that political unity was rare, partial, and temporary, and that South Asia's regional, religious, linguistic, and social diversity has been the norm across most of its documented past, not an exception to an otherwise unified story.

A further complication concerns the very act of naming the region. "India" derives, through Persian and Greek intermediaries, from the Sanskrit name for the Indus River, Sindhu; the term is many centuries older than any state that has ever borne it as an official name, and its geographic reach has varied enormously across history, at different times denoting the Indus valley alone, the entire subcontinent, or the specific territory of the modern Republic of India founded in 1950. "South Asia" is a still more recent term, developed largely in the twentieth century, chiefly in academic and international-relations contexts, as a way of naming the subcontinent and its neighboring island and Himalayan states without privileging any single modern nation-state's name for the whole. This textbook adopts "South Asia" as its primary geographic frame for exactly this reason, while continuing to use "India," "the Indian subcontinent," and "the subcontinent" where they are historically appropriate and clearly understood in context. Section 1.1 examines these terms and their relationships in greater depth.

Section 1.1

Defining South Asia and the Indian Subcontinent

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France or Japan, the geographic referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a single, currently existing state whose borders have been relatively stable for generations. A history of South Asia has no such simple anchor. Definitions of the region's boundaries vary among scholars and institutions, and some academic and diplomatic frameworks also include Afghanistan. For purposes of this textbook, the core South Asian frame consists of seven sovereign states — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives — which this textbook treats as its primary subject, while treating Afghanistan as a closely connected neighboring region discussed wherever it intersects directly with South Asian history. This core region today has a combined population exceeding one and a half billion people, dozens of major languages, every major world religion represented in significant numbers, and a documented history extending back more than five thousand years to the beginnings of the Indus Civilization.

This opening section establishes the central geographic commitment of the entire textbook: South Asia is the organizing geographic frame, and India forms the central narrative within it, but India is not a synonym for the entire region across all periods of its history. This commitment has several consequences that will recur throughout the following chapters. First, this textbook does not treat the modern Republic of India, founded in 1950, as though it had existed continuously since the Indus Civilization, the Vedic period, the Mauryan Empire, or the Mughal Empire, because doing so would misrepresent the political realities of every one of those periods, none of which recognized a state called "India" with anything resembling its modern boundaries, government, or citizenship. Second, this textbook does not treat "South Asia" as though it were culturally, religiously, or linguistically uniform, because the region's defining historical feature, alongside its deep connections, has been its sustained internal diversity. Third, this textbook does not use "India" as a religious category, synonymous with Hinduism, because South Asian history includes major Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, Sikh, Christian, and other religious traditions whose historical development this textbook treats as central rather than peripheral to the region's story.

None of these clarifications is intended to diminish India's centrality to this textbook's narrative. India's geographic scale, demographic weight, and the fact that the book bears the title "Indian History" mean that developments centered in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, the Deccan, and peninsular India receive sustained, detailed attention throughout. But treating "India" as an unbroken civilizational unity extending backward through five thousand years, or treating South Asia as though it were simply India's periphery, would misrepresent the actual historical record this textbook sets out to explain.

Historical Vocabulary
Subcontinent, India, and South Asia

The word India descends, through Old Persian and then Ancient Greek intermediaries, from Sindhu, the Sanskrit name for the Indus River. Persian speakers rendered the initial consonant as "h," producing "Hindu" as a geographic term for the lands beyond that river, from which "Hindustan" and eventually the Greek "India" both derive. In its earliest attested uses, the term denoted the Indus valley itself, not the entire subcontinent; only gradually, as Persian, Greek, and later European geographic knowledge of the region expanded, did "India" come to denote the much larger landmass this textbook studies.

The Indian subcontinent is a geological and geographic term describing the large landmass, once part of an ancient supercontinent, that collided with the Eurasian plate over tens of millions of years, producing the Himalayan mountain arc as a byproduct of that ongoing collision. Geographically, the subcontinent is generally understood to include the territory of present-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal and Bhutan's southern lowlands, bounded by the Himalaya to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west, and the Bay of Bengal to the east.

South Asia is the broadest and most recently developed of the three terms, coming into widespread academic and diplomatic use chiefly in the second half of the twentieth century. It encompasses the Indian subcontinent together with Sri Lanka and the Maldives — island nations not physically part of the subcontinental landmass but historically, culturally, and commercially deeply interconnected with it — and it is the term this textbook adopts as its primary geographic frame precisely because it does not imply that any single modern nation-state's name should stand for the whole historical region.

Section 1.2

Mountains, Plateaus, Plains, Coasts, and Islands

South Asia's physical geography divides into several major zones, each of which has repeatedly shaped where people settled, what they grew, how they traveled, and how political power was organized.

The Himalayan arc, together with the neighboring Karakoram and Hindu Kush ranges, forms the subcontinent's northern frontier — the highest and among the youngest mountain systems on earth, still rising as the Indian tectonic plate continues to press northward into Eurasia. These mountains have never been an absolute barrier; high passes such as the Khyber, the Bolan, and numerous Himalayan routes have carried migrants, armies, pilgrims, and traders for millennia, a theme examined further in Section 1.6. Nepal and Bhutan occupy the southern Himalayan slopes and have developed distinct political and cultural histories examined in Chapters 36 and 91.

South of the mountains lies the vast Indo-Gangetic Plain, built up over geological time by sediment carried down from the Himalaya by the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra river systems. This plain, extending from Punjab in the west through the Gangetic heartland to the Bengal delta in the east, has been the demographic and political core of most major South Asian empires, discussed in detail in Section 1.3.

To the west, straddling the modern India-Pakistan border, lies the Thar Desert, an arid zone that has historically functioned less as an empty barrier than as a frontier zone inhabited by pastoral and agro-pastoral communities and traversed by caravan routes linking Sindh, Rajasthan, and Gujarat.

Peninsular India south of the Gangetic plain is dominated by the Deccan Plateau, an ancient, geologically stable tableland flanked by the Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats mountain ranges, which together shape the peninsula's rainfall patterns by intercepting monsoon winds. The Deccan's rivers — the Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri among the most significant — flow generally eastward toward the Bay of Bengal, supporting distinct regional agricultural and political systems examined especially in Chapters 22, 31 through 33, and 45 through 46.

The subcontinent's coastlines present sharply different environments: the narrow, monsoon-drenched Konkan and Malabar coasts on the west, historically oriented toward Arabian Sea trade with the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Peninsula, and East Africa; and the broader Coromandel coast on the east, oriented toward Bay of Bengal trade with Southeast Asia. The Bengal delta, where the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers meet the sea across present-day Bangladesh and eastern India, is among the most fertile and densely populated regions on earth, and its distinctive riverine and deltaic geography recurs throughout this textbook's treatment of Bengal.

Finally, Sri Lanka, a substantial island separated from the subcontinental mainland by the shallow Palk Strait, and the Maldives, a chain of low coral atolls in the Indian Ocean southwest of the Indian mainland, complete this textbook's geographic frame. Both developed distinct political and cultural histories while remaining closely connected to South Asian and wider Indian Ocean networks, examined in Chapters 24, 36, 91, 96, 103, and 104.

Section 1.3

The Indus and Ganges River Systems

Two river systems have shaped South Asian history more than any other physical feature: the Indus, with its Punjab tributaries, and the Ganges, joined in its lower course by the Brahmaputra.

The Indus River rises in the Tibetan plateau and flows generally southwest through present-day Ladakh and Pakistan before reaching the Arabian Sea through a large delta in Sindh. Along its middle course it is joined by five major tributaries flowing down from the western Himalaya — the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej — whose combined waters gave the surrounding plain its historic name, Punjab, meaning "five waters" in Persian. This river system's seasonal floods deposited fertile silt across a wide floodplain, and it was across the wider Indus or Harappan cultural zone — including the Indus-Punjab river system, the Ghaggar-Hakra region, parts of the Yamuna-Ganga interfluve, and Gujarat, not the Indus valley alone — that the cities of the Mature Harappan civilization arose around the middle of the third millennium BCE, examined fully in Chapters 3 and 4. The Indus system remains, into the twenty-first century, one of the most heavily irrigated and geopolitically contested river systems in the world, an issue examined in its modern form in Chapter 109.

The Ganges River rises in the central Himalaya and flows southeast across the Gangetic plain before joining the Brahmaputra, which itself rises in Tibet and flows through the eastern Himalaya and Assam, in the vast delta that forms most of Bangladesh and part of eastern India. The Gangetic plain's exceptional agricultural fertility, combined with agricultural intensification during the later Vedic period discussed in Chapter 7 — a period in which increasingly widespread iron technology contributed to cultivation and land clearance, although historians debate how decisive iron tools were relative to demographic, ecological, and political factors — supported the population growth and urban expansion associated with the Second Urbanization examined in Chapter 9, and the plain subsequently formed the demographic and political core of the Mauryan, Gupta, Delhi Sultanate, and Mughal states, among others.

These two river systems did not merely provide water for irrigation. They functioned as transportation corridors, as zones of exceptionally dense settlement, as the material basis for the tax revenue that sustained large states and their armies, and, at times, as contested frontiers and disputed resources, a pattern that recurs from ancient irrigation disputes through to twentieth- and twenty-first-century water-sharing agreements between India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh.

Section 1.4

Monsoons, Climate, and Environmental Variation

No single environmental feature has shaped South Asian agriculture, settlement, and historical vulnerability more decisively than the monsoon — the seasonal reversal of wind patterns that brings heavy rainfall to most of the subcontinent between roughly June and September, followed by a long dry season for much of the remaining year. The southwest monsoon, driven by differential heating between the Asian landmass and the surrounding Indian Ocean, delivers the bulk of the region's annual rainfall in a concentrated period, a pattern to which South Asian agriculture, religious calendars, and historical famine and flood cycles have long been closely tied. This summer southwest monsoon is not, however, the only monsoon system that matters to this textbook's geographic frame: southeastern peninsular India and Sri Lanka depend heavily on a second system, the October–December northeast monsoon, which supplies a very substantial share of their annual rainfall and has shaped agriculture and settlement in Tamilakam, the Coromandel coast, and Sri Lanka in ways distinct from the Indo-Gangetic Plain's rainfall pattern, a point this textbook returns to in Chapters 23 and 24.

Monsoon rainfall, however, is neither uniform across the subcontinent nor perfectly reliable from year to year. The windward slopes of the Western Ghats and the northeastern Himalayan foothills receive extremely heavy rainfall, while regions in the rain shadow of these mountains, including large parts of the Deccan interior and much of western India, receive far less and are historically prone to drought. A weak or delayed monsoon in any given year could, and repeatedly did, produce agrarian crisis and famine, a pattern documented from ancient inscriptions through to well-recorded colonial-era famines examined in Chapter 72, and continuing to shape contemporary concerns about monsoon variability under climate change, examined in Chapter 109.

Beyond the monsoon, South Asia's climate varies enormously by region and altitude: the high Himalaya and Karakoram experience alpine and even permanently glaciated conditions; the Thar Desert experiences an arid climate with minimal and unreliable rainfall; the Gangetic plain and much of peninsular India experience a tropical or subtropical monsoon climate; and the southwestern coast and Sri Lanka experience heavy, relatively reliable rainfall supporting different agricultural systems, including rice cultivation and plantation crops discussed in later chapters. Understanding this variation is essential to understanding why South Asian history cannot be told as a single agricultural or economic story, but must instead account for genuinely different regional economies shaped by genuinely different environments.

Section 1.5

Ecological Regions and Human Settlement

The combination of geological structure and monsoon climate produces a set of distinct ecological regions across South Asia, each of which has supported different patterns of human settlement, subsistence, and social organization across history.

The alluvial river plains of the Indus and Ganges systems, with their fertile soils and reliable water supply, supported dense agricultural settlement, surplus production, and eventually urbanization and state formation, discussed throughout Volume I. The semi-arid zones of Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the Deccan interior supported a mixture of dry-farming agriculture and pastoralism, along with distinct traditions of water management, including step-wells and tank irrigation, that allowed settled life to persist in regions of limited and unreliable rainfall. The forested and hill regions of central and eastern India, home to numerous communities often described as Adivasi (a term overlapping with, but not identical to, "indigenous" or "tribal" in the legal or international sense) discussed at relevant points throughout this textbook, supported shifting cultivation, forest-product gathering, and hunting alongside settled agriculture, and these communities' relationships with expanding agrarian states form a recurring, and often difficult, theme addressed particularly from Chapter 17 onward.

The high Himalayan and trans-Himalayan zones supported pastoral and limited agricultural economies adapted to short growing seasons and thin, rocky soils, alongside long-distance trade in goods such as salt, wool, and, in some regions, precious stones. The coastal and deltaic regions, by contrast, supported dense rice-cultivating populations, extensive fishing communities, and port-based commercial economies connected to Indian Ocean trade networks discussed in Section 1.6 and throughout later chapters.

No single ecological region can stand for the whole of South Asian history. A textbook organized only around the Gangetic plain's political history, for example, would systematically neglect the Deccan, the far south, the northeast, the Himalayan states, and the coasts — a pattern this textbook explicitly works to avoid through its attention to regional variation throughout.

Section 1.6

Routes into and across the Subcontinent

Despite its mountainous northern frontier, South Asia has never been historically isolated. Several overland and maritime routes have repeatedly connected the subcontinent to the wider Eurasian and Indian Ocean worlds, carrying migrants, armies, traders, pilgrims, and ideas in both directions.

In the northwest, the Khyber Pass and other routes through the mountains connecting the Kabul region to the Peshawar valley and the Indus plains have served, across many centuries, as a corridor for migration and invasion, from Persian, Greek, Scythian, Kushan, Hun, Ghaznavid, Ghurid, Mughal, and other forces discussed in later chapters. Indo-Aryan-speaking communities, discussed in Chapter 5, are generally understood to have entered the subcontinent through northwestern corridors in and around the Hindu Kush, though reconstructing any single specific route is more precise than the surviving evidence warrants, a caution developed further in that chapter. Further south, routes through Baluchistan, including the Bolan Pass, connected the Indus valley to the Iranian plateau.

Overland routes also extended eastward and northward, connecting South Asia to Central Asia and, through it, to the Silk Road networks linking China, Central Asia, Iran, and the Mediterranean world, a connection examined particularly in the discussion of the Kushan Empire in Chapter 21. High Himalayan passes connected parts of the subcontinent to Tibet, supporting trade in salt, wool, and religious exchange associated with the spread of Buddhism.

Maritime routes proved equally significant. The Arabian Sea connected the subcontinent's western coast to the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Peninsula, the Red Sea, and East Africa, supporting trade in spices, textiles, and other goods documented from the early historic period examined in Chapter 25 through the medieval Indian Ocean world of Chapter 49 and beyond. The Bay of Bengal connected the eastern coast to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, a network that carried Buddhist and Brahmanical religious influence, Sanskrit and Tamil literary culture, and extensive trade goods, discussed particularly in Chapters 23 through 25 and 33.

These routes matter historically because they demonstrate that South Asia's history cannot be understood as a closed, self-contained story. Migration, conquest, trade, and religious and intellectual exchange moved in multiple directions across these routes throughout the entire period this textbook covers, a theme this textbook returns to repeatedly rather than treating outside contact as a series of isolated interruptions to an otherwise self-contained subcontinental history.

Section 1.7

Archaeological, Literary, Epigraphic, and Numismatic Evidence

Historians reconstruct South Asia's past using several distinct and complementary categories of evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations.

Archaeological evidence — excavated settlements, artifacts, skeletal remains, pollen and sediment records, and increasingly, genetic evidence from ancient DNA — provides the primary source of information for periods before, and often for centuries after, the emergence of writing. Archaeology can establish settlement patterns, technology, diet, trade contacts, and, with careful interpretation, some aspects of social organization, but it generally cannot recover language, personal names, political events, or detailed belief systems without corroborating textual evidence.

Literary evidence — the Vedic corpus, the Buddhist and Jain canonical literatures, the Sanskrit epics and Puranas, Sangam Tamil poetry, later court chronicles, religious hagiography, and much else — provides rich information about language, religious belief, social norms, and, in some cases, historical memory, but literary sources were often composed, compiled, or edited long after the events they describe, transmitted orally for generations before being written down, and shaped by the religious, political, or literary purposes of their compilers. Historians must therefore distinguish carefully between what a text reveals about the period it describes and what it reveals about the period, sometimes centuries later, in which it reached the form that survives today.

Epigraphic evidence — inscriptions carved on rock, pillars, copper plates, and temple walls — offers some of the most valuable contemporary documentation available to historians of ancient and medieval South Asia, because inscriptions are typically dated, or datable, and were not usually altered after being carved. Ashoka's rock and pillar edicts, examined in Chapter 16, and the vast corpus of land-grant and temple inscriptions examined throughout Volumes I and II, provide direct evidence of royal ideology, administrative practice, land tenure, and religious patronage, though inscriptions typically represent the perspective of rulers, elites, and religious institutions rather than ordinary people.

Numismatic evidence — coins and their inscriptions, imagery, metal content, and find-spots — allows historians to trace political authority, trade networks, and economic conditions, particularly for periods and regions with limited surviving textual documentation, such as the post-Mauryan northwest examined in Chapter 20.

For later periods, this evidentiary base expands to include Persian and Arabic chronicles and administrative documents, European travelers' accounts, colonial administrative and legal records, census data, newspapers, photographs, and, for the contemporary era, government publications, statistical records, and oral history. Each category of evidence answers some historical questions better than others, and responsible historical writing requires combining multiple kinds of evidence while remaining honest about what each can and cannot establish.

Section 1.8

Languages, Scripts, and Problems of Historical Reconstruction

South Asia is home to several major language families whose historical relationships and geographic distributions bear directly on how this textbook can and cannot reconstruct the region's past.

The Indo-Aryan languages, a branch of the Indo-European language family, include Old Indo-Aryan languages such as Vedic and Classical Sanskrit and, through later Middle Indo-Aryan (Prakrit) and New Indo-Aryan developments rather than direct descent from Classical Sanskrit itself, modern languages including Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Punjabi, Gujarati, Marathi, Sinhala, Nepali, and many others; these languages are spoken predominantly across northern, central, and western South Asia and Sri Lanka. The Dravidian languages — including Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam — are spoken predominantly across southern India and represent a language family unrelated to Indo-European, with an independent and, in the case of Tamil, exceptionally well-documented ancient literary tradition examined in Chapter 23. The Sino-Tibetan languages are spoken across the Himalayan and northeastern regions, including many languages of Nepal and Bhutan and numerous languages of northeastern India, even though Nepal's own majority language, Nepali, is itself Indo-Aryan. Austroasiatic languages, including Munda languages, are spoken by various communities primarily in eastern and central India. This linguistic diversity long predates, and has persisted throughout, every political empire this textbook examines, and it means that no single language can be treated as though it were the sole vehicle of South Asian history.

Writing itself has a complicated history in the subcontinent. The Indus script, attested on thousands of seals and short inscriptions from Mature Harappan sites, remains undeciphered. Despite numerous proposed decipherments over the past century, no reading commands broad scholarly consensus, and historians cannot currently state with confidence what language or languages the script recorded, what it said, or even with certainty whether it represents a full writing system in the same sense as cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphics. This textbook treats claims about Indus language, religion, or political organization derived from supposed script readings with appropriate caution, a principle developed further in Chapter 4.

The earliest securely dated and deciphered inscriptions of South Asia are the third-century BCE edicts of Ashoka, examined in Chapter 16, carved in Brahmi, Kharosthi, Greek, or Aramaic depending on the region in which a given edict was placed. Brahmi became ancestral to most of the later regional scripts used to write Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit languages, Tamil, and the subcontinent's many other languages across the following two millennia, while Kharosthi, ultimately related to Aramaic script, remained primarily a northwestern tradition and did not become the general ancestor of later South Asian writing systems. Understanding which script and language a given historical source uses, and when that script and language came into use, is essential to dating and interpreting South Asian historical evidence responsibly.

Historical Vocabulary
Periodization

Periodization is the historian's practice of dividing the past into named periods — such as "ancient," "early medieval," "medieval," "early modern," "colonial," and "postcolonial" — in order to organize historical narrative and analysis. Periodization is a useful and, within limits, necessary interpretive tool, but it is not a neutral or self-evident feature of the past itself. Different historians, and different national or religious traditions, have periodized South Asian history differently — for example, by dividing it into "Hindu," "Muslim," and "British" periods, a framework, associated with certain colonial-era historians, that this textbook explicitly avoids because it implies that political power in each period straightforwardly reflected the religious identity of the ruling dynasty, and because it obscures the religious diversity present within every period of South Asian history.

This textbook instead organizes its chapters chronologically and thematically, dividing the whole work into six volumes corresponding to the ancient, early medieval and Sultanate, Mughal and eighteenth-century, colonial, nationalist and Partition, and postcolonial periods, while remaining attentive, throughout, to the fact that these divisions reflect political, economic, and institutional turning points rather than absolute breaks in South Asian society, religion, or culture.

Section 1.9

Periodization and the Problem of Modern Borders

The modern political map of South Asia — the Republic of India, the Islamic Republic of Pakistan, the People's Republic of Bangladesh, the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka, the Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal, the Kingdom of Bhutan, and the Republic of Maldives — is, on the timescale of this textbook, extremely recent. India and Pakistan became independent states in 1947; Bangladesh emerged as an independent state from what had been East Pakistan in 1971; Sri Lanka gained independence, as Ceylon, in 1948; Nepal and Bhutan, while never formally colonized, underwent major political transformations in the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries examined in Chapters 91 and 104; and the Maldives gained full independence in 1965.

None of these modern political boundaries existed during the Indus Civilization, the Vedic period, the Mauryan Empire, the Gupta Empire, the Delhi Sultanate, or the Mughal Empire. Applying them retroactively — describing ancient Gandhara, in the region of present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan, as "ancient Pakistani civilization," or describing the medieval Bengal Sultanate as either purely Indian or purely Bangladeshi history — would be historically inaccurate and would misrepresent how the people of those periods actually understood their own political geography. This textbook consistently uses historically appropriate regional terms, such as those introduced in Section 1.2, rather than projecting modern national labels backward, while still using modern national names when discussing the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, when those states did exist and when their citizens do identify with them.

This caution extends to religious and ethnic categories as well. Modern religious communities, political parties, and national identities did not exist in their present form in ancient or medieval periods, and this textbook takes care not to describe ancient Vedic society as "Hindu" in the modern sectarian sense, not to describe every medieval Muslim ruler's policies as representative of "Islam" as a whole, and not to treat any modern ethnic or linguistic community as an unchanging entity extending unbroken from antiquity to the present. At the same time, this textbook does not avoid discussing religious, ethnic, or national identity where it is historically documented and relevant; the goal is precision, not evasion.

Periodization itself, discussed in the vocabulary box above, is one of the historian's most important tools for managing this complexity responsibly. By dividing this textbook's 111 chapters into six volumes organized around major political, economic, and institutional transformations — rather than around the reigns of dynasties treated as though they were the whole of history, or around a simple story of religious succession — this textbook aims to give students an accurate sense of when major changes actually occurred, without implying that those changes were sudden, total, or inevitable.

Section 1.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has established the geographic frame, physical environment, evidentiary foundations, linguistic landscape, and periodization principles that govern the study of South Asian history throughout this textbook. Several threads developed separately across the preceding nine sections come together here into a single interpretive foundation for the 110 chapters that follow.

The geographic argument of Sections 1.1 and 1.2 and the environmental argument of Sections 1.3 through 1.5 are closely linked: South Asia is a historically coherent region not because it has ever been politically or culturally uniform, but because a shared set of physical features — the Himalayan mountain arc, the Indus and Ganges river systems, the monsoon, and a recurring pattern of ecological zones from mountain to desert to delta to coast — has repeatedly structured where people settled, what they grew, how dense their populations became, and how vulnerable they were to drought and flood, across every political system this textbook examines, from the Indus cities through the modern republics. Geography did not determine South Asian history in any simple sense; South Asia's great riverine plains supported the Indus cities, the Mauryan Empire, the Delhi Sultanate, the Mughal Empire, and the colonial and postcolonial Punjab and Gangetic states, each organized on entirely different political, religious, and economic principles. But geography set recurring possibilities and constraints that this textbook will return to throughout, especially in its treatment of agriculture, trade, and environmental history.

The connectivity argument of Section 1.6 establishes a second recurring theme: South Asia's mountainous and maritime frontiers have functioned throughout history as zones of exchange, not merely as barriers. The Khyber Pass, the Arabian Sea, and the Bay of Bengal appear repeatedly across this textbook's chapters — northwestern corridors through and around the Hindu Kush carrying Indo-Aryan-speaking migrants in the second millennium BCE, Persian and Greek influence in the first millennium BCE, Buddhist missionaries and merchants across the early centuries CE, Arab, Turkic, and Central Asian conquerors and settlers from the seventh century CE onward, European trading companies from the sixteenth century, and, in the modern era, migrant laborers, students, and diaspora communities moving in both directions between South Asia and the wider world. Students should resist any account of South Asian history that treats the subcontinent as a sealed, self-contained world periodically "interrupted" by outside contact; connection has been the historical norm, not the exception.

The evidentiary argument of Sections 1.7 and 1.8 establishes a third, methodologically essential theme: the quality and character of historical evidence changes dramatically across the periods this textbook covers, and historians must adjust their claims accordingly. For the Indus Civilization, archaeological evidence is rich but the undeciphered script leaves language, political organization, and belief largely inaccessible to direct textual confirmation. For the Vedic period, a vast oral literary corpus survives, but it was composed and transmitted over centuries before being fixed in writing, and it does not function as a straightforward historical chronicle. From the Mauryan period onward, epigraphic and numismatic evidence increasingly allows historians to anchor political history to securely dated contemporary sources, a pattern that expands further with the Persian, Arabic, and European documentary traditions of the medieval and early modern periods, and further still with the vast documentary, statistical, and photographic record of the colonial and postcolonial eras. This chapter's caution about the Indus script, oral Vedic transmission, and the difference between contemporary and retrospective sources will recur as a standing methodological principle throughout the textbook.

Finally, the periodization argument of Section 1.9 establishes the safeguard that makes responsible use of the preceding themes possible: modern national borders, modern religious communities, and modern ethnic and linguistic identities cannot be projected backward onto periods that did not recognize them without seriously distorting the historical record. This principle will apply with particular force to the treatment of ancient Gandhara and the northwest in Chapters 14 and 20, to the treatment of Bengal across many chapters, to the treatment of the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal Empire in Volumes II and III, and above all to the treatment of Partition in Chapter 86 and the postcolonial histories of Volume VI, where the temptation to read later national outcomes backward into earlier centuries is strongest and the historiographical stakes are highest.

Chapter 2 builds directly on this foundation by turning to the earliest evidence of human settlement and agriculture in South Asia, tracing the long transition from Paleolithic foraging through Mesolithic transitional communities to the early farming societies, exemplified by Mehrgarh, whose economic and social foundations eventually supported the emergence of Indus urbanism examined in Chapter 3. The geographic, environmental, evidentiary, and linguistic framework established in this chapter will be assumed, and built upon, throughout everything that follows.

Primary Source Window

Al-Biruni, Kitab al-Hind (Kitab tahqiq ma li-l-Hind), c. 1030 CE

Creator: Abu Rayhan al-Biruni, Central Asian polymath, astronomer, and scholar · Date: completed c. 1030 CE · Type: Systematic ethnographic, religious, scientific, and geographic account of Indian society, composed in Arabic for a learned audience within the wider Islamic intellectual world

Context: Al-Biruni, born in Khwarezm in Central Asia, spent time in northern India during the era of Ghaznavid campaigns discussed in Chapter 39, learning Sanskrit and studying Indian mathematics, astronomy, and religious literature directly from Brahmin scholars rather than relying solely on earlier secondhand accounts. His resulting work, known in English translation as Alberuni's India, is widely regarded by modern historians as an unusually careful and comparatively evenhanded outside description of early eleventh-century Indian society, though it remains the perspective of an outside observer writing for an outside audience.

Paraphrase: Al-Biruni observed that Indian scholars possessed sophisticated traditions in astronomy and mathematics, comparable in seriousness to those he knew from the Greek and Islamic scientific traditions, while also noting significant differences in religious belief, social custom, and scientific method that he attributed partly to limited communication between Indian and outside scholarly communities.

Analysis: Al-Biruni's work is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates that sophisticated outside description and analysis of South Asian society, religion, and science existed centuries before the era of European colonial scholarship, and because Al-Biruni himself explicitly criticized earlier, less careful outside accounts of India for relying on rumor and prejudice rather than direct study. At the same time, Al-Biruni wrote as an outsider, associated in some respects with the very Ghaznavid campaigns that had disrupted the northern Indian polities he described, and his access to Indian society, while unusually deep for his time, remained shaped by his own religious, linguistic, and political position.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a Central Asian scholar's eleventh-century account of Indian society be considered unusually careful compared to other outside accounts of his era?
  2. What does Al-Biruni's decision to learn Sanskrit and study directly with Brahmin scholars suggest about the kind of evidence historians should look for when evaluating outside accounts of a society?
  3. What are the limitations of relying on any single outside observer, however careful, to reconstruct the full range of South Asian society in a given period?
  4. How does the existence of a work like Al-Biruni's complicate any assumption that serious cross-cultural historical and scientific study of India began only with modern European scholarship?
Historical Debate

Where Should South Asian History Properly Begin?

The question: Should a history of South Asia begin with the Indus Civilization, the first documented urban society in the region, or should it begin earlier, with the much longer span of Paleolithic and Mesolithic human habitation and the early farming societies, such as Mehrgarh, that laid the economic foundations for later urbanism? Historians differ on how much independent weight to give to these long pre-urban periods relative to the urban and literate societies that followed.

Interpretation A — South Asian history begins with the Indus Civilization: Some historians and textbook traditions treat the Mature Harappan urban phase, beginning around 2600 BCE, as the effective starting point of South Asian history proper, on the grounds that only with urbanism, craft specialization, and an extensive inscribed sign or script tradition does the region produce the kind of complex society and material record that historical narrative can meaningfully organize around. On this view, the preceding many thousands of years of foraging and early farming are important background, covered by archaeology and prehistory, but not "history" in the fuller sense this textbook otherwise uses.
Interpretation B — South Asian history begins much earlier, with Paleolithic settlement and early farming: Other historians argue that treating the Indus cities as the starting point risks reducing well over a million years of securely attested hominin occupation, and several millennia of documented agricultural development at sites such as Mehrgarh, to a mere prelude, even though these periods are essential for explaining how and why urbanism became possible in the Indus valley when and where it did. On this view, excluding the deep past from "history" simply because it lacks texts reflects an unnecessarily narrow, text-centered definition of historical study.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record — Paleolithic stone tool assemblages, Mesolithic rock-shelter sites, and the excavated agricultural settlement at Mehrgarh and related sites — but differ in how much independent narrative and instructional weight to give this material relative to the urban Indus Civilization that followed.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically rather than doctrinally: it devotes Chapter 2 to foraging communities and early agriculture before treating the Indus Civilization in Chapters 3 and 4, treating the deep past as historically significant in its own right and as the necessary explanatory foundation for Harappan urbanism, while organizing its overall chapter architecture so that the Indus cities remain the first period to receive extended chapter-length treatment as a "civilization."

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which starting point you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by each choice of starting point.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gets to Name and Bound "South Asia"? Perspectives on Regional Definition

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a twentieth-century Indian nationalist historian: "India" names a civilizational unity with deep cultural continuity stretching from the Indus Civilization to the modern republic, and emphasizing that continuity is essential to understanding the subcontinent's distinctive historical identity, even as this view requires care not to flatten the profound political and religious diversity within that long continuity.
From the perspective of a scholar of Pakistani, Bangladeshi, or Sri Lankan history: Organizing the region's history around "India" as the default frame risks treating the histories of the other South Asian states as peripheral extensions of an Indian story, when in fact each has its own distinct political, religious, and cultural trajectory, particularly from the medieval period onward, that deserves treatment on its own terms.
From the perspective of a twentieth-century Western academic developing regional-studies terminology: "South Asia" offered a seemingly neutral geographic and academic category for organizing university programs, library classifications, and foreign-policy analysis, but this framework itself emerged from a particular Cold War-era institutional context and reflects external analytical needs as much as any single internal regional self-understanding.
From the perspective of an Adivasi or Himalayan community member: Definitions of "India" or "South Asia" organized primarily around the Indo-Gangetic plain's political and literary traditions can obscure the distinct languages, religious practices, and historical experiences of communities in the hill, forest, and high-mountain regions who have often stood at the margins of textual and political records dominated by lowland agrarian states.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Geography as a Historical Actor

What Changed

  • Political control over the Indus and Ganges river systems passed among many different states and empires across five thousand years
  • Names for the region — Sindhu, Hindush, India, Hindustan, South Asia — expanded and shifted in geographic scope over time
  • The technologies used to exploit the subcontinent's rivers and monsoon rainfall changed dramatically, from early canal irrigation to colonial-era canal networks to modern dams and groundwater extraction
  • Modern national borders divided river systems, mountain ranges, and cultural regions that had not previously been politically separated in the same way

What Stayed the Same

  • The Himalayan mountain arc has consistently formed the subcontinent's northern frontier and a zone of both barrier and connection
  • The monsoon has consistently governed the agricultural calendar and remains a major source of both prosperity and vulnerability
  • The Indo-Gangetic Plain has repeatedly served as the demographic and political core of major states, from the Mauryas to the Mughals to the modern Indian and Pakistani states
  • Overland and maritime connections to Central Asia, the Iranian world, the Arabian Sea, and the Bay of Bengal have persisted across every period this textbook examines

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating South Asia's geography as an unchanging, deterministic force that explains everything, or ignoring geography entirely as a mere backdrop to political and religious history.

Cause and Effect

The Monsoon and Historical Life

Long-term causes

The subcontinent's position relative to the Himalaya and the surrounding Indian Ocean produces a seasonal reversal of wind patterns, concentrating most annual rainfall into a few summer months across much of the region.

Short-term causes

Year-to-year variation in monsoon timing, intensity, and geographic distribution, driven by broader Indian Ocean and Pacific climate patterns, produces unpredictable local surpluses, shortfalls, floods, and droughts.

Effect

Agricultural societies across South Asia developed irrigation systems, water storage, cropping patterns, and religious and calendrical practices closely tied to monsoon timing, while remaining historically vulnerable to famine and displacement when the monsoon failed or arrived in destructive excess.

This textbook's response

Environmental history, including monsoon variability, recurs throughout this textbook's treatment of agriculture, famine, colonial economic policy, and contemporary climate change, rather than appearing only in this introductory chapter.

Historical complexity: Monsoon failure alone rarely explains a famine; historians must also examine landholding, taxation, transportation, markets, and government policy, themes developed especially in Chapter 72's treatment of colonial famine policy.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Geography and Region Belong in Everyday Life

Geography and region were not abstractions to the people who lived within them; they structured daily diet, housing, clothing, labor, travel, and religious practice across the periods this textbook studies. A rice-cultivating household in the Bengal delta, a wheat- and barley-growing household on the Punjab plain, a millet-growing household on the semi-arid Deccan, and a pastoral household in the high Himalaya each experienced daily material life shaped directly by the crops their environment supported, the water-management techniques their region required, and the trade and transportation networks available to them.

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society and everyday life examine how ordinary agricultural, pastoral, artisanal, and mercantile communities across these different environments experienced village life, markets, taxation, religious festivals tied to agricultural and monsoon cycles, and, in the modern era, changing technology and administration. Because most surviving pre-modern written sources were produced by a literate religious, administrative, or commercial elite, historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own — peasants, pastoralists, artisans, laborers, women, and forest-dwelling communities — while also drawing on archaeology, material culture, and, for later periods, administrative and statistical records to reconstruct their experiences more fully.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced South Asia as this textbook's organizing geographic frame and has carefully distinguished it from the narrower, and historically much more recent, category of the modern Republic of India. South Asia, as this textbook defines and studies it, is a geographic and historical region encompassing the Indian subcontinent together with Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and the Himalayan states of Nepal and Bhutan, unified not by any single political authority across most of its history but by a recurring set of physical and environmental features: the Himalayan mountain arc, the Indus and Ganges-Brahmaputra river systems, the monsoon, and a persistent pattern of ecological diversity from mountain to desert to plateau to delta to coast to island.

The chapter has emphasized that "India," "the Indian subcontinent," and "South Asia" are related but distinct terms with different histories, geographic scopes, and appropriate uses, and that careful use of these terms is essential to accurate historical understanding. It has traced how "India," beginning as a name for the Indus River and the territory immediately around it, gradually broadened over more than two thousand years of Persian, Greek, Arabic, and European usage before becoming, through the Charter Act of 1833 and then Crown rule after 1858, the name of a broad imperial political system encompassing British India and the princely states — a system that itself divided at independence in 1947 and again with the emergence of Bangladesh in 1971.

Students have been introduced to the subcontinent's major physical regions — the Himalayan arc, the Indo-Gangetic Plain, the Thar Desert, the Deccan Plateau, the coastal lowlands, and the island societies of Sri Lanka and the Maldives — and to the ways in which the monsoon and regional ecological variation have shaped agriculture, settlement, and historical vulnerability to famine and flood across every period this textbook examines. The chapter has also introduced the overland and maritime routes connecting South Asia to Central Asia, the Iranian world, Tibet, the Arabian Sea, and the Bay of Bengal, establishing that connection, not isolation, has been the historical norm.

The chapter established the evidentiary foundations on which the rest of this textbook depends: archaeological, literary, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence, each with distinct strengths and limitations, and each requiring careful handling. It gave particular attention to the undeciphered Indus script, to the oral composition and later transmission of the Vedic corpus, and to the general principle that literary and religious texts describing an earlier period often reveal as much about the period in which they reached their surviving form as about the events they claim to describe. It also introduced the subcontinent's major language families — Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Sino-Tibetan, and Austroasiatic — establishing that no single language has ever been the sole vehicle of South Asian history.

Finally, the chapter addressed periodization and the dangers of projecting modern national, religious, and ethnic categories backward onto periods that did not recognize them. The modern states of India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives are, on the timescale of this textbook, extremely recent, and applying their borders, names, or associated identities to the Indus Civilization, the Vedic period, the Mauryan Empire, the Delhi Sultanate, or the Mughal Empire would misrepresent the political and social realities of those periods. The Historical Debate feature on where South Asian history should properly begin illustrated this challenge concretely: how much independent weight should be given to the deep pre-urban past relative to the urban, eventually literate societies that followed?

These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following one hundred and ten chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of ancient civilizations, classical empires, medieval sultanates, the Mughal Empire, European colonialism, nationalist struggle, Partition, and the postcolonial states of contemporary South Asia.

A physiographic and historical term whose exact boundaries vary by usage, centered on the landmass south of the Himalaya comprising India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh and often extended to neighboring Himalayan and island regions; this textbook uses it interchangeably with "the subcontinent" for that broad core landmass.
India (as a term)
A name deriving from Sanskrit "Sindhu" (the Indus River) via Persian and Greek intermediaries; its geographic scope broadened gradually over more than two millennia, becoming, through the 1833 Charter Act and Crown rule after 1858, the name of a broad imperial political system encompassing British India and the princely states under paramountcy.
Indo-Gangetic Plain
The fertile alluvial plain built by the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra river systems; the demographic and political core of most major northern South Asian empires.
Deccan Plateau
The elevated peninsular tableland of central and southern India, flanked by the Western and Eastern Ghats, home to distinct political and cultural traditions.
Monsoon
The seasonal reversal of wind patterns governing South Asian climate; the summer southwest monsoon brings concentrated rainfall to most of the subcontinent between roughly June and September, while a distinct winter northeast monsoon brings concentrated rainfall to southeastern peninsular India and Sri Lanka between roughly October and December. Historically decisive for agriculture, settlement, and famine vulnerability.
Ecological region
A zone defined by shared climate, terrain, and vegetation that historically supported a distinct pattern of subsistence and settlement, such as the alluvial plains, semi-arid zones, forested hill regions, high mountains, and coastal deltas.
Periodization
The historian's practice of dividing the past into named periods for organizational and analytical purposes; an interpretive tool rather than a neutral feature of the past itself.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, border, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions carved on rock, pillars, copper plates, or other durable materials; a major source of contemporary evidence for ancient and medieval South Asian history.
Numismatics
The study of coins and their inscriptions, imagery, and metal content; valuable for tracing political authority and trade, especially where texts are scarce.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of South Asian history have changed over time and why.
Primary source
A document, object, or record created at or near the time being studied, by a participant or contemporary witness.
Secondary source
A later interpretation of the past, typically by a historian, drawing on primary sources.
Indus script
The still-undeciphered script or sign system attested on Mature Harappan seals and short inscriptions; no proposed decipherment commands broad scholarly consensus, and it remains uncertain whether it constitutes a full writing system in the sense of cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphics.
Indo-Aryan languages
A branch of the Indo-European language family, including Old Indo-Aryan languages such as Vedic and Sanskrit and, through later Middle and New Indo-Aryan developments rather than direct descent from Classical Sanskrit, modern languages including Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Punjabi, Gujarati, Marathi, Sinhala, and Nepali, spoken predominantly across northern, central, and western South Asia and Sri Lanka.
Dravidian languages
A language family unrelated to Indo-European, including Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, spoken predominantly across southern India.
Sino-Tibetan languages
A language family spoken across the Himalayan and northeastern regions of South Asia, including many languages of Nepal and Bhutan and numerous languages of northeastern India (Nepal's majority language, Nepali, is itself Indo-Aryan).
Austroasiatic languages
A language family including Munda languages spoken by various communities primarily in eastern and central India.
Al-Biruni
Eleventh-century Central Asian polymath whose Arabic-language Kitab al-Hind provides a detailed outside ethnographic and scientific account of Indian society.
Adivasi
A term used, particularly in India, for communities often described in English as "indigenous" or "tribal," historically associated with forested and hill regions of central, eastern, and northeastern South Asia; the category overlaps with, but is not identical to, the legal category of Scheduled Tribe or international definitions of "Indigenous peoples," and its scope remains politically and regionally contested.
Mehrgarh
An early farming settlement site in Balochistan (present-day Pakistan), providing key evidence for the agricultural foundations that preceded Indus urbanism, examined further in Chapter 2.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.
Regional diversity
The recognition that South Asia varies significantly by sub-region rather than forming a single uniform society, economy, or culture.
Transregional connection
Historical linkages—commercial, religious, political, or migratory—that cross the boundaries of a single region, connecting South Asia to Central Asia, the Iranian world, the Arabian Sea, and the Bay of Bengal.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces — distinguishing South Asia from India from the Indian subcontinent, treating geography and environment as historically significant without treating them as deterministic, reading evidence carefully according to its kind and its distance from the events it describes, and refusing to project modern national borders backward onto periods that did not recognize them — are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

South Asia's history rests on one of the world's longest and richest archaeological and textual historical records, and it is among the most environmentally varied and most linguistically and religiously diverse historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included some of the world's earliest cities, a millennia-long oral religious and literary tradition, some of history's largest pre-modern empires, centuries of Indo-Islamic state formation, one of the largest and most thoroughly documented early modern empires, two centuries of colonial rule, a mass nationalist movement that reshaped ideas of self-government worldwide, one of the largest forced migrations in recorded history, and, in the postcolonial era, the emergence of several of the world's most populous nations navigating democracy, development, and security in a rapidly changing region and world. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to the region's historical significance — it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that South Asia is a geographic and historical region shaped by mountains, rivers, and monsoon rather than a synonym for any single modern nation-state, that evidence must be read according to its kind and its distance from the events it describes, and that the region's many peoples, languages, and states are integral rather than peripheral to its history, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook — from the Indus Civilization of Part I through the Vedic and classical worlds of Parts II and III, the Mauryan and early historic empires of Parts IV and V, the classical and early medieval kingdoms of Parts VI and VII, the Islamic frontiers and Mughal Empire of Parts VIII through X, the eighteenth-century transformation and colonial conquest of Parts XI and XII, the Rebellion of 1857 and the Raj of Part XIII, nationalism and independence of Parts XIV and XV, and the building and contemporary development of the postcolonial states in Parts XVI through XVIII — with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the deep prehistoric past of Chapter 2 through the unresolved questions of contemporary South Asia examined in Chapter 111. It is a story of many regions, many peoples, and many religious and linguistic communities, joined by shared geography, long-standing connection, and a continuous, often contested, historical experience — not a single unbroken thread and not a predetermined national outcome. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
