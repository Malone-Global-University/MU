Back Matter — Overview

How to Use This Back Matter

The back matter of this textbook is a reference system, not a summary. It is designed to be consulted repeatedly — during problem-solving, before assessments, when reviewing for Calculus I, and whenever a formula, graph, identity, or definition needs to be confirmed. Each appendix is built around a specific topic that recurs across multiple chapters.

Use this back matter actively. When you are working through an identity verification problem and you cannot remember the sum formula for cosine, turn to Appendix G. When you need to check the shape of a parent function before analyzing a transformation, turn to Appendix C. When you are fitting a model to data and need to recall which regression type is appropriate, turn to Appendix Q. This material exists to support your reasoning, not to replace it.

Appendix A

Algebra II Readiness Review

This appendix reviews the Algebra II skills that this textbook assumes. If any of these topics feel unfamiliar, spend time reviewing them before moving through the chapters. Each precalculus topic builds directly on at least one of these foundations.

A.1Factoring Polynomials
Key Factoring Patterns
GCF: 6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3) Difference of squares: a² − b² = (a+b)(a−b) Perfect square trinomial: a² + 2ab + b² = (a+b)² Sum/difference of cubes: a³ ± b³ = (a ± b)(a² ∓ ab + b²) Trinomial (a=1): x² + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q), where p·q = c and p+q = b Trinomial (a≠1): use ac-method or grouping
A.2Exponent Rules
Laws of Exponents
Product rule: aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ Quotient rule: aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0) Power rule: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ Zero exponent: a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0) Negative exponent: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ Fractional exponent: aᵐ/ⁿ = ⁿ√(aᵐ) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ
A.3Quadratic Formula and Discriminant
Quadratic Formula
For ax² + bx + c = 0: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a Discriminant D = b² − 4ac D > 0: two distinct real solutions D = 0: one real solution (double root) D < 0: no real solutions (two complex)
A.4Rational Expressions
Operations with Rational Expressions
Simplify: Factor numerator and denominator; cancel common factors. Multiply: Multiply numerators; multiply denominators; simplify. Divide: Multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor. Add/Subtract: Find LCD; rewrite with LCD; add/subtract numerators.
Restrictions: values that make any denominator zero must always be excluded from the domain.
A.5Solving Systems of Equations
Systems Methods
Substitution: Solve one equation for one variable; substitute into the other. Elimination: Multiply equations to create opposite coefficients; add to eliminate a variable. Graphical: Find intersection point(s) of the two graphs. Matrix/Cramer: Used for larger systems (reviewed in Appendix A supplement).

Pythagorean Theorem
For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c: a² + b² = c² Distance between points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂): d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)

Common Mistake — Horizontal Direction

f(x − 3) shifts the graph right 3 units, not left. The subtraction inside the argument moves the graph in the positive x-direction. Think: the graph reaches its parent position when x − 3 = 0, that is, when x = 3.

Appendix F

Unit Circle Reference

The unit circle is a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Every point on it has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ), where θ is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis. The six trigonometric functions can be read directly from any point on the unit circle.

Appendix G

Trigonometric Identities Reference

G.1Reciprocal and Quotient Identities
Reciprocal Identities
csc θ = 1/sin θ sec θ = 1/cos θ cot θ = 1/tan θ sin θ = 1/csc θ cos θ = 1/sec θ tan θ = 1/cot θ
Quotient Identities
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ cot θ = cos θ / sin θ
G.2Pythagorean Identities
The Three Pythagorean Identities
sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ (derived: divide first identity by cos²θ) 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ (derived: divide first identity by sin²θ)
Each Pythagorean identity has two alternate forms obtained by rearranging: for example, sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ and cos²θ = 1 − sin²θ. These alternate forms are used constantly in simplification and verification.
G.3Even-Odd and Cofunction Identities
Even-Odd Identities
Even: cos(−θ) = cos θ sec(−θ) = sec θ Odd: sin(−θ) = −sin θ csc(−θ) = −csc θ Odd: tan(−θ) = −tan θ cot(−θ) = −cot θ
Cofunction Identities (θ in radians)
sin(π/2 − θ) = cos θ cos(π/2 − θ) = sin θ tan(π/2 − θ) = cot θ cot(π/2 − θ) = tan θ sec(π/2 − θ) = csc θ csc(π/2 − θ) = sec θ
G.4Sum and Difference Identities
Sum and Difference Formulas
sin(A + B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B sin(A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B cos(A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B cos(A − B) = cos A cos B + sin A sin B tan(A + B) = (tan A + tan B) / (1 − tan A tan B) tan(A − B) = (tan A − tan B) / (1 + tan A tan B)
G.5Double-Angle and Half-Angle Identities
Double-Angle Identities
sin(2θ) = 2 sin θ cos θ cos(2θ) = cos²θ − sin²θ = 1 − 2sin²θ = 2cos²θ − 1 tan(2θ) = 2 tan θ / (1 − tan²θ)
Half-Angle Identities
sin(θ/2) = ±√((1 − cos θ)/2) cos(θ/2) = ±√((1 + cos θ)/2) tan(θ/2) = ±√((1 − cos θ)/(1 + cos θ)) = sin θ/(1 + cos θ) = (1 − cos θ)/sin θ
The ± sign in half-angle identities depends on which quadrant θ/2 lies in. Determine the correct sign from the quadrant of the half-angle before computing.

Appendix I

Law of Sines and Law of Cosines Reference

I.1Law of Sines
Law of Sines
For any triangle with sides a, b, c opposite to angles A, B, C: a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C Use when given: AAS, ASA, or SSA (the ambiguous case).
The Ambiguous Case (SSA)

When given two sides and an angle opposite one of them (SSA), there may be 0, 1, or 2 valid triangles. Compare the given side opposite the angle to the height of the triangle: h = b sin A. If the given side a is less than h, no triangle exists. If a = h, one right triangle exists. If h < a < b, two triangles exist. If a ≥ b, one triangle exists.

I.2Law of Cosines
Law of Cosines
a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A b² = a² + c² − 2ac cos B c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C Use when given: SAS or SSS.
When angle A = 90°, cos A = 0, and the formula reduces to the Pythagorean theorem: a² = b² + c². The Law of Cosines is a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem to all triangles.
I.3Triangle Area Formulas
Area Formulas
Standard: Area = (1/2) · base · height Two sides, included angle: Area = (1/2) ab sin C Heron's formula: Area = √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)), where s = (a+b+c)/2

Appendix K

Exponential and Logarithmic Rules Reference

K.1Properties of Logarithms
Logarithm Properties (for b > 0, b ≠ 1, and positive M, N)
Product rule: log_b(MN) = log_b M + log_b N Quotient rule: log_b(M/N) = log_b M − log_b N Power rule: log_b(Mⁿ) = n · log_b M Change of base: log_b M = log M / log b = ln M / ln b Inverse: log_b(bˣ) = x and b^(log_b x) = x Special values: log_b 1 = 0 log_b b = 1
K.2Exponential Growth, Decay, and Continuous Growth
Standard Exponential Forms
Discrete growth/decay: A(t) = A₀ · bᵗ (b > 1 growth; 0 < b < 1 decay) Percentage rate form: A(t) = A₀(1 + r)ᵗ (r = rate as decimal) Continuous growth/decay: A(t) = A₀ eʳᵗ (r > 0 growth; r < 0 decay) Half-life: A(t) = A₀ · (1/2)^(t/h) (h = half-life period) Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) Continuous compound: A = Peʳᵗ
Appendix L

Sequence and Series Formula Sheet

L.1Arithmetic Sequences and Series
Arithmetic Sequence
Explicit formula: aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d Recursive formula: aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + d, with a₁ given Common difference: d = aₙ − aₙ₋₁ Partial sum (n terms): Sₙ = n/2 · (a₁ + aₙ) = n/2 · (2a₁ + (n−1)d)
L.2Geometric Sequences and Series
Geometric Sequence
Explicit formula: aₙ = a₁ · rⁿ⁻¹ Recursive formula: aₙ = r · aₙ₋₁, with a₁ given Common ratio: r = aₙ / aₙ₋₁ Partial sum (n terms): Sₙ = a₁(1 − rⁿ) / (1 − r) (r ≠ 1) Infinite series sum: S = a₁ / (1 − r) (only when |r| < 1)
If |r| ≥ 1, the infinite geometric series diverges and has no finite sum.
L.3Sigma Notation
Reading Sigma Notation
∑(k=1 to n) aₖ = a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + … + aₙ The index k runs from 1 to n; each term is found by substituting k. ∑(k=1 to n) c = cn (sum of a constant n times) ∑(k=1 to n) k = n(n+1)/2 ∑(k=1 to n) k² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6

Parametric Form
x = f(t), y = g(t) (t is the parameter) Eliminating the parameter: solve one equation for t; substitute into the other. Circular motion: x = r cos t, y = r sin t (traces a circle of radius r) Projectile motion: x = v₀cos(θ)·t, y = v₀sin(θ)·t − (1/2)gt²

Appendix O

Vector Formula Reference

Vectors in Component Form
Vector: v = ⟨a, b⟩ or v = ai + bj Magnitude: |v| = √(a² + b²) Direction angle: θ = arctan(b/a) (adjust for quadrant) Unit vector: û = v / |v| = ⟨a/|v|, b/|v|⟩ Addition: u + v = ⟨u₁+v₁, u₂+v₂⟩ Subtraction: u − v = ⟨u₁−v₁, u₂−v₂⟩ Scalar multiplication: c·v = ⟨ca, cb⟩ Dot product: u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ Angle between: cos θ = (u · v) / (|u| · |v|) Perpendicular if: u · v = 0 Parallel if: u = c·v for some scalar c
From Magnitude and Direction to Components

If a vector has magnitude r and makes angle θ with the positive x-axis, its component form is ⟨r cos θ, r sin θ⟩. This connects vectors directly to the unit circle and to parametric equations.

Appendix P

Limit and Continuity Preview Reference

Limit Notation and Meaning
lim(x→c) f(x) = L means: as x approaches c, f(x) approaches L lim(x→c⁺) f(x) right-hand limit: x approaches c from values greater than c lim(x→c⁻) f(x) left-hand limit: x approaches c from values less than c A two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. lim(x→∞) f(x) = L end behavior: f(x) approaches L as x grows without bound
Continuity at a Point
f is continuous at x = c if ALL THREE conditions hold: 1. f(c) is defined 2. lim(x→c) f(x) exists 3. lim(x→c) f(x) = f(c) Discontinuities: removable (hole), jump, or infinite (asymptote)
Average and Instantaneous Rate of Change — Preview
Average rate of change from a to b: (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a) This is the slope of the secant line through (a, f(a)) and (b, f(b)). As b → a, the secant line approaches the tangent line at x = a. Instantaneous rate of change = slope of tangent line = derivative at a (Calculus I).
Bridge to Calculus I

Every formula in this appendix previews a concept that Calculus I will develop formally. The limit is the foundation of the derivative and the integral. Continuity is the condition that makes most calculus techniques valid. The secant-to-tangent transition is how the derivative is defined. This appendix is not the end — it is the doorstep.

Technology Warning — Radian vs. Degree Mode

Always check whether your calculator is in degree mode or radian mode before evaluating trigonometric functions. Most precalculus and all calculus work uses radians. Graphing sin(x) in degree mode produces a nearly flat graph on a standard window because the period is 360, not 2π.

Full Textbook Glossary

Glossary

The glossary defines every major term introduced in this textbook. Terms are listed alphabetically. Each entry identifies the chapter where the term first appears. For related formulas, see the corresponding appendix.

A
Amplitude
Chapter 5
The vertical distance from the midline to the maximum or minimum value of a sinusoidal function. Equals |a| in y = a sin(bx).
Analytic geometry
Chapter 11
The study of geometric objects using coordinate systems and algebraic equations, connecting shape to formula.
Arc length
Chapter 3
The length of a portion of a circle's circumference. For radius r and central angle θ in radians: s = rθ.
Asymptote
Chapter 8
A line that a graph approaches but never touches. May be vertical, horizontal, or slant (oblique).
Average rate of change
Chapter 1
The change in output divided by the change in input over an interval: (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a). Equals the slope of the secant line.
C
Cardioid
Chapter 12
A heart-shaped polar curve with equation r = a(1 ± cos θ) or r = a(1 ± sin θ).
Cofunction identity
Chapter 6
An identity relating a trigonometric function to its cofunction at the complementary angle. Example: sin(π/2 − θ) = cos θ.
Composition
Chapter 2
Applying one function to the output of another. (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). The output of g becomes the input of f.
Conic section
Chapter 11
A curve formed by the intersection of a plane and a double cone. The four types are circle, parabola, ellipse, and hyperbola.
Continuity
Chapter 13
A function is continuous at x = c if f(c) is defined, the limit exists at c, and the limit equals f(c). Informally: no holes, jumps, or asymptotes.
Coterminal angles
Chapter 3
Angles that share the same terminal side. Formed by adding or subtracting multiples of 360° or 2π.
D
Discontinuity
Chapter 13
A point where a function fails to be continuous. Types: removable (hole), jump, and infinite (asymptote).
Domain
Chapter 1
The set of all input values for which a function is defined. Restrictions arise from division by zero, even roots of negatives, and logarithms of non-positives.
Dot product
Chapter 12
For vectors u = ⟨u₁, u₂⟩ and v = ⟨v₁, v₂⟩, u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂. Equals |u||v|cos θ, where θ is the angle between them.
E
Eccentricity
Chapter 11
A measure of how much a conic deviates from a circle. e = c/a. Circle: e = 0. Ellipse: 0 < e < 1. Parabola: e = 1. Hyperbola: e > 1.
Even function
Chapter 2
A function satisfying f(−x) = f(x) for all x in its domain. Graph is symmetric about the y-axis. Example: f(x) = x² or cos x.
Exponential function
Chapter 9
A function of the form f(x) = aˣ (a > 0, a ≠ 1). Models multiplicative change: growth when a > 1, decay when 0 < a < 1.
F
Function
Chapter 1
A rule that assigns exactly one output to every input in its domain. Can be expressed as an equation, table, graph, or verbal description.
Function notation
Chapter 1
The notation f(x), read "f of x," that names the output of function f at input x. f(3) means: evaluate f when the input is 3.
I
Identity (trig)
Chapter 6
An equation involving trigonometric functions that is true for all values of the variable where both sides are defined.
Infinite geometric series
Chapter 10
A geometric series with infinitely many terms. Has a finite sum S = a₁/(1 − r) only when |r| < 1.
Inverse function
Chapter 2
A function f⁻¹ that reverses the input-output relationship of f. If f(a) = b, then f⁻¹(b) = a. The graphs of f and f⁻¹ are reflections over y = x.
L
Limit
Chapter 13
The value that f(x) approaches as x approaches c. Written lim(x→c) f(x) = L. The function need not equal L at x = c.
Logarithm
Chapter 9
The inverse of the exponential function. log_b(x) = y means bʸ = x. Answers the question: "To what power must b be raised to produce x?"
M
Mathematical induction
Chapter 10
A proof technique for statements about all positive integers. Prove the base case; assume true for n = k; prove true for n = k + 1.
Midline
Chapter 5
The horizontal line y = k midway between the maximum and minimum of a sinusoidal function. The vertical shift moves the midline from y = 0.
Multiplicity
Chapter 8
The number of times a factor (x − r) appears in a polynomial. Controls whether the graph crosses (odd multiplicity) or touches (even multiplicity) the x-axis at r.
O
Odd function
Chapter 2
A function satisfying f(−x) = −f(x) for all x in its domain. Graph has 180° rotational symmetry about the origin. Example: f(x) = x³ or sin x.
One-to-one function
Chapter 2
A function where every output corresponds to exactly one input. Satisfies the horizontal line test. Only one-to-one functions have inverse functions.
P
Parameter
Chapter 12
An independent variable t used to express both x and y as functions in parametric equations. Often represents time or angle.
Period
Chapter 5
The horizontal length of one complete cycle of a periodic function. For y = sin(bx), period = 2π/|b|.
Phase shift
Chapter 5
A horizontal translation of a trigonometric graph. In y = sin(b(x − h)), the phase shift is h (right if h > 0, left if h < 0).
Polar coordinates
Chapter 12
A coordinate system where a point is described by (r, θ): r is the distance from the origin and θ is the angle from the positive x-axis.
Pythagorean identity
Chapter 6
The fundamental identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, derived from the unit circle equation x² + y² = 1. Leads to two related identities.
R
Radian
Chapter 3
An angle measure defined by the ratio of arc length to radius: θ = s/r. One full revolution = 2π radians = 360°.
Range
Chapter 1
The set of all possible output values of a function. Determined by evaluating what y-values the function can produce across its entire domain.
Reference angle
Chapter 3
The positive acute angle formed between the terminal side of an angle and the x-axis. Used to evaluate trigonometric functions in any quadrant.
S
Secant line
Chapter 13
A line connecting two points on the graph of a function. Its slope equals the average rate of change between those points.
Sequence
Chapter 10
A function whose domain is the set of positive integers. Each term is an output; the index is the input.
Series
Chapter 10
The sum of the terms of a sequence. A finite series sums a limited number of terms; an infinite series sums all terms and may or may not converge.
Sigma notation
Chapter 10
Compact notation for a series using the Greek letter Σ. The index, lower bound, upper bound, and general term specify what is being summed.
T
Tangent line
Chapter 13
A line that touches a curve at exactly one point and matches the curve's direction at that point. Its slope is the instantaneous rate of change — defined formally as the derivative in Calculus I.
Transformation
Chapter 2
An algebraic change to a function that produces a predictable change to its graph: shifts, reflections, stretches, and compressions.
U
Unit circle
Chapter 4
The circle of radius 1 centered at the origin, described by x² + y² = 1. Every point is (cos θ, sin θ) for angle θ measured from the positive x-axis.
Unit vector
Chapter 12
A vector with magnitude 1. Found by dividing a vector by its magnitude: û = v/|v|. Points in the same direction as v.
V
Vector
Chapter 12
A quantity with both magnitude and direction, represented as a directed line segment or in component form ⟨a, b⟩.
Vertical asymptote
Chapter 8
A vertical line x = a where a function increases or decreases without bound. Occurs at non-canceling zeros of the denominator of a rational function.
