    The Thirty Years' War and the Peace of Westphalia

    European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

    Chapter Opening

    On 23 May 1618, a group of Bohemian nobles threw two royal governors from a window of Prague Castle. All three men who were thrown survived. The war that followed lasted until 1648 and reshaped Central Europe.

    The Thirty Years' War began as a dispute over religious rights and political authority in one kingdom of the Holy Roman Empire and became a series of connected wars involving the Habsburgs, German princes, Denmark, Sweden, France, Spain, and the Dutch Republic. It is often remembered as the last great war of religion, but its causes also included imperial law, dynastic succession, the privileges of noble estates, and rivalry among states.

    The chapter follows the war in four overlapping phases: Bohemian-Palatine (1618 to 1623), Danish (1625 to 1629), Swedish (1630 to 1635), and Franco-Swedish (1635 to 1648). These labels are modern conveniences, because armies, alliances, and negotiations overlapped. It then examines the economic and social effects of the war and the settlement reached at Münster and Osnabrück.

    The war devastated many regions of Central Europe through occupation, forced contributions, famine, and epidemic disease, though losses varied sharply from place to place. The chapter therefore treats civilians, women, villagers, and townspeople as historical actors alongside rulers and commanders.

    The settlement of 1648 recognized Reformed Protestants alongside Catholics and Lutherans, confirmed the rights of the imperial estates, redistributed territory, and recognized Dutch and Swiss independence from their former rulers. It did not end religious conflict, abolish the Empire, or create a system of equal sovereign states, an argument developed in the final sections.

    Evidence includes treaties, imperial decrees, diplomatic correspondence, army and tax records, parish registers, town chronicles, diaries, pamphlets, and engravings. Each has limits, and later literature and national memory have colored the war's image.

    This chapter completes Part X by drawing together the religious divisions studied in Chapters 61 to 66. It builds on Chapter 66's treatment of the French Wars of Religion and the Dutch Revolt, and it prepares for Chapter 68, which begins Part XI on maritime expansion, and for Part XII on states and the balance of power.

    Historical Context

    By 1600 the Holy Roman Empire contained hundreds of territories with their own rulers, laws, and churches. Lutheranism (Chapter 62) and Reformed Protestantism (Chapter 63) had spread widely, and Catholic reform (Chapter 65) had given the Catholic side new organization. The Peace of Augsburg of 1555 had created a legal truce, but it excluded Reformed Protestants and left key questions about church property open.

    The Habsburg dynasty had two main branches. The Spanish branch ruled Spain, much of Italy, the Spanish Netherlands, and overseas empires, while the Austrian branch held the imperial title and ruled Austria, Bohemia, and part of Hungary. The Dutch Revolt (Chapter 66) was still unresolved, and the Twelve Years' Truce between Spain and the Dutch expired in 1621, which connected German events to the Spanish struggle.

    Warfare had also changed. Larger armies, firearms, and artillery fortification increased costs, and rulers relied on mercenaries and credit (see Chapters 55 and 74). Climate conditions of the Little Ice Age (Chapter 2) made harvests vulnerable. Dates in this chapter follow the Gregorian calendar unless otherwise stated, since Sweden used the Julian calendar and Protestant and Catholic territories adopted the Gregorian calendar at different times.

    Section 67.1

    The Holy Roman Empire on the Eve of War

    The Holy Roman Empire in 1618 was a layered political order, not a German national state. It stretched from the Low Countries and Alsace to Bohemia and Silesia, and included Italian fiefs, so that its extent cannot be mapped onto modern Germany. Its ruler was an emperor chosen by seven electors under the Golden Bull of 1356: the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne, the king of Bohemia, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the duke of Saxony, and the margrave of Brandenburg. Since 1438 the title had almost always passed to a Habsburg, but election meant each emperor had to bargain with the electors and sign a capitulation limiting his powers.

    Beneath the emperor stood the imperial estates, those who held a seat in the Imperial Diet: secular princes such as the rulers of Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg, and Hesse, ecclesiastical princes including prince-bishops and abbots, and the free and imperial cities. The Diet met in three colleges, of electors, princes, and cities, and consented to imperial taxation, including funds against the Ottomans. Two supreme courts, the Reichskammergericht at Speyer and the Reichshofrat at Vienna, heard disputes among estates and between rulers and subjects. Imperial Circles organized local defense and the collection of taxes. The emperor's own wealth and soldiers came largely from Habsburg hereditary lands, a distinction that matters because the Habsburg monarchy and the Empire were not the same thing.

    Historians rely on Diet proceedings, court records, capitulations, princely correspondence, and the constitutional treatises of lawyers to reconstruct this system. These sources show that disputes were usually fought through litigation, petitions, and negotiation, which helps explain why religious disputes in the Empire could be deeply legal in character. They also show a limit: the emperor lacked a standing army and a permanent tax system, so enforcing a decision depended on estates and on his own dynastic resources. When that dependence became politicized, as the following sections show, the constitution could stall rather than resolve conflict.

    Section 67.2

    The Peace of Augsburg and the Unresolved Confessional Question

    The Peace of Augsburg of 1555, examined in Chapter 62, was a constitutional compromise rather than a declaration of religious toleration. It permitted the imperial estates, but not their subjects, to adopt either the Catholic or the Lutheran confession, and guaranteed subjects who disagreed the right to emigrate. The later phrase cuius regio, eius religio, coined by a jurist around 1600, summarizes this principle but does not appear in the treaty. The settlement was a political truce intended to preserve order, and its drafters left several questions deliberately vague.

    Three problems proved especially serious. First, Reformed Protestantism, or Calvinism, was not covered, yet it spread in the Palatinate from the 1560s and later in Hesse-Kassel and, after Elector John Sigismund's conversion in 1613, in Brandenburg. Second, the Ecclesiastical Reservation required a Catholic prelate who converted to lose his office and lands, but Protestants disputed it, and the Declaratio Ferdinandea, a statement by King Ferdinand I protecting Lutheran subjects of ecclesiastical lords, was never formally part of the treaty. Third, church lands that had been secularized after 1552, including several northern bishoprics, remained contested.

    By the early seventeenth century these ambiguities mattered more because Catholic reform (Chapter 65), led by Jesuits and by rulers such as the dukes of Bavaria, had given Catholic leaders new energy, while Calvinist princes felt excluded from the legal settlement. The seizure of the Protestant city of Donauwörth by Bavaria in 1607, after riots connected with a procession, and the failure of the Diet of 1608 when Protestant delegations walked out, showed that the legal system could no longer settle religious disputes. Historians stress that these conflicts concerned jurisdiction and property as much as doctrine.

    Section 67.3

    The Protestant Union, Catholic League, and the Bohemian Crisis

    The breakdown of the Diet led to the formation of military alliances. The Protestant Union of 1608, headed by the Calvinist Elector Palatine Frederick IV and advised by Christian of Anhalt, brought together mostly Calvinist and some Lutheran estates. In response, the Catholic League of 1609 was founded under Duke Maximilian of Bavaria. Neither alliance was a unified confessional bloc: Lutheran Saxony, the most important Protestant elector, stayed outside the Union because it distrusted Calvinist leadership.

    The Habsburg family also quarreled. Emperor Rudolf II lost most of his powers in a dispute with his brother Matthias, who took Hungary, Austria, and Moravia in 1608 and Bohemia in 1611. In Bohemia the estates, including Catholics, Utraquists, Lutherans, and the Bohemian Brethren, had long defended their privileges. In July 1609 Rudolf issued the Letter of Majesty, which guaranteed freedom of worship and allowed Protestant estates to build churches and to elect Defenders to watch over the settlement. Because the document was a royal charter, it could be read either as a binding constitutional guarantee or as a concession from a king under pressure.

    The question of succession then reopened the issue. Matthias had no children, and the estates accepted his cousin Ferdinand of Styria as heir in 1617. Ferdinand, educated by Jesuits, had restored Catholicism in his own lands, and Protestant nobles feared he would do the same in Bohemia. Disputes arose over Protestant churches at Braunau and Klostergrab, built on lands the Catholic abbot and archbishop claimed as their own; royal officials ordered the buildings closed or demolished in 1617 and 1618. The Defenders and estates replied that the Letter of Majesty had been violated.

    Section 67.4

    The Defenestration of Prague and the Bohemian Revolt, 1618

    On 23 May 1618, a delegation of Protestant nobles led by Count Thurn confronted the king's regents at Prague Castle. They blamed the regents Jaroslav Martinitz and Vilém Slavata and the secretary Philip Fabricius for the royal letters. They threw the three men from a window, an act with precedents in earlier Bohemian history and a recognized form of protest that was meant to punish counselors rather than depose the king. The three survived, and rival accounts explained this either as providential or as the result of a pile of rubbish below.

    The estates then created a government of thirty directors, raised an army, expelled the Jesuits, and issued an Apology explaining their case in legal terms. They also looked abroad. Ernst von Mansfeld, commanding troops financed by Savoy and the Protestant Union, took Pilsen in November 1618. The significance of the defenestration lay not in the violence itself but in what followed: a political crisis involving the privileges of the estates became an armed rebellion against Habsburg rule.

    The sources include the Apology of the estates, Slavata's memoirs, diplomatic reports, and later Catholic and Protestant pamphlets. All were written by participants or partisans, so historians use them with caution. They also leave out the views of peasants and townspeople who bore the cost of the army and of recruitment, though their experience becomes clearer in the later sections of this chapter.

    Section 67.5

    Ferdinand II, Frederick V, and the Contest for Bohemia

    Matthias died in March 1619, and the Bohemian directors, who had already allied with Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia, sent troops into Austria. In June 1619 Thurn's army reached the walls of Vienna, while the Hungarian prince Gabriel Bethlen of Transylvania joined the rebels later that year. Ferdinand II, besieged in the Hofburg, survived when the assault failed and imperial reinforcements arrived. He then traveled to Frankfurt, where the electors chose him emperor on 28 August 1619, even though the Bohemian vote was disputed.

    On 19 August 1619 the Bohemian estates declared Ferdinand deposed and, on 26 August, elected Frederick V, the young Calvinist Elector Palatine, who was married to Elizabeth, daughter of James I of England. After hesitating, Frederick accepted and was crowned at Prague in November. His supporters hoped for English, Dutch, and Union help. Instead James I refused to support the venture, the Union limited itself to protecting the Palatinate, and Lutheran Saxony supported the emperor.

    Ferdinand meanwhile secured Maximilian of Bavaria and the Catholic League by the Treaty of Munich in October 1619, with the promise that Maximilian would be rewarded, and obtained help from Spain. The result was that a revolt within one kingdom had become an imperial constitutional question, because an elector had accepted another crown against the emperor, and an international one, because Spain, the Dutch Republic, England, and Transylvania all had interests. Dynastic law, imperial law, and confessional loyalty pointed in different directions, which is why the Bohemian crisis cannot be called simply a religious war.

    Section 67.6

    The Battle of White Mountain and the Defeat of the Bohemian Revolt, 1620

    The decisive engagement of the Bohemian war took place on 8 November 1620 at the White Mountain, a low ridge west of Prague. The imperial and League army, composed of forces under Count Bucquoy and Johann Tserclaes, Count of Tilly, and accompanied by Maximilian of Bavaria, had advanced into Bohemia after earlier operations in Upper Austria. Its opponents were the Bohemian estates' troops under Christian of Anhalt, supported by some Hungarian and other contingents. Estimates of the numbers on each side differ. One reconstruction places about twenty-one thousand troops with the Bohemian estates against approximately twenty-six to twenty-eight thousand imperial and League soldiers; other accounts give somewhat different totals.

    The battle itself was short, lasting about an hour or two, and the Bohemian line collapsed. Historians point less to a single tactical error than to deeper weaknesses: unpaid soldiers, divided command, little help from the Protestant Union, which had agreed to neutrality by the Treaty of Ulm in July 1620, and the Saxon invasion of Lusatia on the emperor's behalf. Spanish troops under Ambrogio Spinola had also entered the Palatinate in 1620, forcing Frederick's allies to look to their own defense.

    Frederick left Prague within days and went into exile, and his short reign gave him the mocking name of the Winter King. The defeat ended the rebellion in Bohemia quickly, but it did not end the war: Frederick's lands, his allies, and the question of the Palatinate remained to be settled by force or negotiation.

    Section 67.7

    Habsburg Consolidation and the Transformation of Bohemia

    Habsburg victory was followed by a political and social transformation. On 21 June 1621 twenty-seven leaders of the revolt were executed in Prague's Old Town Square. Hundreds of nobles and burghers lost property through confiscation, and thousands of Protestants, including the educator Jan Amos Comenius, went into exile. Estimates of how much land changed hands are uncertain, but contemporaries and historians agree that a very large share of noble estates passed to new owners, many of them loyal Catholic nobles, foreign officers, and financiers such as Wallenstein and the Liechtenstein family.

    The religious and constitutional changes were equally important. Protestant clergy were expelled in 1621 and 1624, the Jesuits returned, and the Renewed Land Ordinance of 1627 declared the Bohemian crown hereditary in the Habsburg house, limited the powers of the estates, and made Catholicism the only permitted religion. Nobles were given a short period to convert or emigrate. The Letter of Majesty was annulled and, according to later accounts, its seal was cut.

    These measures illustrate why historians describe the outcome as consolidation rather than only punishment. The Bohemian estates lost their ability to elect kings and to bargain with the crown, a change that shaped Habsburg government for centuries. Some of the cost fell on others: wartime currency debasement, the so-called Kipper und Wipper period of about 1619 to 1623, helped finance armies but ruined many savers and townspeople, and a consortium that leased the Prague mint profited from it.

    Section 67.8

    The Palatinate War and the Expansion of Imperial Conflict, 1621–1623

    Frederick's flight made the Palatinate the next theater. Spanish troops from the Low Countries, who had occupied the Lower Palatinate in 1620, were joined by Tilly's League army. In January 1621 the emperor placed Frederick under the imperial ban, an act many Protestants considered unconstitutional because it was issued without the consent of the electors. Mercenary commanders such as Ernst von Mansfeld, Christian of Brunswick, and the Margrave of Baden-Durlach fought on Frederick's behalf, but Tilly defeated them at Wimpfen and Höchst in 1622 and Christian of Brunswick at Stadtlohn in 1623.

    Heidelberg fell in September 1622, and its famous library, the Bibliotheca Palatina, was sent to the pope. In February 1623 Ferdinand transferred the Palatine electoral dignity to Maximilian of Bavaria for his lifetime, a reward for League support that also shifted the balance among the electors toward Catholic rulers. Bavaria occupied the Upper Palatinate as security for its war costs.

    The consequences were international. James I of England, Frederick's father in law, sought a settlement through negotiation with Spain, including a proposed marriage alliance, but gained little. The Dutch Republic, whose truce with Spain expired in 1621, and later France, regarded Spanish troops on the Rhine as a strategic threat. The Palatinate thus linked a German constitutional dispute to the route known as the Spanish Road, along which Spain supplied its Netherlands armies.

    Section 67.9

    Christian IV of Denmark and the Lower Saxon War, 1625–1629

    Danish intervention began in 1625 under Christian IV, who was both king of Denmark and, as duke of Holstein, a prince of the Empire. His motives mixed religion with dynastic and commercial interests: he led the Lower Saxon Circle, hoped to secure the bishoprics of Bremen and Verden for his sons, and wished to protect Danish control of the Sound against Catholic expansion to the Baltic. England and the Dutch Republic promised subsidies in the Treaty of The Hague of December 1625, though their payments were irregular.

    The imperial answer came from two armies, Tilly's League troops and the new army of Wallenstein. In April 1626 Wallenstein defeated Mansfeld at the Dessau Bridge, and in August Tilly defeated Christian at Lutter am Barenberge. Mansfeld died later that year. Imperial forces occupied Holstein and Jutland in 1627, and Wallenstein, who had been made Duke of Mecklenburg in 1628, failed to take the port of Stralsund, a sign of the limits of his power without naval support.

    The Peace of Lübeck, signed in May 1629, restored Christian's lands in exchange for his withdrawal from German affairs. The war's character was visible in northern Germany: armies lived on contributions from the territories where they were stationed, and princes who had remained neutral found their lands occupied. The Danish defeat seemed to complete the Habsburg victory, but it made the emperor stronger than many German princes, Catholic and Protestant, were willing to accept.

    Section 67.10

    Wallenstein, Imperial Military Power, and the Edict of Restitution

    Albrecht von Wallenstein, a Bohemian noble who converted to Catholicism and grew rich on confiscated estates, offered the emperor something he had lacked: an army that did not depend on the League or on the Diet. Created Duke of Friedland and, later, of Mecklenburg, he raised forces whose paper strength reached the tens of thousands, and he financed them largely by contributions from occupied lands, by credit, and by the output of his own estates. This system, known to historians as military entrepreneurship, allowed rapid expansion, but it also meant that the army's survival depended on plunder and on the commander's personal credit.

    The emperor's apparent success encouraged more ambitious claims. On 6 March 1629 Ferdinand issued the Edict of Restitution, ordering the return of church lands secularized since 1552, including two archbishoprics, many bishoprics, and numerous monasteries, and interpreting the Peace of Augsburg to exclude Calvinists. The edict was a legal interpretation, not a new law enacted by the Diet, and its enforcement by imperial commissioners alarmed Lutheran princes, including Saxony, which had remained loyal to the emperor.

    Opposition came from both confessions. Catholic electors, led by Maximilian of Bavaria, resented Wallenstein's power and the burden of his army, and the French diplomatic mission, working through Father Joseph, encouraged them. At the Electoral Diet of Regensburg in 1630 they demanded Wallenstein's dismissal in exchange for a possible election of Ferdinand's son as king of the Romans, which failed anyway. Ferdinand dismissed his general in August 1630, only weeks after Sweden's landing. This sequence explains why military victory did not produce stability: it increased fears of imperial authority among the very estates whose consent the emperor needed.

    Wallenstein's system rested on a close partnership between the army and private finance. He and his associates, including the banker Hans de Witte, advanced money to the emperor and recovered it through contributions levied on occupied lands, while his own duchy of Friedland, built from confiscated Bohemian estates, supplied provisions and equipment. The arrangement gave Ferdinand an army independent of the Diet and the League, but it also meant that the army's costs fell on territories regardless of their confession, which is why Catholic electors complained as loudly as Protestants.

    The Edict of Restitution raised a constitutional as well as a religious question. By issuing it as the supreme judge interpreting the Peace of Augsburg, Ferdinand bypassed the Diet and the parity of Catholic and Protestant judges that many Protestants considered essential. Imperial commissioners began enforcing it in 1629, and Protestant worship was restricted in some cities, including Augsburg. Lutheran Saxony, which had supported the emperor since 1620, now saw its own security threatened, and this is why the edict is often treated as the point at which imperial success began to generate opposition among former allies.

    Section 67.11

    Gustavus Adolphus and Sweden's Intervention, 1630

    Sweden's entry into the war in 1630 grew out of its own regional ambitions as much as from Protestant solidarity. King Gustavus Adolphus had fought Poland since 1621 and secured Livonian gains by the truce of Altmark in 1629, which freed his forces. He viewed the Habsburg and imperial presence on the Baltic coast, including Wallenstein's title in Mecklenburg and his plans for a fleet, as a threat to Swedish security. His public statements stressed the defense of German Protestants, and many Lutherans accepted that justification, though Saxony and Brandenburg hesitated.

    Sweden was a small kingdom with limited manpower and a revenue base that depended on copper exports and customs on the Baltic trade. Its army combined Swedish and Finnish conscripts with large numbers of German, Scottish, and other mercenaries, so that its strength depended on foreign contracts and finance. France, under Cardinal Richelieu, agreed in the Treaty of Bärwalde of January 1631 to pay a yearly subsidy, a Catholic power thereby supporting a Lutheran king against the Catholic Habsburgs.

    Gustavus landed on the island of Usedom in July 1630 and compelled the duke of Pomerania to ally with him. He then advanced slowly into Brandenburg, where he needed the consent of Elector George William. The Protestant estates, meeting at Leipzig in early 1631, sought a neutral third force rather than a Swedish alliance. The delay in German support shows that Swedish intervention was not a simple religious crusade, and that its success depended on events at Magdeburg.

    Section 67.12

    The Sack of Magdeburg and the Battle of Breitenfeld, 1631

    Magdeburg, a Protestant city on the Elbe, had declared for Sweden and been placed under siege by Count Pappenheim and then by Tilly. Swedish help did not arrive in time. On 20 May 1631 imperial and League troops stormed the city, and a fire destroyed most of it. The number of dead is disputed, with older accounts naming as many as twenty thousand of some twenty-five thousand inhabitants, while later work suggests lower figures. Historians also disagree over whether the fire started through the defenders' actions, through the attackers, or accidentally in the confusion.

    The political importance of Magdeburg was greater than any single toll. Printed pamphlets, woodcuts, and sermons spread the story across Europe, and the word Magdeburgisierung came to describe the sack of a city. This literature is valuable evidence of how people understood the war but should be read as propaganda, because writers aimed to rouse fear and sympathy. The disaster increased pressure on Lutheran rulers to reconsider neutrality. Brandenburg and Saxony subsequently aligned with Sweden, but direct military pressure and their own political calculations also shaped those decisions.

    On 17 September 1631 Gustavus and the Saxons met Tilly's army at Breitenfeld, north of Leipzig. The Saxon troops fled early, but the Swedish army, organized in smaller, more flexible brigades with mobile field artillery and drilled musketeers, held its ground and counterattacked. Tilly's army was largely destroyed. Breitenfeld did not by itself win the war, but it demonstrated that the imperial forces could be beaten in open battle and changed the strategic balance.

    The military background clarifies why Magdeburg fell. The city had declared for Sweden, and Gustavus Adolphus sent an officer, Dietrich von Falkenberg, to organize its defense, but the king could not relieve it because the electors of Brandenburg and Saxony had refused him passage and support. The Swedish delay therefore reflected the hesitation of German Protestant rulers, not only the king's own choices. Falkenberg was killed during the assault, and the city, with its people and fortifications, became a symbol of both Catholic victory and Swedish failure.

    The Breitenfeld campaign followed from decisions made by the Saxon elector. After Tilly occupied Leipzig and demanded Saxon compliance with the Edict, John George allied with Sweden, in the process adding Saxon commanders and troops to the king's force. Victory at Breitenfeld gave the allies the initiative: Saxon troops entered Bohemia and occupied Prague in November 1631, while Gustavus moved west. These actions show that Swedish success depended on German alliances, and that the same alliances later proved fragile.

    Section 67.13

    Swedish Victories, the Battle of Lützen, and the Death of Gustavus Adolphus

    After Breitenfeld Gustavus did not pursue the emperor into Bohemia but marched west and south into Franconia and the Rhineland, taking Frankfurt and Mainz in late 1631. In April 1632 he crossed the River Lech against Tilly, who was mortally wounded, and Munich opened its gates in May. Many towns accepted Swedish garrisons, and Gustavus began to treat parts of Germany as an organized territory with the help of Chancellor Axel Oxenstierna.

    The emperor turned again to Wallenstein, who was recalled with unusual authority over the army. The two commanders faced each other near Nuremberg in the summer of 1632, where Wallenstein's fortified camp at the Alte Veste produced a costly stalemate. At Lützen, near Leipzig, on 16 November 1632, the Swedes won a hard battle, but Gustavus Adolphus was killed. Pappenheim also died, and the army held the field without gaining a decisive advantage.

    The Swedish success has often been attributed to a military revolution invented by the king. Historians now emphasize a wider set of explanations: drill and discipline, firepower, regimental organization, French money, a cohesive command, and the disorganization of his opponents. The death of Gustavus raised a political problem, because his heir, Queen Christina, was a child. Oxenstierna organized the Heilbronn League with the southwestern German estates in 1633, but the alliance proved fragile, in part because its members distrusted Swedish aims.

    Section 67.14

    Wallenstein's Fall, Nördlingen, and the Peace of Prague, 1634–1635

    Wallenstein's last year was shaped by suspicion. In 1633 he opened talks with Saxony, Sweden, and France, apparently seeking a general peace that the emperor did not control. His officers' declaration at Pilsen in January 1634 increased imperial fears of mutiny, and Ferdinand removed him. On 25 February 1634 a group of officers, several of them Irish and Scottish, killed him at Eger. Historians still debate whether he planned treason or was trying to force a negotiated peace, because the evidence comes largely from his enemies and from letters whose purpose is uncertain.

    At Nördlingen on 5 and 6 September 1634 an imperial army under Ferdinand of Hungary, the emperor's son, joined a Spanish force led by the Cardinal-Infante Ferdinand, which had marched from Italy. Together they crushed the Swedish and Saxe-Weimar army under Bernhard and Gustav Horn, who was captured. Swedish power in southern Germany largely collapsed, and many of its German allies sought terms. The battle also showed how closely the German war depended on the Spanish Habsburgs.

    The Peace of Prague of May 1635 between the emperor and Saxony, joined by many other estates, tried to restore the Empire's unity. It suspended the Edict of Restitution for forty years and used 1627 as a reference date for church property, but still excluded Calvinists and left out Sweden and France. It also united many estates under imperial military command. For those who opposed it, including Hesse-Kassel and the exiled Palatine family, it did not end the war. France decided to intervene directly, as the next division explains.

    Division IV: France, Sweden, and a European War, 1635 to 1648
    Section 67.15

    France Enters the War: Richelieu and the Habsburg Rivalry

    France's role is central to understanding why the war cannot be reduced to Catholics against Protestants. Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister of Louis XIII from 1624, had already suppressed Huguenot political power at home, culminating in the fall of La Rochelle in 1628, yet he supported Protestant powers abroad against the Habsburgs. His priorities were the security of French borders, which Habsburg lands in Spain, the Netherlands, Franche-Comté, and northern Italy seemed to encircle, and the preservation of French influence in the Rhineland.

    Until 1635 Richelieu preferred subsidies and diplomacy to open war, paying Sweden through the Treaty of Bärwalde and supporting the Dutch. The defeat at Nördlingen changed the calculation: with Swedish power broken in southern Germany, France feared that a Habsburg victory would be complete. When Spanish troops captured the Elector of Trier, a French protégé, in March 1635, France made treaties with the Dutch and Swedes and, in May, declared war on Spain. French and imperial forces then fought each other without a formal declaration of war between the two rulers, a distinction that allowed both sides to preserve diplomatic and constitutional claims.

    This is why historians describe the next phase as the Europeanization of the war. Religion remained significant, since the Swedish alliance still depended on the defense of Protestants and Catholic rulers still spoke of defending the church. But Catholic France at war with Catholic Spain showed that dynastic, territorial, and strategic interests could outweigh confessional solidarity, and the Bohemian crisis had become a general contest over the balance of power.

    Section 67.16

    The War Across Europe: The Rhine, Low Countries, Italy, and Spain

    After 1635 the fighting spread across several connected theaters. In the Rhineland, French armies and Bernhard of Saxe-Weimar, whom France hired after Nördlingen, fought Habsburg forces for control of Alsace and the Upper Rhine, and Bernhard took the fortress of Breisach in 1638. In the Spanish Netherlands, French and Dutch armies pressed Spanish defenders, while the Spanish invasion of Picardy in 1636 took the fortress of Corbie and came near Paris, a year known in France as the year of Corbie. In northern Italy, French and Spanish forces fought over Piedmont and the Milanese.

    Spain faced growing strain. In October 1639 a Dutch fleet under Maarten Tromp destroyed a large Spanish fleet in the Downs, off the English coast. In 1640 Catalonia rose against the Spanish crown, and Portugal proclaimed John IV king, ending its union with Spain. At Rocroi in May 1643 the young Duke of Enghien defeated a Spanish army, an event that damaged Spanish military prestige though not Spanish capacity to fight on. The count-duke of Olivares, Philip IV's chief minister, fell from power in 1643.

    These theaters mattered to the imperial war because Spanish money, troops, and the Cardinal-Infante's army were drawn westward and southward, while France could pay for operations in Germany. The wars in the Empire, the Low Countries, Italy, and Iberia were distinct conflicts with their own origins, but their financial and strategic needs were closely linked, and this explains why peace in the Empire did not immediately end the larger struggle.

    Section 67.17

    The Later Swedish Campaigns and the Exhaustion of Imperial Resistance

    Swedish forces continued fighting after Gustavus's death under commanders who were often German or Swedish aristocrats and professional soldiers. Johan Banér won a victory at Wittstock in October 1636 over imperial and Saxon troops, which helped Sweden recover after the Peace of Prague. In 1642 Lennart Torstensson defeated an imperial army at the second battle of Breitenfeld, and in March 1645 at Jankau in Bohemia he destroyed another, which opened the way toward Vienna, though the Swedes lacked the strength to take it.

    Other campaigns changed the diplomatic balance. Sweden fought and defeated Denmark in the Torstensson War of 1643 to 1645, gaining territory in the Peace of Brömsebro. French and Bavarian armies met at Allerheim near Nördlingen in August 1645 in a costly French success, and in 1646 and 1647 Swedish and French armies jointly pressed Bavaria, which signed a truce before Maximilian broke it again. In the last months of the war a Swedish force took part of Prague in July 1648, the Lesser Town, with Bohemian plunder, just as the diplomats were finishing their work.

    These campaigns show how war and negotiation overlapped. Emperor Ferdinand III, who succeeded his father in 1637, was gradually forced to give up the idea of a victorious peace. Imperial resistance weakened not because of a single defeat but because of repeated losses, the departure of allies such as Brandenburg and Saxony, and the exhaustion of Bavaria. Each army also fought to improve its negotiators' position, a point that helps explain why bloody campaigns continued while the peace congress sat.

    Section 67.18

    Armies, Commanders, Recruitment, and Military Organization

    Armies in the war were generally raised by military entrepreneurs, colonels or generals who contracted with a ruler to recruit and maintain regiments and recovered their costs through pay, credit, and the profits of occupation. Soldiers came from many regions: Germans, Walloons, Scots, Irish, Croats, Swedes, Finns, Italians, and others, and the same man might serve several masters. Field armies usually numbered between ten thousand and forty thousand men, while garrisons held towns and fortresses, so that the total number of men under arms across the war was much larger.

    Infantry formations combined pikemen and musketeers, with muskets steadily increasing in importance, and cavalry included heavy cuirassiers, dragoons, and light horse such as the Croats. Artillery ranged from siege guns to light field pieces, and the spread of Italian style bastioned fortifications made sieges long and expensive. Commanders needed to protect their supply lines, to occupy winter quarters, and to avoid battles unless success was likely, so that marches and sieges occupied more time than pitched battles.

    Supply largely depended on the people the armies occupied. Magazines existed but were limited, and most armies collected contributions of money, grain, fodder, and horses from towns and villages, which meant that campaigns followed the places where food could be found. Camp followers, whose ratio to soldiers varied widely across armies and years, included wives, children, servants, merchants, and sutlers, and performed necessary work in supply, nursing, and cooking. Disease and desertion usually killed or removed more soldiers than combat. Articles of war set discipline on paper, but their enforcement varied with the commander and the pay available.

    Officers and soldiers moved between armies with ease. A colonel's authority came from his contract, his wealth, and his networks of captains and recruiters, and he was responsible for paying, clothing, and sometimes arming his regiment, so that regimental loyalty often rivaled loyalty to the ruler. Armies in German service included Scottish and Irish officers, such as those involved in the murder of Wallenstein, and the Swedish army in Germany came to consist largely of men who were not Swedish subjects, a reality that shaped Sweden's dependence on German allies and finance.

    Tactical change was gradual and widespread. Dutch and Swedish reforms emphasized drill, smaller and more flexible formations, and mobile firepower, while Spanish and imperial armies continued to rely on deeper formations that remained effective in many battles. The expansion of artillery fortifications, as at Stralsund and Breisach, made sieges decisive and slow. For most commanders, supply, winter quarters, and the control of fortified towns mattered more than the pursuit of battle, which explains the long, indecisive character of many campaigns.

    Section 67.19

    War Finance, Coalition Politics, and the Failure of Earlier Peace Efforts

    The war was expensive, and no state in the conflict had a simple means of paying for it. Imperial Circle taxes, authorized by the Diet as monthly payments, produced only part of the sums needed. Spain relied on American silver, loans from Genoese and Portuguese bankers, and Castilian taxes, and declared bankruptcy several times. France raised the direct tax called the taille and sold offices, which led to rural revolts. Sweden used copper, tolls from German ports, French subsidies, and contributions from the territories its armies held.

    Credit and contributions were therefore closely connected. Bankers such as Hans de Witte, who had financed Wallenstein, advanced money against expected revenues, and commanders borrowed against future plunder. Contributions were a form of taxation that armies collected themselves, so that the burden fell on regions near the armies. This system explains why peace was difficult: soldiers expected arrears of pay, and a government that disbanded its army without payment risked mutiny.

    Earlier peace efforts also failed. The Peace of Prague excluded France and Sweden, and plans for a general congress at Cologne in the mid-1630s collapsed. After the Diet of Regensburg of 1640 and 1641, preliminary agreements at Hamburg in December 1641 set the terms for the congress at Münster and Osnabrück. By then every major participant hoped to negotiate from strength, but none could impose its terms, and they were still bargaining over the next campaign as well as the next clause.

    Division V: Devastation and Civilian Experience
    Section 67.20

    Sieges, Sack, Plunder, and Violence Against Civilians

    Violence against civilians was a central part of the war, but it followed different patterns in different places. Under the conventions of the period, a garrison that refused to surrender and was taken by assault could be sacked, and soldiers often claimed the right to plunder. Magdeburg in 1631 is the best known example, but other cities suffered similarly, including Neubrandenburg, taken by Tilly in 1631, and Frankfurt an der Oder, stormed by the Swedes in the same year. Starvation was also used as a weapon, as at Breisach in 1638.

    Outside sieges, civilians were threatened by quartering, theft, rape, torture to reveal hidden property, and forced labor. Sources describe soldiers pouring foul liquid into victims to force them to disclose hiding places, a practice known as the Swedish drink, though the name is misleading because several armies are said to have used it. Villagers sometimes defended themselves by hiding in forests or fortified churches, or by forming armed bands that attacked stragglers, and these peasant defenders were treated harshly in turn.

    Historians must read the evidence carefully. Town chronicles, petitions to rulers, court records, and the chronicle of the Swabian shoemaker Hans Heberle record specific events, but surviving writers were often literate and sometimes wrote for sympathy or compensation. The etchings of Jacques Callot, published in 1633, and printed pamphlets spread images that shaped later memory. These sources show that violence was real and extensive, but they do not justify treating every campaign, or every region, as though it had experienced Magdeburg.

    Section 67.21

    War Economies, Quartering, and the Burden on Communities

    Quartering and contributions were the means by which armies lived, and communities developed ways to negotiate them. Towns and villages had to provide lodging, food, fodder, wagons, and money, according to rates set by local commanders or the Imperial Circles. Officials bargained over amounts, paid for letters of protection called salvaguardia, appealed to higher authorities, or sent delegates to the commander. These negotiations show that war was not simply chaos but also a system with rules, though soldiers frequently exceeded them.

    The economic consequences were severe. Farmers who fled left fields untilled and villages abandoned, and animals, seed grain, and tools were taken. Towns accumulated heavy debts to pay contributions, and trade was disrupted by troop movements and tolls. Currency debasement and rising prices added to the strain. Wage and price records, tax lists, and municipal accounts allow historians to measure some of these changes, though they are incomplete.

    The effects were uneven. Hamburg and Bremen, with neutral status and maritime trade, flourished as refugees and capital arrived, while the Leipzig fair continued despite repeated occupation. Regions on the main military corridors, such as the Rhineland, Franconia, Thuringia, Hesse, Pomerania, and Bohemia, suffered most, and some districts further away were scarcely touched. The war's economic damage depended on location, timing, and the behavior of particular commanders.

    Section 67.22

    Famine, Disease, Migration, and Population Loss

    The greatest killer was disease, not battle. Typhus, dysentery, and plague moved with armies and refugees, and crowded towns and camps provided ideal conditions. Plague outbreaks in the late 1620s and in 1633 to 1635 coincided with troop movements, and in northern Italy the plague of 1629 to 1631 was spread partly by campaigning armies. Harvest failures and the loss of seed and draft animals produced famine in several regions, and the cooler conditions of the Little Ice Age, introduced in Chapter 2, increased the risk of poor crops.

    Population loss is difficult to measure. Historians use parish registers of baptisms and burials, hearth and tax lists, and local chronicles, all of which have gaps, and populations were also affected by migration, so that a missing person was not necessarily a dead person. For the Empire as a whole, modern estimates of loss are uncertain and are often placed between one fifth and one third of the population. Regional studies show much greater variation: parts of Württemberg, Pomerania, Mecklenburg, Thuringia, Hesse, and the Palatinate may have lost a third or more, and a few districts probably lost over half, while some areas in the northwest and a number of cities grew.

    Refugees moved to fortified towns, to the Dutch Republic, to the Baltic coast, and to neutral cities, and Protestant exiles from Bohemia and Austria settled in Saxony, Brandenburg, and elsewhere. Recovery was also uneven. Some regions regained their population within a generation, helped by births, immigration, and the return of peace, while others took much longer. The figures should therefore be presented as estimates and ranges, and not as a single number for the whole Empire.

    Historians face several problems in measuring loss. Parish registers record baptisms and burials but survive unevenly, hearth and tax lists count households that may have fled, and chronicles exaggerate for effect. A village that lost half its households may have lost many through flight, and some returned later, so mortality, migration, and falling birth rates must be distinguished. Detailed local records, such as the burial notes kept by Hans Heberle at Neenstetten, show the severity of particular years but cannot be generalized for the whole Empire.

    Historians also disagree about the war's longer economic effect. Some argue that devastation held back German economic development for generations, while others stress that trade shifts toward the Atlantic and pre-existing regional difficulties were already under way, and that some regions recovered quickly. Rulers in Brandenburg, Mecklenburg, and elsewhere encouraged resettlement after 1648, and recovery depended on immigration, local administration, and credit. The war's demographic effect was therefore severe but varied, and its long-term economic consequences remain debated.

    Section 67.23

    Women, Families, Religion, and Memories of the War

    Women appear in the sources both as victims and as participants. They managed households and farms when men fled, died, or were conscripted, and they worked as nurses, laundresses, sutlers, cooks, and traders in the baggage trains that accompanied armies. The diary of the mercenary Peter Hagendorf records his wives and children traveling with his regiment and records the deaths of many of them. Widows and orphans fell into poverty, and town councils and churches struggled to support them.

    Religion remained part of daily life. Pastors recorded burials and sermons that interpreted the war as divine punishment, and congregations adapted worship to dangers. Some women and men were prosecuted for witchcraft in this period, with particularly severe persecutions in places such as Würzburg and Bamberg in the 1620s and early 1630s, though historians debate how closely witch trials were linked to the war and stress local legal and religious causes. Soldiers' diaries and chronicles also show religion as a source of comfort, as a marker of enemies, and as an excuse for violence.

    The memory of the war changed over time. Grimmelshausen's novel Simplicissimus, published in 1668, gave a satirical picture of soldiers and peasants. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries Friedrich Schiller's history and plays, and later nationalist writers, presented the war as the great catastrophe that had shattered Germany, and Bertolt Brecht's Mother Courage and Her Children, written in 1939, reinterpreted it as a lesson about war and commerce. Modern historians use these works as evidence of later memory, not as direct testimony, and have revised the older picture of uniform national ruin.

    Division VI: The Peace of Westphalia, 1648
    Section 67.24

    Negotiating Peace at Münster and Osnabrück, 1643–1648

    The peace congress grew out of the Hamburg preliminaries of December 1641 and opened in 1644 at two Westphalian towns, Münster and Osnabrück, about fifty kilometers apart. Sweden and its Protestant partners negotiated chiefly at Osnabrück, and France and most Catholic parties at Münster, an arrangement of diplomatic convenience and not a strict division by religion. The imperial delegation was led for much of the time by Maximilian von Trauttmansdorff, France was represented by d'Avaux and Servien under the direction of Cardinal Mazarin, Sweden by Johan Oxenstierna and Johan Adler Salvius, Spain by Peñaranda, and the Dutch Republic by delegates including Adriaen Pauw. The papal nuncio Fabio Chigi and the Venetian ambassador Alvise Contarini served as mediators.

    A distinctive feature was the admission of the imperial estates as full participants rather than letting the emperor negotiate for them, a decision made in 1645 and 1646 after protracted dispute. Well over a hundred delegations attended, and disputes over precedence and ceremony took months. The talks involved many issues: Swedish demands for territory and payment to its army, French claims in Alsace, the rights of the estates, the Palatine inheritance, the position of Reformed Protestants, and compensation for princes who lost lands.

    Negotiation continued during military operations, since each side believed that campaigns could improve its bargaining position. Spain and the Dutch concluded their own treaty first, and France and Spain failed to agree. The settlement for the Empire was concluded in two linked instruments, the Instrument of Peace of Osnabrück, mainly with Sweden, and the Instrument of Peace of Münster, mainly with France, signed on 24 October 1648 in the Gregorian calendar. Implementation required further talks at Nuremberg in 1649 and 1650, mainly concerning the withdrawal and payment of troops.

    The congress had a complicated procedure. Delegations exchanged written proposals through mediators, especially the papal nuncio Fabio Chigi and the Venetian ambassador Alvise Contarini, since many delegations would not meet one another directly. The estates deliberated in the three colleges of the Diet, and the emperor's negotiators had to win their approval. Key concessions came in stages, including imperial offers to France concerning Alsace in 1646, and the pressure of military campaigns in 1646 to 1648, particularly the Swedish and French advances into Bavaria and Bohemia.

    France and Spain failed to reach a peace. Under the 1648 Treaty of Münster, the emperor and imperial estates undertook not to assist France's enemies, including Spain, in the ongoing Franco-Spanish war; the treaty also barred imperial intervention in that war, though France and Spain continued fighting until 1659. Following the signing of the imperial peace instruments on 24 October 1648, the ratifications were exchanged on 8 February 1649. Negotiations at Nuremberg in 1649–1650 then arranged payments, troop withdrawals, and demobilization, illustrating that implementing peace required more than signing the treaties. The settlement was therefore a long process of implementation as well as a single date.

    Section 67.25

    The Religious Settlement: Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed Rights

    The religious provisions of the treaties confirmed the Peace of Augsburg and extended its protections to Reformed Protestants, so that the Empire now recognized three confessions: Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed. The rule that defined most disputes was the normative year. Church property and the public exercise of religion were fixed according to their condition on 1 January 1624, with the Palatinate treated by a different date of 1618, and later seizures were to be reversed. This was a compromise between the Catholic reference dates of the Edict of Restitution and Protestant claims to earlier dates.

    Rulers retained the right to determine the religion of their territories, but a ruler's conversion no longer obliged his subjects to change confession. Subjects whose confession differed from their prince's could have private worship and the right to emigrate with their property after a period of notice. Imperial courts and the Diet adopted parity rules, and in religious matters the Diet divided into Catholic and Protestant bodies, which decided by agreement and not by majority vote. These procedures protected minorities within the constitution and gave religion a legal channel.

    The settlement was not religious freedom. Most Habsburg hereditary lands remained exempt from the Empire-wide confessional protections, permitting the rulers to maintain Catholic predominance in Bohemia and Austria. Limited exceptions included protections for certain Protestant nobles in Lower Austria and Silesia, and permission for three Lutheran Churches of Peace in the Silesian hereditary duchies. Anabaptists and other dissenters had no recognized status, and Jews, though present in imperial cities and territories, were not part of the confessional settlement. Pope Innocent X repudiated treaty provisions that he considered harmful to Catholic and papal rights in the breve Zelo domus Dei, issued around 1650, backdated to November 1648, and published in 1651. The protest did not overturn the settlement.

    Application of the religious clauses required local arrangements. In a small number of imperial cities, including Augsburg, Biberach, Ravensburg, and Dinkelsbühl, Catholic and Protestant residents shared offices under rules of parity, one of the clearest examples of confessional coexistence being built into municipal government. Commissions and courts decided disputed cases of church property by reference to the normative year, so the peace turned many religious claims into legal and archival questions.

    The settlement did not remove coercion. Rulers could still restrict public worship by dissenters, and disputes continued over private worship, schools, and church buildings. The Habsburg hereditary lands, subject to limited exceptions, did not receive the same protections under the settlement, and Protestants there could be pressured to convert or emigrate. Confessional coercion also continued elsewhere in the Empire: in 1731–1732 the prince-archbishop of Salzburg, whose territory was not a Habsburg hereditary land, expelled thousands of Protestants. This later example illustrates the limits of the settlement's protections and the uneven character of coexistence.

    Section 67.26

    The Imperial Constitution After Westphalia

    The treaties confirmed the constitutional position of the imperial estates and did not abolish the Empire. They affirmed the estates' territorial rights, including the powers of government over their lands and subjects, and gave them the right to make alliances with each other and with foreign powers, provided these were not directed against the emperor or the Empire. Many historians stress that this clause confirmed existing practice and did not create sovereignty, since the estates remained members of the Empire and subject to its law.

    The emperor retained significant prerogatives, including the right to confer titles and feudal investitures, to preside over imperial institutions, and to act through the Reichshofrat. Important matters such as new laws, taxes, war, peace, and alliances required the consent of the Diet. The court system and Imperial Circles continued to function, and over time the Diet became a permanent assembly at Regensburg from 1663.

    The result was a more explicitly negotiated order rather than a collapse of imperial government. Habsburg power in the Empire was limited, but it was strengthened elsewhere, especially in its hereditary lands, which are treated in Part XII. The Empire continued as a legal and political framework for many small and medium states until 1806, while France and Sweden acquired a continuing diplomatic role as external guarantors of the peace. That guarantee rested on the treaty obligations, not merely on the territories they had acquired within the Empire.

    The treaties also affected the working of imperial institutions. Protestants gained representation among judges in the Reichskammergericht, in line with the principle of parity, and in the Diet matters of religion were handled by separate Catholic and Protestant bodies, known as the Corpus Catholicorum and Corpus Evangelicorum, which negotiated to reach agreement. Procedures of this kind slowed decisions but protected minorities, and they illustrate the aim of containing confessional conflict through law.

    The result was not weak government alone. The emperor kept an important role in justice, feudal law, and imperial politics, while territorial rulers governed their lands. Many historians see the post-1648 Empire as a flexible legal and political framework in which small and medium states could seek protection, and in which emperors, electors, and foreign powers all took part. This explains why the Empire could survive for another century and a half despite the growth of larger territorial states.

    Section 67.27

    Territorial and Dynastic Settlements

    The territorial provisions redistributed power within and around the Empire. Sweden received Western Pomerania, the city of Wismar, and the secularized territories of Bremen and Verden, which it held as imperial fiefs with seats in the Diet, while the imperial peace required designated Imperial Circles to contribute five million thalers toward the payment and demobilization of Swedish troops. France obtained confirmation of its rights over Metz, Toul, and Verdun, and the cession of Habsburg rights in Alsace, though the treaty language about those rights was ambiguous and later disputed.

    Brandenburg, a middle-ranking German state that was compensated for Swedish gains in Pomerania, received Eastern Pomerania, the secularized bishoprics of Halberstadt and Minden, and an entitlement to Magdeburg that became possession only in 1680. Bavaria kept the electoral dignity and the Upper Palatinate, and Charles Louis, son of Frederick V, regained the Lower Palatinate with a new eighth electorate. Saxony retained Lusatia, Hesse-Kassel received compensation, and the bishopric of Osnabrück was to alternate between a Catholic and a Lutheran bishop.

    These changes strengthened several territorial states, notably Brandenburg and Bavaria, and gave France and Sweden positions in the Empire, which allowed them to take part in its politics. The secularization of church territories shifted the balance toward Protestant rulers, and for many communities it meant a new overlord. Maps of 1648 must show the Empire as a patchwork of territories, enclaves, and overlapping rights, not as a set of modern states.

    Compensation was a major part of the settlement, since many rulers lost lands to the great powers. The Swedish indemnity of five million thalers was to be raised from designated imperial territories to pay its troops and secure demobilization. Hesse-Kassel received compensation, including the abbey of Hersfeld, and Mecklenburg, which lost Wismar to Sweden, received the secularized bishoprics of Schwerin and Ratzeburg. Brunswick-Lüneburg obtained the alternation at Osnabrück. These transfers were possible because church territories could be secularized to satisfy lay rulers.

    The long-term significance lay in the new balance. Sweden, as ruler of Western Pomerania and Bremen-Verden, held positions near the mouths of the Oder, Elbe, and Weser and took part in the Diet as an imperial estate, which gave it both influence and obligations in German affairs. Brandenburg's acquisitions laid the base for its later growth, a development treated in Part XII. France's rights in Alsace were stated in ambiguous terms, and later French claims in the region would rely on that ambiguity.

    Section 67.28

    Dutch Independence, Swiss Autonomy, and the Meaning of Westphalia

    The Peace of Münster between Spain and the Dutch Republic was signed on 30 January 1648 and confirmed with ceremony on 15 May, ending the long conflict described in Chapter 66. Spain recognized the Republic's independence, and the Dutch kept their conquests and the closure of the Scheldt. The Swiss Confederation, represented by Johann Rudolf Wettstein of Basel, obtained exemption from the jurisdiction of imperial courts, confirming a status that had developed over generations.

    The wider war did not end. France and Spain continued to fight until the Peace of the Pyrenees in 1659, and Spain's resources were already strained by the revolts in Catalonia and Portugal. Religious tensions also persisted in the Empire and elsewhere: conflicts over church property, exiled Protestants in the Habsburg lands, and the persecution of Protestants in France after 1685 show that 1648 did not end religious conflict. What it did was to give the Empire a legal system for containing confessional disputes.

    The idea that Westphalia created modern sovereign states is a later interpretation. The scholar Leo Gross popularized the concept in 1948, and historians and international relations scholars such as Andreas Osiander have since argued that the treaties did not establish a system of equal sovereign states, since the Empire continued, the estates remained its members, and many rulers owed obligations to others. Westphalia remains historically important as a settlement that combined security guarantees, religious provisions, and constitutional rules, and it completes Part X by showing that religious division could be managed without being removed. Chapter 68 turns to maritime expansion, and the development of states and the balance of power is examined in Part XII.

    The Dutch settlement ended a conflict that had lasted, with a truce, since the Dutch Revolt described in Chapter 66. Spain recognized the Republic's independence and sovereignty over the territories it held, and Dutch trade interests were protected by the closure of the Scheldt to Antwerp's commerce. Spain's willingness to make peace reflected its need to concentrate on France and on the revolts in Catalonia and Portugal. The Swiss exemption confirmed a separation from the Empire that had developed since the Swabian War of 1499.

    The meaning of 1648 has changed over time. Later writers treated Westphalia as the origin of an international system of sovereign states, a view that scholars including Leo Gross and Andreas Osiander have examined critically. The treaties contained provisions making the signatories responsible for upholding them, but they also preserved a complex imperial constitution in which rulers held rights without full independence. A careful judgment is that Westphalia was an important security and constitutional settlement, whose significance as the birth of modern sovereignty was largely constructed later.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Confession

    In this period a confession meant a religious body with a formal statement of doctrine and a church order, such as Catholic, Lutheran, or Reformed. It differs from modern 'denomination' because states enforced confessional identity and sought uniformity.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Imperial Estates

    Estates (Reichsstände) were members with a seat and vote in the Imperial Diet: princes, prelates, counts, and cities. They held territorial authority under the emperor but were not independent states.

    Historical Debate

    Religious War or Dynastic and Constitutional Conflict?

    The question: Was religion the main cause of the Thirty Years' War, or was religion one element in a broader contest over political authority?

    Interpretation A, religion: Contemporaries described the war in confessional terms, rulers invoked religion, and disputes over church property, Calvinist status, and the Edict of Restitution produced the alliances, from the Union and League to the Swedish intervention.
    Interpretation B, dynasty and state: The conflict was also driven by Habsburg and French rivalry, territorial ambitions, constitutional conflict between emperor and estates, and the financial and military needs of states. Catholic France allied with Protestants against Catholic Habsburgs, and Lutheran Saxony fought alongside the emperor.

    Evidence: Treaties, diplomatic correspondence, sermons, pamphlets, and manifestos, which show that rulers combined religious and political language. Many historians reject a simple choice between the interpretations and argue that religion shaped how political interests were defined. Why it matters: The question affects how the war is classified and whether 1648 is read as a religious or a political settlement. Student task: Identify two events that support each interpretation and explain which events are hardest for each side to explain.

    Multiple Perspectives

    One War, Different Experiences

    Princes and officialsDefended territorial rights, bargained over alliances, and tried to protect their lands from occupation, though few could control armies.
    Villagers and townspeopleFaced quartering, contributions, flight, and hunger, and sought protection by payment or petition.
    Soldiers and camp familiesDepended on pay, plunder, and commanders, and faced disease; women and children often traveled with armies.
    Clergy and religious minoritiesInterpreted events as divine judgment, ministered to communities, and faced exile or conversion when rulers changed confession; Protestant Bohemians lost their rights after 1620.

    These groups did not have equal power: rulers and commanders made decisions, while civilians bore many costs.

    Continuity and Change Over Time

    What changed? Reformed rights were recognized, Habsburg power in the Empire was limited, and France and Sweden gained. What remained continuous? The Empire, the emperor, and confessional divisions. Who experienced change most directly? Civilians in devastated regions and Bohemian nobles. Who experienced continuity? Many rural communities that recovered along older patterns. Why it matters: 1648 settled a war without ending religious conflict.

    Cause and Effect: From Bohemian Revolt to European War

    Long-term causes

    Ambiguities of Augsburg, rising Calvinism and Catholic reform, the weakness of imperial institutions, Habsburg dynastic ambition, and the Spanish and Dutch conflict.

    Short-term causes

    Dispute over the Letter of Majesty, the succession of Ferdinand, and the defenestration of 1618.

    Event or transformation

    The Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick V, turning an internal dispute into an imperial and European crisis.

    Immediate effects

    White Mountain, the Habsburg consolidation of Bohemia, the loss of the Palatinate, and Bavaria's electoral dignity.

    Long-term consequences

    Danish, Swedish, and French interventions, widespread devastation, and the settlement of 1648.

    Historical complexity

    No single cause explains the war: religion, law, dynasty, finance, and strategic rivalry all mattered.
    Everyday Life and Social History

    Households hid grain, fled to fortified towns or forests, or paid protection money to commanders. Women managed farms and sold goods to troops, children were orphaned, and communities negotiated quartering. Church registers record spikes in burials from disease, and tax lists record abandoned farms.

    After the peace, recovery depended on resettlement, new labor, credit, and local government. Some communities rebuilt within a generation, while others remained depopulated much longer.

    Chapter Summary

    The Thirty Years' War began as a dispute over religious rights and constitutional authority in Bohemia and grew into a general European conflict. The Holy Roman Empire's decentralized structure, the unresolved Peace of Augsburg, and Habsburg dynastic politics created the conditions for escalation.

    The defenestration of Prague in 1618 and the election of Frederick V in 1619 turned a regional revolt into an imperial crisis. The defeat of the Bohemian forces at White Mountain in 1620 led to the transformation of Bohemia, with executions, confiscations, exile, and Catholic restoration.

    The Palatinate war brought the Spanish Habsburgs and the League into the Rhineland, and the transfer of the electoral title to Bavaria alarmed Protestant rulers. Danish intervention failed, and Wallenstein's army and the Edict of Restitution made imperial power appear dangerously strong.

    Swedish intervention in 1630, financed in part by France, reversed the military balance at Breitenfeld and Lützen, but Sweden's position collapsed at Nördlingen. The Peace of Prague of 1635 sought to restore order in the Empire without France or Sweden.

    France's entry in 1635 widened the conflict into a Franco-Habsburg struggle, in which confessional alliances were crossed by dynastic and strategic interests. Armies, financed through contributions and credit, lived off the land, so war's costs fell heavily on civilians.

    Violence, famine, and disease caused severe regional population loss, though estimates for the Empire as a whole are uncertain and regional differences were large. Women, villagers, and townspeople negotiated, fled, and survived under occupation.

    The treaties of 24 October 1648 recognized Reformed Protestants, fixed church property by the normative year 1624, confirmed the rights of the estates within the Empire, and redistributed territory to Sweden, France, Brandenburg, and others. The Dutch and Swiss gained recognition.

    The settlement did not end religious conflict, abolish the Empire, or create a modern system of sovereign states, and France and Spain continued fighting until 1659. It nonetheless gave the Empire a legal framework for managing confessional division and ended one of the most destructive wars in European history.

    Chapter Reflection

    The Thirty Years' War shows how religion, law, and dynastic power became bound together in early modern Europe. A conflict that began over the interpretation of a charter in Bohemia ended by reshaping the Empire and the European balance of power.

    It completes Part X by showing the limits of the religious wars. Confessional division was managed through law and treaty but did not disappear, and later persecutions and conflicts show that religious tension continued.

    The war also reveals the costs of early modern warfare for ordinary people, and it requires historians to treat statistics and memory cautiously. Its settlement shaped the Empire, French and Swedish power, and Dutch and Swiss independence.

    The next chapter turns to the Atlantic voyages of Portugal and Spain, and Part XII examines how states financed and organized power after 1648. The contingent character of the settlement, and the later construction of the Westphalian myth, remain central lessons.
