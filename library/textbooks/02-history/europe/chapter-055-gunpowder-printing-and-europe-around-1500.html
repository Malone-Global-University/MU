Gunpowder, Printing, and Europe around 1500

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In the decades around 1500, a Europe still governed largely by lords, bishops, guilds, town councils, and village custom acquired new capacities: weapons that could breach walls in days or hours, presses that could multiply a text by the hundreds, rulers who could tax and borrow more reliably, and ships whose crews could sail far beyond coasts familiar to earlier navigators. None of these changes abolished the older world. Together they changed what rulers, readers, soldiers, merchants, and sailors were able to attempt.

In the spring of 1453, Ottoman gunners serving Sultan Mehmed II bombarded the land walls of Constantinople, fortifications that had been built in the fifth century and had turned back many earlier besiegers. The city fell on 29 May after a siege of roughly seven weeks, an event examined as a political and religious turning point in Chapter 41. In the same decade, in the Rhineland city of Mainz, the goldsmith and entrepreneur Johann Gutenberg and his associates were producing what became the best known book of the fifteenth century, a Latin Bible printed from movable metal type. The two events had no direct connection, and nobody at the time would have placed them in the same category. Yet both illustrate a pattern that this chapter examines: skilled artisans, investors, and rulers in the fifteenth century were combining metalworking, chemistry, mechanics, credit, and organized labor in ways that altered the practical possibilities of war and communication.

Chapter 55 is the capstone of Part VIII. The preceding chapters traced a century and a half of severe disruption: the famines and plague mortality of Chapter 50, the dynastic and fiscal pressures of the Hundred Years' War in Chapter 51, the social conflicts of Chapter 52, the institutional crisis of the western church in Chapter 53, and the wider political geography of Burgundy, the Hanseatic world, Poland-Lithuania, and other regions in Chapter 54. Those chapters showed that late medieval Europe was neither stagnant nor merely collapsing. This chapter asks what kinds of capacities Europeans possessed by about 1500 as a result of those pressures and experiments, and why historians often treat the decades around 1500 as a transition toward what they call the early modern period.

The central argument is that the transition was uneven rather than sudden. Gunpowder artillery transformed siege warfare in some places and decades, but castles, city walls, cavalry, longbows, and crossbows did not disappear. Printing spread with remarkable speed across more than two hundred European towns before 1500, but manuscripts continued to be copied, most people could not read, and the majority of printed books were in Latin and addressed to a small learned and professional public. Monarchies, principalities, and cities acquired more regular taxes, officials, and courts, yet they remained bound by estates, corporate privileges, regional laws, noble power, and church institutions. Portuguese and Castilian seafarers pushed into the Atlantic, but they did so using Mediterranean techniques, Genoese capital, African partners, and older crusading and commercial motives. A textbook that presented 1500 as the moment when the Middle Ages ended and modern Europe began would conceal exactly this mixture.

Historians reconstruct these developments from unusually varied evidence. Surviving guns, including the large bombard known as Mons Meg in Edinburgh, show how early artillery was built. Treasury accounts, muster rolls, town records, and contracts with gunfounders and mercenary captains reveal what weapons and armies cost. For printing, the books themselves, known as incunabula, are the primary evidence, along with colophons, printers' contracts, privileges, wills, inventories, and the surviving letters of contemporaries who encountered the new technology. Maps, portolan charts, ship timbers, and navigational instruments illuminate maritime knowledge, while chronicles, diplomatic dispatches, and later histories supply narrative accounts that must be read critically, since many were written to praise rulers or to dramatize change after the fact.

The chapter also attends to regional range. A narrative that treated Paris, London, Florence, and Mainz as the whole of Europe would leave out the Ottoman gunners in the Balkans, the Hungarian standing army of Matthias Corvinus, the Muscovite expansion under Ivan III, the Hebrew presses of exiles from Iberia, the Church Slavonic printers of Kraków and Cetinje, and the Iberian crowns whose union of Castile and Aragon reshaped the western Mediterranean and the Atlantic. The physical setting examined in Chapter 2 also remains relevant: the Rhine corridor carried Mainz's printers and merchants, the Alpine passes tied Venice to the German lands, and the mines of Saxony, Bohemia, Tyrol, and Hungary supplied the metals on which both coinage and cannon depended.

The chapter follows five movements. Sections 55.1 to 55.5 examine gunpowder weapons, fortifications, infantry and cavalry, and the growing cost of war. Sections 55.6 to 55.8 turn to rulers, revenue, and the different political forms of Europe around 1500. Sections 55.9 to 55.13 treat the printing press, its spread, its readers, and its relationship to authority. Sections 55.14 to 55.16 examine maps, practical knowledge, ships, and the first stages of European maritime expansion. Sections 55.17 and 55.18 weigh continuity against change. Two boundaries govern the chapter. Chapter 56 begins the study of the Italian Renaissance, and the full history of Portuguese and Spanish expansion belongs to Chapter 68. Chapter 55 therefore concentrates on the machinery of transformation, and leaves the cultural movements and overseas systems that used that machinery to later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how gunpowder weapons entered and spread through European warfare between the fourteenth and early sixteenth centuries, and why adoption was gradual and uneven.
  2. Assess the effects of artillery on sieges and fortifications while avoiding the claim that castles and walls became obsolete overnight.
  3. Compare the roles of handguns, pike formations, longbows, crossbows, and cavalry in late fifteenth century armies.
  4. Analyze the connection between the rising cost of war and the growth of taxation, credit, administration, and representative bargaining.
  5. Distinguish among monarchies, composite monarchies, the Holy Roman Empire, city republics, leagues, and ecclesiastical territories as political forms around 1500.
  6. Trace the development of printing from manuscript culture through Gutenberg's work in Mainz to its spread across European towns.
  7. Evaluate the evidence for literacy and readership around 1500, and explain the limits of any claim that print created a mass reading public by that date.
  8. Interpret how print affected the circulation, standardization, and control of texts, and how rulers and church authorities responded.
  9. Describe the maritime, cartographic, and mathematical knowledge on which fifteenth century navigation depended, and connect it to the early Atlantic voyages without narrating them in full.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of military and print "revolutions," and explain why period labels such as "early modern" are analytical conventions.
  11. Contextualize developments in Iberia, Italy, the German lands, France, England, Hungary, Muscovy, the Ottoman Balkans, and the Baltic without treating any one region as the European norm.
  12. Connect the changes of this chapter to the crises of Part VIII and to the subjects of Chapters 56 and 68.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did gunpowder weapons take more than a century to alter European warfare decisively, and what other factors, beyond technology, shaped how they were used?

  8. Why do historians disagree about whether the decades around 1500 witnessed "revolutions" in military affairs and communication, and what is at stake in that disagreement?

Historical Context

Part VIII has argued that the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries were an era of severe crisis and also of transformation. The Great Famine and the Black Death, discussed in Chapter 50, killed a substantial share of the population of many regions, though mortality varied greatly from place to place and estimates remain uncertain. Labor became scarcer and, in many regions, more costly, which contributed to the social tensions and revolts examined in Chapter 52. The Hundred Years' War, examined in Chapter 51, did not merely pit two kingdoms against one another. It pressed rulers to raise money, recruit paid soldiers, negotiate with assemblies, and justify their claims, and it left both the French and English monarchies with new fiscal and military habits. The western church, as Chapter 53 explained, endured the Avignon papacy and the Great Schism, and its leaders faced criticism and organized demands for reform. Chapter 54 broadened the narrative by showing that Burgundy, the Hanseatic towns, Poland-Lithuania, and other powers created durable political and commercial systems in these same decades.

Several long-term conditions framed the period around 1500. First, demographic recovery was under way in many regions by the second half of the fifteenth century, although it was uneven, and modern estimates of Europe's total population around 1500 vary considerably depending on how the continent's borders are drawn. Most Europeans still lived in villages and worked in agriculture, a pattern described in Chapter 34 and Chapter 42, and only a handful of cities, such as Paris, Naples, Venice, Milan, and Ottoman Constantinople, were large by the standards of the day. Second, commercial networks examined in Chapter 43, including Mediterranean, Baltic, and Atlantic trade, had survived the crises and in some cases expanded, creating merchants and banking houses with capital that could be placed in new ventures, whether in printing, mining, ships, or loans to rulers. Third, the environmental conditions discussed in Chapter 2 continued to matter. Wood and charcoal fed furnaces and forges, timber built ships, rivers carried heavy goods such as paper, barrels, and cannon, and the metals of central European mines, including the silver, copper, and iron of Saxony, Bohemia, Tyrol, and the Carpathians, supplied the raw material for coinage and ordnance alike. Some historians also connect the climatic cooling discussed in Chapter 2 to agrarian stress in parts of this period, though the regional evidence is varied.

The intellectual and institutional world into which gunpowder and printing arrived was also not a blank slate. Universities, whose development is traced in Chapter 48, had created a class of trained lawyers, physicians, theologians, and administrators who needed books and could serve rulers. Cathedral schools, urban schools, and the schools of the Brethren of the Common Life in the Low Countries and Germany trained many boys, and to a lesser extent girls, in reading and writing. Mercantile practice produced commercial arithmetic and bookkeeping. Manuscript workshops, stationers, and scribal professions were already supplying a considerable market for texts, as the next sections show. Gunpowder, likewise, arrived in a world with a rich tradition of siege engineering, metalworking, bell founding, and mechanical devices such as the trebuchet. Innovation was layered upon established skill.

Finally, the political map of Europe around 1500 was in motion. The Ottoman Empire, as Chapter 41 explained, had absorbed the Byzantine capital and was a major power in southeastern Europe, with its own highly effective administrative and military system. The union of the crowns of Castile and Aragon through the marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand, the consolidation of Valois France after the Hundred Years' War, the Tudor accession in England in 1485, the Habsburg marriage alliances that joined Burgundian and Spanish inheritances, the rise of Muscovy under Ivan III, and the pressure of Ottoman expansion on Hungary and the Adriatic all shaped the setting in which weapons, books, and ships developed. These histories are summarized here only as far as they explain the changes of this chapter, and later chapters, especially Chapter 74, Chapter 75, and Chapter 76, develop them more fully.

The evidence used in this chapter differs in character from that of earlier ones. Chronicles remain important, but financial records, notarial acts, guild and town regulations, privileges granted by rulers, and printed books themselves are especially valuable because they were produced for practical purposes and were not primarily literary or dynastic narratives. Their strengths are their precision and their connection to ordinary transactions. Their weaknesses are that they survive unevenly, that they often reflect the interests of states and institutions that kept archives, and that they say little about the many people, including most women, peasants, and laborers, who left few documents of their own.

Major People, Groups, and Places

Johann Gutenberg and the Mainz workshop
Goldsmith and entrepreneur from Mainz (died 1468) credited with developing movable metal type printing in Europe, working with financiers such as Johann Fust and craftsmen such as Peter Schöffer. The partnership's disputes, recorded in a lawsuit of 1455, are important evidence for how early printing was financed.
Aldus Manutius and the Venetian printers
Scholar and publisher who founded the Aldine press in Venice in the 1490s, known for editions of Greek and Latin texts and for compact formats. Venice was the largest printing center of the late fifteenth century, in part because of its capital, shipping, and paper trade.
William Caxton
English merchant who learned printing on the Continent, printed in Bruges and Cologne, and established England's first press at Westminster in 1476, publishing many works in English.

Ottoman sultan (reigned 1444 to 1446 and 1451 to 1481) who took Constantinople in 1453 with the aid of large bombards. The Ottoman state combined heavy guns, a standing infantry in the Janissaries, and a provincial cavalry system.
Charles VII, Louis XI, and Charles VIII of France
Valois kings (reigned 1422 to 1461, 1461 to 1483, and 1483 to 1498) who built a permanent tax system, royal companies of paid soldiers, and a powerful artillery establishment. Charles VIII's invasion of Italy in 1494 made French artillery famous.
Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon
Monarchs whose marriage in 1469 and subsequent accessions joined two crowns while leaving their institutions separate. They conquered Granada in 1492, supported the Spanish Inquisition, and sponsored Columbus.

Section 55.1

Europe around 1500: Transformation without a Clean Break

Any chapter that places "Europe around 1500" in its title invites a misunderstanding, namely that the year 1500 marks a boundary. It does not. The year is a convenient central point for a period, loosely the later fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, during which several long processes became visible together. The conventions of periodization examined throughout this textbook, including the labels "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "early modern," were largely devised by later scholars, and different regions and social groups entered new circumstances at different times. A peasant family in the Carpathian foothills, a printer's apprentice in Venice, a Castilian tax collector, an Ottoman provincial cavalryman, and a Hanseatic merchant in Bergen lived in the same decade, but they did not experience a common "new age."

The reasons for resisting a clean break are substantial. Landlords, village communities, and customary law continued to structure rural life across most of the continent, as Chapter 34 described. The church remained the largest single institution in western and central Europe, and the Orthodox church retained comparable importance in the Balkans and eastern Europe. Guilds, town councils, and corporate privileges governed urban work. Dynastic marriage still shaped the map: the union of Burgundian, Habsburg, and Spanish inheritances in the generation after 1477 depended on weddings and deaths rather than conquest. Military and political authority remained widely shared among kings, nobles, towns, bishops, and assemblies. In this setting, new technologies and institutions often reinforced existing structures before they eroded them. Rulers used printing to publish ordinances and indulgence letters, and used artillery to demonstrate royal power over rebellious lords.

At the same time, it would be equally misleading to deny that change was occurring. The same decades saw major demonstrations of new military capacity, a printing trade that had no equivalent in earlier centuries, and a growth in the administrative reach of many governments. The task is to describe change in its specific forms, to ask who experienced it and when, and to avoid projecting later outcomes backward. Printing did not inevitably produce the Reformation, discussed from Chapter 62, and artillery did not inevitably produce the centralized state. Those outcomes depended on additional circumstances. This chapter's purpose is to explain how the capacities that made such outcomes possible came into being.

Historical Vocabulary
Early Modern

Early modern is a scholarly label for the centuries between the late Middle Ages and the age of industrial and political revolutions, with starting dates usually placed somewhere between about 1450 and 1520 and ending dates between about 1750 and 1800. Historians who use it generally emphasize developments such as stronger states, wider literacy and print, overseas expansion, religious division, and new scientific methods. The label is an analytical convention, and it can mislead. It implies a uniform entry into a new era, but different transformations occurred at different dates in different places, and many medieval institutions persisted for centuries. In this textbook, "early modern" signals a gradual transition and not a clean boundary, and Chapter 55 uses it only to describe tendencies visible around 1500.

Section 55.2

Gunpowder Comes to European Warfare

Gunpowder, a mixture of saltpeter (potassium nitrate), sulfur, and charcoal, was developed in China, and knowledge of explosive mixtures and of guns spread across Eurasia through the cultural, commercial, and military exchanges that characterized the Mongol era. The details of transmission remain uncertain, because the surviving evidence is fragmentary. By the early fourteenth century, however, western Europeans clearly knew of gunpowder weapons. Among the earliest firm evidence are an illustrated treatise written in 1326 for the young prince who would become Edward III of England, which depicts a vase shaped cannon firing an arrow, and a Florentine decision of 1326 to acquire metal cannon and shot for the defense of the city. Later in the century, guns appear in the accounts and inventories of towns and rulers across the continent.

Early guns were not obviously superior to existing weapons. They were costly to cast or forge, slow to load, dangerous to their operators, and unreliable. Powder made as a fine dust tended to separate into its components in transport and burned inconsistently. Many early barrels were built of wrought iron bars and hoops, welded or bound together in a manner resembling a barrel, and such "built-up" guns could rupture. Bronze casting, drawing on the skills of bell founders, produced stronger barrels but was expensive because copper and tin had to be purchased in large quantities, as Chapter 2 noted in discussing the distribution of metals. The projectiles of the largest fourteenth and early fifteenth century bombards were often stone, which required skilled masons to carve and could shatter on impact. As a result, rulers and towns commonly purchased guns in small numbers, alongside existing siege equipment, and used them for specific purposes such as defending a gate or breaching a particular wall.

Between roughly 1400 and 1500, a series of incremental improvements changed the picture. Gunpowder was "corned," that is, formed into grains, which made it more stable, more powerful, and easier to load. Bronze guns became lighter relative to their power. Iron shot, which was denser than stone and more destructive, increasingly replaced stone balls in field and siege guns, although casting large iron guns reliably was achieved only later, in the early sixteenth century and after. Gun carriages with wheels and trunnions, the cylindrical pegs that allow a barrel to be elevated, made guns easier to move and aim. Gunners, many of whom were former bell founders, masons, or smiths, formed a specialized occupation with their own manuals, such as the German Feuerwerkbuch tradition, which circulated in manuscript from the early fifteenth century and later in print. These manuals provided recipes for powder and advice on ranges and charges, and they show that gunnery was already a body of technical knowledge shared across borders.

The supply of powder and metal also shaped adoption. Saltpeter was not abundant in Europe and was commonly obtained by processing manure heaps, cellar earth, and stable soil, a labor that rulers sometimes organized through licensed "saltpeter men" with rights to dig on private property, a practice that provoked complaints. Sulfur came principally from volcanic regions such as Sicily, and charcoal from managed woodland that competed with other uses of timber. Weapons production therefore tied military activity to rural labor, forests, mines, and long-distance trade. Cities with metalworking traditions, including Nuremberg, Augsburg, Milan, and Liège, and mining districts in Bohemia, Saxony, Sweden, and the Basque country, became important arms producers. States that could finance such production, or purchase from these markets, gained an advantage, which anticipates the discussion of cost in Section 55.5.

A caution about causes is necessary. Gunpowder weapons did not spread merely because they were effective. Adoption also depended on social and political circumstances: whether a ruler could afford the investment, whether nobles whose status rested on mounted warfare accepted the new weapons, whether skilled craftsmen were available, and whether the geography of the campaign allowed guns to be hauled. Heavy guns required roads, bridges, draft animals, and fodder, so artillery was easiest to use on the plains and river valleys of the North European Plain, northern Italy, and the Danubian lowlands, and harder to bring through the mountain terrain examined in Chapter 2. Historians therefore describe a gradual and uneven adoption, in which guns were first complementary and became decisive only when other conditions favored them.

Historical Vocabulary
Bombard and Artillery Train

A bombard was a large, short barreled siege gun, often made of iron bars and hoops or of cast bronze, firing a heavy stone or, later, iron ball at walls. Examples include the bombard called Mons Meg, made in the Burgundian dominions and sent to the king of Scots in 1457, and the very large guns associated with the Ottoman siege of Constantinople. An artillery train was the organized collection of guns, carriages, powder, shot, draft animals, wagons, gunners, and laborers that accompanied an army. The term matters because effective artillery was a system, not a single weapon. A powerful gun was useless without the carts, roads, horses, and trained personnel to bring it to a target, and by the 1490s the best trains, notably the French, could move much more rapidly than earlier ones.

Section 55.3

Artillery, Sieges, and the Changing Fortified Landscape

The effects of gunpowder were first felt most strongly in sieges. Medieval fortifications, whether the stone castles of lords, the walls of cities, or the great defensive lines of imperial capitals, were designed to resist assault, undermining, and the stone throwing engines of earlier centuries. Tall, relatively thin masonry walls with projecting towers were effective against these. They were less effective against iron or stone balls fired directly at their base. By the mid fifteenth century, rulers with well organized artillery could often reduce a fortification more quickly than could earlier methods. The French campaigns that ended the Hundred Years' War in Normandy and Guyenne between 1449 and 1453, in which royal gunners under the brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau figured prominently, are often cited as a demonstration. At Castillon in July 1453, a French army with substantial artillery defeated an English force under John Talbot, an episode that effectively ended English power in Gascony, as Chapter 51 outlined.

The siege of Constantinople in 1453 offers a second case. Mehmed II commissioned large guns, including those cast by a founder known in Greek and Latin sources as Orban or Urban, who is said to have offered his services first to the Byzantine emperor. The bombardment damaged sections of the Theodosian walls, but the defenders repaired breaches at night, and the final assault of 29 May relied on massed infantry as well as artillery. The episode is a good example of how historians avoid single cause explanations: the guns mattered, but so did Ottoman numbers, naval operations, the defenders' small garrison, and the political isolation of the city, themes examined in Chapter 41. Elsewhere, Ottoman and Hungarian forces on the Danube, Venetian and Ottoman forces in the Aegean and Adriatic, and Muscovite forces under Ivan III, who employed Italian engineers and founders, all expanded their use of guns in the second half of the century.

The most celebrated demonstration came in 1494 and 1495, when Charles VIII of France invaded the Italian peninsula to press a claim to the kingdom of Naples. His army, whose size is estimated at roughly twenty five to thirty thousand men, included a large artillery train of bronze guns mounted on wheeled carriages and firing iron shot. According to the Florentine statesman and historian Francesco Guicciardini, writing decades later, the speed with which this artillery reduced fortresses astonished Italians, who were accustomed to longer sieges. Guicciardini and other chroniclers described the fall of the fortress of Monte San Giovanni in a matter of hours and the massacre that followed. Such accounts must be treated carefully, because they were written to dramatize a turning point and to explain Italian political failure, but modern scholars generally accept that the French train marked a real improvement in mobility and effectiveness.

Primary Source Window

Francesco Guicciardini, History of Italy, on the French Artillery of 1494

Creator: Francesco Guicciardini (1483 to 1540), Florentine lawyer, diplomat, and papal official · Date: written in the late 1530s, published after his death · Type: A narrative history of Italy from 1490 written retrospectively by a participant in later Italian politics

Context: Guicciardini wrote after the Italian Wars had repeatedly devastated the peninsula, including the sack of Rome in 1527. He interpreted the French invasion of 1494 as the beginning of Italy's calamities, and his account of the artillery is part of that framing. He was a child in 1494 and relied on documents, earlier accounts, and the testimony of participants.

Paraphrase of Guicciardini, History of Italy, Book I Guicciardini contrasted the artillery familiar in Italy, which he described as heavy, slow, clumsy to deploy, and fired at long intervals, with the French guns, which he described as lighter, drawn by horses rather than oxen, accompanied by well organized crews, and able to fire quickly and repeatedly. He claimed that walls which had once held out for many days or weeks now yielded in hours, and that the French guns shot iron balls instead of stone. He concluded that Italian rulers, accustomed to older methods, were unprepared for this form of warfare. This is a paraphrase of the content of his argument, not a translation of a single passage.

Analysis: This source is valuable as an early and influential account of how contemporaries and near contemporaries understood the change in artillery. It identifies factors that modern studies also emphasize, including mobility, organization, and projectile material. Its limitations are equally important. Guicciardini wrote forty years later with a thesis about Italian failure. His contrast between old and new may exaggerate the novelty of the French guns, since Italian and other armies had used effective artillery earlier and Italian engineers were already experimenting with new forms of fortification. He also says little about the experiences of civilians and ordinary soldiers, or about the French administrative effort needed to assemble the train.

  2. Who created this source, when, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the way contemporaries understood the transformation of warfare?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, and what other kinds of evidence, such as treasury accounts or surviving guns, would help?

The response of military engineers was not to abandon fortification but to redesign it. Italian architects and engineers, including Francesco di Giorgio Martini and Giuliano da Sangallo, experimented from the later fifteenth century with lower, thicker walls backed by earth, and with projecting angular structures, bastions, from which defenders could sweep the base of walls with crossfire. This design tradition, often called the trace italienne, matured mainly in the sixteenth century, after the period covered by this chapter, and it spread slowly because it was extremely expensive to build. Its later importance is a subject for Chapter 74. For the period around 1500, the essential point is that older fortifications retained value. Many castles continued to serve as residences, administrative centers, and strongholds against rebels and small forces, and many towns still relied on medieval walls for defense against raiders, for customs control, and as symbols of corporate identity.

The social consequences of siege artillery were also significant. A besieged town's population, including women, children, elderly residents, and religious minorities, endured bombardment, hunger, disease, and, in some cases, sack. Siege gunners were often foreign specialists contracted by the ruler. Town authorities spent large sums on guns, gunpowder stores, gun foundries, and rebuilt walls, and councils negotiated the terms of surrender with besiegers who wished to avoid the expense and delay of a prolonged siege. Surrender on negotiated terms, which could protect lives and property, became more attractive when a fortress could be breached quickly, so the threat of artillery shaped diplomacy as much as battle.

Section 55.4

Handguns, Infantry, Cavalry, and the Changing Battlefield

On the battlefield, the relationship between new and old weapons was complex. Portable firearms, known in the fourteenth century as hand cannon and by the later fifteenth century as arquebuses, were slow and inaccurate but could be used by men who required far less training than a longbow archer or a mounted knight. The introduction of the matchlock, in which a slow burning cord was lowered into the powder pan by a lever, allowed the shooter to keep both hands on the weapon and to aim with greater control. Even so, early handguns were noisy, heavy, and vulnerable to wet weather, and for much of the fifteenth century they supplemented rather than replaced bows and crossbows. The English longbow, which gave English armies their victories at Crécy in 1346 and Agincourt in 1415, required years of practice and was a product of a particular social and legal setting in England, and the crossbow remained common in continental armies, as did the pole arm and halberd.

Several other developments mattered at least as much as firearms in changing the battlefield. The first was the rise of disciplined infantry formations. Swiss communities developed dense blocks of men armed with long pikes and halberds, trained to advance in step and to resist cavalry charges. Their victories over Charles the Bold of Burgundy at Grandson and Murten in 1476 and at Nancy in 1477, when Charles himself was killed, as Chapter 54 noted in discussing Burgundy, made the Swiss mercenaries sought after by kings and princes. German imitators, the Landsknechte, were organized from the 1480s under the sponsorship of Maximilian I and became a major source of infantry for Habsburg and other armies. These forces were recruited for pay by captains who contracted with rulers, and they brought tactical skill along with the risk that unpaid soldiers might mutiny or plunder.

The second development was the experimentation with firearms within tactical systems. In the Hussite wars in Bohemia, from about 1419 to the 1430s, armies led by commanders such as Jan Žižka used wagon forts, in which chained carts formed a mobile barricade from which handgunners and light cannon fired at attackers. These formations defeated repeated crusading armies, as Chapter 53 touched on in discussing popular religious movements. In the late fifteenth century, Spanish armies in the long conquest of Granada, between 1482 and 1492, integrated artillery, infantry, and cavalry, and Spanish commanders after 1500 developed further combinations of pikemen and arquebusiers. The Ottoman army, which relied on provincial cavalry holding revenue assignments, called timars, on the paid infantry known as Janissaries, and on a powerful artillery corps, also adapted. Janissaries increasingly carried firearms during the later fifteenth century, and their cohesion and pay distinguished them from many European levies.

Cavalry did not vanish. Heavily armored lancers, the gendarmes of France and similar forces elsewhere, remained prestigious and often effective, particularly in combination with infantry and artillery. Light cavalry, such as Hungarian hussars and the Ottoman sipahis and raiders, were essential for scouting and for fighting on open frontiers. Armor itself improved, with plate armor made in centers such as Milan and Augsburg reaching a high point of sophistication in the late fifteenth century. Social status also shaped the debate over weapons. For nobles whose identity rested on mounted combat, firearms could appear dishonorable, and chivalric literature continued to idealize the knight. Yet noblemen also commanded companies, served as captains and artillery officers, and invested in the new methods. The picture is one of adaptation within existing social structures, not replacement of one class by another.

Rulers also tried to create more reliable forces. Charles VII of France, building on the ordinances of 1439 and 1445, established permanent companies of paid heavy cavalry, the compagnies d'ordonnance, and a militia of archers exempted from some taxes, the francs-archers of 1448, although the latter proved uneven in quality. Charles the Bold of Burgundy issued ordinances for a standing army in 1471, and Matthias Corvinus in Hungary assembled a large mercenary force, often called the Black Army, whose size at its peak is estimated at something in the range of twenty to thirty thousand men. In Castile and Aragon the Santa Hermandad, a league of towns that became a royal police and militia organization after 1476, helped supply forces. These experiments with standing or semi permanent forces were expensive and politically sensitive, and they lead directly to the problem of cost examined next.

Section 55.5

War Becomes More Expensive

The combination of artillery, fortification, and paid soldiers raised the price of war. A prince who wished to besiege a major fortress needed a train of heavy guns, large stocks of powder and shot, wagons and draft animals, engineers, gunners, laborers to dig trenches and repair roads, and enough infantry to protect the guns and to storm a breach. Mercenary soldiers expected regular pay, and when it was late they might desert, mutiny, or plunder the countryside, which damaged the ruler's own subjects. Defensive measures also cost money: rebuilding walls, casting guns, and garrisoning fortresses. The medieval custom of relying on feudal service, on temporary levies of townsmen, or on a lord's retinue did not disappear, but rulers who wanted to project power increasingly needed cash.

It is important to avoid overprecision about the numbers. Estimates of army sizes and of the financial costs of campaigns for this period are drawn from chronicles, which tend to exaggerate, and from financial accounts, which survive only in part and use many different currencies and accounting conventions. Most historians treat figures such as the twenty five to thirty thousand men said to have crossed the Alps in 1494 as approximate. What can be said with more confidence is the direction of change. Over the fifteenth century, the larger European states tended to field larger armies and to spend a growing share of their revenue on war, and they met these costs through a mixture of regular taxation, extraordinary levies, loans, sales of offices, and the exploitation of royal domains and monopolies.

Two features of European economic geography shaped how rulers paid. First, wealth was unevenly distributed. Towns in northern Italy, the Low Countries, southern Germany, and parts of Iberia contained dense concentrations of merchants, craftsmen, and bankers, whereas many rural regions generated little cash. Rulers who controlled urban wealth, or who could borrow from urban merchants, enjoyed an advantage. Second, mining supplied specie and credit. The silver and copper mines of the Tyrol, Saxony, and Hungarian Slovakia, to which Chapter 2 referred, supported financiers such as the Fuggers of Augsburg, who by the 1480s and 1490s advanced loans to the Tyrolean prince and to Maximilian I against the output of mines. Genoese, Florentine, and later South German bankers provided credit that rulers repaid through taxes, customs, or monopoly rights. This dependence on credit anticipates the fiscal pressures of the sixteenth century.

The costs of war fell unequally. Peasants and townspeople bore the burden of direct taxes, billeting, requisitioned food and labor, and the destruction caused by marching armies. Privileged groups, such as nobles and clergy, often enjoyed partial exemption, which led to bargaining over who should pay. Merchants and bankers might profit by lending, supplying armies, or buying offices. Soldiers themselves could gain wages and plunder or die of disease and wounds, since illness killed more campaigners than combat. Communities near the routes of armies suffered most, and the later Italian Wars, though beyond this chapter, would intensify these burdens. The relationship between military expense and political change nevertheless depended on what rulers could persuade or compel their subjects to accept, which is the subject of the next section.

Cause and Effect

Costly War and the Strengthening of Rulers' Fiscal Institutions

Long-term causes

The fiscal and administrative experiments of the high and late Middle Ages, including royal taxation, assemblies of estates, and professional lawyers and clerks, together with the demands of dynastic and frontier warfare during the Hundred Years' War and other conflicts.

Short-term causes

The adoption of artillery and paid infantry, the political need to defend against rivals and, in the east and south, against Ottoman expansion, and the availability of credit from merchants and banking houses.

Event or transformation

Rulers in France, Castile and Aragon, England, Burgundy, the Habsburg lands, Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and other polities expanded regular taxation, created or enlarged permanent military forces, and tightened control of officials and courts.

Immediate effects

More dependable revenues, new tensions with nobles, towns, and assemblies, and new burdens on taxpayers, along with growing reliance on loans and on foreign mercenaries.

Long-term consequences

A pattern of large, costly, credit dependent states whose fiscal and military development, examined in Chapter 74 and later chapters, shaped the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, though not in a single direction.

Historical complexity

Causation ran in both directions. Stronger fiscal systems made larger wars possible, and larger wars required stronger fiscal systems. Some states with considerable military ambition, such as late medieval Burgundy, failed, and others with limited centralization, such as the Empire, endured.
Section 55.6

Rulers, Revenue, and Stronger Political Institutions

The phrase "stronger states" requires immediate qualification. Around 1500, no European government possessed the bureaucracies, standing armies, uniform laws, or national identities that later centuries associated with the modern state, and historians who write about "state formation" generally emphasize that this was a process with varied outcomes. What can be observed in the late fifteenth century is a set of tendencies: rulers sought more dependable revenue, employed more trained officials, expanded their judicial reach, conducted diplomacy through resident ambassadors, and tried to control the use of force within their territories. These efforts took different forms depending on local institutions and on the bargaining power of nobles, towns, and clergy.

Taxation was the central issue. In France, the crown's right to levy the taille, a direct tax, without annual grants from the Estates became established in the mid fifteenth century under Charles VII, providing the monarchy with a reliable income to support the royal companies and artillery. In England, parliamentary grants and customs duties, especially on wool and later broader trade, supported the crown, though English kings could not impose direct taxation without parliamentary consent, a limit examined in Chapter 45. In Castile, the alcabala, a sales tax, and revenues from the military orders and the church were major sources, and Isabella and Ferdinand carefully reorganized the use of these resources. In the Aragonese lands, by contrast, cortes in Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia retained strong powers to bargain over taxes. In the Holy Roman Empire, imperial taxation was limited by the strong position of princes and cities, a point examined in Section 55.8. In the Ottoman Empire, revenue came from agricultural taxes, customs, the poll tax on non-Muslims, and the administrative system of timar assignments, and the sultan exercised strong control over appointments and military organization.

Rulers also relied increasingly on professionals. Lawyers trained in Roman and canon law at universities, as Chapter 48 described, staffed royal councils, chanceries, and courts such as the Parlement of Paris, and they developed theories of royal justice and sovereignty. Treasurers, auditors, and clerks kept accounts that allowed rulers to track revenue. Many offices were sold or inherited, which created a class with vested interests in the system and generated revenue for the crown, but also limited the monarch's ability to dismiss or reform officials. In several kingdoms, rulers also sought control over the church by negotiating the appointment of bishops and the use of church revenue, as in the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges of 1438 in France or the privileges obtained by Ferdinand and Isabella from the papacy in Spain.

Diplomacy became more regular. Italian states, notably Milan, Venice, and Florence, pioneered permanent resident ambassadors in the fifteenth century, who collected information and negotiated continuously rather than being sent for specific missions. By the end of the century, major monarchs and the papacy used similar practices. The Peace of Lodi in 1454 and the subsequent Italian League created a period of relative balance among Italian states, a system that collapsed after 1494. The significance of this development for political thought and culture belongs to Part IX, but its administrative implication belongs here: governments increasingly depended on written reports, cipher correspondence, and archives.

Finally, rulers attempted to monopolize violence. Nobles retained armed retinues, towns maintained militias, and private feuding persisted in many regions. Monarchs pressed claims that only the crown could authorize war and punish serious crimes. The Santa Hermandad in Castile, the French ordonnance companies, the Tudor suppression of noble retinues after 1485, and the Empire's Eternal Public Peace of 1495, which prohibited private war, are examples. Success was partial and contested. Local elites continued to exercise judicial and military authority, and in many areas a ruler's power depended on negotiating with them rather than overriding them. The result was a more intensive but still negotiated political order, not an absolute one.

Section 55.7

France, England, Iberia, and the Uneven Consolidation of Monarchy

The monarchies of western Europe emerged from the crises of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries by very different routes. A comparative treatment shows that "consolidation" was not a single model, and it avoids treating France or England as the template. This section outlines the principal patterns around 1500. The detailed political histories of these kingdoms belong to Chapters 74 to 77 and to the national and regional chapters that follow.

France: recovery through taxation and territorial gathering

After the French victories of the 1440s and 1450s, the Valois kings faced a kingdom devastated by war yet possessing a large population and agricultural base. Charles VII (reigned 1422 to 1461) won the right to a permanent taille and organized royal companies, and the crown cooperated with provincial estates and with the Parlement of Paris, which registered royal edicts and acted as the kingdom's highest court. Louis XI (1461 to 1483) was a skilled and ruthless manipulator who defeated a coalition of princes, helped bring down Charles the Bold of Burgundy, and used the death of the last Burgundian duke in 1477 to seize the duchy of Burgundy, though the Burgundian Netherlands passed to the Habsburgs through Mary of Burgundy's marriage to Maximilian. The duchy of Brittany, long semi independent, was drawn into the French orbit when Anne of Brittany married Charles VIII in 1491 and then Louis XII in 1499. By 1500 the French crown ruled a large and relatively unified territory, but it was a patchwork of provinces with their own customs, laws, and privileges, and French kings still had to bargain with provincial estates and powerful nobles. The Estates General convened at Tours in 1484 demonstrated that representatives continued to press for lower taxes and greater consultation.

England and the British Isles: civil war and Tudor recovery

England's path was marked by dynastic conflict. The Wars of the Roses, a series of struggles between supporters of the houses of Lancaster and York from the 1450s, ended when Henry Tudor, a Lancastrian claimant with Welsh and French support, defeated Richard III at Bosworth in August 1485, and he consolidated his position over the following years, notably at the battle of Stoke in 1487. Henry VII (reigned 1485 to 1509) rebuilt royal finances through careful management of crown lands, customs, and fines, and relied on trusted councillors and on judicial bodies that could act against powerful subjects who intimidated local courts, including the council sitting as what became known as the Court of Star Chamber. He also curbed the maintenance of private retinues by nobles. The English crown depended less on direct taxation than the French and less on mercenary armies than many continental rulers, partly because England's insular position and its tradition of parliamentary consent shaped its military choices. It is important not to treat England as synonymous with Britain. Scotland remained an independent kingdom under James IV, with its own parliament and its alliance with France. Wales, conquered under Edward I, was incorporated more fully under later Tudor legislation. In Ireland the English lordship was limited largely to the Pale around Dublin, and Poynings' Law of 1494 and 1495 subordinated the Irish parliament to English approval.

Iberia: composite monarchy, conquest, and religious exclusion

The Iberian Peninsula was divided among several Christian crowns and the Nasrid emirate of Granada. Isabella of Castile, who gained the throne in 1474 after a civil war against rivals supported by Portugal, and Ferdinand of Aragon, who succeeded his father in 1479, ruled as partners but retained separate institutions, laws, and cortes for each kingdom. Historians call this arrangement a composite monarchy, a concept important in Chapter 75. Castile, the larger and more populous kingdom, provided much of the revenue and manpower. Aragon, with its Mediterranean empire in Sicily, Sardinia, and Naples, and its constitutional limits on royal power, provided an extension of political interests in the central Mediterranean. The monarchs reorganized the royal council, brought the Castilian military orders under royal administration, strengthened royal justice, and in 1478 secured papal approval for an Inquisition under royal supervision, aimed principally at conversos, descendants of Jewish converts to Christianity who were suspected of secretly practicing Judaism.

The Granada War, fought between 1482 and 1492 with artillery, siege engineering, and large financing from church and city sources, ended with the surrender of Granada in January 1492, closing Muslim political rule on the peninsula after nearly eight centuries, as Chapter 47 discussed in connection with the Iberian dimension of crusading. The Castilian and Aragonese crowns promised the inhabitants of Granada religious and legal protections that were soon eroded. In March 1492, by the Alhambra Decree, the monarchs ordered Jews in their realms to convert or leave by the end of July, and tens of thousands departed, many to Portugal, North Africa, Italy, and the Ottoman Empire, while others converted. Portugal itself, under Manuel I, ordered Jews to leave or convert in 1496 and 1497, producing forced baptisms. After 1500, Castile forced the Muslims of the kingdom to choose between baptism and exile, creating the converted Moriscos. These measures belong to a pattern of religious uniformity pursued by rulers who were also strengthening their administrative reach, and they show that "stronger monarchy" had costs for minorities whose presence had long been part of Iberian society. Many exiles carried skills, languages, and trade connections to the Mediterranean world, and the Sephardic communities of the Ottoman Empire and Italy became important centers of commerce, scholarship, and printing.

Portugal, a smaller kingdom, was centralized under John II (reigned 1481 to 1495), who curbed the power of the high nobility, and under Manuel I (1495 to 1521), whose reign coincided with the arrival of Portuguese ships in India. Portuguese rulers combined royal control of overseas trade with the licensing of private enterprise, a pattern reviewed in Section 55.16 and examined fully in Chapter 68. Across these western kingdoms, then, consolidation took the form of a series of negotiated and often violent arrangements, shaped by different legacies of war, dynastic inheritance, and religious policy.

Section 55.8

Empires, Principalities, Cities, and Political Alternatives

A narrative that concentrated on France, England, and Spain would imply that European politics tended toward territorial monarchy. In fact, the political landscape around 1500 contained many other forms, and some of the most economically dynamic and culturally productive regions were not monarchies at all. Part IX will examine the Italian city-states in detail, but several alternatives deserve notice here.

The Holy Roman Empire, treated in Chapter 46, was a layered structure of electors, princes, prince-bishops, imperial cities, counts, knights, and free communities under an elected emperor, in this period Frederick III (died 1493) and then his son Maximilian I. The Diet of Worms in 1495, in the face of Ottoman and French threats, produced a set of reforms often described as the beginning of an imperial constitution. It proclaimed an Eternal Public Peace that forbade private warfare, established an Imperial Chamber Court (Reichskammergericht) to hear disputes, and sought to introduce an imperial tax called the Common Penny. Reform continued after Worms: an initial arrangement of six Imperial Circles to organize defense and enforcement developed around 1500, and the circle system was expanded and reorganized into ten circles in 1512. The success of these measures was partial: princes resisted taxation they did not control, Maximilian often pursued Habsburg dynastic interests, and the Empire remained decentralized. Yet the reforms gave the Empire legal and institutional features that persisted until 1806. It would be a mistake to describe it as a failed Germany, since it contained non-German-speaking regions, including Bohemia and parts of northern Italy, and since its princes and cities valued its protections of their privileges.

Other communities had no monarch at all. The Swiss Confederation, formed by alliances among rural valleys and towns, defended its autonomy against the Habsburgs, as in the Swabian War of 1499. The war and the Peace of Basel strengthened the Confederation's already extensive practical autonomy and reduced the effective reach of certain imperial institutions, especially the Imperial Chamber Court, but they did not constitute a formal separation from the Holy Roman Empire, and the Confederates continued to regard themselves as members of it. Venice, Genoa, and Florence were governed by oligarchic or republican institutions, though Florence under the Medici had a more concentrated pattern of rule. The Hanseatic League, examined in Chapter 54, was a network of towns that defended commercial privileges through collective action and occasional naval and economic warfare. Church territories were also important political units. The Papal States were governed by the pope as a temporal ruler, and under Alexander VI (1492 to 1503) and Julius II (1503 to 1513) papal armies and diplomacy were central to Italian conflict. Prince-bishops such as the archbishop-electors of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier were simultaneously spiritual leaders, territorial princes, and members of the imperial electoral college, so the Mainz printers worked under the authority of an ecclesiastical ruler.

The east and southeast contained further forms. Poland-Lithuania, linked by dynastic union and developing a bicameral diet, saw its nobility win growing legislative powers, a process in which the later constitution of 1505 is an important landmark. In the Baltic, the Teutonic Order lost much of its territory to Poland after the Thirteen Years' War, concluded by the Peace of Thorn in 1466. The Kalmar Union tied together Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under a common crown, though Swedish nobles repeatedly resisted Danish control. Hungary, after the death of Matthias Corvinus in 1490, passed to Jagiellonian kings and faced growing Ottoman pressure. Muscovy under Ivan III absorbed Novgorod, saw the standoff with Khan Ahmad's Great Horde on the Ugra River in 1480 become the conventional marker of the effective end of Great Horde overlordship, although historians debate when practical subordination had actually ceased, and began to claim a distinct imperial and Orthodox dignity, while engaging Italian specialists to fortify the Kremlin. The Crimean Khanate, a successor to the Golden Horde, came under Ottoman suzerainty in 1475. The Ottoman Empire itself, as Section 55.4 noted, combined cavalry, infantry, and artillery under a highly centralized dynasty, and its European provinces in Rumelia included Orthodox, Catholic, Jewish, and, increasingly, Muslim communities.

Historians have sometimes grouped the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires as "gunpowder empires," a label associated with the scholars Marshall Hodgson and William McNeill. The concept has been criticized because it implies that firearms alone explain imperial success, but it is useful in reminding students that the Ottoman state, no less than French or Spanish monarchy, was an early adopter of artillery and a major actor in European diplomacy and war. Taken together, the political forms of Europe around 1500 were more varied than the later emphasis on nation states suggests, and the fiscal and military pressures of the period affected all of them, albeit differently.

Section 55.9

From Manuscript Culture to Movable Type

Printing arrived in a Europe that already possessed a large and sophisticated culture of the written book. Before the middle of the fifteenth century, texts were reproduced by hand on parchment, made from animal skins, and increasingly on paper, which was cheaper. Paper, produced from pulped rags, had come to Europe from the Islamic world and was manufactured in Iberia and then in Italian centers such as Fabriano by the thirteenth century, and paper mills multiplied in Italy, France, and the German lands during the following two centuries. Without a substantial supply of paper, printing on the scale that followed would not have been economically feasible, which suggests how much the print revolution depended on earlier commercial and technical developments.

Manuscript production was organized in several ways. Monks, nuns, and canons copied books in monastic scriptoria for their own libraries and for patrons. The Brethren of the Common Life and similar groups in the Low Countries and Germany copied texts as part of devotional and educational work. Professional scribes and stationers served university towns, where, from the thirteenth century, systems such as the pecia method allowed students to rent sections of authorized texts for copying. In Italian, French, and Flemish cities, workshops produced richly illuminated books of hours, Bibles, and chronicles for nobles, rulers, and wealthy merchants. Rulers formed libraries, such as the one of Matthias Corvinus in Buda, whose manuscripts reflected humanist and Italian tastes. Hebrew scribes produced Torah scrolls, prayer books, and legal commentaries for Jewish communities, and Muslim and Arabic-writing scribes sustained a manuscript culture in Granada and across the Ottoman world. Orthodox monasteries in Byzantine, Balkan, and Rus lands copied liturgical and devotional texts in Greek and Church Slavonic.

Reproduction of images and brief texts by block printing preceded movable type. Woodblock prints of saints, devotional pictures, and playing cards were produced in European towns from about 1400. By the mid fifteenth century, "block books" combined images and short texts cut on a single block. These products indicate a market for inexpensive, mass produced material, and they accustomed buyers to printed pictures and texts. East Asian printing, including Korean metal type in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, predates Gutenberg, but there is no evidence of direct transmission to Europe, and historians generally treat the European invention as independent in its specific techniques and in the alphabetic setting that made movable type particularly suitable.

The manuscript trade was not static or decaying before the press. Evidence from the fifteenth century shows growth in lay ownership of books, in vernacular translations, and in commercial copying shops. Demand for texts was rising in universities, in the offices of lawyers and administrators, in merchant households, and among devout laypeople. The rise in demand helps explain why a technology capable of reproducing texts more cheaply and uniformly found customers quickly. It also explains why the transition from manuscript to print was gradual: scribes continued to work, wealthy patrons commissioned handwritten luxury books, and printers imitated manuscript layout and decoration. Many early printed books left spaces for rubrication and illumination to be added by hand, so that they looked like manuscripts.

Section 55.10

Gutenberg and the Printing Press

Johann Gutenberg was born in Mainz around 1400 into a patrician family and trained as a goldsmith, a craft that involved precise metalworking, punch cutting, and the handling of alloys. Surviving legal records show that he lived in Strasbourg in the 1430s and 1440s, where he was involved in business ventures and in a lawsuit over a partnership, and that he returned to Mainz by about 1448. There he borrowed money from the financier Johann Fust, and, with the assistance of skilled workers including Peter Schöffer, who later became Fust's partner and son in law, he developed a system of printing from movable type. The sources for his life are largely legal and financial, and much about the process of invention remains uncertain. Gutenberg's name does not appear on the Bible attributed to him, and his role is established by documents such as the record of the Fust lawsuit of 1455 and by later testimony.

Gutenberg's achievement was not a single invention but an integrated system. He devised a method for casting individual metal letters in large numbers. A letter was cut in relief on a steel punch, which was struck into softer metal to make a matrix, a mold placed in an adjustable hand mold into which molten alloy, principally lead with tin and antimony, was poured to cast identical pieces of type. Compositors assembled the pieces in lines in a frame, which could be locked into a page forme, inked, and printed. The press itself drew upon existing screw presses used for wine, paper, and linen. The ink was another innovation: scribes' water based inks did not adhere well to metal type, so printers developed oil based inks, using varnish and soot, which gave a dense, durable black. Each element was familiar to someone in the Rhineland economy, but combining them successfully and economically was the novelty.

The great product of this work was the Latin Bible, often called the 42-line Bible after the number of lines in most of its columns, completed by about 1454 to 1455. It was printed in two volumes, probably in a run of roughly 180 copies by common modern estimates, some on paper and some on vellum. Fewer than fifty copies survive in whole or in substantial part. Buyers acquired the copies as unbound sheets and had them rubricated, decorated, and bound locally, so no two are exactly alike. Gutenberg also printed shorter works, such as grammars, and indulgences, and a printed calendar and an appeal against the Ottoman threat have been attributed to the press of this period. The type imitated the formal Gothic script of German manuscripts, which gave the printed page a familiar appearance.

The commercial history is revealing. In 1455 Fust sued Gutenberg for repayment of loans and interest, and the court's decision allowed Fust to take over a significant portion of the printing equipment. Fust and Schöffer then produced the Mainz Psalter of 1457, a handsome book with large initials printed in two colors, which includes a colophon giving the date and the printers' names, making it the earliest dated printed book of which we have a complete copy. Gutenberg himself appears to have continued printing in some form, and in 1465 the archbishop of Mainz, Adolf of Nassau, granted him an annual provision as a court servant. He died in 1468. In 1462, in a conflict over control of the archbishopric, Adolf's forces captured Mainz, and the dispersal of printers and journeymen that followed spread knowledge of the craft to other cities. Gutenberg's own financial history shows that technical innovation did not guarantee commercial success, a theme that recurs in the histories of early printers.

He expressed a wish to obtain a copy for his correspondent, but doubted that this would be possible. This is a paraphrase of the substance of the letter and not a translation of any single passage.

Analysis: This letter is valuable because it dates the Bible's production close to 1455, offers an early reaction by an educated reader, and suggests that the product was desirable for its appearance and legibility and not merely its novelty. It also indicates how word of the new technology traveled through correspondence among churchmen and humanists. Its limits are significant. The number of copies is hearsay, the description concerns only part of the work, and Piccolomini was a sophisticated observer whose reaction cannot stand for that of ordinary buyers. The letter says nothing of Gutenberg's own role, the working conditions in the shop, or the reaction of scribes.

  2. Who created this source, and what position and interests did he bring to it?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the production and sale of the Mainz Bible?
  4. What can historians not establish from this letter alone, and what other evidence, such as legal records and surviving copies, would be needed?

Why did this particular combination prove reproducible? Several factors worked together. The alphabet required only a limited set of characters, though printers needed many variants and abbreviations to imitate scribal practice. Paper was increasingly available. The financial structure of the enterprise, through loans and partnerships, attracted merchants who understood the possibility of selling many identical goods. The technique could be taught and carried by journeymen, and Mainz's political troubles scattered them. Finally, the product answered an existing market among clergy, universities, and lay readers. Printing was therefore the outcome of metallurgy, mechanics, chemistry, commerce, and demand, and not simply of individual genius.

Section 55.11

The Spread of Printing across Europe

Printing spread along existing commercial, ecclesiastical, and university routes. In the 1460s German printers took the craft to Strasbourg, Cologne, and Bamberg, and to Italy, where the clerics Conrad Sweynheym and Arnold Pannartz set up a press at the Benedictine monastery of Subiaco in 1465 and moved to Rome in 1467. Venice, which already had a large capital base, a skilled labor force, a flourishing paper trade, and access to the markets of the Mediterranean and the German lands through the Alpine passes, soon became the leading printing center. In 1469 Johannes de Spira, a German printer, received a monopoly privilege from the Venetian authorities, and Nicolas Jenson, a Frenchman trained in the craft, established a press in 1470 and developed a graceful roman type. By the 1490s Aldus Manutius was producing carefully edited Greek and Latin texts and later introduced smaller formats and italic type, which are discussed further in Chapter 57. In 1470 the Sorbonne in Paris hosted a press, and printing expanded in Lyon in the 1470s, in the Low Countries at Utrecht, Bruges, and Antwerp, in Buda, where the Chronicle of the Hungarians appeared in 1473, in Kraków, and in Valencia and Barcelona on the Iberian coast.

England's first press followed an unusual path. William Caxton, a London merchant who had lived for years in Bruges, learned the craft in Cologne, printed in Bruges in the early 1470s, including an English translation of a romance about Troy, and in 1476 set up a press at Westminster. He published works in English, many of them translations and chivalric and devotional texts, and his choices of dialect and spelling influenced the development of written English, though a historian should not exaggerate his role in "standardizing" the language. Elsewhere printing took distinct forms. Hebrew printing began in Italy in the 1470s, including the printing of Rashi's commentary at Reggio di Calabria in 1475. Jewish printers continued to work in Iberia, with a Hebrew Pentateuch printed at Faro in Portugal in 1487, and after the expulsions of 1492 exiles carried the craft to Constantinople, where David and Samuel ibn Nahmias established one of the earliest Hebrew presses, commonly dated to 1493 on the evidence of the colophon of their edition of Jacob ben Asher's Turim, although the dating has been debated in the bibliography. Church Slavonic printing in Cyrillic began in Kraków in 1491 with the work of Schweipolt Fiol, and in Cetinje, in the Montenegrin lands, in the 1490s under Orthodox sponsorship. A missal in the Croatian recension of Church Slavonic, printed in Glagolitic script, is securely dated to 1483, though its place of printing is uncertain.

The pattern of spread suggests several conclusions. Printing flourished where there were merchants with capital, markets with literate customers, supplies of paper, and political conditions that allowed the trade to operate: university towns, episcopal seats, commercial cities, and ports. By 1500 presses had operated in well over two hundred towns, with Venice, Paris, Lyon, Cologne, Nuremberg, Augsburg, and Basel among the leaders. Nuremberg's Anton Koberger ran a large enterprise with branches and agents who sold across Europe, and he published the richly illustrated Nuremberg Chronicle in 1493. Book fairs, especially at Frankfurt and Lyon, supported distribution. Printers also depended on a hierarchy of local and international markets, so that the effective unit of the book trade was often a network of towns and not a nation.

The expansion was not smooth. Many printers went bankrupt, as the market for printed books became saturated in the 1470s and 1480s with large editions of Latin works that sold slowly. Printers shifted to different products, including indulgences, almanacs, schoolbooks, devotional works, and vernacular literature. Others moved from town to town. Printing also did not spread uniformly across the continent's religious and linguistic areas. In the Ottoman lands, Muslim authorities and scribal communities did not adopt Arabic script printing until the early eighteenth century, when Ibrahim Müteferrika established a press in Istanbul in 1727. Historians have long debated why. A widely repeated story that Sultan Bayezid II banned printing in 1485 is not supported by reliable evidence according to many scholars, and more recent studies emphasize factors such as the prestige and economic interests of scribes and calligraphers, the structure of the book market, the different technical demands of connected Arabic script, and the availability of other means of distributing texts. In any case, Jewish, and later Armenian and Greek, printers in Ottoman cities did print in their own scripts.

Estimates of the total output of the first fifty years of printing vary widely. The Incunabula Short Title Catalogue records tens of thousands of surviving editions printed before 1501, and scholars estimate that somewhere between roughly eight and twenty million copies may have been produced, but those figures depend on assumptions about average print runs, which were commonly a few hundred copies, and on the survival of evidence. A clear majority of editions, often estimated at around three quarters, were in Latin, with the remainder in vernacular languages such as German, Italian, French, Dutch, English, and Spanish. These numbers should be treated as informed approximations rather than exact counts.

Historical Vocabulary
Incunabula

Incunabula (from the Latin for "swaddling clothes" or "cradle") is the name given to books printed in Europe before 1501. The convention is a modern bibliographical one, with the year 1500 chosen as a convenient end point, and it can suggest a sharper chronological boundary than existed. Early printed books often imitated manuscripts, with abbreviations, hand rubrication, and illumination, and some printers stopped imitating them only gradually. Incunabula matter because they are both a body of texts and a body of physical evidence: their typefaces, paper, bindings, ownership marks, and handwritten annotations reveal who produced and read them, and how.

Section 55.12

Books, Readers, and the Expansion of Literacy

Who read the new books? The first answer is that the principal readers were those already literate: clergy, university teachers and students, lawyers, physicians, notaries, officials, and merchants. A large proportion of early printed works consisted of Bibles, liturgical books, sermons, theology, canon and Roman law, university textbooks, grammars, and classical authors. Printing made these texts cheaper and more uniform, which benefited institutions that depended on them, such as parish clergy, schools, and chanceries. Printed books remained expensive for most people, because paper was often the largest single cost of production and because wages were low, but a printed volume was typically considerably cheaper than a comparable manuscript, which widened ownership.

The second answer is that print also reached beyond the learned world. Vernacular works, including devotional manuals, books of hours, chivalric romances, travel narratives, calendars and almanacs, practical handbooks, and satirical works, found readers among merchants, craftsmen, and townspeople. Sebastian Brant's Ship of Fools, printed in Basel in 1494 in German with woodcuts, was widely read and translated, and illustrated works made content accessible to those who read little. Single sheets, such as indulgences, broadsides, and prints of saints, circulated more widely than books. A text could also reach listeners as well as readers, since reading aloud in households, workshops, taverns, and religious communities remained common. The boundary between oral and written culture was porous.

Literacy itself is difficult to measure. Historians use indirect indicators such as the ability to sign one's name, the presence of books in wills and inventories, school records, and the volume of correspondence. These indicators measure different skills: reading, writing, and signing were not identical, and many people could read printed or familiar texts without being able to write. For these reasons no reliable continental figure exists for 1500. Modern estimates suggest very low rates in rural areas and among women, higher rates among urban men, and the highest rates among clergy and in certain commercial regions such as northern Italy, the Low Countries, and parts of southern Germany, with overall literacy in many regions perhaps in the range of one in ten adults or lower. These figures are uncertain and should not be applied uniformly. Education was provided by cathedral and monastic schools, municipal and guild schools, the schools of the Brethren of the Common Life, Italian abacus schools teaching commercial arithmetic, and private tutors. Girls were usually excluded from universities, but noblewomen, urban women, and nuns often read devotional literature, owned books of hours, and sometimes taught children to read.

Literacy also differed by language community. Jewish communities placed a high value on Hebrew literacy among men and, to a smaller extent, among women, and Hebrew printing grew rapidly as discussed above. Orthodox clergy and monastic communities read Greek and Church Slavonic, and Church Slavonic books appeared in print in Kraków and Cetinje in the 1490s. Muslim readers in the Ottoman lands used Arabic, Persian, and Ottoman Turkish manuscripts, and Muslims in Iberia sometimes wrote Spanish in Arabic script, a practice called aljamiado. The Romani communities who were appearing in the written records of western and central Europe from the early fifteenth century, in places such as the German lands, France, and Iberia, transmitted their language and culture largely orally, and are visible to historians mainly through the records of the authorities who regulated or expelled them. For example, the imperial diet at Freiburg in 1498 declared Roma to be spies of the Ottomans, treating a migrating minority as a security threat. Literacy, in short, was spread over many languages and scripts, and Latin was only one of them.

Women's participation in the book trade deserves notice. Women appear as readers, owners, and patrons, and as workers in the print shops, where records show wives and widows of printers continuing workshops, and, in Florence, the nuns of the convent of San Jacopo di Ripoli, whose press operated between 1476 and 1484, apparently took part in typesetting. These examples show that print workshops were family and community enterprises, though evidence for women's labor is scattered and often incidental.

Everyday Life and Social History

Soldiers, Printers, and Readers: Ordinary People and the New Machinery

The innovations of the period reshaped ordinary working lives in different ways. For soldiers, the age of gunpowder meant new occupations, such as gunner, powder maker, and gunfounder, and new risks. Mercenary companies, such as Swiss and Landsknecht units, were close knit communities with their own customs and discipline, accompanied by women and servants who cooked, nursed, and scavenged. Town councils hired gunners as municipal officials, and villages near saltpeter workings contended with the digging and carting. Civilians in the paths of armies faced billeting, requisition, and the threat of plunder, and besieged towns saw their food stores dwindle, their buildings damaged, and their communities divided about whether to surrender.

In print shops, the work was organized in teams. Compositors selected type from cases and assembled lines, pressmen inked and pulled sheets, correctors read proofs, and apprentices learned the craft over years of service. A press team, according to later evidence, might produce a few hundred impressions in an hour, and workers could be paid by the sheet, by the week, or through shares. Shops were often cramped and noisy, with the smell of ink and damp paper, and journeymen moved from city to city, forming a mobile and sometimes unruly labor force. Paper mills, where rags were macerated in water-powered stamping mills, supplied the trade, and rag collectors, mill workers, and carriers were all tied into the economy of books. Readers, meanwhile, bought cheap woodcuts and almanacs at fairs, borrowed or inherited books, and annotated them, and the survival of handwritten notes in margins allows historians to see how individuals used their books in study, devotion, and commerce.

Section 55.13

Print, Authority, Religion, and Public Controversy

Reproduction changed how texts circulated, and it changed the relationship between texts and authority. First, print allowed more identical copies, which could support standardization of liturgical books, legal texts, school grammars, and calendars. Governments, churches, and universities used it for this purpose, printing missals and breviaries, statutes, proclamations, and tax notices. Printing in vernacular languages contributed to the written use of languages such as German, French, English, and Italian, though standardization was slow, and dialect and spelling variation persisted for generations. The claim that print created fixed texts has been questioned, because early printed books contained errors, were corrected during production, and varied from copy to copy, and because different printers issued competing editions of the same work.

Second, print enabled rapid dissemination of news, appeals, and polemic. One of the earliest products of Gutenberg's press was probably printed indulgence forms, issued in 1454 and 1455 in connection with a campaign to raise money for the defense of Cyprus against the Ottomans, and a printed appeal, sometimes called the Türkenkalender, urged Christendom to resist the Turks. Pamphlets about the Ottoman threat circulated in the German lands in the following decades, as did accounts of the preaching of the Dominican Girolamo Savonarola in Florence, executed in 1498, whose sermons were printed in cheap formats. Print could also spread fear and hatred. After the accusation in 1475 that Jews in Trent had murdered a Christian child, Simon, a charge that was false and led to executions, printed pamphlets and images spread the story across the German lands and Italy, and the cult of "Blessed Simon" persisted for centuries. Heinrich Kramer's Malleus Maleficarum of 1486, a treatise on witchcraft, was reprinted several times, though historians caution against attributing the later witch hunts to print alone.

Third, authorities sought to control the new medium. In 1486 Archbishop Berthold of Henneberg of Mainz issued an edict requiring prior censorship of printed books, in particular translations of religious works into German, reflecting concern about uncontrolled vernacular religious reading. In 1501 Pope Alexander VI issued a bull directing ecclesiastical authorities in several German provinces to supervise printing and prevent the publication of erroneous or scandalous books. Rulers granted privileges that protected printers from competition and gave authorities leverage over what was printed, as Venice did from 1469. These measures were scattered and difficult to enforce because books crossed borders easily, but they show that church and secular authorities recognized printing as a new political and religious factor.

Fourth, print interacted with the concerns of the late medieval church discussed in Chapters 53 and 61. Devotional books, Bibles, and vernacular guides to pious living were popular, and church reformers and critics alike used printed texts. Humanist scholarship, examined in Chapter 57 and Chapter 59, depended on printed editions of classical and biblical texts. The consequences for religious conflict became dramatic after 1517, as Chapter 62 explains. For the period of this chapter, the important point is that the conditions for rapid, large scale circulation of religious argument had been created, but the outcome was not predetermined. Printing equally served orthodox purposes, supporting the church's own educational and devotional programs, and in the decades around 1500 most printed religious books were conventional in content.

Multiple Perspectives

The Arrival of Print: Perspectives from Different Communities

From the perspective of a monastic or professional scribe: The abbot Johannes Trithemius wrote in 1492 in praise of scribes, arguing that copying by hand disciplined the spirit and that parchment lasted longer than paper, even though he had his own work printed. Scribes with commissions for luxury books and devotional texts might retain work, but those who copied university texts or routine documents faced competition from cheaper printed copies.
From the perspective of a printer-publisher and investor: Printing offered a chance to sell many copies of a text, but it required large outlays for type, presses, paper, and wages before any revenue came in. Success depended on choosing titles that would sell, finding distribution networks, and surviving competition. Many failed, and the skill of printers like Jenson, Koberger, and Manutius lay as much in business as in craft.
From the perspective of a university teacher, lawyer, or church official: Printed texts reduced costs, supplied identical copies to students and officials, and enabled new kinds of scholarship. They also raised the danger of errors multiplied across hundreds of copies and of unauthorized or heretical writings escaping oversight, which prompted censorship and the search for reliable editors.
From the perspective of an urban craftsman, merchant, or woman reader of limited means: Cheap almanacs, devotional prints, vernacular books, and practical manuals brought some reading within reach, though most people still encountered texts through listening or images. For many, the most familiar printed item was probably a single sheet such as an indulgence or a woodcut.
From the perspective of Jewish exiles and minority communities: Hebrew printing allowed communities to preserve and distribute their own texts, and exiles from Iberia carried the craft to Italy and Constantinople. Yet the same medium circulated accusations like the Trent ritual murder story, and Jewish communities faced both new opportunities and new vulnerabilities.
Section 55.14

Knowledge, Maps, Mathematics, and Technical Information

The new capacities of war, government, and navigation rested on practical and theoretical knowledge, and print altered how that knowledge circulated. Geography offers a clear example. Mediterranean seafarers used portolan charts, practical sailing charts that showed coastlines, ports, and compass bearings, with networks of lines radiating from compass roses, from at least the late thirteenth century. These charts were made by specialist cartographers in Genoa, Venice, Majorca, and Catalonia and were based on the experience of pilots and on written sailing directions. Alongside them existed learned world maps, often produced in monastic and scholarly settings, which blended biblical, classical, and travelers' information. The most elaborate of these, the large world map of the Venetian monk Fra Mauro completed around 1450, incorporated reports from Portuguese, Arab, Ethiopian, and other informants.

The Latin translation of Ptolemy's Geography, produced in Florence about 1406 from a Greek text brought from Byzantium, introduced a mathematical method of locating places by coordinates, a method examined further as part of the humanist recovery of Greek learning in Chapter 57. Printed editions with maps appeared at Bologna in 1477 and Ulm in 1482, the latter with woodcut maps, and many more followed. Ptolemy gave European scholars a systematic framework, but his picture of the world had serious errors, including an underestimate of the size of the earth that encouraged optimistic estimates of the distance westward to Asia. Martin Behaim, a Nuremberg merchant who had spent time in Portugal, constructed a terrestrial globe in 1492 that shows the state of European knowledge on the eve of Columbus's voyage, and it is as notable for what it left out, including the Americas, as for what it contained.

Mathematics and calculation also developed in response to practical need. Merchants in Italian, Flemish, and German cities learned commercial arithmetic in schools using Hindu-Arabic numerals, which had spread from the thirteenth century and had replaced Roman numerals in most commercial work by the fifteenth. The Treviso Arithmetic of 1478 was the first printed arithmetic book, and in 1494 the Franciscan friar Luca Pacioli published in Venice a large compendium of arithmetic, algebra, and geometry that included a description of the double entry bookkeeping system used by Italian merchants. Astronomy and calendar making were similarly practical. Johannes Regiomontanus established a printing press in Nuremberg and published astronomical tables, and his Ephemerides were used for navigation. The Sephardic astronomer Abraham Zacuto, who left Castile after 1492 and served the Portuguese crown, compiled tables for determining latitude from the sun that were adopted by Portuguese navigators and printed in the 1490s. These examples show the circulation of knowledge among Jewish, Christian, and Muslim scholars through translation, exile, and trade.

A note on dating is necessary here. Europeans used the Julian calendar throughout this period, and by 1500 it had drifted by about nine days from the solar year because its year was slightly too long, a discrepancy that astronomers recognized and that was corrected only by the Gregorian reform of 1582, itself adopted at different times in different states. The beginning of the year also varied from place to place, with some regions starting the year on 25 March or at Easter, so that "1494" could mean different spans in different jurisdictions. This textbook uses modern dating, with the year starting on 1 January, and it notes variations only where they affect understanding.

Technical information also moved increasingly into print. Roberto Valturio's De re militari, printed at Verona in 1472 with woodcut illustrations of siege engines and war machines, was among the earliest printed technical treatises, though the machines it depicted were often fanciful. Practical manuals on gunnery, fireworks, mining, metallurgy, medicine, and herbal remedies circulated in both manuscript and print, and printed herbals such as the German Gart der Gesundheit of 1485 combined text and images. Engineers like Francesco di Giorgio Martini and Mariano Taccola produced illustrated manuscripts on machines and fortifications. Print made it easier for craftsmen and engineers to share ideas, but the relationship was not simple. Guilds guarded trade secrets, many of the most important skills were learned through apprenticeship rather than reading, and manuscript copies of technical works continued to circulate alongside printed ones. The result was an increasingly integrated culture of written and practical knowledge, including works that in later centuries contributed to the development examined in Chapter 81.

Section 55.15

Ships, Navigation, and the Expansion of Maritime Horizons

European voyages along the African coast and across the Atlantic depended on ships and skills that had developed over centuries in the Mediterranean, the Atlantic, and the North and Baltic Seas. This section describes the enabling conditions, and Chapter 68 treats the voyages themselves. The medieval cog, a broad, high sided sailing ship with a single square sail and a stern rudder, carried bulk goods in the North Sea and Baltic, as Chapter 54 noted in connection with the Hanseatic towns. Mediterranean traders used galleys, powered by oars and used especially for high value cargo and warfare, and sailing vessels with lateen sails, whose triangular rigs allowed them to sail closer to the wind. During the fifteenth century shipbuilders on the Atlantic coast of Iberia and elsewhere combined features of these traditions. The carrack, a large, roomy ship with several masts and a combination of square and lateen sails, could carry cargo and withstand ocean conditions. The caravel, a smaller, maneuverable vessel with lateen sails, became associated with Portuguese exploration because it could sail effectively against the wind along the African coast and into shallow waters. Heavy shipboard artillery expanded rapidly in the late fifteenth century, and watertight lower-hull gunports emerged gradually around the turn of the sixteenth century. The old attribution of their invention to a Breton shipwright in 1501 is traditional rather than securely documented.

Navigation combined older practices with new ones. The magnetic compass, known in Europe from the twelfth or thirteenth century, allowed pilots to maintain a heading when clouds obscured the sun and stars. Mediterranean pilots relied on experience, sailing directions, and portolan charts to move from point to point, a method known as dead reckoning in which speed, time, and direction are estimated. On the open Atlantic along the African coast, pilots needed to know their north-south position, and the Portuguese adapted instruments such as the quadrant and the mariner's astrolabe, derived from astronomical instruments developed in the Islamic world and Iberia, to measure the height of the Pole Star or, further south, the sun. Declination tables like those of Zacuto and Regiomontanus allowed latitude to be calculated. Longitude, by contrast, could not be determined reliably at sea until the eighteenth century, which produced significant errors in estimating east-west distances.

Knowledge of winds and currents mattered as much as hardware. Sailing south along the northwest African coast was relatively easy, because the prevailing northeast winds blew toward the equator, but returning north against those winds was difficult. Portuguese pilots in the fifteenth century learned to sail out into the Atlantic in a wide arc, using the westerlies of the mid-Atlantic to return to Iberia, a technique often called the volta do mar. Similar knowledge of the Atlantic's wind systems allowed Columbus and his successors to sail west on the trade winds and return on the westerlies. Learning these systems depended on the accumulated experience of fishermen, merchants, and sailors, many of them from Atlantic ports like Lisbon, Lagos, Seville, Bristol, and Bayonne, and on the Basque, Breton, and Galician seafaring traditions. Genoese and other Italian merchants, who had worked the Mediterranean trade and the sugar trade, brought capital, commercial networks, and sailing expertise to Iberian enterprises.

Maritime work was dangerous and socially diverse. Crews were often mixed, including Iberians, Italians, Basques, and others, and the enslaved were put to labor on vessels and in ports in the Mediterranean as well as the Atlantic. Shipwrecks and disease were common, and sailors lived in cramped, wet conditions on a diet of biscuit, salted meat, and dried fish. Shipbuilding relied on large amounts of timber, naval stores such as resin and pitch, iron fittings, and canvas, supplied from the Baltic, Iberia, and elsewhere, which links this section to the resource geography of Chapter 2. The expansion of maritime horizons was therefore a social and economic enterprise, dependent on shipyards, investors, pilots, mapmakers, and laborers, and not only on the vision of famous captains.

Section 55.16

Atlantic Islands, African Routes, and an Expanding Geographic Horizon

By about 1500 the maritime capacities described in Sections 55.14 and 55.15 had enabled a rapidly widening geographic horizon. This section establishes that transition with a few representative milestones. The detailed narrative of Iberian expansion belongs to Chapter 68, the conquest of the Americas to Chapter 69, Atlantic slavery to Chapter 70, and the Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires to Chapter 71.

Atlantic islands were the first testing ground. Portuguese and Castilian expeditions settled or conquered the Madeira, Azores, and Canary archipelagos during the fifteenth century, the last at the expense of the Guanche peoples. On Madeira, sugar cultivation required capital, irrigation, and labor, and the labor force increasingly included enslaved people. This experiment, building on earlier sugar plantations in the Mediterranean, helped create institutions later transferred across the Atlantic, as Chapter 70 examines. Slavery itself was not new to Europe, since Mediterranean and Black Sea slave trades, partly controlled by Italian merchants, had long supplied enslaved people to Italy, Iberia, and the eastern Mediterranean.

Portuguese captains meanwhile moved steadily down the African coast in search of gold, ivory, and enslaved people. Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa in 1488, and Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in India in 1498. In 1492, Christopher Columbus, a Genoese mariner in Castilian service, reached islands in the Caribbean. These voyages depended on African political and commercial systems as well as European skill. Portuguese traders worked through local rulers, intermediaries, and existing networks that carried West African gold across the Sahara and along the coast, and African states negotiated contact on their own terms. The motives of the voyagers were mixed, combining the search for gold and spices, rivalry with Muslim powers, crusading ideals, royal ambition, and merchant enterprise.

Two cautions apply. First, the idea that the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453 "closed" the trade routes to Asia and thereby caused exploration is an oversimplification. Trade between Asia and Europe continued through Egypt, Syria, and the Black Sea, and Venetian merchants remained prosperous into the sixteenth century. Second, the effects of these voyages on most European life were small in 1500 compared with their later consequences. Most Europeans knew little of the Americas or the Indian Ocean, and overseas profits did not yet transform European economies. Chapter 73 examines how overseas wealth, commodities, and slavery later changed European societies. For the period around 1500 the essential point is that Europeans had acquired new maritime capabilities and a wider horizon whose long-term impact was still unforeseen.

Section 55.17

What Changed, and What Remained Medieval?

The preceding sections describe substantial innovations. This section asks what they changed and what they left intact, and it links the chapter's evidence to a continuing scholarly argument about the nature of the period. Gunpowder weapons coexisted with swords, lances, bows, and pikes. Siege artillery transformed some campaigns and prompted new fortification design, but castles remained useful as residences and administrative strongholds for centuries, and the labor of building and maintaining fortifications remained largely traditional until the sixteenth century. The armies themselves relied on a combination of paid professionals, noble retinues, urban militias, and feudal obligations. Armor, cavalry, and chivalric values persisted and continued to be defended by nobles and rulers.

Printing coexisted with manuscript production, oral storytelling, preaching, images, and public ritual. Many people encountered texts by hearing them, and sermons, songs, and plays remained primary vehicles for religious and political messages. Most printed books were in Latin, the language of the learned, and the vernacular printed book did not displace the Latin world of the church, universities, and chanceries until later. The large proportion of the population who were illiterate depended on others to read documents for them, a reality that shaped relations among clergy, notaries, lawyers, and ordinary people.

Stronger monarchies remained tied to estates, privileges, local elites, and the church. Rulers still governed through negotiation with noble families, urban oligarchies, and ecclesiastical institutions. Dynastic marriage and inheritance continued to shape the map, as the Habsburg and Spanish inheritance of Charles V, born in 1500, would show. Law remained diverse, rooted in custom and local jurisdiction. Religious life remained dominated by the sacraments, saints, pilgrimages, and confraternities, and the church retained enormous economic and moral authority. Exploration and expansion built on Mediterranean crusading traditions, Genoese banking, and Iberian rivalry with Muslim powers. The mental world of most Europeans remained organized by Christian cosmology, belief in providence, astrology, and local custom. Chapter 49 describes the patterns of everyday life that continued, in many places, with little interruption.

Historical Debate

Revolution or Gradual Change? Gunpowder, Print, and the "Early Modern" Transformation

The question: Did the military and communication changes of the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries amount to "revolutions" that transformed European society and made the early modern period distinct from the Middle Ages, or were they cumulative, uneven developments whose significance was shaped by other factors?

Interpretation A: The changes were transformative and relatively rapid. Michael Roberts, in a lecture of 1955, proposed a "military revolution" in the period 1560 to 1660, and Geoffrey Parker, in a study published in 1988, extended and revised the thesis by arguing that artillery and the new fortifications of the trace italienne forced states to field larger armies and develop more powerful fiscal and administrative systems, helping to explain the growth of the European state and its global power. For printing, Elizabeth Eisenstein, in a work of 1979, argued that the printing press was an agent of change that produced new standards of preservation, standardization, and dissemination, contributing to the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution. In these views the decades around 1500 were the starting point of fundamental changes.
Interpretation B: The changes were gradual, uneven, and dependent on context. Clifford Rogers has argued that significant military changes occurred earlier, in the era of the Hundred Years' War, with the rise of infantry, while Jeremy Black and others have argued that the idea of a single military revolution overstates the pace of change and neglects social, political, and cultural factors, as well as the importance of non-European warfare. Kelly DeVries and others have shown the long history of medieval gunpowder weapons. For print, Adrian Johns, in a study of 1998, questioned the claim that printed texts were inherently fixed and reliable, showing that trust in books depended on social practices and that piracy and error were common, and Andrew Pettegree and others have emphasized the commercial risks of early printing and the continued importance of manuscripts and oral communication. On this view, change was real but slow, shaped by the circumstances in which technologies were adopted.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on surviving guns, fortifications, treasury accounts, muster rolls, and campaign narratives, and on printed books, printers' records, probate inventories, and the physical evidence of annotations and bindings. They differ in the weight given to technology versus social context, in the periods emphasized, and in the extent to which they regard Europe's experience as distinctive. Some scholars favor an intermediate view, emphasizing "punctuated" change, with periods of rapid innovation followed by adaptation.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether historians explain the rise of the European state and European global power mainly by technological and military factors, and whether they treat printing as the cause of later intellectual and religious change or as a medium whose effects depended on prior conditions. It also affects how periodization labels are used.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence, such as comparative data on literacy or on state revenues, would help evaluate them. Then state which combination of factors you find most persuasive and why.

The debate suggests how to read this chapter's evidence. Its sections show innovations that were genuinely significant for those who experienced them, such as the inhabitants of a town facing French artillery in 1495, the readers who could purchase an inexpensive printed text, or the sailors whose knowledge of the Atlantic winds opened new routes. They also show the limits of those innovations, which depended on cost, politics, geography, and social habit. The most defensible conclusion is that the decades around 1500 were a period in which several slow transformations reached a threshold of visibility, and it was their combination, rather than any single invention, that gave the era its character.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Europe around 1500: What Changed, What Continued, and for Whom

What Changed

  • Siege artillery became mobile and effective enough to breach many older fortifications quickly, and engineers began designing new defenses
  • Rulers raised larger revenues, borrowed more, and fielded more paid soldiers, building administrative structures that supported these efforts
  • Printing, established in hundreds of towns, multiplied texts, reduced their cost, and created a commercial book trade and new forms of authorship, publishing, and censorship
  • Navigational knowledge, maps, and ship design allowed European mariners to reach the Atlantic islands, the African coast, the Americas, and the Indian Ocean
  • Religious boundaries hardened in parts of Iberia, with the conquest of Granada and the expulsion or forced conversion of Jews and Muslims

What Stayed the Same

  • Agrarian life, village custom, lordship, and the household economy continued to structure the lives of most Europeans
  • Cavalry, bows, castles, city walls, and chivalric culture remained important in war and politics
  • Manuscripts, preaching, images, and oral communication continued alongside print, and most people could not read
  • Political authority remained shared among monarchs, nobles, towns, estates, and the church, and dynastic inheritance continued to reshape political boundaries
  • Christianity, in Latin and Orthodox forms, remained the principal framework of belief and institutional life, with Islam and Judaism present as minority and, in the Ottoman lands, majority and minority religious communities

Who experienced change most directly? Townspeople in the path of artillery campaigns, taxpayers in states building new fiscal systems, printers and booksellers, merchants and bankers, university and legal professionals, seafarers and Atlantic islanders, and the Jewish and Muslim communities of Iberia, who faced the most abrupt consequences of consolidating power.

Who experienced greater continuity? Most villagers, many women whose work was household and agricultural, and communities beyond the reach of presses and artillery trains, for whom the rhythms of the farming year, the church calendar, and local obligation dominated daily life.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both forms of experience prevents two opposite errors: treating 1500 as a revolutionary break, and ignoring the real capacities that new technologies and institutions created.

Section 55.18

Europe around 1500: An Uneven Transition

The preceding sections have followed four interlocking transformations. In war, gunpowder artillery, new infantry formations, and costly armies altered the conduct of sieges and campaigns. In government, rulers responded to the costs of war with new taxes, offices, courts, and diplomacy, although the results ranged from consolidated monarchies to composite states, imperial confederations, republics, and the Ottoman system. In communication, printing multiplied texts through an expanding network of presses and commercial towns and increased the possibilities of both learning and control. In navigation, shipbuilding, instruments, and maps allowed seafarers to extend the reach of European commerce and power into the Atlantic and beyond. The four processes were connected through shared institutions, including cities, courts, merchants, and banks, and through the movement of skilled people and capital, but they were not parts of a single plan.

No single innovation "created modern Europe." The idea would have puzzled people of the time, who generally understood their world in terms of Christendom, dynastic loyalty, local community, and tradition. They did not call themselves medieval or early modern. Change occurred at different rates in different regions. Northern Italy, the Low Countries, southern Germany, and the Atlantic ports of Iberia were centers of commercial, financial, and technical innovation. France and England consolidated monarchical power, while the Empire, the Swiss Confederation, and Italian republics maintained other political forms. In the east, Muscovy, Hungary, Poland-Lithuania, and the Ottoman Empire developed their own combinations of military organization and fiscal and administrative practice. Different social groups likewise experienced the period differently, and the benefits of new capacities were unequally distributed. Taxpayers, soldiers, civilians in sieges, enslaved people, and religious minorities frequently bore costs that the narratives of progress omit.

For the following chapters, the significance of Chapter 55 lies in the capacities it describes. The Italian city-states of Chapter 56 and the humanist scholars of Chapter 57 would use printing to circulate classical texts, as Chapter 60 examines in discussing printing and the Renaissance legacy. The religious crises treated beginning with Chapter 61 and Chapter 62 would take place in a world of printed pamphlets, state authorities with greater administrative reach, and armies equipped with artillery. The Portuguese and Spanish voyages of Chapter 68 would build on the maritime knowledge, ship designs, and financial networks that this chapter identifies. The fiscal-military pressures sketched here are developed in Chapter 74, and the Habsburg, French, and other dynastic struggles in Chapters 75 and 76. Chapter 55 therefore closes Part VIII by showing that the late medieval crisis was also a period of invention and adaptation, and it prepares the transition into Part IX without presupposing that the later outcomes were inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the military, political, informational, and maritime capacities that Europe possessed around 1500, and it has argued that they represent an uneven transformation, not a sudden break between a "medieval" and a "modern" world. As the capstone of Part VIII, it showed that the crises of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries were accompanied by invention, adaptation, and institutional growth, and it avoided presenting the outcomes of later chapters as predetermined.

Gunpowder weapons, known in western Europe by the 1320s, took more than a century to alter warfare decisively. Early guns were expensive and unreliable, and their adoption depended on improvements such as corned powder, bronze casting, iron shot, and wheeled carriages, on the availability of saltpeter, sulfur, charcoal, and metal, and on political and financial conditions. By the mid fifteenth century, heavy artillery contributed to the French victories at the end of the Hundred Years' War and to the Ottoman capture of Constantinople, and in 1494 the French artillery train that invaded Italy demonstrated the speed with which fortifications could be breached. Yet castles and walls retained roles, engineers began developing new defenses, and the full trace italienne belongs largely to the sixteenth century.

On the battlefield, handguns and artillery coexisted with longbows, crossbows, cavalry, and pikes. The Swiss and German infantry formations, the Hussite wagon forts, the Ottoman combination of Janissaries, provincial cavalry, and artillery, and the Spanish experiments in Granada show that tactical change came from many sources. Armored cavalry remained prestigious and effective in many contexts. Armies increasingly consisted of paid professionals and mercenaries, and rulers experimented with standing forces in France, Burgundy, Hungary, and Castile, producing financial and political challenges.

The rising cost of war pushed rulers to seek reliable revenue and administration. The French crown secured a permanent direct tax, the Castilian monarchs reorganized royal finance and the Santa Hermandad, the Tudors rebuilt royal revenue and curbed noble retinues, the Empire adopted a set of reforms at Worms in 1495, and the Ottoman state relied on a powerful central administration. These developments depended on lawyers, financiers, and bankers such as the Fuggers, and they strengthened rulers without making them absolute. The political map included not only monarchies but composite monarchies, an elective empire, a confederation of Swiss communities, urban republics, the Papal States, ecclesiastical principalities, the Hanseatic network, Poland-Lithuania, Muscovy, and the Ottoman Empire. Religious policy was part of consolidation in Iberia, where the conquest of Granada was followed by the expulsion or forced conversion of Jews and Muslims.

Printing from movable metal type, developed in Mainz in the 1450s by Johann Gutenberg and his associates, combined existing techniques of metalworking, press mechanics, oil based ink, and paper into a system that could produce many identical books. It spread along commercial, university, and ecclesiastical routes to hundreds of towns, with Venice, Paris, Lyon, Cologne, Nuremberg, and Augsburg among the leaders, and it was adapted to Hebrew, Church Slavonic, and Glagolitic scripts. The first fifty years of printing produced tens of thousands of editions, though estimates of copies vary widely, and many printers failed financially.

The new books reached mainly those already literate, including clergy, university men, lawyers, officials, and merchants, but they also supplied vernacular readers and, through woodcuts and single sheets, a broader public. Literacy remained limited and uneven, and evidence for it is indirect. Print enabled standardization, rapid dissemination of news and polemic, and wider access to texts, but it also multiplied errors, carried hostile accusations such as the Trent ritual murder story, and prompted efforts at censorship by church and secular authorities. Its religious consequences after 1517 were possible, not predetermined.

Knowledge of geography, mathematics, astronomy, and technique circulated in manuscript and print. Portolan charts, Ptolemy's coordinates, commercial arithmetic, bookkeeping, astronomical tables, and technical manuals formed a body of practical and theoretical knowledge, to which Jewish, Muslim, and Christian scholars contributed. Ships such as the carrack and caravel, instruments such as the compass, quadrant, and astrolabe, and knowledge of Atlantic winds and currents, accumulated by fishermen, merchants, and pilots, enabled voyages to the Atlantic islands, the African coast, the Americas, and India. These voyages relied on Genoese capital, African partners and markets, and Iberian crusading and commercial motives, and their consequences, including the growth of Atlantic slavery, lay mostly in the future.

The chapter's historiographical discussion showed that scholars disagree about whether these changes constituted revolutions. Interpretations associated with Roberts, Parker, and Eisenstein emphasize transformative effects, while those associated with Rogers, Black, DeVries, Johns, and Pettegree emphasize earlier roots, gradual change, and the importance of social context. The most defensible conclusion is that several long processes reached visibility around 1500 and reinforced one another, while institutions, beliefs, and social structures with medieval origins continued. The chapter's materials prepare the study of the Italian Renaissance in Chapter 56, the religious conflicts beginning with Chapter 61, the Atlantic expansion of Chapter 68, and the state building of Chapter 74.

Key Terms

Bombard
A large, short barreled siege gun, built of iron bars or cast bronze, that fired heavy stone or iron balls against walls.
Artillery train
The guns, carriages, powder, shot, animals, wagons, and personnel that together allowed an army to conduct sieges and bombardments.
Corned powder
Gunpowder formed into grains, which made it more stable, more powerful, and easier to load than fine powder dust.
Saltpeter
Potassium nitrate, the key ingredient of gunpowder, obtained in medieval Europe largely by processing manure and cellar earth.
Arquebus
An early shoulder fired handgun, usually with a matchlock mechanism, used by infantry from the later fifteenth century.
Matchlock
A firing mechanism in which a slow burning cord was lowered into the powder pan by a lever, allowing the shooter to keep both hands on the weapon.
Pike formation
A dense block of infantry armed with long pikes, trained to hold or advance together, made famous by the Swiss and imitated by German and other forces.
Landsknechte
German mercenary infantry organized under Habsburg sponsorship from the 1480s, modeled in part on the Swiss.
Compagnies d'ordonnance
Permanent companies of paid heavy cavalry established by Charles VII of France in the 1440s.
Trace italienne
A design of fortification with low, thick walls and angled bastions that allowed defenders to cover the base of walls with crossfire, developed in Italy from the late fifteenth century and mainly applied in the sixteenth.
Janissaries
The paid, slave-origin and later recruited infantry corps of the Ottoman sultans, increasingly equipped with firearms in the later fifteenth century.
Timar
An Ottoman revenue assignment granted to a provincial cavalryman in return for military service.
Fiscal-military state
A scholarly term for a state whose institutions, taxation, and credit were shaped by the demands of maintaining armed forces and conducting war.
Composite monarchy
A political arrangement in which a ruler governs several territories, each with its own laws and institutions, as with Castile and Aragon after 1479.
Taille
The principal direct tax of the French crown, which became a regular levy in the mid fifteenth century.
Estates
Assemblies of representatives of clergy, nobility, and towns that negotiated taxation and law with rulers in many European realms.
Imperial Chamber Court
The supreme court of the Holy Roman Empire, established at the Diet of Worms in 1495 to resolve disputes and uphold the Eternal Public Peace.
Alhambra Decree
The edict of March 1492 by which Ferdinand and Isabella ordered Jews who refused baptism to leave Castile and Aragon.
Conversos
Jews who converted to Christianity, and their descendants, many of whom faced suspicion and inquisitorial investigation in late medieval Iberia.
Movable type
Individually cast letters that can be arranged, printed, and reused, as developed in Europe by Gutenberg and associates in the mid fifteenth century.
Incunabula
Books printed in Europe before 1501, so named from the Latin for "cradle."
Colophon
A statement at the end of a book giving information such as the printer, place, and date of production.
Printing privilege
A grant by a ruler or city giving a printer exclusive rights to print a work or to operate a press for a period of time.
Vernacular
The everyday spoken language of a region, as opposed to Latin, the language of the church and the learned.
Portolan chart
A Mediterranean sailing chart showing coastlines and ports with networks of compass lines, drawn from pilots' experience.
Carrack
A large, multi-masted sailing ship combining square and lateen sails, used for oceanic and Mediterranean cargo and, increasingly, armed trade.
Caravel
A small, maneuverable ship with lateen sails, associated with Portuguese voyages along the African coast.
Volta do mar
The Portuguese technique of sailing out into the Atlantic to catch the westerlies for the return from West Africa to Iberia.
Julian calendar
The calendar used throughout Europe until reformed in 1582, which by 1500 had drifted about nine days from the solar year.
Early modern
A scholarly label for the centuries between the late Middle Ages and the age of revolutions, whose boundaries and meaning are debated.
Military revolution
A historiographical thesis, associated with Michael Roberts and Geoffrey Parker, that changes in weapons, tactics, and fortification drove the growth of European states.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 55 matters because it shows how the late Middle Ages ended without ending. The crises of plague, war, schism, and social conflict that fill Part VIII did not simply destroy the medieval order, and they did not produce a modern one by themselves. They pressed rulers, merchants, craftsmen, scholars, and sailors to devise solutions, and by about 1500 the results were visible in artillery trains, tax systems, printing presses, and ocean going ships. Understanding these capacities is essential for students who go on to study the Renaissance, the Reformation, the religious wars, and the creation of Atlantic empires, because those subjects cannot be explained without them.

The chapter also reveals how different parts of Europe were connected. The Rhine corridor carried books and merchants, the Danube carried armies and cannon, the Mediterranean carried Genoese and Venetian capital, the Atlantic carried Portuguese caravels, and Jewish exiles carried skills and printing presses from Iberia to Italy and the Ottoman Empire. The Ottoman state, Muscovy, Hungary, and Poland-Lithuania participated in the same military and fiscal pressures as France, Castile, and England. A history built only around western monarchies and Italian cities would miss this wider system, which is one reason this textbook follows the center-periphery rule of treating Europe as a many centered space.

The chapter contributes to the argument of Volume II by showing that Christendom, in its Latin and Orthodox forms, was never static, and that its institutions proved adaptable under pressure. At the same time it prepares the move to Volume III, in which print, artillery, and maritime expansion become central to the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the creation of states and empires. Several continuities survived well beyond 1500: the dependence of rulers on negotiated consent and credit, the tension between access to texts and efforts to control them, the exclusion and coercion of religious minorities in the name of unity, and the unequal distribution of costs and benefits among social groups.

Finally, the chapter invites students to practice a habit of mind central to historical study: to take innovation seriously without treating it as destiny.
