Empire, Consumption, and the Transformation of Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Sugar cut on a Caribbean plantation by enslaved laborers, refined in a European port, and stirred into a cup of tea in a middling household was one commodity chain, and each link changed the society that touched it.

Three settings, taken together, show the chapter's scope. On a Caribbean plantation, enslaved men, women, and children cut and processed sugar cane under violence and with high mortality, and the product was shipped in barrels across the Atlantic. In a European port, merchants sold it on commission, underwriters insured the cargo, refiners boiled and molded it, and customs officers counted it and collected duties. In a household, perhaps in London, Amsterdam, or Bordeaux, a woman stirred it into tea or used it to preserve fruit, with little reason to think about the labor behind it. These are generalized scenes, not records of particular people, but historians can document each stage from plantation accounts, port records, and household inventories.

Chapters 68 through 72 traced how Europeans explored, conquered, traded, settled, enslaved, and fought overseas. This chapter reverses the direction of inquiry. It asks what those activities changed inside Europe between roughly 1550 and 1800, with the greatest attention to the years after 1650.

The answer lies in mechanisms, not slogans. A commodity became cheaper and altered diets. A merchant house insured a cargo and extended credit. A government taxed imports and borrowed against the revenue. A botanist received a specimen from a local healer. A sailor from West Africa or South Asia settled in a European port. Each can be traced in customs ledgers, probate inventories, merchant letters, parliamentary statutes, and printed advertisements, and each affected different Europeans in different ways.

The chapter advances seven connected arguments. Overseas goods changed consumption. Particular ports, merchants, and financial institutions grew stronger. Slavery and coerced labor were bound to European wealth, though their contribution to industrialization remains debated. Empire created migration and new questions of belonging. Overseas encounters expanded science, often by appropriating non-European expertise. Imperial rivalry shaped taxation, borrowing, and state power. And all of these changes were geographically and socially unequal.

Two cautions govern the chapter. Europe is its organizing subject, but not its only agent: African producers, enslaved workers, Indian weavers, Chinese potters, Asian merchants, and Indigenous knowledge holders shaped the systems examined here. And imperial imports did not by themselves create a consumer society, since domestic agriculture, household labor, retail networks, and urban growth mattered as well. The chapter closes Part XI and leads to Chapter 74, on the early modern state.

The evidence for these changes is varied. Customs ledgers and the Sound Toll Registers show goods moving through ports, merchant letters show credit and risk, probate inventories show what households owned, and newspapers and trade cards show what was offered for sale. Parliamentary statutes, charters, and pamphlets record policy and argument, and the journals, collections, and correspondence of naturalists record knowledge. Each source has limits, and the voices of enslaved workers, Indian weavers, African intermediaries, and Indigenous guides appear mostly through the records of others.

The chapter belongs to Part XI, which has traced how Europeans reached, conquered, traded with, and settled overseas. Chapter 73 is its culminating analytical chapter, and it connects that expansion to the rest of Volume III, which turns in Part XII to the dynastic and fiscal states through which European power was organized. Its eight divisions move from global connections through commodities, finance, slavery and wealth, migration, knowledge, and politics to a separate chapter review.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the mechanisms by which overseas commerce changed European consumption.
  2. Distinguish the introduction of a commodity from its widespread adoption.
  3. Analyze how insurance, credit, banking, and public debt worked and why they mattered.
  4. Connect colonial slavery to European commerce while separating profit, employment, and industrial causation.
  5. Assess migration and mobile communities, including Black and Asian residents of European cities.
  6. Evaluate the difference between knowledge exchange and knowledge appropriation.
  7. Trace how overseas rivalry affected taxation, borrowing, and state capacity.
  8. Compare the consequences of empire across Britain, the Dutch Republic, France, Iberia, and inland Europe.
  9. Interpret a statute as historical evidence.
  10. Evaluate competing arguments about empire and European economic growth.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Through what mechanisms did overseas goods change daily life, and for whom?
  2. Why can imperial imports not alone explain the consumer revolution?
  3. How can historians distinguish commercial volume from profit, state revenue, and household welfare?
  4. Did financial innovation arise from empire, from war, or from both?
  5. Was colonial commerce profitable, influential, and necessary for industrialization, and are those the same question?
  6. Whose knowledge made European science possible, and how was it acknowledged?
  7. Why did some European regions gain far more from empire than others?
  8. How did people in Europe themselves criticize the system that supplied their goods?

Historical Context

By 1550 European connections with Africa, Asia, and the Mediterranean were already old. Italian and Iberian merchants had long handled Asian spices and textiles, and banking, bills of exchange, and maritime insurance had medieval roots in Italian and Mediterranean commerce. Chapters 68 and 69 explained how Iberian voyages and the conquest of the Americas redirected these connections, bringing American silver into circulation. Chapter 70 examined Atlantic slavery, Chapter 71 the Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires, and Chapter 72 British and French expansion.

This chapter assumes that background. It does not narrate voyages or colonial wars. Its evidence is of a different kind: customs records, which show goods entering ports but also include re-exports; probate inventories, which list household possessions at death but over-represent property owners; merchant correspondence, which survives unevenly; tax and excise accounts; advertisements and newspapers; and the collections, letters, and publications of naturalists. Each source answers some questions and cannot answer others.

Division I · Sections 73.1 to 73.4

Global Empire and the Transformation of European Societies

This division sets out the analytical framework: what the connections were, how wealth moved, where effects fell, and how they can be measured.

Section 73.1

Overseas Empire and the Changing Structure of European Society

Empire reached European life through at least eight channels. Trade brought goods and moved ships. Consumption changed what households ate, wore, and owned. Investment tied the savings of merchants, landowners, and widows to distant ventures. Employment grew in shipyards, warehouses, refineries, and the workshops that supplied export markets. Migration carried Europeans outward and, in smaller numbers, brought Africans, Asians, and Americans into European cities. Taxation turned imported goods into public revenue. Political power shifted toward groups whose wealth or services depended on overseas commerce. Knowledge, from maps to medicines, flowed into European collections and universities. This section defines these channels so that later sections can test each against evidence.

The channels did not operate equally everywhere. A Lisbon customs official, a Bristol sugar refiner, a Silesian linen weaver, a Genoese banker, and a Norwegian timber exporter all experienced empire, but through different mechanisms and with different results. Social position mattered as much as place. A merchant with capital could convert a single successful voyage into land, office, and influence. A dock laborer might gain steady but seasonal and often dangerous employment. A farm household far inland might encounter empire only as a rise in the price of salt or the arrival of a printed cotton at a market stall. A historian who writes simply that Europe was transformed by empire has said very little, because the question is who changed, how, and by what route.

The chapter therefore proceeds by mechanism. Where a commodity changed a diet, the chapter asks who could afford it and when. Where a financial institution grew, it asks what problem the institution solved and whether overseas trade or European war supplied the main pressure. Where a scientific collection expanded, it asks whose knowledge made the collection possible. This method also guards against two opposite errors. One treats empire as the single cause of European power and prosperity. The other treats overseas expansion as a distant sideshow that left European societies largely untouched. Neither survives contact with the evidence.

Chronology matters as well. Silver from the Americas altered European and Asian economies from the sixteenth century. Large changes in everyday consumption are most visible after about 1650 and especially in the eighteenth century. The financial and fiscal consequences of overseas rivalry intensified in the decades of war after 1688. The antislavery movement and the new political economy of the 1770s and 1780s mark the end of the chapter. The chapter does not carry the story into mechanized industry, which belongs to Chapter 93, or into the nineteenth century partition of Africa, which belongs to Chapter 104. The broader domestic history of population, agriculture, and consumption is reserved for Chapter 85.

Section 73.2

The Atlantic, Indian Ocean, and the Circulation of Global Wealth

The most important flows of wealth did not run in a single direction or in a neat triangle. Silver mined in the Americas, notably at Potosí from the 1540s and in New Spain, entered the Spanish treasury and private hands. Much of it passed through Seville and later Cádiz into the accounts of foreign creditors and merchants. Part flowed onward to Asia, both through European trading companies that exported bullion to buy Indian cottons, spices, and Chinese goods, and through the Pacific route from Acapulco to Manila. Chinese and Indian markets absorbed silver in large quantities, because Asian producers could supply goods Europeans wanted and European producers could offer little in exchange except metal. Historians agree on the importance of these flows but not on their exact quantities, and any single figure for silver in a given decade should be treated as an estimate.

Asian manufactures moved the other way. Indian cotton textiles, produced by weavers, spinners, dyers, and printers in Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal, reached Europe, West Africa, and Southeast Asia. Chinese porcelain, silk, and tea, and Japanese lacquer, reached European markets through the Portuguese, Dutch, English, French, and other companies. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600 and the Dutch United East India Company in 1602. Both combined trade, finance, and armed force, as Chapter 71 explains. Their importance here is that they moved large quantities of goods and capital between continents under corporate organization.

Plantation commodities formed a third circuit. Sugar, tobacco, indigo, and later coffee and cotton were produced in the Americas largely by enslaved Africans and shipped to European ports. African slave dealers and rulers received European goods, including textiles from India and Europe, metals, firearms, and alcohol, in exchange for captives. The details of that trade belong to Chapter 70. What matters here is that European manufactures and Asian textiles became inputs into a system that produced goods for European consumers, so that distant labor regimes and household purchases were linked.

Credit connected the circuits. A merchant in Amsterdam might finance a Baltic grain cargo, insure a ship bound for Batavia, and hold bills drawn on London. Because commercial paper moved far more easily than coin, credit allowed regions with little direct trade to participate indirectly. This is why the chapter speaks of global wealth rather than simple exchange. Capital, goods, labor, and information moved at different speeds along routes that interacted. A war in one region, a poor harvest in another, or a collapse of credit in a financial center could change prices thousands of kilometers away.

The Pacific route was central to the connection between silver and Asian goods. The Spanish Pacific return route was established in 1565, when an expedition from the Philippines successfully reached New Spain; after Manila was founded in 1571, regular galleon commerce linked Manila and Acapulco, carrying American silver westward and returning with Chinese silk, porcelain, and other goods that circulated in the Americas and, in smaller quantities, in Spain. Chinese demand for silver was strong in part because the Ming and Qing states increasingly collected taxes in silver, and merchants there could sell goods at prices that attracted bullion from several directions, including Japan. Historians therefore describe an interconnected system in which developments in China, Japan, the Americas, and Europe affected one another, and they caution against treating Europe as its sole organizer. What Europeans controlled was a growing share of the shipping and credit that connected the regions, and the profits from carrying and exchanging goods were substantial even when the Europeans did not set the underlying demand.

Section 73.3

The Uneven Geography of European Imperial Transformation

Portugal and Spain held vast overseas territories, yet their domestic economies gained less than the size of their empires might suggest. Spanish silver and Portuguese Asian and Brazilian trade enriched the crowns and some merchants, but a large share of the commercial profit, and much of the manufacturing that supplied colonial markets, passed to Italian, Dutch, English, French, and German firms operating through Seville, Cádiz, and Lisbon. The Spanish monarchy repeatedly borrowed against incoming silver, and foreign bankers collected the returns. Direct ownership of empire and economic benefit from empire were therefore different things.

The Dutch Republic converted a small territory into the center of European commerce. Amsterdam combined shipping, a staple market for Baltic grain and timber, insurance, banking, and a large re-export trade in Asian and colonial goods. Dutch wealth owed relatively little to colonial territory in the Americas and a great deal to services, shipping, and finance. Britain developed a different pattern after the mid seventeenth century. Navigation legislation channeled colonial trade through British ships and ports. Sugar and tobacco brought customs revenue and supported refining and re-export. Bristol, Liverpool, Glasgow, and London each specialized in different branches of Atlantic trade. France grew a large Atlantic sector centered on Nantes and Bordeaux, with Saint-Domingue becoming a major sugar and coffee producer in the eighteenth century, and much of its colonial produce was re-exported to northern and central Europe.

Regions without colonies were often entangled in empire all the same. Linen weavers in Silesia, Westphalia, and Ireland sold cloth that was shipped via Hamburg, Cádiz, or Bristol to clothe plantation workers and supply colonial markets. Scandinavian and Baltic producers sold timber, iron, pitch, and tar that built and equipped ships. Swiss, German, and Italian merchants and bankers financed and insured ventures. Italian glass and silk and German metalwares reached overseas buyers. Inland consumers bought sugar, tobacco, and cotton goods that arrived by river, canal, and road from the great ports.

The result was a patchwork. Port cities and their immediate hinterlands felt the largest effects. Manufacturing districts supplying colonial and re-export markets felt them in the form of demand. Some regions, such as parts of the Mediterranean and eastern Europe, were integrated more weakly. The distinction between direct colonial ownership and indirect participation through shipping, manufacturing, finance, or consumption is the key to reading the chapter. It also prevents a map of European empires from being mistaken for a map of European economic change.

Section 73.4

Measuring the European Consequences of Empire

Historians cannot measure the effects of empire with one number, and part of their work is knowing which question each source can answer. Customs records show goods entering and leaving ports and the duties collected. In Britain, the Inspector General of Imports and Exports began keeping systematic ledgers in 1696, but the official values were fixed to earlier prices and so do not track market prices. Volumes are a better guide than values. The Sound Toll Registers kept by Denmark at the Oresund record ships and cargoes passing between the North Sea and the Baltic across centuries, and they are a rich source for the Baltic trades. Company records of the English and Dutch East India Companies survive in large archives.

Merchant accounts and correspondence show how credit and risk worked but survive unevenly, and the best preserved belong to the most successful firms. Probate inventories list the goods in a household at death and show, for example, when tea equipment, cotton furnishings, or porcelain appear. They over-represent property owners, are not available everywhere on the same terms, and record possessions at death, not purchasing patterns. Tax records help trace revenue but not household welfare. Newspapers and advertisements show what was offered for sale, not who bought it.

Several distinctions must be kept in view. The quantity of a commodity imported is not the quantity consumed, because many goods were re-exported. Nominal prices and values differ from inflation adjusted ones. Merchant profits differ from investor returns, wages, and government revenue. The profits of slave trading or plantation ownership differ from the wider economic effects of colonial commerce. A national average conceals the gap between a London merchant and a rural laborer. A historian who reports that sugar imports rose has not yet shown that most households ate more sugar.

These cautions frame the whole chapter. Historians have documented a rapid expansion of intercontinental trade, but they have also shown that early modern globalization had limits. Prices in many markets did not converge, transport was slow and risky, and the great majority of Europeans lived by farming and local exchange. The chapter therefore does not assume that European markets were already fully integrated by 1700. Quantitative evidence is introduced only where its source, period, units, and limits can be stated.

Historical Vocabulary
Re-export trade

The importing of goods into a port and their sale onward to another market, usually after storage, sorting, processing, or repackaging. In Amsterdam, London, and Hamburg a large share of imported sugar, tobacco, and cotton cloth was handled this way. The term matters because customs records of imports overstate what a country consumed. It also explains why ports and merchants could prosper from goods that most of their own population never used.

Division II · Sections 73.5 to 73.11

Global Commodities and the European Consumer Revolution

Each section asks when a commodity arrived, when it became affordable, to whom, and with what limits. Imports alone did not create the consumer revolution, since domestic agriculture, household labor, and retail networks supplied the money, goods, and shops through which imported goods reached buyers.

Section 73.5

Sugar and the Transformation of European Diets

In the Middle Ages sugar was a costly import, used as a spice, a medicine, and a display of wealth. Its production moved west across the Mediterranean and then to the Atlantic islands and Brazil. In the seventeenth century Dutch, English, and French planters developed the Caribbean as the main supplier, with Barbados an early center of English sugar from the 1640s. As output rose and shipping improved, prices fell. By the eighteenth century sugar was an article of ordinary purchase in parts of northwestern Europe, though not everywhere and not for everyone.

The change in use was gradual. Sugar sweetened tea, coffee, and chocolate, and it entered jams, preserves, puddings, cakes, and confectionery. It also served as a preservative, so households could store fruit through the winter. Per capita consumption in Britain rose severalfold between the later seventeenth century and 1800, according to most estimates, though the figures depend on imperfect import and re-export records. Consumption in France, the Dutch Republic, and the German lands also grew, while much of southern and eastern Europe consumed far less. Honey and fruit syrups continued to supply sweetness for poorer households, and beet sugar belongs to the following century.

The sugar trade created a distinctive European industry. Raw sugar arrived in barrels or hogsheads and was refined in London, Bristol, Amsterdam, Hamburg, Bordeaux, and other ports. Refining required furnaces, fuel, copper pans, skilled boilers, and unskilled labor, and refineries were conspicuous, hot, and dangerous workplaces. Refiners, grocers, and confectioners formed a chain of business that depended on a steady flow of raw sugar from the colonies.

The link to slavery was direct. Sugar cultivation and processing in the Caribbean and Brazil relied overwhelmingly on enslaved Africans, working under brutal conditions that produced high mortality. European demand for cheaper sugar encouraged expansion of that system. The anthropologist Sidney Mintz argued that sugar served as cheap calories for a growing industrial workforce and that this helped link plantations and factories. Historians have challenged parts of this thesis, noting that the timing of consumption, the role of other foods, and the differences among regions complicate it. What is secure is that the sweetening of European diets was inseparable from a system of coerced labor, a point returned to in Division IV.

The form in which sugar was sold shows how it moved through society. Refiners cast sugar into hard conical loaves, which grocers sold by weight and which households cut with special nippers, and finer or whiter grades commanded higher prices. Cheaper brown and muscovado sugars were bought by poorer households, and the gradations of quality were part of social distinction. Regional differences were wide. Hamburg and Amsterdam refined sugar and sent it by river and sea into the Baltic and central Europe, while in much of eastern and southern Europe sugar remained scarce and dear well into the eighteenth century. Sugar also appeared in display, as in elaborate table decorations for banquets, and in medicine, where it continued to be prescribed. These uses mean that the growth in consumption cannot be read from import totals alone, since the same ton could be a luxury in one household and a staple in another.

Section 73.6

Tea, Coffee, and the New Culture of Consumption

Coffee came to Europe from the Red Sea region and the Ottoman world, where Yemen was the early center of cultivation and Mocha an export port. Coffeehouses appeared in London and Oxford in the 1650s, and similar establishments spread across Amsterdam, Paris, Vienna, Venice, and German cities. Tea came from China. The Dutch brought it to Europe in the early seventeenth century, and its popularity grew in the Netherlands and later in Britain, where the East India Company became a major importer. Europeans soon began to grow coffee in their own colonies, in Java and later in the Caribbean, so that African and Arabian beverages came to rest on colonial labor as well.

The two drinks fit different social settings. Coffeehouses were places of business, news, and conversation. Merchants and insurers gathered at certain houses, and Lloyd's in London grew from such a setting. Historians have long debated how far coffeehouses created a new public sphere, since they were often restricted by gender, wealth, and political suspicion. Tea was more strongly associated with the household, with the equipment of cups, pots, trays, and tables, and with a domestic ritual often managed by women. Tea gave rise to a market in porcelain, silver, and later British ceramic ware.

Taxation shaped what people drank. High duties on tea in Britain encouraged large scale smuggling, and in 1784 the Commutation Act sharply reduced the duty, which helped legal imports rise. In France coffee and chocolate were more common than tea, and the Dutch drank tea widely. In the German lands, rulers worried about coffee imports and the drain of money, and Prussia restricted coffee in the 1780s. These differences show that a new beverage did not mean a single European culture of consumption.

The social reach of the new drinks also varied. Prosperous urban households adopted them earlier. By the eighteenth century tea in Britain had reached many middling and some poorer homes, especially in towns, while much of rural Europe still relied on beer, wine, cider, or water. The new drinks therefore changed habits where incomes, markets, and shops allowed, and they left many areas largely unchanged.

Coffee production itself shifted under European influence. The Dutch East India Company began to grow coffee on Java at the beginning of the eighteenth century, with the first small commercial consignments reaching the Netherlands in 1711 to 1712 and much larger annual exports developing after 1721, and French and British planters later expanded coffee cultivation in the Caribbean, especially in Saint-Domingue, using enslaved labor. These developments reduced the dependence of European importers on Red Sea suppliers and lowered prices, which widened the market. At the same time, Ottoman coffeehouses continued to flourish in Istanbul and other cities, and coffee remained a central drink of the Ottoman world, including its European provinces. The history of coffee therefore ran along both a European and an Ottoman track, and it connects the chapter to the Mediterranean and Balkan worlds that appear elsewhere in this textbook.

Section 73.7

Tobacco, Chocolate, and Changing European Habits

Tobacco and cacao came from the Americas, where Indigenous peoples had long cultivated and used them. Tobacco reached Europe in the sixteenth century and spread rapidly, by pipe, as snuff, and chewed. Colonies in Virginia and Maryland, and in Brazil, became major suppliers. The new habit drew criticism. King James I of England condemned smoking in a tract of 1604, and moralists and physicians debated its effects. Even so, consumption spread across social ranks, with snuff becoming fashionable among elites in France and Britain in the eighteenth century.

Governments quickly saw a source of revenue. Customs duties on tobacco were a large part of the British revenue from colonial goods, and the French crown placed tobacco under a monopoly managed through tax farmers, from the 1670s. Spain and other states created their own monopolies. Where duties were high, smuggling flourished. Tobacco was also re-exported in large quantities from British and Dutch ports to the Continent, and the trade employed merchants, shippers, processors, and retailers.

Chocolate reached Spain from Mesoamerica in the sixteenth century. At first it was drunk hot, sweetened, and often spiced, and it was an expensive drink of the court and the clergy. It spread through Italy, France, and northern Europe, and chocolate houses opened in London in the seventeenth century. Its cost fell as cacao supplies from Venezuela, the Caribbean, and elsewhere grew, though it remained a drink of the wealthier for much of the period. Doctors argued about whether it was a medicine, a food, or an indulgence, and religious writers debated whether it broke fasting rules.

These habits illustrate how consumption made empire visible in daily life. A pipe of Virginia tobacco or a cup of sweetened chocolate rested on the labor of enslaved and coerced workers and on Indigenous agricultural knowledge. Consumers rarely saw that labor. Debates about luxury, health, and morality often focused on the consumer, and only later did critics turn attention to the producer, as Section 73.22 shows.

Consumption also produced distinctive patterns of work and regulation inside Europe. Tobacco reached buyers as loose leaf, manufactured rolls, pipe tobacco, and powdered snuff, each requiring different merchants, mills, shopkeepers, and packaging. The fiscal interests of states encouraged official monopolies and policing, yet could also encourage illegal distribution when duties raised prices too far. The spread of chocolate required another supporting market in sugar, spices, utensils, and skilled preparation. A fashionable drink could therefore generate demand for several overseas commodities and a number of local services at once. That linkage helps explain why consumption history is more than a history of changing tastes: it also concerns workplaces, public revenue, retail institutions, and the unequal ability to participate in fashionable habits.

Section 73.8

Indian Cotton Textiles, Calicoes, and European Fashion

Indian cotton cloth was among the most important manufactured goods in world trade. Weavers, spinners, dyers, and printers in Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal produced fabrics that were light, washable, and bright in color, with dyes that held fast in ways European dyers struggled to match. European companies carried plain and printed cottons to Europe, and the same cloths also supplied markets in Africa and Southeast Asia. In Europe they were bought for clothing, bed hangings, curtains, and furnishing. Calico, chintz, and muslin were among the names for these fabrics.

European producers of wool, silk, and linen saw a threat, and governments responded. France prohibited printed cottons from 1686, a ban that lasted until 1759, though smuggling was widespread. In England an act of 1700 restricted imports of printed calicoes and certain Asian silks for wear. When this proved insufficient, Parliament passed the Calico Act of 1721, which restricted the use of printed cottons in clothing and furnishing. Weavers in London protested and, in 1719, there were attacks on people wearing the fabrics. The controversy was about employment and fashion, and it was also about the principle that a state could shape what its subjects bought.

Restriction produced imitation. European manufacturers learned to print cotton and linen and developed new products. Exceptions in the English laws, notably fustians, which combined a linen warp with a cotton weft, and muslins, allowed a domestic cotton industry to grow. Printworks appeared in the Netherlands, Switzerland, Marseille, and elsewhere, and after French restrictions ended there was growth at Mulhouse and Jouy. The result was not a simple ban followed by decline. Indian cloth shaped European tastes, and European makers adapted Indian technique and design to compete.

The consequences within Europe were mixed. Fashion became more changeable, and ordinary people could afford printed cloth more than they could afford silk. Domestic textile workers in some regions lost work or protested, while others gained work in printing and finishing. Chapter 93 treats the British cotton industry and mechanization, and this section stops at the point where imitation, protection, and new demand prepared that development without determining it.

The Indian side of the trade deserves separate attention. Weaving, spinning, dyeing, and printing supported large numbers of artisans in Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal, and exports to Europe were one part of a wider system that also supplied Asian and African markets. European companies bought through brokers and advanced money to weavers, a practice known as the dadni system, in order to secure cloth of the desired quality and quantity. After the East India Company's victory at Plassey in 1757 and its acquisition of the right to collect revenue in Bengal in 1765, the company's political power affected how it dealt with weavers and merchants, and historians continue to debate how far Indian textile production was disrupted and how far it continued to adapt. For present purposes, the point is that European fashion and European protection depended on the labor and skill of producers who had no voice in the English laws that restricted their goods.

Section 73.9

Porcelain, Lacquer, Decorative Arts, and the European Material World

Chinese porcelain was produced on a vast scale at Jingdezhen and other kilns and had been traded across Asia for centuries before Europeans entered the market. Portuguese merchants carried it to Europe from the sixteenth century, and Dutch companies shipped very large quantities in the seventeenth. Producers adapted to their customers, supplying blue and white wares, dinner and tea services, and pieces decorated with European coats of arms to order. After 1757 the Qing court confined European trade to Canton, and porcelain, tea, and silk moved through that port in the eighteenth century. Japanese lacquer reached Europe mainly through the Dutch at Nagasaki, together with Chinese lacquered furniture and screens.

Imports shaped European craft production through imitation. Potters in Delft developed tin glazed earthenware in blue and white that echoed Chinese designs, and it was sold widely in the Netherlands and abroad. Cabinetmakers in England, France, and the Netherlands practiced japanning, a technique that imitated lacquer with European varnishes. The long search for the secret of hard paste porcelain ended in Saxony, where Johann Friedrich Bottger and others achieved it around 1708 to 1710, and the Meissen factory was founded in 1710. Other states followed, and in England factories at Chelsea, Bow, and elsewhere began production from the 1740s. Imitation did not simply copy. European makers combined Asian forms with local styles, and the results helped establish a distinct material culture.

Taste and collecting carried these objects into noble and royal interiors. Porcelain rooms, display cabinets, and lacquered furniture signaled wealth and cosmopolitan knowledge. Chinoiserie, the European fashion for Chinese styled decoration, was often more a European invention than an accurate reflection of China, and it could blend Chinese, Japanese, Indian, and European motifs. As prices fell, porcelain and its imitations passed to middling households, especially because of tea, which required cups, pots, and saucers.

The effect was uneven. Skilled European potters, cabinetmakers, and painters gained work and new techniques. Consumers in the Netherlands, England, and France could buy much more than before. Large parts of eastern and southern Europe, and most rural households everywhere, kept using wood, pewter, coarse earthenware, and local textiles. The Asian craftsmen who made the originals appear in European sources mainly as anonymous suppliers, although their skill and design shaped the whole exchange.

Imitation changed the meaning of these objects. Delft potters produced glazed earthenware that could resemble imported blue-and-white porcelain in appearance but not in material composition; the porcelain developed at Meissen in the early eighteenth century involved a different technical achievement, using European materials and kiln knowledge to make hard-paste porcelain. Consumers did not necessarily regard the substitute as inferior. A locally made vessel might be easier to replace, better suited to a household's purse, or more readily available than an imported one. The resulting circulation of patterns, shapes, and skills complicates a simple division between Asian inventors and European buyers. Asian artisans remained central to global demand, while European manufacturers experimented with reproducing, adapting, and marketing designs that had already acquired prestige through commerce.

Section 73.10

Spices, Dyes, and the Materials of Global Commerce

Pepper, cloves, nutmeg, mace, cinnamon, and other spices were the classic goods of the Asian trade. Their importance in value declined across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, partly because supply increased and prices fell, and partly because textiles, tea, and other goods grew faster. Pepper in particular became a cheaper seasoning that reached many households. The fine spices of the Moluccas remained costly and tightly controlled by the Dutch company, whose methods are discussed in Chapter 71. For Europeans the change was visible in kitchens and apothecaries, where spices were used as flavoring, preservative, and medicine.

Dyes mattered because color was central to European textiles and status. Indigo, obtained from plants cultivated in India and later in the Caribbean, South Carolina, and Saint-Domingue, produced a strong blue and competed with woad, a European plant dye. Woad growers in Thuringia, Languedoc, and elsewhere lobbied for restrictions, and some German authorities prohibited indigo for a time on the ground that it was harmful. Indigo eventually prevailed because it was stronger and, as supply increased, cheaper. Cochineal, a red dye made from insects raised on cactus in Mexico, was a Spanish colonial export of great value. Indigenous cultivators in Oaxaca produced much of it, and Spain profited from its export to the dyeing centers of Europe. Logwood, from Central America and the Caribbean, was used for black and purple dyes, and brazilwood gave a red.

These materials affected European manufacturing. Better and cheaper dyes improved the appearance and range of woolens, silks, and cottons and supported textile trades in the Netherlands, France, England, and Italy. Dyers formed specialized guilds and crafts, and dye houses clustered in towns with water. The raw materials also created disputes over trade and monopoly, with importers, growers, and manufacturers pressing governments for protection or access.

The materials of global commerce show that consumption was not only the purchase of finished goods. Much imported wealth entered Europe as inputs to workshops, and many Europeans encountered empire as the color of cloth or the taste of food. This is also where the chapter's caution about evidence is clearest, since a household record of a blue coat does not reveal whether its dye came from woad or indigo.

Dyestuffs reveal how a small ingredient could matter across several industries. Cochineal from the Americas provided a brilliant red color prized by European dyers, while indigo supplied blue to textile printers and finishers. Merchants faced the problem of judging quality when similar-looking shipments differed in concentration, preparation, or contamination. Producers and dyers had to combine imported ingredients with mordants, water, heat, and locally transmitted craft knowledge. A change in access to a dye therefore affected not only a customs ledger but also the cost and appearance of clothes, tapestries, and furniture. The circulation of these materials was neither a smooth replacement of European crops nor the simple triumph of imports; older dyes and substitute ingredients remained useful wherever price and availability demanded them.

Section 73.11

Shops, Advertising, Households, and the Expansion of Consumer Markets

Imported and imitated goods reached households through a changing retail system. Fairs, weekly markets, and itinerant peddlers continued to serve much of Europe, especially in rural areas, and peddlers carried small goods such as ribbons, pins, and printed cloth to places without shops. In larger towns, permanent shops multiplied. By the eighteenth century London, Amsterdam, and Paris had streets lined with shops that displayed goods in windows, offered a wide range of wares, and competed for customers. Shopkeeping provided a living for many families, and shops often sold on credit, so that purchasing was bound to relationships of trust and obligation.

Advertising developed in step. Newspapers carried notices of goods newly arrived from overseas, and shopkeepers distributed trade cards, engraved or printed with their address and wares. Auction notices and printed catalogues reached collectors. Fashion itself became a market, with periodicals and prints spreading styles, and by the 1780s publications devoted to dress appeared in France and England. These sources show what was offered and how sellers described it, but they do not by themselves show who bought.

Households were the unit of decision in much of this economy. Women managed domestic purchases, often kept the household accounts, and ran shops, stalls, and millinery and dressmaking businesses, though their legal and financial position varied by place and marital status. Servants and apprentices were consumers and also vendors of second hand clothing, which formed a large market in itself. Many families could afford imported goods only by buying cheap, used, or imitation versions, or by working longer. Some historians, notably Jan de Vries, have argued that households in northwestern Europe increased their paid work to purchase market goods, in what he called an industrious revolution.

Class and region set the limits. Rich households in port cities filled rooms with goods from several continents. Urban artisans and shopkeepers owned fewer, chosen items such as a teapot, cotton curtains, or a looking glass. Rural laborers owned few. Eastern and southern Europe saw a slower spread of shops and fewer imported goods. The expansion of consumer markets thus depended on income, credit, retail networks, and domestic production as much as on the supply of overseas goods, a point developed in the debate below and in Chapter 85.

Retail evidence gives historians a way to reconstruct this transition without assuming that advertising equals sales. A trade card identified a shop, displayed its sign, and associated the merchant with goods and qualities desirable to prospective buyers. An invoice could confirm one transaction, but not the size or social composition of a shop's entire clientele. Together with probate inventories, account books, and tax registers, such ephemera make it possible to ask who sold imported goods, which products were bundled together, and whether a household purchased them occasionally or habitually. A cup, a parcel of tea, and a loaf of sugar might be bought from different dealers or through one grocer, depending on the neighborhood and decade. These small transactions help connect the global routes described earlier with recognizable economic decisions inside European streets.

Historical Vocabulary
Consumer Revolution

A historians' term for the growth, especially between about 1650 and 1800, in the range of goods bought by ordinary households. It is a label, not a single event, and historians disagree about its timing, causes, and geography. It matters here because overseas goods and their European imitations were part of it, alongside domestic production, retail change, and household labor. It should not be read as meaning that all Europeans became consumers at once.

Historical Debate

Did Global Imports Drive the European Consumer Revolution?

The question: Did imported global commodities primarily drive the growth of consumption in early modern Europe, or were domestic production, household labor, and market development more decisive?

Interpretation A: Historians such as Maxine Berg emphasize that Asian goods and their European imitations created new tastes, new products, and new industries, and that competition with imports stimulated European invention and design.
Interpretation B: Other historians stress that income, credit, retail systems, urban growth, and household labor determined who could buy, and that much new consumption involved domestic goods such as linen, earthenware, and furniture.

Evidence: Both draw on probate inventories, trade records, advertisements, and price series, each of which is incomplete. Why it matters: The answer affects how much weight empire carries in explaining household change. The positions overlap, since imports, imitation, and domestic production reinforced each other. Commerce, Finance, and the Development of European Economic Institutions

Overseas trade expanded and redirected commercial and financial institutions that already had medieval and Mediterranean roots. The sections that follow explain how those institutions worked and what problems they solved.

Section 73.12

Atlantic and Asian Trade and the Growth of European Commercial Cities

Overseas trade changed European port cities in different ways, because each specialized in a different part of the system. Lisbon handled Asian goods and, from the eighteenth century, Brazilian gold and sugar. Seville, with its river port on the Guadalquivir, managed the Spanish silver fleets until the silting of the river and the transfer of the main institutions of the American trade to Cadiz in 1717 shifted that role. Amsterdam combined an immense warehouse capacity, a financial market, shipping, and a re-export trade that sent goods to the Baltic, the German lands, and elsewhere. London was at once the capital, the seat of the East India Company, a financial center, and the largest British port.

The Atlantic trades also created newer ports whose growth was tied to specific commodities. Bristol traded in sugar and took part in the slave trade. Liverpool, which opened a commercial enclosed wet dock in 1715, rose to prominence in the eighteenth century and became the leading British slaving port. Glasgow merchants imported Chesapeake tobacco and re-exported much of it to the Continent. In France, Nantes was the leading port of the French slave trade, and Bordeaux combined the wine trade with the import and re-export of sugar and coffee from the Caribbean. Hamburg linked the Atlantic and Baltic worlds as a gateway for German and central European merchants.

The connection to inland production was close. Ports needed goods to export, and inland districts supplied them. Yorkshire and West Country woolens, Birmingham metalwares and guns, Lancashire and Silesian textiles, and Irish provisions were among the goods that moved through ports to Africa, the Americas, and Asia. Rivers, canals, and roads carried imports inland, and port merchants maintained correspondents in regional towns. A port was therefore not an isolated enclave. It was the visible edge of a network that reached into workshops and farms.

Growth came with costs. Port populations expanded, often through migration from the countryside, and housing, sanitation, and public order came under strain. Waterfronts brought warehouses, quays, and new streets, but also crowded quarters and epidemic disease. Wealth generated by trade was displayed in new merchant houses, exchanges, and public buildings, while the labor on which that wealth rested, from dockers to the enslaved Africans carried in the ships, remained largely out of view. The benefits were concentrated in a small number of cities, which is why Section 73.37 compares winners and losers.

Ports did not merely receive goods; they reorganized them for further circulation. Imported raw sugar might be refined, sold wholesale, packed for an inland river journey, and exported again, with each stage using workers and capital. The amount unloaded at a quay was therefore not a measure of consumption by the city's inhabitants. Cities also differed in their dependence on particular regions: a Baltic staple port relied heavily on grain, timber, iron, and naval stores, whereas a Caribbean-facing Atlantic port might specialize in sugar, tobacco, or the supply of plantation colonies. Commercial historians reconstruct these roles from manifests, prices, customs entries, credit records, and the changing location of warehouses. Such evidence can show the development of a port economy but does not, on its own, demonstrate that the benefits reached the city's poorest residents.

Section 73.13

Shipbuilding, Maritime Labor, Warehousing, and Supporting Industries

Overseas commerce required ships, and ships required a large complex of trades. Dutch yards developed an efficient cargo vessel, the fluyt, in the late sixteenth century, designed to carry large cargoes with a small crew. The yards at Zaandam used wind powered sawmills to process timber, a mechanization that lowered costs. English, French, and other yards built large merchant ships and warships, and shipbuilding became one of the largest employers in port towns. A ship also needed ironwork, anchors, rope, sailcloth, pitch, tar, barrels, and provisions, so that each launch drew on many specialized workers.

Many supplies came from the Baltic and Scandinavia. Timber, masts, hemp, flax, tar, and iron moved from Norway, Sweden, Poland, and the Russian Baltic ports to western yards, and Britain also drew masts from North America. This links the colonial and Baltic trades and shows how overseas commerce raised demand in regions with no colonies. Ireland supplied salted beef, butter, and other provisions to ships and colonies, and ports like Cork grew from that trade.

Maritime labor was large, mobile, and dangerous. Crews often included men from several countries, and sailors faced harsh discipline, low and delayed wages, shipwreck, and diseases such as scurvy and fever. Mortality on long voyages, especially to the tropics, could be very high, and ship crews on slave voyages suffered heavy losses as well as inflicting terrible suffering. In Britain, the navy relied on impressment, the forced recruitment of seafarers, which provoked resistance in port towns. Sailors' wives and mothers managed households while men were away, sometimes with the help of allotments of pay or credit from local shopkeepers.

Ashore, dock laborers, porters, coopers, ropemakers, sailmakers, refinery workers, clerks, and carters served the trade. Warehousing and processing added value to imported goods and kept money in the port economy. The key distinction for the chapter is that employment created by the handling of colonial goods differs from profit derived directly from slave trading or plantation ownership, a distinction developed in Division IV. Maritime work could provide steady wages, but it was often seasonal and insecure.

Workers in the same maritime economy often lived through different rhythms of employment. Shipwrights and rope-makers might work on lengthy building contracts, whereas porters and casual dock laborers were hired when a vessel arrived and dismissed when a cargo had been handled. Sailors faced illness, wrecks, delayed wages, and separation from their families; their absence shifted work to wives, relatives, and creditors at home. There were also conflicts between private merchants and navies competing for the same experienced sailors, especially during major wars. These problems were part of the social cost of a commerce often described solely in terms of ships and tonnage. They remind historians that a larger trading fleet could raise employment in an aggregate account while simultaneously exposing individual households to insecurity and coercion.

Section 73.14

Merchant Capital, Partnerships, and the Expansion of Commercial Investment

Long distance trade needed capital and ways to share risk. The simplest form was the family firm, in which brothers, cousins, or in laws pooled money and trusted one another across distances. Partnerships formed for a single voyage or a fixed period allowed investors to spread money across several ventures. Ships were commonly owned in shares, in Britain often in sixty fourths, so that a modest investor could hold a small part of many vessels. Merchants used correspondents in other ports who bought, sold, and forwarded goods for a commission, and the surviving letterbooks of such merchants show a constant exchange of prices, news, and instructions.

The joint stock company extended this principle. The Dutch East India Company, chartered in 1602, raised capital from a large number of subscribers, and its capital became permanent in the following decade, with shares that could be traded. The English East India Company developed a comparable structure. Investors, including merchants, officials, landowners, widows, and foreigners, could take a share in profits without managing the trade. These companies also held monopoly rights and exercised political and military powers, which separated them from modern firms, and Chapters 71 and 72 discuss their governance.

Merchants diversified. A successful firm might invest in ships, insurance, sugar refineries, country estates, government debt, and urban property. Religious and ethnic networks such as Quakers, Huguenots, Sephardic Jews, Armenians, and Protestant merchants of Switzerland and Germany provided trust and information, though they are treated more fully in Section 73.25. Great houses like Hope and Company in Amsterdam, and later Baring Brothers in London, combined trade and finance. Capital came from many sources, including inheritance, land, rents, and savings, and overseas profits were only one of them.

The system had limits. Risk was real, and failures, bankruptcies, and fraud were common. Capital was unevenly distributed, with small producers and ordinary workers excluded from the most profitable ventures. Evidence is skewed toward successful firms whose papers survived. Commercial investment grew in importance, yet it remained a small part of the overall European economy, which was still overwhelmingly agrarian. What changed was the existence of a class of merchants and financiers with large amounts of mobile capital, whose interests increasingly shaped state policy, as later sections show.

Trust provided a form of commercial infrastructure as important as the warehouse. A firm could not inspect every cargo or monitor every agent across oceans, so it relied on repeated correspondence, references, accounting practices, kinship ties, and the expectation that a damaged reputation would prevent future business. Merchants sometimes used several correspondents in one port to compare reports, yet the distance and slow speed of letters made fraud, misunderstanding, and sudden price changes difficult to prevent. This organization also helps explain why diaspora networks mattered in European commerce. They could connect partners who shared a language or legal tradition across state boundaries, but they never guaranteed honest dealing. Historians can sometimes reconstruct these networks from surviving letters, while recognizing that the accounts of failed or less literate traders are much harder to recover.

Section 73.15

Credit, Marine Insurance, Bills of Exchange, and Commercial Risk

Overseas trade tied up money for long periods and exposed it to loss. A cargo might take a year or more to be bought, shipped, sold, and paid for, and ships were lost to storms, rocks, disease among crews, privateers, and war. Merchants developed ways of managing this risk, and most of these methods were not new. The bill of exchange, a written order to pay a sum in another place and currency, had been refined by Italian merchants and bankers in the Middle Ages, and marine insurance had been practiced in Genoa and other Mediterranean cities since the fourteenth century. Asian merchants, including Gujarati and Armenian traders, also used credit instruments and brokers across the Indian Ocean. Atlantic and Asian trade enlarged these tools and moved their center of gravity to Amsterdam and London.

Bills of exchange allowed a merchant to send value without sending coin. A Dutch exporter of grain could be paid by a bill drawn on a merchant in London, who in turn settled accounts with others, so that debts canceled across cities. A bill could be sold at a discount to someone who wanted cash now, which linked trade credit to the banking system. Behind the instruments lay chains of trust. Merchants relied on correspondents whose reputations were known, and a failure in one city could spread through the network.

Insurance worked by spreading losses across many people. In Amsterdam an official chamber of insurance was set up in 1598 to regulate the business. In London, individual underwriters gathered at coffeehouses, and the establishment kept by Edward Lloyd, active from the late 1680s, became the center of marine underwriting. Lloyd's List, which published shipping news, began in 1734. In 1720 Parliament chartered two corporations, the Royal Exchange Assurance and the London Assurance, with a restriction on other corporate marine insurers that left room for individual underwriters at Lloyd's. Insurance needed information, so merchants collected news of ports, prices, wars, and weather, and printed price currents circulated widely.

These arrangements reduced risk without removing it. Credit could expand too far, and financial crises hit trading cities, notably in 1763, after the Seven Years' War, when Amsterdam houses collapsed, and in 1772, when a banking failure in Scotland and difficulties of the East India Company spread through London and Amsterdam. Such crises show that overseas commerce depended on fragile structures of confidence. Insurance and credit also benefited merchants more than the sailors, dockers, and enslaved people whose labor carried the cargoes, since losses to them were not insured in the same way.

Information was the other great resource of commercial risk management. Merchants relied on letters, printed price currents, and shipping lists to learn what goods were in demand and which ships were overdue, and a rumor of war or a storm could change prices overnight. Insurers depended on this news, and they also depended on law, since disputes over whether a loss was covered were settled in courts that developed rules of marine insurance. Shocks tested the system. The Lisbon earthquake of 1755 destroyed shops, warehouses, and cargoes and disrupted commercial correspondence and markets beyond Portugal. Whether particular foreign underwriters paid substantial claims depends on contracts and surviving accounts, so the scale of insured losses cannot safely be inferred merely from the scale of destruction. Such episodes show how widely risk was distributed across cities and how a disaster in one port could affect financial houses elsewhere.

Historical Vocabulary
Marine Insurance

A contract in which underwriters, in exchange for a premium, agree to bear part of the loss if a ship or cargo is lost at sea. The practice had Mediterranean roots, and it grew in Amsterdam and London as overseas trade expanded. It matters because it allowed merchants to take on long and risky voyages by spreading loss. It should not be confused with modern insurance of persons, and it also covered voyages that carried enslaved people, a reminder of how fully slavery was embedded in commerce.

Section 73.16

Banks, Securities, Public Credit, and Financial Institutions

Amsterdam set the model for early modern financial institutions. The Exchange Bank, founded in 1609, held deposits and allowed merchants to settle accounts by transfer, which gave Amsterdam a stable, trusted currency in a Europe of many coins. The shares of the Dutch East India Company were traded on the exchange, and the Amsterdam bourse developed techniques of speculation that attracted comment. Joseph de la Vega described its workings in his Confusion de Confusiones of 1688, a valuable contemporary account of trading in company shares. Dutch investors also held large amounts of foreign government debt, which made Amsterdam a source of capital for other states.

London developed its own institutions. The Bank of England was established in 1694, and its immediate purpose was to raise money for the government at war with France. Subscribers lent a large sum to the crown in return for a charter and the right to issue notes and conduct banking business. The Bank was not founded to serve colonial commerce, and it should not be treated as a product of empire. Still, it fitted a wider pattern in which merchants, financiers, and the state developed a relationship of mutual dependence. The Bank of Scotland followed in 1695, and other joint stock banks and country banks spread in the following decades.

Securities markets grew with public debt and overseas companies. The English East India Company, the Bank of England, and the South Sea Company, set up in 1711 to take on government debt, issued shares and bonds traded in coffeehouses around Exchange Alley. The South Sea bubble of 1720, in which the company's share price rose steeply and then collapsed, and the contemporary collapse of John Law's Mississippi scheme and Banque Royale in France, showed both the attractions and the dangers of the new instruments. Both episodes damaged confidence and also prompted regulation and caution.

The result was a financial system in which private capital and government finance were closely interwoven. Investors who held public debt had a stake in the state's survival and tax revenue, and governments that could borrow cheaply gained advantages in war. These institutions drew on older European and Asian precedents and on the specific demands of war. Empire supplied some capital and some of the trade that gave the system its business, but the fiscal needs of states were at least as important.

Foreign capital played a large role in these markets. By the eighteenth century Dutch investors held a considerable amount of British government debt and other securities, which meant that Amsterdam capital helped finance the wars of Britain and other states. This gave Dutch investors a stake in foreign governments, and it made the financial fortunes of Amsterdam and London dependent on each other. When wars or panics disturbed confidence, as in 1763 and 1772, losses passed between the two centers. Financial institutions thus did not merely reflect national economies. They created transnational ties among investors, and they were among the earliest examples of the kind of integration that later periods would extend.

Section 73.17

Customs, Taxation, Public Debt, and the Financing of European Power

Overseas trade supplied governments with revenue because imports and exports passed through ports where they could be counted and taxed. In Britain, customs duties on goods such as sugar, tobacco, and tea made up a large part of public income, and the excise, a tax on domestic production and consumption of items such as beer and spirits, became still more important. Because duties fell on goods consumed by many people, imported commodities were a broad tax base. The consumer revolution examined in Division II therefore had a direct fiscal side, since every cup of sweetened tea contributed to the state.

Revenue supported borrowing. A government that could show lenders a dependable stream of customs and excise could issue bonds at lower interest, and it could pledge particular taxes to pay interest on particular loans. Britain, which fought many wars between 1688 and 1815, financed them to a large degree by borrowing, and its public debt grew many times over across the eighteenth century. The Royal Navy was one of the largest items of spending, and the navy in turn protected the commerce that produced revenue, which created a circle linking ships, trade, taxes, and debt.

Other states followed different paths. France collected many indirect taxes through tax farmers, private syndicates that advanced money to the crown and then collected revenue, including customs and the tobacco monopoly. Its borrowing, especially for the war of American independence, contributed to the financial crisis that preceded the Revolution, which Chapter 87 examines. Spain relied heavily on American silver and on loans from bankers, and the crown repeatedly suspended payments in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The Dutch Republic taxed heavily at provincial level and maintained a large public debt supported by wealthy investors.

The link between trade and fiscal capacity was not simple. War could raise demand for revenue and reduce trade at the same time, because of blockades, privateering, and higher insurance costs. Governments also relied on revenue from many sources, including land taxes and domestic excise, so overseas trade was only one part of the picture. The full account of bureaucracy, armies, and centralization belongs to Chapter 74. What matters here is that commercial expansion, consumption, and finance helped some states to borrow, tax, and fight on a larger scale than before.

Collecting these revenues required administrative effort. In Britain, the customs service employed a large number of officers at ports and along the coast, who inspected cargoes, assessed duties, and tried to prevent smuggling, and the excise service did similar work inland. These agencies were among the largest civil departments of the state. Smuggling nevertheless flourished where duties were high, as with tea, tobacco, and spirits, and it involved coastal communities, merchants, and sometimes officials, which made enforcement a political and social problem as well as a fiscal one. The Commutation Act of 1784 was in part an attempt to reduce smuggling by lowering the tax on tea. The effort to collect revenue from trade thus produced bureaucratic growth, and it linked the habits of consumers to the capacity of the state.

Division IV · Sections 73.18 to 73.22

Colonial Labor, Slavery, Wealth, and Economic Inequality

This division follows the consequences of coerced colonial labor inside Europe. It does not repeat the account of the slave trade and plantation regimes in Chapter 70. It asks how slave produced goods entered European economies, who gained, and what historians can and cannot conclude.

Section 73.18

Enslaved Labor and the European Economy of Colonial Commodities

Plantation commodities entered the European economy through a chain of transactions that began with coerced labor. Enslaved Africans and their descendants grew and processed sugar, tobacco, indigo, rice, and coffee in the Americas, and they did so under conditions of violence and, in the sugar colonies, very high mortality. Planters shipped the produce to merchants in European ports, who sold it on commission, arranged freight and insurance, and often advanced credit to the planter against the next crop. Many planters became deeply indebted to these merchants, which tied the colonies to European finance. The merchants in turn sold sugar to refiners, tobacco to processors and re-exporters, and indigo and coffee to dealers across the Continent.

Each step generated income for Europeans. Merchants earned commissions, interest, and trading margins. Shipowners earned freight, and underwriters earned premiums. Customs officers collected duties for the state, and refiners, grocers, and retailers added value. At the end of the chain stood consumers who paid for sweetened tea or tobacco and often had little knowledge of how the goods were produced. The slave trade itself was financed by the same networks of credit and insured by the same underwriters. The Zong case of 1781, in which the crew of a British slave ship killed many enslaved people and the owners then claimed on their insurance, came before the courts of King's Bench in 1783 as a dispute over the policy, and it shows how thoroughly slavery was embedded in commercial law and risk management.

The system operated in several European states. British, French, Dutch, Portuguese, Danish, and Spanish merchants and ships took part, though in different proportions, and each state set its own rules for trade with its colonies. Dutch investors financed plantations in Suriname, and Danish merchants in Copenhagen traded to the Caribbean. French and British ports were the largest. In every case demand in Europe set the system in motion and sustained it, and the supply side depended on the work of enslaved people and on African political and commercial intermediaries who supplied captives, as Chapter 70 explains.

It follows that European consumption cannot be separated from coerced labor. The question for historians is not whether the connection existed but how large its economic effects were and how they were distributed, which the next sections consider. It is also a question of responsibility and memory. Merchants, investors, officials, and consumers participated in different ways and to different degrees, while the people who bore the cost were the enslaved and, in other ways, the workers and communities in Africa disrupted by the trade.

The European end of the commodity chain could separate consumers from the people who produced its goods. Sugar sold in a grocer's shop appeared as a grade, a price, and a familiar ingredient rather than as a record of forced labor on a particular estate. Yet commercial documents often reveal that the connection was perfectly visible to merchants, insurers, and investors. Insurance policies, bills of sale, plantation accounts, and correspondence show how enslaved people were treated as property in transactions that connected colonial estates to European capital. An analysis of European consumption must therefore keep coerced producers in view even when the local records of a refinery or retailer name only cargoes and creditors. The task is not to presume that every European consumer knew every detail, but to avoid writing the labor that made the commodity possible out of the history.

Section 73.19

Colonial Production and European Manufacturing Employment

Colonial commerce supported many European workers who were not slave traders or planters. Sugar refineries employed boilers, skimmers, and laborers in London, Bristol, Amsterdam, Hamburg, Bordeaux, and other cities, and in the eighteenth century refining was one of the most visible industries in port towns. Tobacco was cut, spun, and ground into snuff in workshops, and Spain established a large state tobacco factory at Seville in the eighteenth century. Warehouses employed porters, coopers, and clerks, and barrels, baskets, and sacks were made for each cargo.

Manufacturing for colonial and African markets was an important source of demand. Birmingham and other districts produced iron goods, guns, tools, and brass wares for the slave trade and for plantations. Textile districts made cloth for plantation use and for the African trade, and Indian cottons were also bought for that trade. French printworks produced printed cotton cloth, called indiennes, in large amounts, and some of it was shipped to Africa. Linen weavers in Silesia, Westphalia, Brittany, and Ireland supplied coarse cloth used for clothing enslaved people. Rope, sailcloth, and shipbuilding trades depended on the same traffic.

Employment effects must be distinguished from profits. A refinery worker in Bristol received a wage, and a weaver in Silesia was paid for cloth, but neither received a share in plantation or slave trade profits. Their jobs depended on colonial markets, which made their livelihoods sensitive to war, price changes, and political decisions. Estimates of how many Europeans worked in occupations tied to colonial commerce are uncertain, because the records do not distinguish products by final use, and historians generally treat such estimates cautiously. The proportion of the total workforce was small compared with agriculture, yet it was significant in port cities and in particular manufacturing districts.

Regional concentration is the key point. A few ports and districts depended heavily on colonial trade, while most of Europe had little connection with it. Women and children worked in finishing, spinning, and printing, usually at low pay. Workers in colonial occupations were exposed to unemployment when trade was interrupted, and dock and refinery work was often dangerous. The employment effects were real, but they were uneven and are not evidence by themselves that colonial commerce drove European economic development as a whole.

The geography of supporting employment complicates the notion of a single colonial windfall. A sugar refinery near a major port could depend on steady imports, fuel supplies from surrounding districts, transport workers, and customers beyond the city. A Midlands metalworker might manufacture goods eventually shipped overseas without ever meeting a colonial merchant. Domestic producers also faced competition from imported manufactures, as the calico disputes illustrated. Thus some groups gained work while others pressed for protection; some industries sold more into overseas markets while others struggled to hold a local market. The historian's responsibility is to trace these different paths rather than aggregate them into one claim that empire either enriched or impoverished all domestic labor.

Section 73.20

Merchants, Investors, Property, and the Unequal Distribution of Imperial Wealth

The largest gains from colonial commerce went to a limited number of merchants, shipowners, planters, financiers, and officials. In Britain, the so called West India interest included absentee planters and merchants who had influence in Parliament. In France, shipowners in Nantes and Bordeaux built fortunes from the slave trade and colonial produce, and in the Dutch Republic, Amsterdam houses financed plantation loans. These fortunes were not made uniformly. Voyages and ventures failed as well as succeeded, and some historians who have studied the profits of slave voyages find average returns that were respectable rather than extraordinary, with wide variation among individual ventures and years.

Wealth was converted into other forms of property. Merchants bought country estates and town houses, invested in government debt, banks, insurance, and canals, and sometimes entered Parliament or purchased titles. Landed families took part in colonial ventures and married into merchant families, and the line between landed and commercial wealth blurred in several states. Patronage followed, in the form of donations to churches, hospitals, schools, and public buildings, some of which survive and now stand at the center of debates about memory.

The distribution of costs was unequal. The enslaved bore the heaviest costs. Sailors on slave voyages faced high mortality and harsh discipline, and workers in ports lived with the dangers of dockwork and epidemic disease. Taxpayers paid for navies and wars to protect trade, while the benefits of that protection accrued disproportionately to those who owned ships and cargoes. Consumers paid prices that included duties. In regions with little connection to overseas trade, people experienced only modest effects, and in some places, the competition from imports hurt local producers.

Historians therefore avoid describing a general enrichment of Europe. They describe the concentration of imperial wealth in particular ports, families, and institutions, and they ask how that concentration shaped politics and culture. The evidence comes from merchant papers, wills, tax records, and records of investments, and each is partial. Wealth that left no documents is easy to overlook, and so are the experiences of those who did not benefit.

Commercial fortunes could shape European public institutions without being identical to state revenue. Successful merchants purchased urban property, funded charities, supported churches and schools, and sometimes acquired political office or influence over parliamentary seats. Their ability to lend to governments could give their opinions on tariffs, war, and colonial monopolies particular weight. Yet inventories, tax rolls, and investment records also show that portfolios changed over a lifetime: an individual might move money from risky voyages into government funds or land, and not all heirs remained merchants. The social effect of overseas wealth therefore lay partly in the possibility of converting commercial earnings into more durable forms of status. That conversion could broaden an urban elite while reproducing inequalities of property, gender, and access to credit.

Section 73.21

Slavery, Capital Accumulation, and the Debate over European Industrialization

The relation between slavery and European industrialization has been debated since Eric Williams argued in Capitalism and Slavery (1944) that profits from the slave trade and plantations helped finance British industry and that slavery was abolished when it ceased to pay. Later research has tested both claims. The chapter does not repeat the discussion of slavery's economic role in Chapter 70. It asks which claims about Europe can be separated and evaluated, and it reserves factories, steam power, and full industrial causation for Chapter 93.

Three questions must be kept apart. The first is whether colonial commerce was profitable, and for whom. The second is whether that commerce significantly influenced European economic development, through markets, raw materials, investment, finance, or institutions. The third is whether it was a necessary cause of industrialization, meaning that industrialization would not have occurred without it. A yes to the first does not imply a yes to the second, and a yes to the second does not imply a yes to the third. Many arguments in the literature slide from one to another.

Scholars have approached the problem with different methods. Some compare the profits from the slave trade and plantations with total investment in the economy and find the share small, which leads them to doubt that these profits could have financed industry on a large scale. Stanley Engerman has been a leading voice for this view. Others, such as Joseph Inikori, emphasize markets, arguing that Atlantic demand for British manufactures, including textiles, metalwares, and guns, stimulated production and regional specialization. Still others look at raw materials and land. Kenneth Pomeranz has argued that access to American land and resources eased ecological constraints on some European economies, a claim that belongs in a wider comparison of Europe and Asia. Historians of cotton have shown how plantation supply of raw cotton was central to later textile expansion, a point for Chapter 93.

Other explanations also deserve weight. Agricultural change, population growth, coal, technology, skilled labor, domestic markets, financial institutions, and state policy all contributed to European growth, and several historians treat them as the main causes. The most defensible reading is that colonial commerce and slavery mattered to particular regions, industries, and institutions, and that their precise contribution is difficult to isolate. A responsible account avoids two errors, one that treats slavery as the sole source of European capital and another that dismisses it because it supplied only a share of investment.

The history of abolition has also been used to test the argument. Williams held that slavery was abolished because it had become unprofitable, and Seymour Drescher's study of the period, which he called Econocide, argued the reverse, that British slave colonies remained economically significant and profitable when the slave trade was ended in 1807, so that abolition resulted from moral and political mobilization and not from economic decline. This finding does not settle the question of slavery's contribution to growth, but it shows that political and moral action could move against economic interest. Historians now generally treat economic and ideological explanations as interacting, and they emphasize the need to study particular regions and industries, since the weight of colonial trade was far greater for Liverpool or Bristol than for the English midlands or the Scottish highlands.

Section 73.22

Moral Criticism, Antislavery Consumption, and European Public Debate

Criticism of slavery existed from the beginning of the Atlantic system. Some Spanish clergy and Quakers condemned it on religious grounds, and in 1688 Quakers at Germantown, Pennsylvania, protested against it. British Quakers organized against the slave trade from the mid eighteenth century. The Somerset case of 1772, in which Lord Mansfield ruled that James Somerset could not be forcibly removed from England, was often read as a declaration that slavery was illegal there, though the decision was narrower and slavery continued in the empire. Granville Sharp and others pressed the case, and the public debate grew.

Black writers and activists were central. Ottobah Cugoano, born on the Gold Coast and enslaved, published Thoughts and Sentiments on the Evil of Slavery in 1787, arguing that slavery itself was a crime and not only the trade. Olaudah Equiano published his Interesting Narrative in 1789 and promoted it widely, and Ignatius Sancho's letters, published after his death in 1780, showed a Black Londoner participating in literary society. Members of the group known as the Sons of Africa corresponded with officials and sought allies. Their testimony contradicted the pro-slavery argument that Africans were suited to bondage and gave the movement authority.

Organized campaigns followed. The Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade was founded in London in 1787, and Thomas Clarkson gathered evidence. Wedgwood's medallion bearing the figure of a kneeling Black man and the words "Am I not a man and a brother" circulated as a badge. In France, the Society of the Friends of the Blacks was founded in 1788. Petitions to Parliament mobilized hundreds of thousands of signatures in 1788 and 1792, and the abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 belongs to Chapter 99.

The sugar boycott of 1791 and 1792 connected consumption to politics. Pamphlets, including one by William Fox, urged readers to abstain from West India sugar and rum, and contemporaries and historians have estimated that several hundred thousand people took part. Women played a central role as household purchasers and as organizers, and some tea tables served East India sugar instead. The boycott shows that the consumer revolution created a new political tool, and it also reveals contradictions. Abolitionists, like most Europeans, lived in a world supplied by slave labor, and critics of slavery often accepted wider imperial commerce. Defenders of slavery argued from property rights, economic necessity, and racial claims, and they remained powerful. Many Europeans, including the Dutch and Iberians in this period, saw little organized abolitionism.

Division V · Sections 73.23 to 73.27

Migration, Demography, and the Changing Social Composition of Europe

European societies were not culturally uniform before overseas expansion, and Europe had been connected to Africa, Asia, and the Mediterranean through trade, slavery, warfare, and migration for centuries. What changed after 1500 was the scale, organization, and geographical reach of particular connections.

Section 73.23

European Emigration and the Human Networks of Empire

Empire drew people out of Europe in many roles. Settlers left Castile, Extremadura, and Andalusia for the Spanish Americas, and Portuguese migrants went to Brazil, the Atlantic islands, and Asian ports. English, Scottish, Irish, German, and French settlers went to North America and the Caribbean. Soldiers, sailors, merchants, clerks, missionaries, surgeons, and skilled craftsmen sailed in the service of states and companies. The Dutch East India Company alone sent a very large number of Europeans to Asia over its history, commonly estimated at around a million, many of them from the German lands and Scandinavia as well as the Netherlands. Mortality in tropical posts and on voyages was high, and many never returned.

Not all migrants chose to go. In the seventeenth century a large part of the English migration to the Chesapeake consisted of indentured servants, who bound themselves to labor for a term of years in return for passage. Convicts were transported from Britain to America after the Transportation Act of 1718. Some young men were recruited, tricked, or pressed into service, and others left to escape poverty, debt, persecution, or war. Religious groups also migrated, such as Puritans to New England, Quakers to Pennsylvania, and German Pietists and others to Pennsylvania in the eighteenth century.

Emigration affected particular communities more than Europe as a whole. In most states, overseas migrants were a small percentage of the total population, and population growth overshadowed the outflow. Effects were strongest in regions with strong maritime connections, such as Andalusia, the Basque coast, western Portugal, southwestern France, and parts of the British Isles and the German southwest. Departures changed households, since fathers, sons, and husbands left, sometimes permanently. Remittances and inheritances flowed back, and returning migrants sometimes brought wealth that changed local property and status, though the evidence for how many succeeded is limited.

Networks followed families and localities. Migrants wrote home, and letters carried news, requests, and goods. Chain migration drew relatives and neighbors to the same colonies and trades. Missionaries, especially Jesuits and Protestant evangelists, linked European religious institutions with overseas communities and returned information to Europe. These networks mattered for later developments in knowledge and politics, since they formed channels along which news, goods, and ideas moved.

The effects of emigration at home are easily overlooked because the most visible evidence survives in colonial records. Departures altered the size and composition of families, created demands for replacement labor, and encouraged correspondence and remittances linking villages and cities to overseas settlements. A soldier's family and a merchant's family did not experience absence in the same way. The former might confront interrupted pay and uncertainty, while the latter might manage business through agents and letters. Return migration could bring goods, savings, knowledge, and sometimes new status, but voyages also ended in death or permanent separation. These outcomes were shaped by age, gender, occupation, and access to resources, so they cannot be inferred from migration totals alone.

Section 73.24

Africans, Asians, and Other Migrants within European Cities

People from Africa, Asia, and the Americas lived in European cities long before the eighteenth century, and their numbers grew with the expansion of empire. Lisbon had a substantial African population in the sixteenth century, many of them enslaved, and Seville also had a visible Black community. Amsterdam, Bordeaux, Nantes, Paris, Bristol, Liverpool, and London developed Black populations, as did port towns in Scotland and Ireland. Estimates for London in the eighteenth century range from a few thousand to around ten thousand or more, and historians treat all figures as uncertain because the sources do not record race systematically.

These residents held varied positions. Many were enslaved or recently freed servants brought by merchants, planters, officers, and captains. Others were sailors, musicians, soldiers, craftsmen, laborers, and small traders. South Asian seamen known as lascars worked on East India Company ships and were stranded in London and other ports at times, while Indian servants and nursemaids accompanied returning officials. A few individuals gained prominence. Ignatius Sancho ran a shop in London and wrote letters that were published after his death, and Olaudah Equiano and Ottobah Cugoano became authors. Angelo Soliman was a Black courtier in Vienna, and Abram Petrovich Gannibal, born in Africa, rose in Russian service. Dean Mahomed, from India, published an account of his travels in Cork in 1794.

Legal status was unclear and varied by state. French edicts of 1716 and 1738 tried to regulate the presence of enslaved people in France, and a decree of 1777 prohibited the entry of Black people, with uneven enforcement. In England, the Somerset case of 1772 limited forcible removal but did not end slavery in the empire. In the Dutch Republic and Portugal, practices differed. Employment, residence, and marriage were therefore shaped by local custom and the goodwill of employers, and exclusion from guilds and many occupations limited opportunity. Some people were baptized, married locally, and raised families, and many ended up in poverty.

The evidence is scattered, found in parish registers, court cases, newspaper advertisements for runaways, wills, and a few autobiographies, and it was created by people with their own purposes. Advertisements for runaway servants document enslavement but also resistance. These communities should not be treated as only passive victims or as exceptional. They show that European cities contained people shaped by empire in daily life, as workers, neighbors, and sometimes critics of the system.

The condition of poor Black Londoners drew public notice in the 1780s. After the end of the American war, many Black people who had served the British or escaped slavery, along with others who had come earlier as servants and sailors, were without work or shelter in London, and in 1786 a committee was formed to relieve the Black poor. The following year a group of several hundred Black settlers, with some white companions, sailed for Sierra Leone under a scheme that its sponsors presented as a refuge and that many participants experienced as an uncertain and costly resettlement. Abolitionists, philanthropists, and officials debated whether the plan served the settlers or removed an unwanted population, and the episode shows how questions of belonging in Europe were tied to wider imperial projects.

Section 73.25

Merchant Diasporas, Cross-Cultural Intermediaries, and Mobile Commercial Communities

Trade across political and religious boundaries depended on communities who shared language, faith, and kinship over long distances. Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain in 1492 and Portugal in the 1490s resettled in Amsterdam, Hamburg, Livorno, Bordeaux, and London, and some maintained family ties with Brazil, the Caribbean, North Africa, and the Ottoman Empire. They were active in sugar, tobacco, chocolate, diamonds, and finance, and the Portuguese Synagogue in Amsterdam, completed in 1675, was a sign of a prosperous community. Jews were formally readmitted to England in 1656 by an informal arrangement, and in many places they faced legal restrictions on residence, property, and occupation.

Armenian merchants from New Julfa, near Isfahan, built networks stretching from India and Southeast Asia to Venice, Marseille, and Amsterdam, trading silk, textiles, and other goods. Greek and Italian merchants operated in the eastern Mediterranean and in the Black Sea ports. Huguenots expelled from France after 1685 carried skills and capital to the Dutch Republic, England, and German states. Quakers in Britain and Pennsylvania formed trading networks based on trust and a reputation for honesty. Genoese, Flemish, and German merchants and bankers lived in Seville, Cadiz, and Lisbon, where they handled much of the Spanish and Portuguese colonial trade.

Cross-cultural intermediaries made European commerce possible outside Europe. Indian brokers, called banyans and dubashes, negotiated for European companies, advanced credit, and managed local knowledge. In Canton, merchants of the Hong guild dealt with foreign traders under the rules of the Qing court. African rulers, merchants, and brokers set prices and terms for the trade on the African coast. Interpreters, scribes, and mediators of many origins translated language and custom. Europeans depended on them for information about markets, goods, and politics, though European records rarely give them full credit.

These networks moved information and capital across borders faster than states could. A merchant in Amsterdam might learn of a price change in Surat or a shipwreck near Cadiz through a relative or coreligionist. Diasporas were also vulnerable. Minorities could be subject to discrimination, expulsion, or confiscation, and the same connections that made them useful could provoke suspicion. The 1753 attempt in Britain to ease naturalization for Jews was repealed the following year after a public outcry, which shows that commercial value did not bring full social acceptance.

Section 73.26

Gender, Families, Domestic Labor, and the Social Effects of Imperial Commerce

Imperial commerce reached women through shops, workshops, households, and investment, and its effects differed with class and legal status. In towns, women worked as milliners, haberdashers, grocers, tea dealers, pawnbrokers, and market sellers, and trade cards and shop accounts show them as proprietors and not only as assistants. Many shopkeepers were widows who continued a husband's business, and in some cities widows held considerable commercial roles. In England, the law of coverture limited married women's legal control over property, although custom and equity allowed some exceptions, and in the Dutch Republic and parts of France rules on inheritance and partnership gave women different, sometimes stronger, positions.

Households were also sites of work and purchase. Women generally managed domestic consumption, selected tea services, textiles, and furnishings, and kept accounts. Domestic service grew in port and capital cities, and young women from the countryside filled it, often under low wages and the control of employers. The cost of living and the availability of imported goods affected family budgets, so that wives of artisans and laborers weighed whether to buy a cheap printed cotton or a pound of sugar. Women in the households of sailors and soldiers ran families alone for long periods and sometimes depended on credit from local shopkeepers.

Women also invested. Records of stockholders in the Bank of England and the East India Company include a notable minority of women, among them widows and unmarried women who held public funds as a source of income. Some women owned shares in ships, and others owned or inherited plantations and enslaved people in the colonies, so that women were participants in slavery as well as critics of it. The boycott of 1791 and 1792, discussed in Section 73.22, drew on women's authority over the household table.

Contemporary commentators often criticized consumption as feminine and luxurious, blaming women for fashions in dress and tea drinking while ignoring the male merchants who supplied them. The gendered effects of commerce were unequal. Some women gained income and independence, while many others, including servants, spinners, and finishers, worked for low wages in insecure conditions. The evidence of wills, shop records, and account books allows historians to see these roles more clearly than formal law alone suggests.

Women's economic participation was not confined to choosing objects for a domestic interior. Widows might inherit stock or debts and continue a shop; unmarried women and servants could work in retail, processing, or household production; and merchant wives often oversaw credit relationships when male partners were at sea. Formal rules about property and professional membership could restrict their independence, and those rules varied among European jurisdictions. Because tax and company documents were usually written under male names, female work can become invisible in broad estimates of commercial activity. Household accounts, legal disputes, letters, and advertisements offer partial ways to recover it. These sources make clear that the consumer revolution depended on the management of labor and money as much as the purchase of imported goods.

Section 73.27

Cosmopolitan Cities, Racial Hierarchies, and the Boundaries of European Belonging

Port cities brought together people of many origins. Amsterdam had large communities of Jews, Huguenots, Germans, Scandinavians, and others. London included Dutch, Huguenot, Jewish, Irish, African, and South Asian residents, with Muslims and other visitors from the Ottoman and Moroccan worlds passing through. Coffeehouses, exchanges, churches, and markets were meeting places. Parish registers record marriages between people of different backgrounds, and neighborhoods near the docks mixed languages and trades.

This openness coexisted with hierarchy. Early modern Europeans classified people mainly by religion, legal status, and rank, and categories such as Christian and heathen, free and enslaved, or noble and commoner mattered more than a settled idea of race. Over the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries the language of lineage and color began to harden. Spanish rules on purity of blood targeted converts from Judaism and Islam, and colonial legal systems classified people by ancestry. The French Code Noir of 1685 regulated slavery in the colonies. In the eighteenth century naturalists such as Carl Linnaeus classified humans into varieties, and Johann Friedrich Blumenbach's work of 1775 divided humanity into groups, ideas that Chapter 103 will treat as part of the later development of racial science. The categories of 1600 should not be confused with those of the nineteenth century.

The boundaries of belonging varied by place and could shift. Jews were required to live in a ghetto in Venice from 1516 and in Rome, while enjoying fuller rights in some Dutch cities. Roma were targeted by harsh laws in many states, including expulsion and forced labor, even when they had lived locally for generations. Baptism did not guarantee equal treatment for Africans and Asians. Social mobility existed for a few, but most people of color in Europe faced barriers in guilds, marriage, and employment.

These tensions suggest that commercial openness did not produce equality. Cities welcomed merchants who brought capital and skills, and also created law and custom that excluded or subordinated others. Imperial wealth and imperial ideas of difference grew together. Understanding both is essential to the argument of this chapter, since the same networks that supplied sugar, porcelain, and knowledge also shaped ideas about who belonged in Europe.

Legal status did not map neatly onto physical presence. A sailor from South Asia, a free Black writer, a person held in domestic slavery, and an African merchant visiting a European port could all inhabit the same city but face different rights and restrictions. Courts, church records, advertisements, and published testimony reveal occasional legal challenges and acts of self-assertion, but they represent only a fraction of lived experience. A cosmopolitan trading center was therefore not automatically a tolerant or equal one. To analyze changing ideas of belonging, historians must compare formal law, public argument, and everyday treatment, and distinguish an individual's religion, occupation, birth, and legal condition rather than collapse them into a single racial category.

Division VI · Sections 73.28 to 73.33

Science, Natural History, Knowledge, and the Expansion of European Intellectual Horizons

Overseas connections enlarged European knowledge, often through non-European guides, interpreters, healers, sailors, and cultivators. This division distinguishes knowledge exchange from knowledge appropriation and attends to chronology, since the institutional imperial science of the nineteenth century should not be projected back onto earlier decades.

Section 73.28

Global Geography, Cartography, and the Reorganization of European Knowledge

Maps were tools of navigation, trade, and claim. Portuguese and Spanish authorities treated charts as state secrets, and the Spanish Casa de Contratacion maintained a master chart, the Padron Real, which pilots were supposed to update with new information. Dutch mapmakers in Amsterdam, including the Blaeu and Hondius families, produced printed atlases that gathered information from many sources and sold it widely. Mercator's projection of 1569 served navigators by representing lines of constant compass bearing as straight lines. The Dutch East India Company employed its own cartographers, among them Hessel Gerritsz from 1617, and kept many of its charts private.

States later organized hydrography more systematically. France created the Depot des cartes et plans de la marine in 1720, and Britain's Admiralty established a hydrographic office in 1795, near the end of the period. The solution of the longitude problem in the eighteenth century, aided by Parliament's Longitude Act of 1714 and by the chronometer, made oceanic positions more accurate. Surveys by navigators such as James Cook produced charts of coasts and islands, and published accounts spread the results. Mapping was also political. Maps asserted claims in treaties, disputes over boundaries, and the promotion of colonies.

Maps depended on information from outside Europe. Local pilots, coastal traders, and Indigenous guides supplied knowledge of coasts, rivers, and winds, and Asian and Arab charts and sailing directions circulated. European maps often left blanks or filled them with rumor, especially of the interior of Africa. The French geographer Jean Baptiste Bourguignon d'Anville, in his map of Africa of 1749, deliberately removed unverified features, which left much of the interior empty and made the limits of knowledge visible.

The effect on European knowledge was substantial but uneven. Printed maps and atlases made a global picture available to educated readers and merchants, and shaped how Europeans imagined the world. They also embedded European names, categories, and claims, and concealed the contributions of non-European informants. A map is therefore evidence both of what Europeans learned and of how they chose to represent, and sometimes to claim, what they learned.

An atlas could make an empire appear coherent even where control on the ground was fragile. Coastal outlines often rested on navigators' surveys, while the interiors of large landmasses could be drawn from travelers' reports, older maps, or speculation. Maps also selected which names and boundaries to display, which was a political as well as a technical decision. Merchants wanted reliable harbors, distances, and routes; officials might want lines implying jurisdiction; naturalists looked for the distribution of species or climates. The history of mapping thus connects practical navigation, European claims to authority, and contributions by pilots and residents whose names were not always recorded in the printed map. A map is an interpretation of evidence, not merely an illustration of settled facts.

Section 73.29

Botany, Natural History, Medicine, and the Exchange of Useful Knowledge

Europeans encountered thousands of unfamiliar plants, animals, and materials overseas, and the knowledge of how to use them usually came from people who lived there. Peruvian bark, the source of the later drug quinine, reached Europe in the seventeenth century and became a leading remedy for fevers, including malaria, often distributed by Jesuits. The stories of how it was first introduced are partly legendary, but the evidence points to Andean knowledge of the tree. Ipecacuanha from Brazil, guaiacum from the Caribbean, and many other medicines entered European pharmacies, and merchants and apothecaries traded in them as valuable commodities.

Some European naturalists acknowledged their sources, and others did not. Garcia de Orta, a Portuguese physician in Goa, published his Colloquies on the simples and drugs of India in 1563 after discussions with local physicians and merchants. The Hortus Malabaricus, published at Amsterdam in the late seventeenth century under the direction of Hendrik van Rheede, the Dutch governor of Malabar, was compiled with the help of Indian physicians and scholars, notably Itty Achuden, a Kerala physician whose knowledge the work records. Maria Sibylla Merian traveled to Suriname in 1699 and published her Metamorphosis of the insects of Suriname in 1705, and she wrote that she learned from enslaved and Indigenous people there. In Jamaica, Hans Sloane gathered plants and information in 1687 and 1688 with the help of enslaved Africans and local healers.

Natural history followed commerce. Plants of potential economic value, such as spices, dyes, drugs, timber, and food crops, attracted particular interest. Classification systems, notably the binomial naming system that Carl Linnaeus used in his Species Plantarum of 1753, helped organize specimens from all over the world, and Linnaeus's students traveled to collect for Uppsala. Classification gave European scholars authority, and it often replaced local names and the places and people from whom knowledge was obtained.

It is useful to distinguish exchange from appropriation. Exchange suggests mutual benefit and acknowledgment, as in some collaborations between European scholars and local experts. Appropriation describes cases where knowledge was taken, credited to Europeans, or used for profit without the consent or recognition of its holders. Both occurred, often within the same project, and the sources, written mostly by Europeans, make the contributions of local specialists easier to overlook than to document.

Section 73.30

Botanical Gardens, Learned Societies, and Imperial Scientific Institutions

Gardens and societies gave natural knowledge an institutional home. University gardens were established at Padua in 1545 and Leiden in 1590, and the king's garden at Paris, founded in 1635, became a center of research and teaching. The Chelsea Physic Garden was founded in London in 1673 by apothecaries. The Dutch East India Company supplied plants to the garden at Amsterdam, and Dutch colonies such as Cape Town served as stopping places for plant transfers. The Royal Society of London, founded in 1660, and the Paris Academy of Sciences, founded in 1666, published reports from correspondents overseas and encouraged observation and collection.

Joseph Banks gave British science a major patron in the later eighteenth century. Banks sailed on James Cook's first Pacific voyage, became President of the Royal Society in 1778, and from about 1773 advised the royal gardens at Kew. He maintained a vast correspondence with collectors, gardeners, naval officers, and officials, and arranged plant exchanges. Kew's development as a national botanical institution with a worldwide network belongs largely to the nineteenth century, under later directors. It should not be projected back onto Banks's lifetime, although his work laid important foundations.

Economic interests shaped the movement of plants. The most famous example is breadfruit. Banks and others promoted transporting breadfruit trees from the Pacific to the Caribbean as food for enslaved people, and the voyage of HMS Bounty under William Bligh in 1787 to 1789 ended in a mutiny. A second expedition under Bligh succeeded in delivering plants to St Vincent and Jamaica in 1793. The French administrator Pierre Poivre moved spice plants to Mauritius in the 1750s and 1770s in an effort to break the Dutch monopoly. These cases link science with imperial strategy and slave labor, and also with Indigenous knowledge of cultivating the plants.

Learned societies also created wider scientific communities. Letters, specimens, and reports moved among Leiden, Uppsala, Paris, London, and many provincial and overseas towns. Women, though largely excluded from formal membership, took part as collectors, illustrators, and patrons. Colonial settlers, missionaries, and officials formed local networks and sent reports. The institutions of knowledge thus grew from the combined efforts of many hands, though credit and authority went mostly to those in European centers.

Section 73.31

Collections, Museums, Cabinets of Curiosity, and the Material Culture of Knowledge

Collecting was a central practice of early modern natural history. Princes, physicians, apothecaries, and merchants assembled cabinets of curiosities, which combined natural specimens, artifacts, antiquities, and works of art. Ole Worm's museum at Copenhagen, the collection of Rudolf II at Prague, and the Tradescant collection that formed the basis of the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford in 1683 are well known examples. Dutch physicians and merchants assembled collections of shells, insects, and preserved specimens, and much of this material came from the East and West Indies.

Hans Sloane assembled one of the largest collections of his age, drawing on his time in Jamaica, his purchases, and exchanges with collectors worldwide. He married the widow of a Jamaican planter, which brought him income from plantations worked by enslaved people. When he died in 1753, Parliament passed an act to purchase his collection, and it formed the foundation of the British Museum, which opened in 1759. The history of that collection shows how scientific and commercial networks were connected to slavery and empire.

Collections organized knowledge through classification and display. Specimens were arranged, labeled, and catalogued, and the order of the cabinet reflected the collector's ideas about nature and human societies. Objects from outside Europe, including tools, textiles, weapons, and religious items, entered collections through trade, gift, purchase, and sometimes seizure. The voyages of Cook brought back large numbers of Pacific objects, which reached private and public collections.

These practices raise evidentiary and ethical questions. Records of how objects were acquired are often thin, and the people who made them are seldom named. Where objects were taken under unequal power relationships, including violence or coercion, the circumstances affect how they are understood. Historians read catalogues, correspondence, and labels with attention to what they reveal and what they omit, and modern debates over return and display have made these questions a matter of public interest, although those later debates fall outside this chapter.

Collecting was also a social practice, and some of its leading figures were women. Margaret Cavendish Bentinck, Duchess of Portland, assembled one of the largest private collections of shells and natural history objects in eighteenth century England and corresponded with leading naturalists. Her museum was dispersed at auction after her death in 1785, a reminder that private collections were often broken up and that the permanent public institution was the exception. The British Museum, when it opened in 1759, admitted visitors by application and ticket, not freely, and its first holdings combined natural history, books, manuscripts, and antiquities. The institutions that later became public museums thus grew from private wealth, much of it linked to commerce and empire, and the question of who was entitled to see and interpret the collections was settled only gradually.

Section 73.32

Travel Writing, Ethnography, Visual Representation, and Ideas of Human Difference

Travel accounts were among the most widely read books of the early modern period. Collections such as Richard Hakluyt's of the late sixteenth century, the illustrated volumes issued by Theodor de Bry from the 1590s, and the large eighteenth century compilations of voyages brought reports from Asia, Africa, and the Americas to European readers. Jean de Lery described the Tupinamba of Brazil, and travelers such as Francois Bernier, Jean Chardin, and Jean Baptiste Tavernier wrote of Mughal India and Persia. Missionaries, especially Jesuits, sent reports on China, Japan, India, and the Americas. Engravings spread images that conveyed meanings of their own, and some were produced by artists who never saw the places they depicted.

Authors and readers interpreted what they described through existing frameworks. Religion supplied categories of Christian, Jewish, Muslim, and heathen, and classical learning supplied comparisons with ancient Greeks and Romans. Writers also used overseas societies to comment on Europe. Montaigne's essay on cannibals in the 1580s turned an account of Brazilian customs into a critique of European cruelty, and Montesquieu's Persian Letters of 1721 imagined Persian visitors criticizing French society. The abbe Raynal's history of the two Indies, published from 1770 with contributions from Diderot and others, criticized slavery and colonial conquest even as it celebrated commerce.

Descriptions of other peoples could be sympathetic, curious, hostile, or exploitative, and they often mixed several attitudes. Stereotypes about Africans, Asians, and Indigenous Americans developed and circulated through print. Some authors attributed differences to climate and custom, and others began to sort humans into classified groups, as Section 73.27 noted. Such classifications drew on travel reports whose accuracy was uneven and whose authors were rarely neutral. They were not yet the fully developed racial science of the nineteenth century, discussed in Chapter 103.

Readers and sources also crossed in the other direction. Asian, African, and Pacific visitors to Europe wrote or spoke about what they saw, and Mirza Abu Taleb Khan, an Indian visitor to Britain and Ireland from 1799 to 1802, wrote an account of his journey. Even so, most surviving travel literature in European languages reflects European authors, publishers, and markets. Historians therefore read these works as evidence of European perceptions as much as of the societies described, and they look for other evidence to test what they claim.

The same travel account could be read for very different purposes. A merchant might look for commodities and ports, a naturalist for unfamiliar plants, a missionary for religious practices, and a political writer for examples of laws or social arrangements. Publishers selected episodes that attracted readers, and illustrations could amplify the appearance of difference. Consequently, the popularity of a narrative tells historians something about the European market for information but does not guarantee the accuracy of its descriptions. Comparing the account with local sources, material objects, and other travelers can reveal mistranslation or selective observation. This distinction is vital when European writing is used to characterize societies that already possessed their own institutions and explanations of the world.

Section 73.33

Scientific Voyages, the Pacific, and the Widening European Worldview

In the second half of the eighteenth century, states sent ships to the Pacific on voyages that combined exploration, science, and imperial rivalry. Louis Antoine de Bougainville circumnavigated the globe in 1766 to 1769 and reached Tahiti in 1768. His party included the botanist Philibert Commerson, and Jeanne Baret, who traveled disguised as a man as Commerson's assistant and is recognized as the first woman to complete a circumnavigation. Bougainville's published account of 1771 influenced European views of Tahiti, and Denis Diderot used it as a springboard for a philosophical dialogue, written in the 1770s and published later, on European and Polynesian morals.

Britain's Royal Society and Admiralty sponsored James Cook's first voyage on HMS Endeavour, which sailed in 1768. Its purposes included observing the transit of Venus at Tahiti in June 1769 and then carrying out secret instructions to search for lands to the south. Joseph Banks and the Swedish naturalist Daniel Solander joined as naturalists. At Tahiti the party took on Tupaia, an expert navigator and priest from Raiatea, who drew a chart of the islands of the central Pacific and helped communicate with other Polynesians. He died at Batavia in 1770. Cook then charted New Zealand, where encounters with Maori included violence in which Cook's men killed several people, and the eastern coast of Australia, where he landed at the place later called Botany Bay in April 1770. Cook made two further voyages and was killed in Hawaii in 1779.

The voyages enlarged European knowledge. They brought back charts, specimens, drawings, and descriptions, and they improved the use of chronometers and methods against scurvy. Mai, a Polynesian from Raiatea, traveled to England on Cook's second voyage in 1774, was received in society and painted by Joshua Reynolds, and returned home on the third voyage. The accounts of the voyages were widely read and shaped views of Pacific peoples as both exotic and as subjects for study.

They also raised questions about sovereignty and authority. Cook's instructions in principle called for the consent of Indigenous peoples before taking possession of land, but in practice his parties claimed territory in the name of the Crown without a negotiated agreement, and the British later acted on the assumption of sovereignty over Australia and New Zealand with consequences examined in later volumes. Indigenous peoples were guides, hosts, adversaries, and negotiators, and their own accounts, preserved in oral tradition and later writing, differ from European records. The notion of discovery thus hid prior habitation and local knowledge, and it merged scientific curiosity with imperial claim.

The Pacific voyages also had consequences for the peoples visited, which European records record unevenly. Contact brought new diseases, exchange of iron tools and other goods, and in some places violence and conflict over resources and customs. Historians debate the precise effects, including the spread of venereal disease in the Pacific, because the evidence is incomplete and attributions are difficult. Cook himself expressed concern about the introduction of disease, and at Hawaii in 1779 misunderstandings and tensions over supplies, theft, and ritual contributed to the fighting in which he was killed. Pacific communities were not passive. Tahitians, Maori, Hawaiians, and Aboriginal peoples pursued their own interests in dealing with the visitors, sometimes welcoming them and sometimes resisting, and their later accounts, preserved in oral history, provide a counterweight to the narratives of discovery that European publications created.

Division VII · Sections 73.34 to 73.38

Imperial Competition, Political Culture, and Europe's Uneven Transformation

This division links the economic, social, and cultural changes of the earlier divisions to political institutions and public argument. It does not retell the wars of Chapter 72 or the full development of the early modern state, which Chapter 74 takes up.

Section 73.34

Imperial Rivalry, Naval Warfare, and the Strengthening of European States

Overseas rivalry influenced how states spent money, borrowed, and organized power. Navies were expensive. A ship of the line required timber, ironwork, rope, sails, guns, and a large crew, and the dockyards that built and repaired fleets, such as Portsmouth and Chatham in England and Brest and Toulon in France, were among the largest enterprises of the age. They employed thousands of skilled workers and purchased supplies from many regions, including Baltic naval stores. Government contracts created a class of suppliers, shipbuilders, and victuallers whose businesses depended on public spending.

States responded differently. The Dutch Republic, which had been the leading naval and commercial power in the seventeenth century, found the cost of a large navy and a land army increasingly hard to bear in the eighteenth. France built a large navy under Colbert in the 1660s and 1670s, saw it weakened in later wars, and rebuilt it after the Seven Years' War. Spain's Bourbon rulers reformed the fleet and colonial administration in the eighteenth century. Britain gave the navy a central place, and its governments were willing to borrow heavily for it. The cost of the Seven Years' War, which Chapter 72 examines, left the British public debt nearly double its prewar level, and the attempt to raise revenue from the American colonies after 1763 followed from that burden.

Competition also affected who had influence. Merchants, shipowners, financiers, and contractors lobbied for protection of trade routes, colonies, and monopolies, and their interests were represented in Parliament and in the courts of other states. Naval victories and defeats had political effects, since defeat could damage governments and create pressure for reform. Debates arose between those who favored maritime and colonial strategy and those who preferred continental commitments, and between taxpayers and the beneficiaries of public spending.

The result was a pattern in which commercial expansion, war, taxation, and credit supported each other in some states, and strained others. Overseas rivalry did not by itself create the early modern state, but it increased its fiscal and administrative capacity, a theme that Chapter 74 develops through the study of armies, bureaucracies, and centralization.

War finance changed the administrative meaning of overseas trade. Customs officers, clerks, naval victuallers, and creditors were part of a system that could turn commercial activity into supplies and loans for governments. During war a larger fleet required timber, iron, rope, food, and wages, linking the treasury to contractors and inland producers. The same machinery produced controversy over evasion, patronage, corruption, and debt service. Parliament in Britain could vote taxes and scrutinize borrowing, whereas other European monarchies negotiated with different corporate bodies and lenders. These contrasts help explain why imperial rivalry affected states differently without reducing government capacity to the number of colonies possessed.

Section 73.35

Mercantilism, Commercial Monopoly, and the Rise of New Political Economy

European governments regulated overseas trade extensively. The English Navigation Acts, beginning in 1651 and extended in 1660 and 1663, required that certain colonial goods be carried in English ships and shipped through English ports. France under Colbert used tariffs, subsidies, and chartered companies to promote domestic manufactures and colonial trade. Spain and Portugal restricted colonial commerce to licensed ports and merchants. Chartered companies, including the English and Dutch East India Companies and the Royal African Company, held monopolies in specified trades. The Royal African Company lost its monopoly in 1698, when Parliament opened the trade to private merchants, which is a reminder that monopolies could be contested and changed.

Later writers grouped these policies under the label mercantilism. The term was popularized by critics, including Adam Smith, who called it the mercantile system, and many historians regard it as a convenient label for a varied set of practices and ideas, not a single doctrine. Some writers, such as Thomas Mun in the 1620s and 1630s, argued that a favorable balance of trade would bring wealth. Others stressed the encouragement of manufactures, shipping, and employment, or the strength of the state in rivalry with others. The aim of power and the aim of wealth were closely tied.

Critics challenged this order. In France the physiocrats of the 1750s and 1760s, led by Francois Quesnay, argued that agriculture was the source of wealth and that trade should be freer, and Vincent de Gournay is associated with the phrase laissez faire. David Hume argued in 1752 that trade imbalances would correct themselves through the movement of prices and money, and that one nation's prosperity did not require another's poverty. Practical reform followed in some places, notably Spain's decree of 1778 permitting freer trade among more ports of the empire.

Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations of 1776 gave the critique its fullest form. Smith argued that monopoly benefited a small group of merchants while raising prices for consumers and diverting capital to less productive uses, and that the colonial system cost the metropolis more than it gained, particularly through the expense of defense. His argument did not end state regulation, and he accepted some, such as navigation laws for defense. States continued to defend monopolies and restrictions long after 1776. The importance of the new political economy lies in its showing that the claims of empire could be questioned in economic terms.

Section 73.36

Empire, Printing, Patriotism, and European Public Opinion

Print carried empire into public life. Newspapers and periodicals multiplied in London and other cities in the eighteenth century, and they reported on fleets, colonies, trade, and wars. Pamphlets argued over taxation, monopoly, and policy. Travel books and illustrated atlases supplied images and information. Coffeehouses, clubs, and reading societies provided places where such material was read and discussed. Advertisements, as Section 73.11 noted, made imported goods part of the same public world.

Patriotic celebration attached to naval and commercial success. The capture of Portobello in 1739 by Admiral Edward Vernon during the war with Spain was celebrated with medals, songs, and pottery, and the London district called Portobello commemorates it. The song Rule, Britannia, which first appeared in 1740 in a masque with words by James Thomson and music by Thomas Arne, linked national identity to the sea. Similar celebrations occurred in other states. Critics noted that patriotic enthusiasm served commercial interest and political advantage.

Interest groups used the press and petitions. East and West India merchants, planters, manufacturers, and shipowners lobbied Parliament and wrote pamphlets, and anti-monopoly voices challenged them. Controversy over the East India Company grew in the 1770s and 1780s, and Edmund Burke's campaign against Warren Hastings, who was impeached in 1788, made questions of conduct in India a matter of public concern. In France, Raynal's history of the Indies and other works criticized conquest and slavery, and in the Netherlands, pamphlet debates addressed commercial decline and reform.

Public opinion did not speak with one voice. Arguments for empire cited trade, employment, security, and national honor, and arguments against cited cost, corruption, injustice, and violence. Antislavery campaigns of the 1780s and 1790s, discussed in Section 73.22, added a moral argument that cut across commercial ones. The existence of a public that debated overseas power meant that empire could be defended or condemned in print, and that governments had to consider opinion, in ways that differed by state and censorship regime.

Printed criticism was itself shaped by commercial and political circumstances. A pamphlet against a monopoly might come from a competing merchant, while a defense of naval expansion could reflect the interests of shipowners or contractors. Travelers and naturalists published accounts that supplied genuine information but also offered dramatic stories for a paying readership. Black abolitionist writers used these same print networks to challenge claims that commerce excused human bondage. Readers, correspondents, and religious associations could carry the debate beyond metropolitan governments, though censorship, literacy, and the price of print limited participation. Public opinion was therefore neither a single verdict nor a force independent of economic interests: it was a contested arena in which people attempted to define what imperial power meant and whether it could be justified.

Primary Source Window

Adam Smith, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), Book IV, Chapter VII, "Of Colonies"

Creator: Adam Smith, Scottish moral philosopher and political economist · Date: published in London, 1776 · Type: Printed treatise. Students should use a scholarly edition, such as the Glasgow Edition, rather than an anthology extract.

Context: Smith wrote as Britain was in conflict with its American colonies, and his chapter on colonies is one of the longest in the book. It discusses the motives for founding colonies, the advantages of new lands, and the effects of the colonial monopoly.

Paraphrase, not a quotation: Smith argued that the monopoly of the colonial trade enriched a small body of merchants at the expense of British consumers and taxpayers, that it directed capital into less productive channels, and that the cost of defending and governing colonies exceeded the benefits.

Analysis: The chapter is a leading statement of the case against monopoly and the colonial system, and it shows the new political economy in an argued form. It reflects Smith's views and the debates of his time, and it does not represent all opinion. Smith also treats the discovery of America and the Cape route as major events in human history, yet he judged much of the policy that followed to be harmful. The chapter cannot tell us how policy changed in practice, or how colonized peoples and enslaved workers experienced the system.

Section 73.37

European Winners and Losers: Comparing the Consequences of Empire

Britain gained a large commercial, financial, and naval position. London and the Atlantic ports grew, public credit supported war, and imperial trade fed shipping, finance, and manufacturing. These gains came with costs and reversals. The loss of the thirteen American colonies in 1783 removed a major part of the empire, public debt was heavy, and domestic poverty and inequality continued alongside commercial prosperity. The Dutch Republic held a pre-eminent position in finance and shipping into the eighteenth century but lost relative strength in manufacturing and naval power. The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War of 1780 to 1784 damaged its trade, and the Dutch East India Company, burdened with debt, lost its charter in 1799.

France developed rich Atlantic ports and a large colonial sector, with Saint-Domingue among the greatest sugar and coffee producers of the age, and Bordeaux and Nantes grew accordingly. The losses of Canada and most of its Indian possessions in 1763 reduced its position, while war debts contributed to a fiscal crisis in the 1780s. Colonial wealth was concentrated in port cities and a few families, and much of France remained agrarian. Portugal received gold and other wealth from Brazil in the eighteenth century, yet Britain dominated much of its trade after the Methuen treaty of 1703, and the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 devastated the capital. Spain's Bourbon reforms improved administration and trade, but the economy remained heavily agrarian and dependent on colonial revenue.

Regions with few or no colonies were entangled in different ways. German linen and metalware districts, Swiss and Italian textile producers, and Scandinavian iron and timber exporters supplied goods that moved through the great ports. Poland-Lithuania and the Baltic lands exported grain, timber, and flax to the west through Danzig and other ports, a trade that tied their landowners and the serfs who worked for them to western markets and strengthened the systems of labor that Chapter 85 will discuss. Russia exported iron, hemp, and timber to Britain and other western states. The Ottoman Empire both traded with Europe and competed with it, and the Mediterranean world saw a shift in the balance of trade toward Atlantic powers.

Comparison shows that no single pattern of gain applied. Having colonies did not guarantee strength, and lacking them did not rule out participation. Gains depended on institutions, access to capital and credit, ports, military power, and the structure of domestic economies. Within each state, merchants, financiers, and some manufacturers gained more than farmers, laborers, and those who lived far from the coast. Colonized and enslaved peoples bore the heaviest costs. Empire thus contributed to uneven development both between and within European societies.

Section 73.38

Empire and the Transformation of Europe: Historical Synthesis

The evidence examined in this chapter supports a cautious but substantial conclusion. Overseas connections changed European consumption, as tea, sugar, tobacco, cottons, and porcelain became part of everyday life for many households in northwestern Europe and some elsewhere. They expanded and redirected commercial and financial institutions, including insurance, credit, banks, and securities, and linked these to government borrowing and taxation. They tied European wealth to slavery and other forms of coerced labor, and they brought migrants, travelers, and ideas into European cities. They enlarged the range of European knowledge, often through the unrecognized work of non-European specialists, and they gave new force to debates about monopoly, political economy, public opinion, and morality.

The chapter has also rejected two simple claims. One holds that empire alone created modern Europe. The evidence shows instead that European changes depended on domestic agriculture, population growth, technology, institutions, household labor, retail networks, and the pressures of war, and that imperial trade interacted with these. The opposite claim holds that overseas expansion made little difference. This too fails, since customs revenue, port growth, financial institutions, consumption, migration, and scientific collections all show effects that cannot be explained without empire. The question is always who changed, how, and by what mechanism.

Continuity was as important as change. Most Europeans in 1800 still worked in agriculture, lived in rural communities, and consumed little that came from overseas. Older practices of credit, insurance, and long distance trade continued, with Mediterranean and Asian precedents, as did social hierarchies of rank, religion, and gender. The consumer revolution reached some regions and classes far more than others, and the benefits of empire were concentrated in ports, among merchants and investors, and in states able to borrow and fight. The costs fell most heavily on enslaved Africans, colonized peoples, and many workers in Europe.

The chapter also shows European history as part of wider systems. African, Asian, American, and Pacific peoples were producers, traders, intermediaries, informants, and critics, and their actions shaped what Europeans could do and know. European agency is not the only agency. The outcomes were not inevitable. Different decisions about trade, finance, war, and labor were possible, and historical actors argued for them.

This synthesis leads to Chapter 74. Chapter 73 has examined how overseas connections affected economies, institutions, material culture, knowledge, and political capacity. Chapter 74 turns to the internal institutions through which European states attempted to organize and exercise power, including taxation, armies, bureaucracy, law, and diplomacy, and to the limits of centralization. Chapter 85 will return to consumption in a broader domestic framework, and Chapter 93 to the industrial transformation whose commercial preconditions were discussed here.

Cause and Effect

How Demand for Overseas Commodities Changed European Commerce, Labor, Finance, Revenue, and Consumption

Long-term causes

Older merchant networks, Mediterranean and Asian credit practices, Iberian voyages, plantation slavery, rising incomes in parts of northwestern Europe, and the growth of port cities.

Short-term causes

Falling prices of sugar and other goods, new retail outlets, changing fashion, wars that raised fiscal needs, and reductions of duties such as the Commutation Act of 1784.

Immediate effects

Growth of ports and refining, new employment in shipping and processing, wider use of credit and insurance, higher customs revenue, and broader consumption of imported goods.

Long-term consequences

Larger financial markets and public debts, concentrated mercantile wealth, new political arguments over monopoly and slavery, and a growing public that debated empire.

Historical complexity: Each effect had domestic as well as overseas causes. Colonial commodities did not make the consumer revolution alone, and war shaped finance as much as trade did. The costs and gains fell unevenly on different classes and regions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Household Consumption, Commercial Institutions, and Knowledge Networks, c. 1550 to 1800

What changed? Sugar, tea, tobacco, printed cottons, and porcelain became more available and cheaper in northwestern Europe. Insurance, bills of exchange, banks, and public securities grew in scale. Scientific collecting and correspondence reached across oceans.

What remained continuous? Agriculture employed most people, rank and religion structured society, and older Italian and Asian credit practices underlay new institutions. Local markets and household production continued to supply most everyday goods.

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of ports, merchants, refiners, dockworkers, and urban middling households. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural households in much of eastern and southern Europe, and most farm laborers.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows a real but uneven transformation, and it warns against assuming that all Europeans or all regions shared the same experience of empire.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Households: Comparative Material Culture

The descriptions below are composite patterns that historians draw from probate inventories and other records. They are not records of particular households.

A prosperous merchant household in Amsterdam or London might contain porcelain tea services, cotton and silk hangings, lacquered or japanned furniture, books and maps, and a store of sugar, spices, and tobacco. Servants prepared meals and tea, and some servants or enslaved attendants came from outside Europe. Such a household bought through merchants, shops, and auctions, and its wealth often derived from trade.

A middling urban household of shopkeepers or artisans in a large port or capital might own a few pieces of earthenware or imitation porcelain, a tea kettle, printed cotton curtains or bed furnishings, a looking glass, and small quantities of sugar and tea. Women managed purchases, sometimes buying second hand, and the household balanced the cost of such goods against food, fuel, and rent.

A rural household in much of Europe relied on locally made goods such as wooden and coarse earthenware vessels, homespun linen and wool, and food from the farm. Imported goods, where they appeared, were few, and might include salt, a small amount of spice, or a ribbon or handkerchief bought from a peddler. Probate inventories reveal these contrasts but under-record the poorest households and those without recorded property.

Section 73.39

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This section consolidates the chapter's argument. Between about 1550 and 1800, overseas connections changed European societies through identifiable mechanisms: demand for commodities altered consumption and employment, trade enlarged commercial and financial institutions, taxation and borrowing tied imperial commerce to state power, migration and mobile communities altered the social composition of cities, and collecting and correspondence widened the range of European knowledge. Each mechanism had domestic partners, including agriculture, household labor, retail, and war finance, and none acted alone.

The geographical pattern was uneven. Port cities, mercantile families, financial centers, and districts supplying overseas markets felt the largest effects. The Dutch Republic and Britain combined shipping, credit, and re-export. France and Britain built large Atlantic sectors on slave produced goods. Iberian states held vast empires and gained less than their size implied. Central, northern, and eastern European regions took part through manufacturing, raw materials, and consumption. The measures historians use, whether volumes, values, revenue, profit, or household welfare, answer different questions, and no one indicator captures the whole.

The central terms to carry forward are consumer revolution, re-export trade, marine insurance, fiscal-military state, mercantilism, proto-industrialization, diaspora, and the distinction between knowledge exchange and knowledge appropriation. The debates to carry forward concern whether empire and slavery contributed significantly to industrialization, and whether imports or domestic factors drove consumption. Chapter 74 now turns from these global relationships to the internal institutions of early modern states.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Europe's growing connections with the Americas, Africa, and Asia transformed European economic institutions, consumption, social relations, intellectual life, and political power between the sixteenth and late eighteenth centuries. Its method has been to trace mechanisms, asking in each case who changed, how, and with what limits. It has not retold the voyages, conquests, plantation systems, or wars narrated in Chapters 68 to 72.

The first division set out the framework. Empire reached Europe through trade, consumption, investment, employment, migration, taxation, political power, and knowledge. Silver, textiles, plantation goods, and credit moved along intersecting circuits, and the effects fell unevenly, depending on a state's institutions, ports, and manufacturing. Measuring the effects is difficult, because customs records, merchant accounts, and probate inventories each answer only some questions, and import volumes, re-exports, profits, revenue, and welfare must be kept apart.

The second division examined consumption. Sugar, tea, coffee, tobacco, chocolate, printed cottons, porcelain, lacquer, spices, and dyes entered European life, but at different dates and prices, and for different classes and regions. The history of the printed cottons showed protection, imitation, and adaptation, and the Calico Act of 1721 illustrated how domestic manufacturers used law to defend their interests. The debate over the consumer revolution showed that imported goods and domestic production, retail, and household labor worked together.

The third division explained how commercial and financial institutions worked. Ports grew, shipbuilding and its supporting trades employed many, and merchants pooled capital through family firms, partnerships, and joint stock companies. Credit, bills of exchange, and marine insurance managed risk, and older Mediterranean and Asian methods were enlarged. The Bank of England, founded in 1694 to finance war with France, showed the close tie between finance and the fiscal-military state, and customs and excise revenues on imported goods supported public borrowing.

The fourth division connected colonial coercion with European wealth. Slave produced commodities entered European commerce through merchants, shippers, insurers, refiners, and retailers. Employment effects differed from profits, and gains concentrated among merchants, investors, and planters, while the enslaved bore the heaviest costs. Historians distinguish whether colonial commerce was profitable, whether it influenced development, and whether it was necessary for industrialization. Opposition to slavery grew in Europe through religious critics, Black writers such as Cugoano and Equiano, abolitionist organizations, and the sugar boycott of 1791 and 1792.

The fifth division treated migration and belonging. Europeans left for colonies and trading posts, and Africans, Asians, and others lived in European cities, often in unclear legal status. Merchant diasporas and cross-cultural intermediaries moved capital and information. Women worked as retailers, household managers, investors, and servants, and cities mixed openness with hierarchy as ideas about difference hardened without yet becoming the racial science of the nineteenth century.

The sixth division examined knowledge. Maps, botanical and medical knowledge, gardens, learned societies, and collections expanded European horizons, often through the work of local specialists whose contributions were unevenly acknowledged. Travel writing shaped ideas of difference, and the Pacific voyages of Bougainville and Cook combined science with imperial claim and raised questions about sovereignty. The distinction between exchange and appropriation was central.

The seventh division linked these changes to politics. Overseas rivalry increased naval spending, borrowing, and fiscal capacity, and the debate over monopoly and mercantilism produced a new political economy culminating in Adam Smith's work of 1776. Print created a public that debated empire. Comparison of Britain, the Dutch Republic, France, Portugal, Spain, and regions with few colonies showed uneven gains and limits. The synthesis rejected both the claim that empire alone created modern Europe and the claim that it made little difference, and it led to Chapter 74.

Key Terms

Consumer revolution
A historians' label for the growth in the range of goods bought by ordinary households, especially from about 1650 to 1800.
Re-export trade
The sale onward of imported goods to another market, so that imports overstate local consumption.
Marine insurance
Contracts in which underwriters accept a share of the risk of loss at sea in exchange for premiums.
Bill of exchange
A written order to pay a sum in another place, allowing value to move without coin.
Joint stock company
A business that raises capital by selling transferable shares to many investors.
Fiscal-military state
A state organized to raise taxes and loans in order to fight wars.
Public debt
Money borrowed by a government, repaid from tax revenue, and held by investors.
Customs duty
A tax on goods entering or leaving a state, a major source of revenue on imports.
Excise
A tax on the production or sale of goods within a state.
Mercantilism
A later label for varied policies that used regulation to promote national trade and power.
Monopoly
The exclusive right to a branch of trade, granted by a state to a company or group.
Navigation Acts
English laws from 1651 that required colonial trade to use English ships and ports.
Proto-industrialization
Rural household manufacturing for distant markets before factory production.
Calico
Cotton cloth from India, often printed, that became fashionable in Europe.
Indiennes
The French name for printed cotton cloths in Indian style.
Coverture
The English legal doctrine that merged a married woman's legal identity with her husband's.
Diaspora
A dispersed community that maintains ties across borders, as among Sephardic Jewish and Armenian merchants.
Cross-cultural intermediary
A broker, interpreter, or merchant who mediated between societies, such as a banyan or dubash.
Lascar
A South Asian seaman employed on European ships.
Cabinet of curiosities
A collection of natural specimens, artifacts, and curiosities assembled by a collector.
Botanical garden
A garden for the study and cultivation of plants, often connected to medicine and trade.
Binomial nomenclature
Linnaeus's system of naming species with a genus name and a species name.
Knowledge appropriation
The taking and use of knowledge without acknowledging or compensating its holders.
Abolitionism
The movement to end the slave trade and slavery.
Physiocrats
French thinkers of the mid eighteenth century who held that agriculture was the source of wealth.
Impressment
The forced recruitment of seamen into a navy.
Privateer
A privately owned ship authorized by a state to attack enemy shipping.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows empire as part of European history itself. Overseas connections entered kitchens, ledgers, parliaments, laboratories, and city streets, and they did so unevenly, with real gains for some and heavy costs for others.

It reveals larger processes that later chapters develop, including the growth of fiscal capacity examined in Chapter 74, the broader consumer society of Chapter 85, and the commercial preconditions of Chapter 93. Many of the arguments about slavery, belonging, and knowledge continued well beyond 1800.

For Volume III, the chapter closes Part XI by connecting Renaissance and Reformation Europe to the dynastic and imperial states that follow. It also reminds students that European change was never produced by Europeans alone, and that outcomes were contingent, not inevitable.
