Caesar, Civil War, and the Fall of the Republic

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

By 30 BCE the Roman Republic had not been formally abolished. Consuls, praetors, tribunes, assemblies, and the Senate all still existed. What had vanished was the competitive settlement in which no single man could permanently monopolize armies and political decision making. Nearly every actor who helped destroy that settlement claimed to be defending the Republic.

In early January of 49 BCE, Marcus Tullius Cicero, one of the most experienced senators in Rome, wrote letters to his friend Titus Pomponius Atticus that reveal a man who did not know what was about to happen. He worried about Gnaeus Pompey, whose intentions he could not read. He worried about Gaius Julius Caesar, whose army stood in northern Italy. He wondered whether to remain near Rome, to follow Pompey, or to attempt some form of mediation. Cicero did not write as a man who knew that a civil war was inevitable, or that the Republic he had served for decades was entering its final phase. He wrote as a participant in a crisis whose outcome was still open. This chapter asks students to keep that uncertainty in view, because the story of the fall of the Republic is often told backward, from the Principate of Chapter 21 toward a supposedly inescapable collapse.

Chapter 19 ended with Sulla's death in 78 BCE and with a heavily altered Republic in which political killing, the use of armies against Rome itself, and emergency powers had all been shown to be possible. This chapter follows the consequences across nearly half a century, from 78 to 30 BCE. It examines a generation of commanders, Pompey, Crassus, Caesar, Antony, and the young Octavian, and of politicians such as Cicero, Cato, Clodius, Brutus, and Cassius. Its central problem is not the biography of any one of them. It is why a political order that had governed Rome's expansion for centuries, described in Chapter 15 and tested in Chapters 16 and 17, proved unable to contain competition among powerful commanders and coalitions, and how repeated civil wars destroyed the republican settlement even though almost every participant invoked liberty, the Senate, or the restoration of the Republic.

The chapter's narrative has several stages. The decade after Sulla saw his constitutional arrangements unravel while the Republic came to depend on individuals with extraordinary powers. Pompey's commands against pirates and King Mithridates showed how far those powers could extend. The conspiracy of Catiline in 63 BCE returned the problem of emergency measures to the center of politics. The informal alliance of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus then reshaped the competition, and Caesar's conquest of Gaul gave him wealth, an army, and a reputation to rival Pompey's. When the alliance broke, the result was war, and the victory of Caesar led not to stability but to a dictatorship whose character alarmed many senators. His assassination on the Ides of March in 44 BCE opened a second, more destructive round of conflict, in which the legally constituted Second Triumvirate, the proscriptions, Philippi, and finally Actium left one man without an equal.

The people affected were far more numerous than the aristocrats who dominate the surviving sources. Italian farmers lost land to soldiers. Gallic communities were conquered, enslaved, or displaced. Provincials in Greece, Anatolia, Egypt, and North Africa paid for armies they had not raised and often did not want. Slaves, freedpeople, urban citizens, tax collectors, and veterans all shaped and were shaped by the conflict. The chapter therefore alternates between the politics of the elite and the experience of those who lived within, and often paid for, that politics.

The evidence is unusually rich but also unusually partisan. Cicero's speeches and letters survive in large numbers and allow historians to watch events from the inside, though he wrote as a participant with his own interests. Caesar's Commentarii on the Gallic and civil wars are among the most important surviving accounts, but they were published political literature designed to present his conduct favorably. Sallust wrote a moralizing monograph on Catiline. Later writers, including Plutarch, Appian, Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, wrote generations or centuries afterward, drawing on lost contemporary works and on the retrospective assumptions of the Augustan age. Coins, inscriptions, and archaeology supplement the literary tradition, but the story is still told largely through the voices of the winners and of a senatorial class writing about itself.

This chapter stops in 30 BCE, when Octavian stood without a military rival after the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra. It does not explain the constitutional settlements, titles, and administrative structures that Octavian, later Augustus, built afterward. Those belong to Chapter 21. The task here is narrower and, in some ways, harder: to explain how a political order ended while its institutions and vocabulary remained.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the arrangements of Sulla began to unravel after 78 BCE and why the post-Sullan Republic depended on extraordinary commands.
  2. Assess the career of Pompey as both a product and a cause of the erosion of normal republican sequencing.
  3. Analyze the conspiracy of Catiline and the execution of citizens in 63 BCE as a dispute about emergency powers and legality.
  4. Distinguish the informal First Triumvirate from the legally constituted Second Triumvirate.
  5. Trace Caesar's Gallic command, evaluating its military, economic, and political consequences and its human costs.
  6. Interpret the breakdown of 50–49 BCE as a contested legal and political crisis rather than a simple story of one man's ambition.
  7. Describe the geography and strategy of the civil wars from 49 to 45 BCE across the Mediterranean.
  8. Evaluate whether Caesar's dictatorship ended the Republic in practice, distinguishing formal office from political reality.
  9. Compare the motives of the conspirators of 44 BCE and explain why the assassination produced no workable settlement.
  10. Explain the proscriptions, the battle of Philippi, and the road to Actium as steps toward one man supremacy.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the fall of the Republic and the evidence on which each depends.
  12. Read partisan sources such as Cicero's letters and Caesar's Commentarii critically, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later tradition.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a constitution designed to prevent individuals from dominating politics come to rely on individuals with extraordinary powers?
  2. Were extraordinary commands destroying the Republic, or were they rational adaptations to governing a Mediterranean empire with one year magistracies?
  3. How real were the constitutional disagreements of the late Republic, and how far were legal arguments merely cover for power?
  4. At what point did constitutional bargaining between Caesar and his opponents become civil war, and who bore responsibility for that transition?
  5. How did the conquest of Gaul change the relationship between a commander, his army, and the political community at Rome?
  6. What did it mean for Caesar to hold dictatorial power when Rome already knew the dictatorship as a legitimate office?
  7. Why did the assassination of Caesar fail to restore the Republic, and what does that failure reveal about the political order the conspirators sought to defend?
  8. How should historians evaluate sources written by participants, victors, and later authors with their own political purposes?
  9. Who paid for the civil wars, and how did ordinary Italians and provincials experience them?

Historical Context

By the 70s BCE Rome governed, directly or through client relationships, most of the Mediterranean basin, a process traced in Chapters 16 and 17. Its governing institutions, examined in Chapter 15, had grown from those of a city state: annually elected magistrates, a Senate composed largely of former magistrates, and popular assemblies of citizens. Those institutions now had to oversee provinces in Spain, Gaul, Africa, Macedonia, Asia, and elsewhere, to fight wars far from Italy, to supply large armies, and to manage a citizen body that included Italian communities enfranchised after the Social War. The annual rhythm of magistracies, limits on repeated office, and senatorial oversight had been designed for smaller problems.

Chapter 19 traced how that system came under strain. Land, debt, and the status of Rome's Italian allies produced conflict from the time of the Gracchi. Changes in recruitment and military service traditionally associated with Marius, together with long overseas campaigns and increasingly important veteran land settlements, strengthened the political relationship between some soldiers and commanders without creating a single Marian reform or automatically transferring soldiers' loyalty to individual generals. Sulla marched an army on Rome in 88 and again in 83 to 82 BCE, held the dictatorship without a time limit, proscribed his enemies, and settled veterans on confiscated land. He then introduced reforms intended to restore senatorial control, including limits on tribunician power, and he resigned the dictatorship in 79 BCE and died in 78. The precedents remained even where the reforms did not: armies had been used against the city, citizens had been legally proscribed, and a commander had settled his soldiers by force.

Rome's wealth had also changed. Conquest brought plunder, indemnities, tribute, and new opportunities for profit, and it enlarged the slave economy that Chapter 18 examined. Senators and equestrians competed for command, provincial resources, and public contracts. Tax farming companies, whose members were mainly equestrians, exerted influence over policy in the provinces. Debt was widespread among the elite and among poorer citizens, and the city of Rome had become a large urban population that depended on imported grain and could be mobilized politically by tribunes and gang leaders. These pressures did not by themselves cause civil war, but they shaped the constituencies that politicians tried to mobilize.

Finally, the Republic did not simply await its end. Contemporary Romans did not know that a Principate would follow, and many believed that the crises of the age could be resolved within existing institutions. The men who appear in this chapter argued constantly about what those institutions permitted. They appealed to precedent, to ancestral custom (mos maiorum), to law, and to the safety of the Roman people. Understanding those arguments, and taking them seriously as arguments, is essential to understanding why the settlement failed.

Section 20.1

The Republic after Sulla

The paradox of 78 BCE is easy to state. Sulla had claimed to restore senatorial government, and he had intended his measures to prevent any one man from repeating what he had done. Yet within a decade much of his architecture was being dismantled, and the Republic was relying on individuals with unusual powers to solve its problems. The question is why a constitution designed to prevent ambitious individuals from dominating politics almost immediately depended on ambitious individuals.

Sulla's reforms included restrictions on the tribunate, an enlarged Senate, more praetors and quaestors, and rules about the sequence and age of magistracies intended to slow the rise of young men. Some of these measures stayed in place for decades. Others proved politically impossible to maintain. The tribunes, who could propose legislation and veto measures, had been stripped of much of their authority, but tribunician agitation was a familiar part of Roman politics and their powers were soon reclaimed. Equestrians resented senatorial control of the courts. Those who had lost land or status under the proscriptions, and their heirs, remained hostile to the Sullan order, and those who had gained from it were anxious to protect their gains.

The first challenge came from within the ruling class. In 78, the consul Marcus Aemilius Lepidus proposed measures that undercut Sulla's settlement, including grain distribution and the recall of exiles, and when the Senate resisted he raised an army in Etruria and marched toward Rome in 77. His colleague Quintus Lutatius Catulus and the young Pompey defeated him, and Lepidus died in Sardinia. What mattered for the future was not the defeat of Lepidus but the way it was achieved: Pompey, then about twenty-eight and holding no magistracy, was given imperium, which is to say lawful command, by the Senate. Once again, an emergency was being solved by placing an individual outside the ordinary career sequence.

In other words, the Republic was functioning but unstable. The annual elections took place, the Senate debated, the courts operated, and provincial governors were dispatched. Yet each new military emergency, in Spain, in the Mediterranean, and in Anatolia, encouraged the appointment of a proven commander with a large force and a long term. The generation of Pompey, Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar grew up in a political culture in which extraordinary reputation was more valuable than regular progress, and in which the memory of Sulla's success, and of Marius before him, remained vivid.

Historical Vocabulary
Extraordinary Command, Imperium, and Provincia

Imperium was the lawful authority to command armies and to exercise judicial and administrative power. Consuls and praetors held it while in office, and could be given it for provincial governorship as proconsuls or propraetors. A provincia originally meant the task or sphere assigned to a magistrate, which might be a war or a territory. An extraordinary command was one that departed from the normal pattern: it might be granted to a private citizen, exceed the usual term of one year, cover an unusually broad area, or include unusual resources.

Extraordinary commands were not novel, and they were not by definition unconstitutional. They were voted through the Senate or the assemblies. What made those of the 70s to 50s BCE historically significant was their scale, their frequency, and the fact that they gave commanders prolonged relationships with armies whose soldiers could become increasingly dependent on successful commanders for booty, advancement, and veteran rewards, along with resources and prestige that no ordinary magistrate could match.

Section 20.2

Pompey, Crassus, and Extraordinary Power

Pompey's early career already broke with normal Roman expectations. He raised a private army in Picenum to support Sulla in 83 BCE, was granted a triumph after campaigns in Sicily and Africa in 81 while still an equestrian who had held no magistracy, and was given the nickname Magnus, the Great. After the defeat of Lepidus he was sent to Spain in 77 with proconsular imperium to deal with Quintus Sertorius, a Roman commander of the Marian party who had established a rival government in Spain with the support of Iberian communities and Roman exiles. The Sertorian War lasted until 72 to 71, when Sertorius was assassinated by his own subordinates, and Pompey and Metellus Pius finished the campaign. It was a serious civil conflict fought in a province, and it showed that Roman forces could sustain a rival political authority far from Italy.

Meanwhile, in Italy, the slave revolt of 73 to 71 BCE, known as the Third Servile War, began when gladiators escaped from a training school at Capua under the leadership of Spartacus, a Thracian. Their numbers grew as slaves from rural estates joined them, and they defeated several Roman forces before Marcus Licinius Crassus, entrusted with command, defeated the main body in 71. Pompey, returning from Spain, intercepted survivors and claimed to have finished the war. The episode is relevant here for three reasons. It exposed the weakness of the regular Roman military response, it reinforced Crassus's stature, and it intensified rivalry between Crassus and Pompey. It also reflects the reliance of the Roman economy on enslaved labor described in Chapter 18. The evidence for Spartacus's aims is thin and comes almost entirely from Roman authors, and it does not justify modern portrayals of him as an abolitionist revolutionary. What is clear is that the revolt was a large military threat and that men who solved such crises gained political capital.

Pompey and Crassus both stood for the consulship of 70, and both were elected, although Pompey had not held the quaestorship or praetorship and was below the legal age. The Senate waived the requirements. During their term the consuls dismantled central parts of Sulla's settlement. Full tribunician powers were restored. A new law reorganized the juries so that senators, equestrians, and the tribuni aerarii formed the three panels of jurors, addressing complaints about senatorial corruption highlighted in the prosecution of the governor Gaius Verres, in which Cicero made his name. Neither man had been a champion of the popular cause in earlier years. Each recognized that Sulla's arrangements could not continue, and each had an interest in flattering important constituencies.

Pompey's career prompts a question that is more useful than a simple verdict of unconstitutional behavior. Were extraordinary commands destroying the Republic, or were they rational adaptations to the problems of governing a Mediterranean empire whose demands exceeded the capacity of one year magistrates? There is force in both answers. The commands solved real problems, and they were granted by lawful votes, often at the urging of senators who saw no better alternative. Yet each one made the next easier to justify, weakened the principle that authority should be shared and rotated, and made a commander's continued success politically indispensable. They also produced a new problem: when a commander returned, how would his arrangements be ratified and his soldiers rewarded?

Section 20.3

Pirates, Mithridates, and Pompey's Eastern Settlement

In 67 BCE the tribune Aulus Gabinius proposed a law giving Pompey an extensive command against the pirates who disrupted Mediterranean shipping and threatened Rome's grain supply. The command reached across the sea and up to fifty miles inland, and provided a large fleet and substantial funds, along with a large number of subordinate commanders. Senatorial opposition was strong, precisely because of the scale of the authority, but the law passed. According to the tradition, Pompey cleared the seas with remarkable speed, in a matter of months, a claim that should be treated with caution because it comes from sources sympathetic to him. The pirates were not a single organization but a set of maritime communities and opportunists, many of them made possible by the weakening of Hellenistic states and by the disruption of Rome's own wars, and Pompey resettled many in coastal cities.

The next step was the command against Mithridates VI of Pontus and his son in law, Tigranes of Armenia. Mithridates had fought Rome three times, had ordered the massacre of Roman and Italian residents in Asia in 88, and had become a symbol of eastern resistance. The war had been prosecuted by Lucius Licinius Lucullus with success, but his troops mutinied and his command was eroded. In 66 the tribune Gaius Manilius proposed transferring the eastern command to Pompey. Cicero, in his speech on the Manilian law, supported the measure, and Caesar was among its backers. Pompey defeated Mithridates, who fled to the Crimea and died in 63, and dealt with Tigranes through negotiation.

What followed was a reorganization of the eastern Mediterranean on a scale no Roman had attempted. Pompey created or enlarged provinces including Bithynia and Pontus, and Syria, made agreements with client kings and city states, and intervened in Judea in a succession dispute between Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II, capturing Jerusalem in 63. These arrangements shaped Rome's relationship with the region for generations, and they were undertaken by one man with almost no senatorial supervision. The local communities and rulers involved were not passive: they competed to secure Pompey's favor, and his settlement reflected their negotiations as well as Roman priorities.

The difficulty came when Pompey returned. In 62 he disbanded his army at Brundisium, an act often cited as evidence that he was no Sulla, and he celebrated a great triumph in 61. Yet he now needed the Senate to ratify his eastern settlement and to provide land for his veterans, and a group of senators, including Cato, Lucullus, and Crassus, obstructed him. This failure of the normal system to reward a successful commander led directly to the coalition of 60. It is a critical point: Pompey did not need to threaten the Republic to become a problem. The Republic had no established means of absorbing the fruits of a command on this scale.

Section 20.4

Cicero, Catiline, and Emergency Politics

While Pompey was in the east, politics at Rome turned on debt, ambition, and fear. Lucius Sergius Catilina, a patrician who had been implicated in earlier scandals, stood for the consulship of 64 and again of 63 and was defeated both times. Cicero, a "new man" from an equestrian family of Arpinum with no consular ancestors, won the consulship of 63 by broad support, including from senators who preferred him to Catiline. During 63 BCE, especially in the autumn, Catiline gathered support among indebted aristocrats, disaffected veterans of Sulla's settlements, and rural poor in Etruria, where Gaius Manlius raised forces in his name. Cicero, informed by informants, denounced him in the Senate, and Catiline left the city.

The conspirators still in Rome tried to enlist envoys of the Allobroges, a Gallic people, who reported the approach to Cicero. Letters bearing the conspirators' seals were seized, and five men, including a former consul, Publius Cornelius Lentulus Sura, were arrested. The Senate debated their fate on 5 December 63 BCE. Caesar, then praetor-elect, argued that execution without trial was improper and proposed instead lifelong imprisonment and confiscation of property. Cato argued for death and prevailed. Cicero ordered the executions carried out. Catiline was defeated and killed in battle at Pistoria in early 62.

The established evidence includes Catiline's failed career, the debt crisis, the assembling of armed followers in Etruria, the arrests, the Senate debate, and the executions. The interpretation is much less certain. Cicero's speeches present the affair as a narrow escape from a plot to burn Rome, and Cicero came to see himself as the savior of the Republic. Sallust's monograph, written a generation later, turned it into a moral story about aristocratic decadence. Later tradition made Catiline a monster. Modern historians tend to see a genuine, if poorly organized, rebellion, fed by real debt problems, and exaggerated by Cicero for political benefit. The precise extent of the plan in Rome itself is unknowable.

The crucial constitutional question concerned the execution of Roman citizens without the ordinary process of trial and the right of appeal. Chapter 19 examined the earlier use of the Senate's emergency decree, the senatus consultum ultimum, which called on the magistrates to see that the state suffered no harm. Its legal force was disputed: supporters argued that it authorized extreme measures against those who had made themselves public enemies, opponents that only law and the assemblies could deprive a citizen of life. The disagreement was real. Caesar's speech, as reported by Sallust, is a reminder that a principled objection existed and that it was not merely tactical. In 58 the tribune Clodius carried a law making it a crime to execute citizens without trial, aimed at Cicero, who went into exile and returned in 57. The precedent of 63 therefore became a political weapon, and the idea that emergencies justify suspending ordinary safeguards remained contested.

Section 20.5

The First Triumvirate and the Politics of Coalition

By 60 BCE three of Rome's most powerful men had reasons to cooperate. Pompey needed land for his veterans and ratification of his eastern arrangements. Crassus, with financial and equestrian connections, wished to protect the interests of the tax collectors who had bid too high for contracts in Asia and needed relief. Caesar, returning from Spain, wanted the consulship of 59 and a major provincial command afterward, and he wanted to avoid the prosecution that might follow if he left office without one. The Senate, led by Cato and others, had blocked each of them separately. Together they were much harder to resist.

Ancient and modern writers have called this arrangement the First Triumvirate. That label is a modern convention, and it has caused confusion. The alliance was informal, a private agreement among three competitors that had no legal standing and created no office. It was not an ideological friendship, nor a plan to overthrow the Republic. It was a bargain, and it is much better understood as a temporary coalition of aristocrats whose interests happened to converge.

Historical Vocabulary
The First and Second Triumvirates

The First Triumvirate is a modern label for the informal alliance of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus formed around 60 BCE. It was not a magistracy and had no legal basis. The Second Triumvirate was different: in 43 BCE the lex Titia established Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus as triumviri rei publicae constituendae, three men for setting the state in order, with a fixed term of five years. It was an extraordinary commission created by law, and its members held formal powers to make appointments and legislation.

The distinction matters. The first was a private arrangement that worked around the constitution. The second was a legal instrument that displaced ordinary government while claiming to restore it.

Caesar was elected consul for 59 with Marcus Calpurnius Bibulus as his colleague, and he used the year to enact what the coalition needed. A land law provided allotments for Pompey's veterans and for poorer citizens, including in Campania. Pompey's eastern settlement was ratified. The tax collectors received relief on their Asian contract, and Ptolemy XII of Egypt was recognized as king after a large payment. Bibulus, who tried to block business by declaring unfavorable omens, was ignored and withdrew to his house, so that contemporaries joked that the consuls of the year were "Julius and Caesar." Caesar also received, through a tribunician law, the provinces of Cisalpine Gaul and Illyricum for five years, and the Senate later added Transalpine Gaul. Pompey married Caesar's daughter Julia, sealing the alliance. In 56, at a conference at Luca, the three renewed the arrangement: Caesar's command was extended, and Pompey and Crassus were to be consuls in 55 and then receive provinces, Spain for Pompey and Syria for Crassus.

Opposition was strong, but it lacked tools. Cato and other senators tried to obstruct legislation, and the use of religious and procedural devices was consistent with Roman practice. Yet Caesar's consulship broke with the accepted expectation that consuls cooperate with the Senate, and it showed how effective coordinated political pressure and popular support could be. The lesson was not lost on others, and it fed the sense that legality had become a matter of who had the votes and the muscle.

Section 20.6

Caesar in Gaul: Conquest, Command, and Political Power

Caesar arrived in his provinces in 58 BCE with a legal mandate to govern Cisalpine Gaul, in northern Italy, and Transalpine Gaul, the Roman province in the south of what is now France, together with Illyricum on the Adriatic. "Gaul" in this chapter means the region of the ancient Celtic-speaking and other communities of what is now France, Belgium, and neighboring lands, not a modern nation. It contained dozens of peoples with distinct political systems, rivalries, and alliances, some of which had long dealings with Rome.

Caesar presented his first campaigns as defensive. When the Helvetii, a people from modern Switzerland, sought to migrate west, Caesar blocked and defeated them. He then turned against Ariovistus, a Germanic leader who had established himself in eastern Gaul at the invitation of one Gallic faction, and defeated him. In 57 he moved against the Belgae in the north, and in 56 against the Veneti, a seafaring people of Brittany. Each campaign, whatever its stated cause, extended Roman control, and Caesar sought and obtained senatorial thanksgivings that publicly ratified his success. In 55 he built a bridge across the Rhine as a demonstration and made expeditions to Britain in 55 and 54, whose military results were limited but whose propaganda value was considerable.

The war entered a grim phase after the revolt of the Eburones in 54 to 53, when Caesar's forces suffered a severe loss and he responded with devastation. In 52 a widespread rising under Vercingetorix, an aristocrat of the Arverni, united many communities, though not all, and posed the most serious threat of the war. Caesar suffered a reverse at Gergovia but ended the revolt by besieging Vercingetorix in the hill town of Alesia while defending against a large Gallic relief army, through a double line of fortifications. Vercingetorix surrendered, was held for years, and was executed after Caesar's triumph in 46. Vercingetorix was not the leader of "the Gauls" from the beginning, and modern French national memory, which made him a founding hero, is a much later construction, as this book's discussion of national narratives emphasizes.

Understanding how Caesar succeeded requires attention to more than battles. Roman armies depended on grain supply, roads, fortified camps, engineering, and disciplined legions, and Caesar exploited divisions among Gallic communities, some of which allied with him. His army was rewarded with plunder and promises, and his staff included capable subordinates. He also communicated with Rome through dispatches and, later, the Commentarii, a spare, third-person account that presented his actions as necessary and successful. Modern scholars treat this text as both invaluable evidence and sophisticated political self-presentation, written to influence opinion at Rome, and they compare it with other evidence, including archaeology at Alesia and other sites.

The human cost was enormous. Caesar's forces killed, enslaved, and displaced very large numbers of people, and some communities were destroyed. Ancient figures for casualties, including those given by later writers who say that a million died and another million were enslaved, are unreliable, and Caesar's own figures are suspect because he had an interest in emphasizing his victories. Even so, the conquest was violent by any measure. In 55 he attacked Germanic migrants, the Usipetes and Tencteri, during what may have been a truce, and Cato reportedly proposed handing Caesar over to them. Slave dealers followed the army, and the wealth from Gaul, in plunder, captives, and indemnities, financed Caesar's political ambitions, including public building and payments to supporters in Rome.

Politically, the Gallic command gave Caesar what none of his rivals then possessed: a long term, a large and loyal army, a reputation to match that of Pompey, and independence from the Senate. The five year term of 58 was extended, and from the standpoint of his opponents this meant he would leave office at a moment of his choosing, with a force at his back. In the 50s it was not clear that this would lead to war. It was clear that Rome had never before faced a commander so strong and so far from its supervision.

Section 20.7

The Coalition Breaks Apart

The alliance that had held Caesar and Pompey together depended on personal ties, and these disappeared in quick succession. Julia died in childbirth in 54 BCE. In 53 Crassus, who had gone to Syria and undertaken a campaign against the Parthian Empire in search of glory to match his partners, was defeated at Carrhae, in what is now southeastern Turkey, and killed. The disaster damaged Roman prestige in the east and removed the one man who had balanced Caesar and Pompey. The three man arrangement, which had always depended on mutual need, was now a rivalry between two.

At Rome, political violence had become endemic. Publius Clodius, a patrician who had been adopted into a plebeian family so he could become a tribune, mobilized urban crowds, organized associations, and sponsored the free distribution of grain. His rival Titus Annius Milo led a counter-gang. Elections were disrupted, and the city was often unsafe. In January 52 Milo's followers killed Clodius on the Appian Way, and Clodius's supporters burned the Senate house in a funeral pyre. The Senate, invoking emergency measures, turned to Pompey, who was appointed sole consul, without a colleague, an unusual step for a man who claimed to defend traditional norms. He later took Metellus Scipio, his new father in law, as colleague.

Pompey then enacted several laws that bore on the coming crisis. One required an interval of five years between holding a magistracy and taking a province, intended to curb the abuse of ambitious men going straight from office to command. Another had been passed with his consent, the so-called law of the ten tribunes, allowing Caesar to stand for the consulship without returning to Rome to register in person. Yet Pompey's own later legislation seemed to contradict it. Caesar's supporters argued that he had been promised the right to stand in absentia, while his enemies argued that he must become a private citizen and could then be prosecuted. It also mattered when his command legally ended, and the sources and modern scholars disagree, since it depended on the interpretation of the Luca arrangements and of a law fixing the earliest date for debating the succession to provinces.

The stakes were personal and political. Caesar had many enemies among senators, some of whom hoped to prosecute him, and he was aware that a private citizen who had broken procedures in 59 and in Gaul was vulnerable. He needed to move directly from the command to a second consulship, to keep his imperium and its immunities. Pompey, for his part, drifted toward the senatorial majority, which offered him a place as its champion, while retaining his own command in Spain, governed through legates, and his proximity to Rome. Neither side was seeking war. Each expected the other to give way, and each believed that its own position was legitimate.

Section 20.8

Crossing the Rubicon and the Civil War

The events of 50 and early 49 BCE resemble a sequence of missed exits. Caesar's legal position was that his command and his right to stand in absentia were secured. His opponents' position was that his command must end and his candidacy require his presence. Proposals for both to lay down commands, or for compromise, were discussed in the Senate and among intermediaries, including Cicero, but each failed. In December 50 the tribune Gaius Scribonius Curio, a supporter of Caesar, carried a proposal that both Caesar and Pompey disarm, which the Senate approved by a large majority, but the consul Gaius Claudius Marcellus, a hardliner, ignored it. In January the Senate rejected Caesar's proposals, which were presented by his supporters, including Mark Antony and Quintus Cassius as tribunes, and demanded that he give up his provinces by a fixed date.

On 7 January 49 the Senate passed the senatus consultum ultimum, directing the magistrates and Pompey to protect the state. The tribunes Antony and Cassius, who had tried to veto measures against Caesar, left the city, fearing for their safety, and joined him. From Caesar's perspective, the tribunician veto, the traditional protection against the Senate, had been overridden by force. From the Senate's, Caesar was refusing a legal demand to step down, and was leaving Rome no choice. Both accounts had force. The important question is: at what point did constitutional bargaining become civil war?

Caesar crossed the Rubicon, the small river marking the boundary between his province and Italy proper, around 10 to 11 January 49, with a single legion, the Thirteenth. The traditional accounts of Suetonius and Plutarch, written long afterward, give a dramatic scene and a famous phrase, but their exact wording cannot be verified, and Caesar's own narrative does not mention the river. By entering Italy at the head of an army he was in breach of the law, whatever the merits of his position, and he knew it. He then advanced quickly. Many Italian towns welcomed him, and he offered lenient terms to opponents. At Corfinium he captured Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus and let him go, an early instance of the policy of clementia or mercy, which he intended to differentiate himself from Sulla.

Pompey's strategy was to withdraw from Italy, gather forces in the east, and return, in part because his available troops in Italy were untried and his strength lay in Spain and the eastern provinces, where his clients and veterans were. He sailed from Brundisium in March 49, taking most of the consuls and senators with him. Caesar entered Rome, seized the treasury against the objection of a tribune, and turned first to Spain, where Pompey's legates commanded seven legions. He secured the surrender of the Pompeian armies at Ilerda in the summer of 49 while a siege of Massilia continued. He was named dictator briefly at the end of 49 to hold elections, was elected consul for 48, and resigned the dictatorship after eleven days.

In January 48 Caesar crossed the Adriatic and confronted Pompey in Epirus. After a setback at Dyrrhachium, he drew Pompey inland to Thessaly, where on 9 August 48, by the unreformed calendar of the time, the armies met at Pharsalus. Pompey had larger forces, especially cavalry, but Caesar's veterans and tactics prevailed, and much of the Pompeian army surrendered. Pompey fled to Egypt, hoping for support from the young king Ptolemy XIII, whose father he had once helped. Instead, on the advice of his ministers, Pompey was murdered on landing near Pelusium in late September. The Egyptian court apparently calculated that killing Pompey would win Caesar's favor.

Caesar followed and became involved in the succession dispute in Egypt. Ptolemy XIII and his sister Cleopatra VII, who was about twenty-one, were joint rulers under their father's will, and were in open conflict. Cleopatra should be understood first as a ruler fighting for her throne and her kingdom's independence within a Mediterranean dominated by Roman power. She allied with Caesar, and the Alexandrian War of 48 to 47 followed, in which Caesar was besieged in the city before reinforcements arrived. Ptolemy XIII died in the fighting, and Cleopatra was restored, ruling jointly with a younger brother, Ptolemy XIV. Cleopatra bore a son in 47, Ptolemy Caesar, known as Caesarion, and the question of whether Caesar was the father was later politicized and cannot be resolved. Caesar did not, as far as we know, publicly acknowledge the child.

Caesar then dealt with Pharnaces II of the Bosporan kingdom, a son of Mithridates, whom he defeated at Zela in 47, in a campaign later summed up in a famous three word boast whose exact attribution cannot be confirmed. He returned to Rome, settled disputes over debt and the mutiny of his troops, and turned to Africa, where the Pompeian remnant, led by Metellus Scipio, Cato, and the Numidian king Juba I, had gathered. At Thapsus in April 46 he won a decisive victory, and Cato, unwilling to accept Caesar's pardon, took his own life at Utica, becoming for later generations a symbol of republican resistance. The last major opposition, led by Pompey's sons Gnaeus and Sextus, was defeated at Munda in Spain in March 45, in a battle whose outcome was closer than the others. Gnaeus was killed. Sextus escaped.

The war was fought across the Mediterranean because the Roman world was a single strategic system: Italy provided manpower and financial centers, Spain, Africa, and the east provided resources and allies, and the sea connected them. Caesar's strengths were speed, veteran troops, and a policy of negotiation and mercy. Pompey's strengths were the east, the fleet, and the authority of the Senate. The cost fell on provincial communities that provided money, grain, ships, and men, and on Italian towns that had to choose sides.

Section 20.9

Caesar's Dictatorship and the Problem of Monarchy

Caesar did not become emperor or king in the later imperial sense, and the phrase "first emperor" misdescribes both his position and Roman categories. Rome already knew the dictatorship, an emergency office of limited term, and held by figures such as Cincinnatus in tradition and, more recently, Sulla. What was new was the duration, concentration, repetition, and political context of Caesar's powers. He first held the dictatorship briefly in 49 BCE to conduct elections; after Pharsalus he received a second dictatorship for one year in late 48; in 46 he was granted the dictatorship for ten years; and in early 44 he became dictator perpetuo, dictator in perpetuity. He also held consulships, often without colleagues who exercised independent authority, and he received tribunician inviolability and the authority to nominate magistrates.

Formally, the Republic continued. The Senate, magistrates, and assemblies still met, and the elections went ahead. In practice, Caesar exercised overwhelming influence over political outcomes. He nominated candidates, increased the number of magistrates, such as praetors, to reward supporters, and enlarged the Senate to about nine hundred, including centurions, provincials, and Gauls, a change that diluted the traditional aristocracy. He also controlled the armies and their commanders, the provinces, and the treasury. Cicero, in the speeches and letters of these years, described a political life in which normal deliberation had lost its meaning, although he also expressed relief that the fighting was over.

Caesar's program was substantial. Some measures addressed real problems. He reduced the number of recipients of the free grain distribution, dealt with debt by adjusting terms and forgiving some interest, though he refused a general cancellation, and sent tens of thousands of veterans and urban poor to new colonies, including Carthage and Corinth, both refounded. He extended citizenship in northern Italy and to some provincials, and reformed municipal government. He sponsored the reform of the calendar in 46 BCE, with the assistance of Alexandrian astronomers, replacing the increasingly disordered Roman lunisolar calendar with a solar year of 365 days and an intercalary day every fourth year. The year 46 was extended to 445 days to realign the calendar with the seasons, and the new Julian calendar took effect on 1 January 45. The Julian calendar remained dominant in much of Europe for centuries; the Gregorian reform was introduced in 1582 and adopted at different times in different regions. Caesar also planned building projects, a public library, and campaigns against Dacia and Parthia.

Historical Vocabulary
Clementia and Libertas

Clementia, mercy, was the policy by which Caesar pardoned defeated opponents, including Cicero and Brutus, and refrained from proscribing. It was a virtue, and a political tool that contrasted him with Sulla. Yet it carried an awkward implication: the authority to pardon implied authority over life and death, a power that a free citizen body was not supposed to concede to any man. Libertas, liberty, meant for senators the ability to participate in politics as equals, free from domination, rather than a modern set of individual rights. Both Caesar and his opponents claimed to defend it.

Honors accumulated. Caesar was given a triumphal robe, a gilded chair, statues, a place among the gods in some contexts, and a month named for him, Julius, in place of Quintilis, after his death. On 15 February 44 at the festival of the Lupercalia, Mark Antony offered him a diadem, the symbol of Hellenistic kingship, and Caesar refused it, perhaps to test public opinion. Whether he wished to be king cannot be established. He was never crowned, and the word rex was toxic in Roman politics. But contemporaries interpreted these gestures in light of his power, and many suspected that only the name was missing.

This raises the question of what "fall of the Republic" means. If it means the abolition of republican offices, it did not happen. If it means the end of a system in which competing aristocrats shared power and no individual could monopolize armies and decisions, it happened under Caesar, or at least Caesar's dictatorship showed how it could happen. Like Sulla, he separated formal office from political reality. Unlike Sulla, he did not resign. Nor did he construct a durable institutional alternative. His powers depended on him personally, on his army and his supporters, and this made his position fragile as well as dominant.

Section 20.10

The Ides of March and the Failure of Assassination

On 15 March 44 BCE, at a meeting of the Senate in a hall attached to the Theatre of Pompey, Caesar was stabbed to death by a group of senators. Ancient tradition says that about sixty were involved and that Caesar received twenty-three wounds, though these numbers are uncertain. The leaders were Marcus Junius Brutus, Gaius Cassius Longinus, and Decimus Junius Brutus, all of whom had received favors from Caesar, some had fought for Pompey and been pardoned, and several held office at his pleasure. Caesar had recently dismissed his bodyguard. He was to leave for a campaign against Parthia within days.

The conspirators were not united by a single motive, and they were not modern democrats. Brutus, whose family traditions and philosophical formation stressed the overthrow of tyranny, saw himself as defending libertas against monarchy, and his coins later celebrated the "Ides of March." Cassius had personal grievances and a military reputation. Others resented the loss of aristocratic status, the diminishing of the Senate, or the way that competition for offices and provincial commands had been closed off. Some were simply afraid that Caesar's permanent dominance had ended any meaningful role for the old elite. Their ideal was a Republic ruled by its aristocracy, and the interests of ordinary citizens, of Italians, and of provincials played little part in their thinking.

The assassination itself was not irrational, and from their perspective it followed the tradition of removing those who sought kingship. Their major failure was not the act but the absence of a settlement afterward. They had no plan for the armies, for Caesar's veterans, for Antony, who was consul and controlled Caesar's papers, or for the many people who owed their positions to Caesar's acts. Cicero, who was not in the plot, lamented that the deed had been done "with the courage of men and the plans of children," in a paraphrase widely attributed to him, though the exact wording varies and should not be relied on. On 17 March the Senate agreed to an amnesty for the conspirators while confirming Caesar's decisions.

Antony seized the initiative. After Caesar's will was opened and its provisions became known, the gifts to Roman citizens and his gardens helped turn public feeling toward him. At the funeral, Antony's oration and the presentation of Caesar's body and wounds further inflamed the crowd, and public opinion turned against the killers. The will, which left Roman citizens a sum of money and public gardens, named as principal heir and adoptive son Gaius Octavius, Caesar's grandnephew, then eighteen years old and studying at Apollonia in Illyricum. Brutus and Cassius left Rome and, by the summer, headed east. The young Octavius returned to Italy, took the name Gaius Julius Caesar, known to historians as Octavian to distinguish him from his adoptive father, and claimed his inheritance, appealing to Caesar's veterans. This chapter uses "Octavian" for him until the change of name and title in 27 BCE, which Chapter 21 explains.

For a few months in 44 to 43, Cicero attempted to rebuild senatorial authority by opposing Antony in a series of speeches, the Philippics, and by cultivating Octavian. The Senate authorized Octavian to command troops, and in the spring of 43 forces under the consuls Hirtius and Pansa and Octavian defeated Antony at Mutina in northern Italy. Both consuls died. The Senate, ignoring Octavian's claims, turned to the Liberators, and Octavian, demanding the consulship, marched on Rome. He was elected consul in August 43 at nineteen, and, after negotiation with Antony and Lepidus, the alliance that had opposed Antony became a partnership with him.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Roman Republic Fall?

The question: Historians have long debated why the republican political system gave way to one man rule. The question is important not only for Roman history but for the broader problem of how political orders decay, and it draws together the themes of Chapters 15 to 20.

Interpretation A, structural explanations: Some emphasize that a city state constitution with annual magistrates and a small governing elite could not govern a Mediterranean empire. Others stress social and economic factors: debt, land hunger, inequality, the concentration of wealth, and the need to settle veterans. Military explanations focus on long commands and armies whose loyalty was to their generals, because the state did not provide for soldiers after service.
Interpretation B, political and contingent explanations: Others stress intensifying competition within the aristocracy, in which the resources available to a successful commander overwhelmed the norms that had limited rivalry. Still others argue that the Republic retained substantial resilience, and that specific decisions by Pompey, Caesar, Cato, the Senate, the assassins, Antony, and Octavian drove the escalation. In this view collapse was not inevitable, and different choices in 50 or in 44 might have produced different outcomes.

Evidence: The evidence includes the careers of individual commanders, legislation on land and debt, the size and composition of armies, the frequency of extraordinary commands, the accounts of contemporaries such as Cicero and Sallust, and the fact that ordinary institutions continued to function in many respects. These do not point in one direction. Explanations focused on structure struggle to explain timing, while those focused on contingency struggle to explain why so many separate crises had similar outcomes.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors affects whether the fall of the Republic seems tragic accident, structural inevitability, or the result of choices by identifiable people. Many recent historians reject a single-cause explanation and combine institutional, social, military, political, and contingent factors, while disagreeing substantially about their relative weight.

Section 20.11

The Second Triumvirate, Proscriptions, and Philippi

In November 43 BCE, Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus met near Bononia and agreed to share power. On 27 November a tribune, Publius Titius, carried the lex Titia, which established them as triumviri rei publicae constituendae for five years. Unlike the alliance of 60, the Second Triumvirate was a legal office with authority to appoint magistrates, issue edicts with the force of law, and command provinces and armies. The irony deserves emphasis. The three men claimed extraordinary authority in order to constitute or restore the Republic, while exercising powers that displaced ordinary republican government. They divided the western provinces, with Antony taking Gaul, Lepidus Spain and Narbonese Gaul, and Octavian Africa, Sicily, and Sardinia, and prepared to fight Brutus and Cassius, who held the east.

To pay for an army of some forty legions and to eliminate their enemies, the triumvirs published proscription lists, as Sulla had done. Named persons were outlawed, could be killed with impunity, and had their property confiscated, with rewards for killers and informers and penalties for those who sheltered them. Ancient sources report that some three hundred senators and about two thousand equestrians were proscribed, but the figures are uncertain. The proscriptions were an instrument of vengeance, since many of those listed were personal enemies, and of finance, since wealthy men were targeted for their property. The stories of betrayal and courage, with slaves and family members protecting or betraying the proscribed, reveal the social depth of the terror.

Historical Vocabulary
Proscription

Proscription was the public posting of names of citizens who were declared outlaws. Those listed lost legal protection, could be killed by anyone, and forfeited their property to the state, and their heirs were often disqualified from office. Sulla introduced the practice in 82 to 81 BCE, and the triumvirs revived it in 43. It differed from a judicial sentence: it bypassed trial, and it linked political elimination directly to confiscation. The triumviral proscriptions were not a simple copy of Sulla's: their scale, their financing of a major war, and the involvement of three rivals made them a distinct episode, though the earlier precedent made them thinkable.

Cicero was among the victims. His name was put on the list, apparently at Antony's insistence, and Octavian, who had cultivated him, acquiesced. Cicero was overtaken while trying to escape and killed on 7 December 43. His head and hands, which had written and delivered the Philippics, were displayed on the Rostra. His career, from consul in 63 to proscribed victim in 43, shows how far the Republic had changed in twenty years. His writings survive in extraordinary volume, but they are the writings of a partisan, and must be weighed as such, alongside sources hostile to him.

In 42 BCE, Antony and Octavian crossed to Greece to confront Brutus and Cassius, who had gathered forces from the eastern provinces and client kings, in part by extracting large sums from cities in Asia and Greece. At two battles near Philippi in Macedonia, some weeks apart in October, the Liberator armies were defeated. In the first, Cassius, believing the battle lost, took his own life, and in the second Brutus did the same. The victory largely ended the organized military challenge of Caesar's assassins.

Philippi should not be described as the day the Republic ended. Consuls, magistrates, the Senate, and claims to republican legitimacy remained. Sextus Pompey held Sicily and the sea, Lepidus retained forces, and Antony and Octavian were not yet enemies. Yet Philippi removed the last army that fought on behalf of the tradition of senatorial liberty against the heirs of Caesar. After it, the question was which of the victors would prevail, and on what terms, rather than whether the old Republic could be restored by force.

Section 20.12

From Republic to One-Man Supremacy, 42–30 BCE

After Philippi the triumvirs divided the Roman world again. Antony took the east and Gaul, Octavian returned to Italy to settle veterans and take Spain, and Lepidus, whom they distrusted, was left Africa. The arrangement placed the two ambitious men in different spheres with different problems. Antony had to raise money and prepare for an attack on Parthia to avenge Carrhae. Octavian had to find land for tens of thousands of discharged soldiers, and that meant confiscating property from Italian communities, an issue examined in the Everyday Life feature below.

Resistance to the confiscations, combined with tension between the triumvirs, led to the Perusine War in 41 to 40. Lucius Antonius, the brother of Mark Antony and consul, and Antony's wife Fulvia rallied opponents of Octavian's settlements, and were besieged at Perusia in Etruria until famine forced surrender in early 40. Octavian treated the city harshly. War was averted between the triumvirs by the Treaty of Brundisium later in 40, which confirmed the division and included the marriage of Antony to Octavian's sister Octavia. The Perusine War shows that the civil wars were not only clashes of grand armies but conflicts over land and money that touched Italian towns directly.

Sextus Pompey remained an obstacle. From Sicily he commanded a fleet that threatened Italy's grain supply, and he sheltered proscribed persons. The triumvirs recognized him by the Treaty of Misenum in 39, but the peace broke down. Octavian's admiral, Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, built a fleet and defeated Sextus at Naulochus in 36. Sextus fled to the east and was killed in 35. Lepidus, who had joined the campaign, tried to claim Sicily, and was deprived of his command by Octavian, retaining only the office of chief priest, the pontifex maximus. The triumvirate became a duopoly.

In the east, Antony met Cleopatra at Tarsus in 41 and formed a partnership that combined political, military, and personal elements. Cleopatra's Egypt was the richest kingdom in the region, and Antony needed her resources for the Parthian campaign, which failed in 36. Antony reorganized the eastern client kingdoms and assigned territories and titles to Cleopatra and her children, arrangements formalized in the ceremony called the Donations of Alexandria in 34. Antony's actions were consistent with the long Roman practice of managing the east through client rulers. They were also easy to portray as un-Roman, and Octavian's propaganda, aided by poets and pamphleteers, depicted Antony as enthralled by a foreign queen. Historians must be careful: much of what survives about Cleopatra was shaped by Augustan hostility and later Roman sexualized stereotypes, and does not give a reliable picture of her as a ruler, diplomat, and administrator.

The triumviral powers expired at the end of 33 or 32, with the exact date disputed, and each man claimed to be defending the state. Antony divorced Octavia in 32. Octavian obtained and published, in a dubious act that Roman opinion found shocking, the contents of Antony's will, which said that Antony wished to be buried in Alexandria and acknowledged Caesarion, though the reliability of the account is debated. Italy and the western provinces swore an oath of loyalty to Octavian, and he had war declared not on Antony but on Cleopatra. This was a deliberate choice. Antony was a Roman commander with Roman legions and a Roman following. Casting the conflict as a foreign war, rather than a civil war, was a strategy of legitimation, and modern historians should not repeat it as if Actium were a contest between Rome and Egypt.

The decisive encounter came on 2 September 31 BCE off Actium on the western coast of Greece. Agrippa's blockade had reduced Antony's supplies and morale. In the naval engagement, Antony and Cleopatra broke through the blockade and sailed south, abandoning much of their fleet, and Antony's land army soon surrendered. The details of the battle, including whether Antony's escape was planned, are debated, since the sources were shaped by Octavian's version. In the summer of 30 Octavian advanced on Egypt. Antony's forces deserted, and on 1 August 30 Alexandria fell. Antony took his own life, and Cleopatra, after failing to negotiate, did the same. Caesarion was killed, and Egypt, the last major Hellenistic kingdom, came under Roman control, in an arrangement whose details belong to Chapter 21.

By the end of 30 BCE Octavian was without an equal military rival. Every competitor had been defeated, killed, or subordinated: Pompey, Caesar's opponents, the Liberators, Sextus Pompey, Lepidus, and Antony. The Republic had not been abolished. The magistrates were elected, the Senate met, and its language of liberty and law continued. What had disappeared was the competitive settlement in which no individual could monopolize armies and decision making. The question facing Rome was no longer whether the old balance could simply resume but what political order one victorious leader would construct from its surviving institutions.

Extraordinary commands became routine, emergency measures became precedents, alliances became armies, and armies became instruments of political survival. The intervals between crises shortened after 52, and the wars of 49 to 45 and 43 to 30 followed in rapid succession. The timeline also shows that the Republic's institutions continued to function during much of this period, so the steps were not obvious to contemporaries as steps toward the end.

Primary Source Window

Cicero, Letters to Atticus, January to March 49 BCE

Creator: Marcus Tullius Cicero, senator, orator, and former consul · Date: January to March 49 BCE · Type: Private correspondence to his friend Titus Pomponius Atticus, preserved and later collected and published

Context: In the weeks after Caesar entered Italy, Cicero was outside Rome, uncertain of his position and consulted by both sides. His letters to Atticus were written as events unfolded, and were not intended for publication in this form. They therefore differ from Caesar's published narrative of the same months.

Paraphrase of the letters, not a translation Across these letters Cicero describes changing rumors about Caesar's movements and terms. He expresses bewilderment at Pompey's plans, including the decision to abandon Rome and then Italy. He agonizes over whether to leave Italy to join Pompey, whom he distrusted, or to remain neutral, and he weighs the consequences for his family and reputation. He criticizes both leaders, worrying that each seeks power for himself, and he considers how Caesar's conduct, including his clemency, might change the character of the struggle. He also asks Atticus for news and advice, revealing how little reliable information circulated and how quickly matters changed.

Analysis: The letters are valuable because they show uncertainty in real time. Cicero did not know how the crisis would end, and he wrote as though several outcomes remained possible. They show that contemporaries did not experience the conflict as a clash between an obvious tyrant and an obvious defender of the Republic. They also show elite anxieties about property and status. At the same time, Cicero was a single senator, with strong self-interest and a talent for self-dramatization, and his opinions about Pompey and Caesar changed with circumstances. The letters say nothing about the views of soldiers, Italian townspeople, or provincials, and Atticus's replies are lost. Because the letters were collected later, editorial choices may have shaped what survives.

Multiple Perspectives

The Civil Wars as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed from patterns in the surviving evidence. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

A senatorial aristocrat: Sees the crisis as a struggle over dignitas, the standing of the ruling class, and over whether the Senate can control commanders. Some, like Cato, resist to the death. Others, like Cicero, hesitate, and many seek accommodation with the victor, while fearing for their property and their status.
A veteran soldier of Caesar: Owes loyalty to a commander who shares danger, pays well, and promises land. His future depends on the victory of the man who leads him, since the state provides no reliable pension. He may also feel proud of his service and of the bond with his legion.
A Gallic community: Faces conquest, tribute, killing, and enslavement. Some communities allied with Caesar against rivals, and others joined the revolt of 52. Whatever choices they made, they lived under an army that they could not stop.
An urban Roman citizen: Depends on grain, employment, and the security of the streets. May follow tribunes such as Clodius or support Caesar for his popular measures and public spectacles, while experiencing violence, shortages, and anxiety during each new war.
A provincial or client ruler in the east: Has to choose which Roman to support and pays the price of miscalculation. Requisitions and taxes for Pompey, Caesar, the Liberators, and Antony fell in rapid succession on the same cities.
A proscribed Roman and his family: Faces death, flight, and loss of property. Relies on the loyalty of relatives, freedpeople, and slaves, some of whom protect and some of whom betray.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Republic in 78, 44, and 30 BCE

What Changed

  • In 78, a Sullan settlement still tried to limit ambitious individuals. By 44, one man held permanent dictatorial powers. By 30, no rival to Octavian remained.
  • The Senate went from a body of some six hundred, to about nine hundred under Caesar, to a body reduced by proscription and civil war.
  • Long commands, civil war, and expectations of veteran rewards increasingly tied the fortunes of many armies to individual commanders, and the practice of settling veterans by confiscation had become normal.
  • Resources of provinces and allied or client kingdoms, including Ptolemaic Egypt, were increasingly mobilized by individual leaders.

What Stayed the Same

  • Magistracies, elections, the Senate, and assemblies existed throughout, and republican language remained the language of legitimacy.
  • Slavery, patronage, the dominance of landowning elites, and the dependence of the city on imported grain persisted.
  • Rural communities and provincial populations continued their agricultural routines, although war disrupted them.

Who experienced change most directly: senators and equestrians on the proscription lists, soldiers who depended on commanders, and Italians and provincials whose land or resources were taken. Who experienced greater continuity: many rural and provincial families whose daily life continued in familiar forms, and those in cities whose main concerns remained food and work. Why the pattern matters: a political order can end while its forms survive, which is why formal institutions alone cannot show whether a republic remains.

Cause and Effect

From Extraordinary Command to Civil War

Long-term causes

The governance of an empire by a city state constitution, the dependence of soldiers on commanders for rewards, debt and land problems, aristocratic competition, and precedents set by Marius and Sulla.

Short-term causes

Failure to ratify Pompey's settlement, the coalition of 60, Caesar's long command, the deaths of Julia and Crassus, urban violence, and the dispute over Caesar's candidacy and command in 50 to 49.

Event and immediate effects

Caesar's entry into Italy in January 49 began a civil war in which Caesar controlled Italy within weeks and later defeated Pompey and the Pompeians across the Mediterranean, at great cost to provinces.

Long-term consequences

The concentration of power under Caesar, the assassination, a second round of civil war, the Second Triumvirate, and the sole supremacy of Octavian, followed by the constitutional problem of Chapter 21.

Historical complexity: This sequence should not be read as a deterministic chain. At many points, including the debate of December 50 and January 49, the legislation of 52, and the aftermath of the Ides of March, different decisions were possible. Structural pressures made civil war more likely, but the specific outcome depended on choices by individuals, and on chance events such as the death of Crassus.

Everyday Life and Social History

Land, Soldiers, and Italian Towns

What did civil war mean for a small Italian town? After Philippi, Octavian was obliged to settle a large number of veterans, and Italy offered land. Communities designated for colonies lost farmland to soldiers, sometimes including the best fields, and residents were displaced, or forced to work as tenants on land they had owned. Ancient sources mention eighteen cities marked for confiscation, and later tradition connects the poetry of Virgil to the loss of land near Mantua, although the biographical details are uncertain. Owners could appeal to patrons or seek compensation, but many lost their livelihoods, and there were riots and armed resistance, including the Perusine War.

Soldiers, on the other hand, were often farmers or laborers who had enlisted for pay and a future. Many were poor, and they viewed land as their due after years of service, and they lived in the anxious knowledge that if their general lost, they would lose everything. The result was hostility on both sides, and a settlement that solved one problem by creating another. Provincial communities suffered too, with requisitions of grain, ships, and money for each army, and with the plundering that followed defeat. The evidence for such experiences comes mostly from elite writers and from archaeological and inscriptional traces of colonies, and it seldom preserves the voices of the dispossessed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the political settlement that emerged after Sulla failed to contain competition among powerful commanders, and how the wars that followed ended the republican balance without formally abolishing the Republic. Its central argument is that the collapse resulted from the interaction of structural pressures and contingent decisions. An institutional framework designed for a city state governed a Mediterranean empire. Soldiers depended on commanders. Aristocratic competition intensified. At key moments individuals chose escalation over compromise.

The decade after 78 BCE showed the paradox of the post-Sullan order. Sulla's arrangements were dismantled quickly, and the Republic relied on individuals with extraordinary powers to meet military emergencies, including the revolt of Lepidus, the war of Sertorius in Spain, and the slave revolt of Spartacus. The consulship of Pompey and Crassus in 70 restored the tribunate and reorganized the courts, but it also confirmed that reputation could outweigh the normal sequence of office.

Pompey's commands against the pirates and Mithridates extended that principle. They solved real problems and were granted by lawful vote, yet they created a commander with resources and prestige beyond any ordinary magistrate, and a political system that had no established means of rewarding him. The senatorial resistance to his requests helped drive the coalition of 60 BCE.

The conspiracy of Catiline brought emergency powers to the center of politics. The execution of citizens without trial in 63 was defended as necessary and attacked as illegal, and the argument was not a mere pretext. It shaped Cicero's later exile, and it foreshadowed later uses of extraordinary measures. The First Triumvirate, a modern term for an informal alliance among Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, allowed its members to bypass senatorial opposition, and it should be distinguished from the legally constituted Second Triumvirate of 43.

Caesar's conquest of Gaul brought wealth, army, and prestige, at an enormous human cost to Gallic peoples. His own Commentarii are both a major source and a work of political persuasion, and ancient casualty figures must be treated with caution. The deaths of Julia and Crassus, the breakdown of order at Rome, and disputes over Caesar's command and candidacy destroyed the balance between the two great rivals. The crossing of the Rubicon in January 49 was the culmination of a contested legal crisis in which both sides had arguments and both bore responsibility.

The civil war of 49 to 45 was Mediterranean in scale, reaching Spain, Greece, Egypt, Anatolia, and North Africa. Caesar prevailed at Pharsalus, Thapsus, and Munda through speed, veteran troops, and clemency, while provincial communities paid the costs. In Egypt, Cleopatra VII appeared first as a ruler in a dynastic struggle. Caesar's dictatorship combined real reforms, including the calendar, colonies, and extension of citizenship, with a concentration of power that alarmed many senators. Republican forms survived, but the political reality was different.

The assassination of 44 BCE removed Caesar but produced no settlement. The conspirators were divided in motive and had no plan for armies, veterans, or Antony, and their aristocratic conception of liberty had little to offer others. The will of Caesar brought Octavian into politics. The legally constituted Second Triumvirate, established by the lex Titia, claimed to restore the state while displacing normal government. Its proscriptions, financed by confiscation and marked by the killing of Cicero, exceeded the earlier terror of Sulla in some respects, though the circumstances differed.

Philippi destroyed the principal military force of Caesar's assassins but did not end the Republic in a single day. Confiscation for veteran settlements produced rebellion in Italy, the Perusine War, and the war with Sextus Pompey. The partnership of Antony and Cleopatra, and Octavian's decision to present Actium as a war against a foreign queen, ended the rivalry between the last two triumvirs. Octavian was left without a military equal in 30 BCE.

The Republic had not been abolished, but the competitive settlement that had defined it was gone. The chapter has emphasized evidence throughout: Cicero's letters show uncertainty, Caesar's Commentarii show self-presentation, and later writers show Augustan hindsight. It has also emphasized the experience of those who lived through the wars, including Gallic communities, Italian landowners, soldiers, proscribed Romans, and provincials. Chapter 21 takes up the question that Octavian's victory posed: what order would be built from the surviving institutions.

Key Terms

Imperium
The lawful authority to command troops and exercise judicial and administrative power, held by consuls, praetors, and provincial governors.
Provincia
The sphere of responsibility assigned to a magistrate, later a territory governed by a Roman commander.
Extraordinary command
A military or administrative appointment that departed from normal rules on office, term, scale, or the holder's status.
Proconsul
A former consul or a magistrate given imperium to govern a province or command an army after office.
Tribune of the plebs
An annually elected official who could propose legislation and veto other officials' actions, and who was legally inviolable.
Senatus consultum ultimum
The Senate's emergency decree calling on magistrates to protect the state, whose legal force was disputed.
Dictator
A magistrate appointed in emergencies with supreme authority for a limited term, an office revived by Sulla and Caesar in altered forms.
Dictator perpetuo
Dictator without a time limit, the title given to Caesar in early 44 BCE.
First Triumvirate
A modern label for the informal alliance of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, which held no office.
Second Triumvirate
The legally constituted board of Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus, established by the lex Titia in 43 BCE.
Lex Titia
The law of November 43 BCE that created the triumvirate for constituting the state.
Proscription
Public listing of outlawed citizens who could be killed with impunity and whose property was confiscated.
Veteran colony
A settlement of discharged soldiers on land, often taken from existing communities.
Clementia
Mercy toward defeated opponents, used by Caesar as both a virtue and a means of political legitimation.
Libertas
Liberty as understood by the Roman elite, meaning freedom from domination and the ability to take part in public life, not modern individual rights.
Mos maiorum
The custom of the ancestors, an unwritten body of precedent invoked in political argument.
Commentarii
Caesar's narratives of the Gallic and civil wars, written in a plain style as both record and political argument.
Liberators
The name the assassins and their supporters gave themselves, who claimed to have freed Rome from tyranny.
Triumph
A ceremonial procession granted to a victorious commander, a mark of status and political capital.
Equestrians
Wealthy Romans below the senatorial order, prominent in finance, tax collection, and commerce.
Client king
A ruler who governed a kingdom in alliance with Rome and subject to Roman influence, as in Egypt, Numidia, and Judea.
Julian calendar
The solar calendar introduced by Caesar in 46 BCE and effective in 45, replacing the older calendar that had drifted from the seasons.
Donations of Alexandria
The 34 BCE arrangement in which Antony assigned eastern territories to Cleopatra and her children, used in Octavian's propaganda.
Ptolemaic kingdom
The Hellenistic monarchy of Egypt, ruled by the dynasty that included Cleopatra VII, and annexed by Rome after 30 BCE.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can end without being abolished. The Roman Republic survived in name and form through decades of civil war, and its language of liberty and law remained available to every faction. What ended was a balance among competitors. Understanding this helps students see why institutions alone do not guarantee the constraints they were designed to impose, and why the decisions of individuals, made under real pressures and with incomplete knowledge, matter to outcomes that later look inevitable.

The chapter also connects regions that later narratives often separate. Rome's civil wars were fought in Italy, Gaul, Spain, Greece, Anatolia, Egypt, and North Africa, and they were paid for by peoples from all these regions. The conquest of Gaul incorporated a large part of continental western Europe into the Roman world, at a human cost that Roman sources tend to minimize, and the annexation of Egypt ended the last major Hellenistic kingdom. The Mediterranean unity created by these wars was political and military as well as commercial and cultural, and it shaped the world that Chapter 22 describes at its height.

Within Volume I, the chapter completes the movement from a city state to an empire whose scale placed its old institutions under severe and repeated strain. Chapter 15 described the republican order, Chapters 16 and 17 its expansion, Chapter 18 its social foundations, and Chapter 19 its crises. Here the strains produced sustained civil war and a victor without rivals. The Principate that follows was not the inevitable result, and it was not simply an imposition on an unwilling Rome. It was a construction, made by particular people from the ruins of a competitive settlement that had already been destroyed.

Chapter 21 takes up that construction: how Octavian, later Augustus, built a new political order from surviving republican institutions, and how he presented it as restoration. Students should carry forward the habits of mind developed here: reading partisan sources carefully, distinguishing form from reality, and asking what was contingent and what was structural. Later, in Volume II and beyond, the memory of Caesar, Brutus, and the Republic itself would be invoked again and again, in debates about tyranny, liberty, and the legitimacy of power, which is why the difference between the history and its memory is so important.
