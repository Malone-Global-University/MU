Crusaders, Ottomans, and the End of Byzantium

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 1204 and 1453 the political order that had made Constantinople the capital of the largest Christian state in southeastern Europe broke apart, was partly reassembled, and was finally replaced. The city that fell to Latin crusaders in 1204 and to Ottoman forces in 1453 was the same city, but the world around it had been transformed.

Two objects in two different cities help frame this chapter. High above the entrance of the basilica of San Marco in Venice stand four gilded bronze horses, today represented on the facade by replicas, and Venetian tradition and most historians connect their arrival in Venice with the conquest of Constantinople in April 1204, when they were carried away from the Hippodrome. Their original date and place of manufacture are uncertain, but their later history is not: they became trophies, displayed in a city whose merchants had profited enormously from the destruction of a rival capital. In Constantinople itself, the great church of Hagia Sophia, completed under Justinian in 537 and examined in earlier chapters of this Part, passed through an equally striking sequence of identities. It was the cathedral of the Orthodox patriarchs, then a Latin cathedral under a Venetian patriarch after 1204, then an Orthodox church again after 1261, and after 29 May 1453 the principal mosque of an Ottoman city. Between the horses and the church lies the story of how a medieval empire lost its capital twice, recovered it once, and ceased to exist as a state.

This chapter is the capstone of Part VI. Chapters 36 through 40 traced the Byzantine state after Justinian, the controversy over images, the missionary expansion of Orthodox Christianity among the Slavs, the formation of Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus, and the growing separation of Latin and Greek Christianity that is conventionally dated to 1054 but that, as Chapter 40 argued, was a long and uneven process. Chapter 41 shows what happened to that entire eastern Mediterranean and Balkan system between roughly 1200 and 1453. Its subject is a span of about 250 years, several competing states, two imperial transformations, and a final siege. It covers the Fourth Crusade and the sack of Constantinople in 1204, the fragmentation of the Byzantine world into Latin and Greek successor states, the restoration of Greek imperial rule in 1261, the Bulgarian and Serbian expansions of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the civil wars and plague that weakened the fourteenth century empire, the emergence of the Ottomans from a Turkish frontier principality, their advance into the Balkans, the failed attempts at western aid and church union, and the conquest of Constantinople in 1453.

The central argument of the chapter is that southeastern Europe was transformed from a region dominated by a powerful Byzantine Empire into a fragmented competitive field. In that field Byzantine successor states, Latin possessions, Bulgarian and Serbian powers, Italian commercial republics, Hungarian kings, and Turkish principalities all pursued their own interests. The Ottoman state emerged from this competitive frontier environment and eventually created a new imperial order out of it. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 was therefore the culmination of centuries of political fragmentation, economic and military change, interstate competition, and Ottoman expansion. It was not the inevitable collapse of a civilization, and it was not simply the result of a single battle or a single weapon.

Students should be especially wary of two opposite narrative habits. The first is the story of Byzantine decadence, in which an exhausted empire waits for a younger and more vigorous conqueror. The second is a providential story, in which the Ottomans were destined from their beginnings to conquer Constantinople. Neither survives close contact with the evidence. The empire that crusaders conquered in 1204 was weakened, but its capture depended on a chain of contingent decisions examined in Section 41.2. The empire restored in 1261 was a smaller and poorer state, but it produced remarkable scholarship, art, and theology for two more centuries. The early Ottomans were one of several Turkish frontier polities in Anatolia, and for much of the fourteenth century they were less obviously powerful than their rivals. Historians therefore ask how particular outcomes arose, and not merely why they were bound to occur.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually multilingual and unusually uneven. Greek historians such as Nicetas Choniates, George Akropolites, George Pachymeres, Nikephoros Gregoras, John VI Kantakouzenos, and, for the fifteenth century, Doukas, George Sphrantzes, Laonikos Chalkokondyles, and Kritoboulos describe events from Byzantine perspectives that were shaped by their authors' social positions and political loyalties. Latin sources include the crusade narratives of Geoffroi de Villehardouin and Robert de Clari, the letters of Pope Innocent III, Venetian and Genoese notarial and state records, and the Chronicle of the Morea, which survives in several versions. Slavic evidence includes charters, monastic foundation documents, saints' lives, and the law code associated with Stefan Dušan. Ottoman evidence presents a particular problem: the earliest surviving narrative histories of the dynasty were written in the later fifteenth century, long after the events they describe, and were shaped by the needs of a now imperial court. Archaeology, coins, seals, fortifications, and surviving buildings supplement all of these.

The chapter belongs to Volume II, whose central concern is the formation and diversification of medieval Latin, Byzantine, Orthodox, northern, central, and eastern Europe. By ending Part VI in 1453, it closes the story of the Roman imperial state in the east without ending the history of Orthodox Christianity, Greek learning, or Balkan societies, all of which continue under Ottoman and other rule. Several themes treated only briefly here are developed elsewhere: the crusading movement as a whole in Chapter 47, the Black Death in Chapter 50, the Western Schism and conciliarism in Chapter 53, the movement of Greek scholars to Italy in Chapter 57, and the Ottoman position in the European state system in Chapter 79. Chapter 42 turns back to the agricultural and demographic expansion of the High Middle Ages and thus begins a new Part, but readers should carry from this chapter the recognition that Europe's eastern and southeastern history did not stand outside the medieval world described in Volume II. It was a central part of it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the weaknesses of the Byzantine state in the late twelfth century and why the capture of Constantinople in 1204 was a contingent outcome rather than an inevitable one.
  2. Trace the sequence of decisions that turned the Fourth Crusade from an expedition toward Egypt into an attack on Constantinople, distinguishing the motives of crusaders, Venetians, Byzantine claimants, and the papacy.
  3. Assess the sack of 1204 using Greek and Latin sources, and explain why it deepened the division between Latin and Orthodox Christians.
  4. Compare the Latin Empire and the Greek successor states of Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond as competing claimants to Roman imperial legitimacy.
  5. Analyze the restoration of 1261 and explain why a restored Byzantine Empire could not recover the power of the pre-1204 state.
  6. Describe the political transformation of the Balkans through the Second Bulgarian Empire and the Serbian kingdom and empire under the Nemanjić dynasty, without projecting modern national states backward.
  7. Evaluate the roles of civil war, plague, fiscal weakness, and Italian commercial power in the fourteenth century contraction of Byzantium.
  8. Interpret competing scholarly explanations for the rise of the early Ottoman state, including debates over ghaza, frontier society, and administrative continuity.
  9. Trace Ottoman expansion into Thrace and the Balkans from 1354 to 1444, distinguishing conquest, vassalage, negotiated surrender, and incorporation.
  10. Distinguish the historical battle of Kosovo in 1389 from the later national mythology surrounding it.
  11. Explain why efforts at church union and western military aid failed to save Constantinople, and why many Orthodox Christians opposed union.
  12. Evaluate the causes of the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and explain what did and did not end in that year.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Fourth Crusade end at Constantinople, and what does the answer reveal about the relationship between ideals, debts, and political opportunity?
  2. After 1204, who could plausibly claim to be the continuation of the Roman Empire, and what evidence did each claimant use?
  3. In what sense was the Byzantine Empire restored in 1261, and in what sense was it not?

  6. How did one Anatolian frontier principality become the leading power in southeastern Europe, and what are the limits of the sources used to answer that question?
  7. Why did Latin Christendom's crusades and councils fail to prevent the Ottoman conquest of the Balkans and Constantinople?
  8. How do later national and religious memories, such as those surrounding Kosovo or 1453, shape what is remembered about the events themselves?

Historical Context

Students entering this chapter should recall the principal outcome of the preceding chapters of Part VI. By the middle of the twelfth century the Byzantine Empire had recovered from the crisis of the late eleventh century, when the defeat at Manzikert in 1071 and the loss of much of Anatolia to Turkish groups had seemed to threaten its survival. The Komnenian emperors, beginning with Alexios I in 1081 and continuing through John II and Manuel I, rebuilt the army, reorganized the fiscal and military system around grants of land and revenue to aristocratic families, and restored Byzantine power in the Balkans, the Aegean, and western Anatolia. Constantinople, with a population that scholars estimate in the hundreds of thousands at its peak, though with wide uncertainty, remained the largest city in Christian Europe and a major commercial and cultural center. Byzantine diplomacy reached from Hungary and the Balkans to Italy, the crusader states, Egypt, and the Turkish sultanates of Anatolia.

That recovery had limits. Manuel I's defeat by the Seljuk sultan of Rum at Myriokephalon in 1176 ended hopes of reconquering the Anatolian interior. The Komnenian system depended on the personal authority of capable emperors, on the loyalty of aristocratic families who held lands and privileges, and on a close but uneasy relationship with Italian merchant republics, above all Venice. Venice had received trade privileges within the empire since the late eleventh century, including exemption from customs duties that native Byzantine merchants had to pay. Such privileges, granted by imperial charters called chrysobulls, were originally payment for naval assistance. Over time they created Italian commercial communities in Constantinople whose wealth, legal status, and political weight aroused resentment among Greek merchants and townspeople. Resentment also ran the other way: Manuel I's arrest of Venetians in 1171 and the massacre of Latin residents of the city in 1182 left lasting bitterness in Venetian and other Latin memory.

The wider Balkan and eastern Mediterranean setting had also changed. Hungary had become a major power on the northern frontier, contesting Dalmatia and the Danubian lands with Byzantium. Serbian leaders in the highlands of Raška asserted growing autonomy. Bulgarian lands, incorporated into the empire after Basil II's conquest in the early eleventh century, were ripe for revolt. In western Europe, the crusading movement, discussed fully in Chapter 47, had by 1200 created an expectation that Latin Christians could act collectively under papal authority against enemies of the faith, and the memory of the First and Second Crusades included frequent tension with Byzantium. The institutional and theological separation examined in Chapter 40 meant that Greek and Latin Christians increasingly viewed each other with suspicion, even though ordinary contact, trade, intermarriage, and diplomacy across the boundary continued.

Students should also note a methodological point about dates. Byzantine, Slavic, and many contemporary sources used the Julian calendar and often counted years from the supposed creation of the world, with annual cycles called indictions used for administrative purposes. This textbook uses BCE and CE dating and gives dates in the Julian calendar used by all the societies discussed here, which was not replaced in Catholic Europe by the Gregorian calendar until 1582. For example, the battle of Kosovo was fought on 15 June 1389 in the Julian calendar, equivalent to 23 June in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. Modern Vidovdan is observed on 28 June because 15 June Julian corresponds to 28 June Gregorian under the present thirteen-day calendar difference. Likewise, the fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453 is given in the Julian calendar. Where the difference matters, the text will say so.

Core Vocabulary Preview

The following key terms appear throughout this chapter. Students should understand each term before proceeding; each is defined here and again in the Key Terms review.

Chrysobull
A solemn Byzantine imperial charter authenticated by a gold seal, used for grants, privileges, and treaties, including the commercial privileges of Venice.
Pronoia
A conditional Byzantine imperial grant of revenues, properties, or privileges, often but not exclusively to military beneficiaries. It could support imperial military organization, though later hereditary grants could reduce direct imperial control.
Latin Empire
The crusader state founded at Constantinople in 1204, which ruled the city and parts of Thrace until Nicaean forces recovered it in 1261.
Successor states
The Greek polities, including Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond, that continued Byzantine traditions after 1204 and competed for imperial legitimacy.
Rhomaioi
The Greek name by which the subjects of the Byzantine Empire called themselves, meaning Romans.
Autocephaly
Ecclesiastical self-governance of a church under its own head, as with the Serbian archbishopric established in 1219.
Despotate
A territory ruled by a despot, a high Byzantine title, as in the Despotates of Epirus and the Morea, and the later Serbian Despotate.
Hesychasm
A tradition of contemplative prayer in Orthodox monasticism, defended by Gregory Palamas and affirmed by church councils in the fourteenth century.
Palaiologan
Relating to the dynasty founded by Michael VIII Palaiologos, which ruled the restored Byzantine Empire from 1261 to 1453, and to the art and learning of that era.
Union of Lyons
The 1274 agreement by which Michael VIII accepted union of the Greek church with Rome, repudiated after his death.
Beylik
A Turkish principality in Anatolia, ruled by a bey, that emerged after the decline of Seljuk authority.
Ghaza
A term for frontier warfare used in Turkish and Persian sources, whose role in early Ottoman expansion is debated.
Timar
An Ottoman grant of revenue from land assigned to a cavalryman in return for military service.
Sipahi
An Ottoman cavalryman, often supported by a timar.
Vassalage
A relationship in which a ruler kept territory while paying tribute and providing troops to a superior, as many Balkan lords did under Ottoman suzerainty.
Janissary
A member of the Ottoman sultan's household infantry, recruited originally largely from captives and later from levies of Christian youths.
Filioque
The Latin phrase meaning "and from the Son," added in the West to the creed in describing the procession of the Holy Spirit, a central Latin-Greek theological dispute.
Conciliarism
The view that general councils held authority superior to, or independent of, the pope, which shaped western church politics during the Ferrara-Florence negotiations.
Theodosian Walls
The fifth century land fortifications of Constantinople that protected the city for roughly a thousand years.
Stavraton
A silver coin introduced in the later fourteenth century as Byzantine gold coinage largely ceased.
Zealots of Thessalonica
A faction that ruled Thessalonica from 1342 to 1349 during the Byzantine civil war, in conflict with local aristocrats.
Section 41.1

Byzantium on the Eve of the Fourth Crusade

This section is a bridge from Chapter 40 and not a retelling of the Schism. It asks what condition the Byzantine state was in at the end of the twelfth century. After Manuel I's death in 1180, the empire entered a period of dynastic instability. His young son Alexios II was overthrown in 1183 by Manuel's cousin Andronikos I Komnenos, whose short and violent reign (1183 to 1185) began with the massacre of Latin residents of Constantinople in 1182, an event in which Italian merchants, among them Pisans and Genoese as well as Venetians, were killed or sold into slavery. Andronikos was overthrown and killed in 1185, and the throne passed to Isaac II Angelos. In 1195 Isaac's brother Alexios III Angelos deposed and blinded him, and Isaac's young son, also named Alexios, was later to become the central figure in the diversion of the Fourth Crusade.

The Angeloi emperors presided over a period of territorial loss and provincial fragmentation. In 1185 a Norman fleet and army from Sicily sacked Thessalonica, the second city of the empire, and although the invaders were driven out, the shock exposed the weakness of provincial defenses. In 1185 and 1186 the brothers Peter and Asen, leaders among the Bulgarians and Vlachs of the Balkan mountains, rebelled against the imposition of new taxes and, with support from the Cumans of the northern steppe, began the restoration of a Bulgarian state examined in Section 41.7. Cyprus broke away under a Komnenian relative in 1184 and was taken in 1191 by Richard I of England. In the Serbian lands, Stefan Nemanja established a largely independent principality. In parts of Greece and Anatolia, local magnates such as Leo Sgouros in the Peloponnese and central Greece carved out semi autonomous domains. These losses did not mean the empire was on the verge of collapse, but they reduced revenue and reduced the ability of the emperor to command loyalty.

Structural problems compounded personal failures. The Komnenian state also relied on conditional grants of revenues, properties, or privileges, often described by the term pronoia and frequently, though not exclusively, given to military beneficiaries. Such grants could strengthen imperial military organization by rewarding service. Under weaker emperors and in some regions they could also empower provincial elites and complicate tax collection, but that effect was neither inherent nor uniform. At the same time the empire's navy, neglected in the late twelfth century, could no longer easily defend the coasts or project power without Italian ships. Revenue from trade increasingly passed into the hands of Venetian and, later, Genoese and Pisan merchants whose privileges reduced the customs revenue the state received. The Byzantine government sometimes tried to balance one Italian power against another, as in the grants to Pisa and Genoa, but this reinforced Latin commercial influence rather than ending it.

It is important not to overstate the empire's weakness. Constantinople in 1200 was still enormously wealthy, still defended by walls that had resisted countless sieges, still the center of a bureaucracy, a church, and a diplomatic network with no real Latin equivalent. Its fall in 1204 was not predetermined by the Angeloi's failures. Previous attacks, including Bulgarian, Arab, Rus, and earlier crusader forces, had failed. What was different in 1203 and 1204 was the combination of a large, experienced western army, Venetian naval expertise, a rival claimant inside the empire who invited the attackers, and an emperor, Alexios III, who fled rather than fight. The chain of decisions leading to this point is the subject of the next section.

Historical Vocabulary
Chrysobull

A chrysobull (from Greek words meaning "golden seal") was a solemn imperial charter issued by Byzantine emperors, authenticated by a gold seal attached to the document. Chrysobulls conferred land, tax exemptions, trade privileges, and ecclesiastical rights, and they also recorded treaties with foreign powers. The chrysobull issued to Venice in 1082 under Alexios I, and its later renewals, gave Venetian merchants exemption from customs duties and quarters in Constantinople. Slavic rulers also issued charters modeled on Byzantine practice, and Stefan Dušan's charters to monasteries often used the same Byzantine form.

The term matters here because commercial privileges were not abstract economic facts. They were written grants, repeatedly negotiated, withdrawn, and restored, and each change affected the relationship among the emperor, Greek merchants, and Italian communities. Students should avoid thinking of the privileges as a simple "surrender" of sovereignty. Emperors used them as diplomatic and military instruments, yet their cumulative effect was to shift commercial advantage toward Italian merchants.

Section 41.2

The Fourth Crusade Turns toward Constantinople

Pope Innocent III proclaimed a new crusade in 1198, with the aim of recovering Jerusalem, which Saladin had taken in 1187, by first attacking Egypt, the center of Ayyubid power. French and Flemish lords, led first by Count Theobald of Champagne and, after his death in 1201, by Marquis Boniface of Montferrat, contracted with the Republic of Venice for ships and provisions. The treaty of 1201, under the leadership of the elderly Doge Enrico Dandolo, committed Venice to transport a very large army and fleet, with sources giving figures of several thousand knights and tens of thousands of infantry, for a sum reported as 85,000 silver marks. These figures come from narrative sources written after the event and should be treated as indications of scale, not exact accounting. What is clear is that far fewer crusaders reached Venice in 1202 than the contract had anticipated, and that they could not pay the agreed price.

The consequence was a debt, and the debt determined events. Venice, which had committed substantial resources to building a fleet, could not simply absorb the loss. The Doge proposed that the crusaders help Venice recover the Dalmatian city of Zara (modern Zadar), which had rebelled against Venetian authority and placed itself under the king of Hungary, himself a crusader. Some crusaders objected, and Innocent III had forbidden attacks on Christian cities, but the army took Zara in November 1202 and the pope excommunicated those who took part, later lifting the sentence for most of the crusaders while keeping it, at least for a time, against the Venetians. Even in this act we see how crusaders, Venetians, and the papacy pursued different aims within a single enterprise.

At Zara the crusaders received an offer that changed everything. Alexios Angelos, son of the deposed Isaac II, had escaped from Constantinople and sought western help to restore his father. He was connected by marriage to Philip of Swabia, a claimant to the German throne whose court was friendly to the crusade leaders. Alexios promised, through his envoys, that if the crusaders restored his family he would pay a very large sum, provide troops for the Egyptian expedition, maintain a force in the Holy Land, and bring the Greek Church into obedience to Rome. Some crusaders, including Boniface, were attracted by the financial promise, by the possibility of resolving the debt, and by the claim that a legitimate emperor had been wrongfully deposed. Other crusaders were troubled, and some left the expedition. Pope Innocent III did not authorize the diversion, though he hoped for church union.

The fleet reached Constantinople in June 1203. The crusaders seized the fortified suburb of Galata, broke the chain across the Golden Horn, and on 17 July launched a combined assault by land and sea. Alexios III fled the city. The Byzantine aristocracy, rather than bringing Isaac II out of prison and then dismissing the crusaders, restored him with his son as co-emperor, Alexios IV, on 1 August. The new emperors could not deliver their promises. The treasury was depleted, the Orthodox clergy and populace had no intention of accepting submission to Rome, and attempts to raise money by stripping church ornaments inflamed popular anger. Fighting between Latin troops and Greek residents led in August to a great fire that destroyed large districts of the city. In January 1204 a palace coup led by Alexios Doukas, nicknamed Mourtzouphlos, deposed Alexios IV, who was soon strangled, and Isaac II died. Mourtzouphlos was crowned Alexios V.

Historians have long debated whether the diversion was a premeditated plot, perhaps by Venice, or the result of contingent circumstances. The older view of a Venetian conspiracy has been largely abandoned. Studies by Donald Queller and Thomas Madden, and by Jonathan Phillips, emphasize a sequence of decisions in which the debt, the Zara episode, Alexios's offer, and the failure of the offer each narrowed the options available to the crusade leaders. This chapter follows that approach, while recognizing that Venice benefited enormously and that Venetian leaders had strong commercial reasons to support an outcome that favored them in Constantinople. After Alexios V's seizure of power, the crusaders and Venetians agreed in March 1204 on a partition treaty providing for the election of an emperor and the division of the empire's lands and revenues, with Venice receiving a large share. After a first assault failed on 9 April, a second attack on 12 April breached the sea walls along the Golden Horn. Alexios V fled, and the crusaders entered the city.

Section 41.3

The Sack of Constantinople, 1204

The conquest of the city was followed by three days of pillage. Nicetas Choniates, an eyewitness who held high office in the Byzantine government and fled the city, described Latin soldiers killing civilians, assaulting women, breaking into houses and churches, and tearing apart sacred objects for their precious materials. Latin accounts do not deny the pillage, but they portray it differently. Villehardouin, a leader of the crusade, stressed the unimaginable quantity of booty and the rules supposedly governing its division, and Robert de Clari, a knight of lesser rank, described the city's wonders and the division of loot with more fascination than guilt. Pope Innocent III, who had not authorized the attack, expressed both a desire for church union and, in a letter that survives, dismay at the crusaders' atrocities against fellow Christians. Different texts thus pursue different purposes, and historians must weigh them accordingly.

Source criticism is essential for estimating what happened. Greek writers had every reason to emphasize Latin brutality, and Choniates wrote partly as a lament for a lost world and as a political argument in the context of the exiled Greek court. Latin chroniclers had reasons to justify or minimize. Casualty figures in many accounts are very large but unreliable, and no trustworthy count of the dead exists. Modern historians generally accept that large numbers of civilians were killed and many more lost property, that churches and monasteries were looted and desecrated, that fires in 1203 and 1204 destroyed large parts of the city, and that a vast quantity of precious objects was carried away. Beyond that, estimates of the number of victims, the amount of treasure, and the precise sequence of looting should be treated with caution.

The most visible consequence was a transfer of cultural property to the west. Relics, including pieces associated with the Passion of Christ, were taken to churches in Venice, France, Germany, and elsewhere. The Venetian treasury at San Marco received icons, reliquaries, and the bronze horses. In 1239 the Latin emperor Baldwin II, desperate for funds, sold the Crown of Thorns, which he had pawned to Venetian merchants, to King Louis IX of France, who built the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house it. Many ancient and Byzantine sculptures in Constantinople were melted down for metal. Losses of manuscripts are harder to measure; the surviving evidence indicates that important libraries and collections suffered, but historians disagree on the scale. The Latin conquerors converted Hagia Sophia into a Latin cathedral and installed a Venetian, Thomas Morosini, as patriarch, a symbolic reversal of the order the Greeks had known.

The longer religious significance is great. Chapter 40 argued that the division between Latin and Greek Christianity grew gradually and that the events of 1054 were less decisive than later traditions suggested. The sack of 1204, followed by the replacement of Greek bishops with Latin prelates and by attempts to impose Latin practice, transformed an institutional and theological disagreement into a lived memory of violence and occupation. For many Orthodox Christians, the Latins were no longer merely Christians with a different practice but conquerors who had desecrated the holiest church in the Orthodox world. This memory strongly colored Greek responses to later proposals for union, as Section 41.13 shows. At the same time, historians increasingly note that Latin and Greek communities continued to coexist in many places after 1204, and that the hardening of positions did not eliminate cooperation.

Primary Source Window

Nicetas Choniates, History, on the Sack of Constantinople in 1204

Creator: Nicetas Choniates, Byzantine official and historian · Date: written in the years after 1204, partly in exile at Nicaea · Type: Greek narrative history by an eyewitness, with a rhetorical and moral purpose

Context: Choniates held senior administrative posts in the Angeloi government and was in Constantinople during the conquest. He escaped the city with his family and later joined the court of Theodore I Laskaris at Nicaea, where he continued to revise his history. His account of the capture is among the most detailed and best known, and it was written for an audience of educated Greeks who had lost their capital.

Paraphrase of Choniates' account (not a quotation) Choniates reports that Latin soldiers broke into churches and tore down sacred furnishings, that Hagia Sophia was stripped of its altar and treasures, and that animals were brought into the church to carry away the plunder. He claims that a woman was seated on the patriarch's throne in a gesture of mockery. He contrasts the conduct of the crusaders, fellow Christians who had taken the cross, with the more merciful treatment that he says Saladin's soldiers had shown the Latin inhabitants of Jerusalem in 1187. He also describes the flight of residents, including his own family's escape, and mourns the destruction of ancient statues and of the city's beauty. This is a summary of themes across several passages, not a translation of any single continuous passage.

Analysis: The account is valuable because it is detailed, contemporary, and written by someone who saw events directly. It reveals how educated Greeks understood the sack: as a moral outrage by Christians against Christians, and as the destruction of a cultural inheritance. It also reveals the writer's own anger, his political position in the Nicaean emigration, and his use of classical and biblical models. Choniates cannot tell us how Latin soldiers understood their actions, how the Venetians organized their share, or how ordinary Greek residents who were not part of the elite experienced the days afterward. Latin sources, such as Villehardouin and Robert de Clari, and the later letters of Innocent III, provide counterweights, but they too reflect their authors' interests. Historians therefore compare accounts rather than relying on any one.

Section 41.4

The Latin Empire and the Byzantine Successor States

After 1204 there was no single Byzantine state. The conquerors elected Baldwin of Flanders as emperor in May 1204 and divided the empire according to the partition treaty. Venice took a large share of the capital, key ports, and islands, including eventually Crete, which it acquired from Boniface of Montferrat, and Negroponte (Euboea) and other strategic locations. Boniface became king of Thessalonica. Other Latin knights established the Principality of Achaea in the Peloponnese, under the Villehardouin family, and the Duchy of Athens, held by the de la Roche family. Marco Sanudo, a Venetian noble, established a duchy in the Aegean islands. The result was a patchwork of western lordships in Greek lands, held together by feudal ties that were often weaker in practice than in theory. This Latin Empire of Constantinople, as modern historians call it, ruled a limited territory around the capital and in Thrace and was chronically short of manpower and money.

The Latin Empire suffered early disaster. In April 1205 the Bulgarian ruler Kaloyan, whose alliance the Latins had spurned, and his Cuman allies defeated the Latin army at Adrianople, capturing Baldwin, who died in captivity. His brother Henry of Flanders, emperor from 1206 to 1216, was an able ruler who sought accommodation with the Greek aristocracy of Thrace and Macedonia and with the Orthodox population. After him the empire declined. Peter of Courtenay, Robert of Courtenay, and the last emperor Baldwin II struggled with limited resources. Baldwin II spent years traveling in western Europe to raise money and military assistance, pawning relics and, in some accounts, even stripping the lead from the roofs of the city. The Latin Empire survived for 57 years, but it never became a strong state.

Greek political life continued in several centers, and the central question after 1204 was which of them, if any, could claim to be the continuation of the Roman Empire. The Empire of Nicaea, founded by Theodore I Laskaris, son in law of Alexios III, ruled western Anatolia from Nicaea (modern İznik). Theodore gained the loyalty of many refugees, received the Orthodox patriarch in exile, and was crowned emperor around 1208. In Epirus, in northwestern Greece, members of the Komnenos Doukas family established a state that soon expanded; Theodore Komnenos Doukas captured Thessalonica from the Latins in 1224 and was crowned emperor there. On the southeastern Black Sea coast, Alexios and David Komnenos, grandsons of Andronikos I, founded the Empire of Trebizond in 1204 with the support of their Georgian relatives. Each claimed Roman or imperial legitimacy; each drew on different bases of support.

These competitors faced Latin, Bulgarian, and Turkish rivals as well as each other. Ivan Asen II of Bulgaria defeated Theodore of Epirus at Klokotnitsa in 1230, ending Epirote hopes of recapturing Constantinople and making Bulgaria, for a time, the strongest power in the Balkans. Nicaea defeated the Seljuk sultan of Rum at Antioch on the Meander in 1211, securing its eastern frontier for a time. The victory of Nicaea over its rivals should not be written as predetermined. Epirus held Thessalonica, the second city of the empire, and had a powerful army in the 1220s. Bulgaria could have become the heir to Byzantine imperial authority in the Balkans. Nicaea's eventual success depended on its secure Anatolian base, its strong rulers, its control of the patriarchate, and the weakness and internal disunity of the Latin Empire.

Historical Vocabulary
Romania, the Rhomaioi, and "Byzantine"

The people whom modern historians call Byzantines called themselves Rhomaioi, Romans, and their state the Roman Empire. The word "Byzantine" derives from the ancient name of Constantinople and became a standard scholarly label in later centuries, but it was not the term used by the empire's inhabitants. Latin Europeans after 1204 used Romania to refer to the lands of the former Byzantine Empire, as in the Latin states of "Romania," while Latin writers often called the Byzantine Greeks "Greeks" and sometimes denied that they were truly Roman, a rhetorical position in the competition over imperial legitimacy. Turkish and later Ottoman sources used Rum, from the same root, for Anatolia and for Orthodox Christians.

These terms matter because the competition after 1204 was a competition over the meaning of "Roman." The Nicaean emperors insisted that they were Romans in succession to the empire of Constantinople. The Latin emperors claimed the imperial title by conquest and papal approval. Bulgarian and Serbian rulers adopted imperial titles with their own interpretations. This textbook uses "Byzantine" as a convenient modern term while reminding students that it conceals the continuity of the Roman identity claimed by the empire's rulers.

Section 41.5

Nicaea and the Restoration of 1261

The Empire of Nicaea grew from a refugee state into the most effective Greek power. Theodore I Laskaris (1205 to 1221) defended his territory against the Latins, who held part of northwestern Anatolia early on, and against the Seljuk sultan of Rum, and he organized the exiled church. His successor John III Doukas Vatatzes (1221 to 1254) expanded Nicaean power into Thrace and Macedonia, cooperated with Bulgaria against the Latin Empire in 1235, and absorbed Thessalonica in 1246 after the decline of Epirote power and the death of Ivan Asen II. Vatatzes is also remembered for economic policy: he encouraged agriculture on imperial and aristocratic estates, tried to limit the purchase of luxury imports, and supported schools, hospitals, and learned culture. Nicaea was also the center of intellectual life in exile, supporting scholars such as George Akropolites and Nikephoros Blemmydes, and it preserved a Greek educational tradition that would feed the Palaiologan revival.

Nicaea also had to deal with the Mongol invasions. In 1243 the Mongols defeated the Seljuk sultan of Rum at Köse Dağ in eastern Anatolia, reducing him to a tributary and permanently altering the Anatolian balance. The Mongol campaigns of 1241 and 1242 also devastated Hungary and shook Bulgaria and the Balkans, as discussed in Section 41.7. Because Nicaea's eastern frontier was relatively secure after Mongol intervention, its emperors could concentrate on the European theater. Theodore II Laskaris (1254 to 1258), a learned and forceful ruler, fought Bulgaria and Epirus and clashed with the Nicaean aristocracy. His death left a child emperor, John IV Laskaris, and a regency dominated by the aristocrat Michael Palaiologos, who became co-emperor in 1259.

Michael's rise to power set the stage for the restoration. In 1259 he defeated a coalition of Epirus, Achaea, and the Sicilian ruler Manfred at the battle of Pelagonia, capturing the prince of Achaea, William II Villehardouin, who was later ransomed in exchange for strongholds in the Peloponnese, including Mistra. In March 1261 Michael signed the Treaty of Nymphaion with Genoa, promising commercial privileges in return for naval assistance against Venice. The Latin Empire was by then in desperate condition. In July 1261 a small Nicaean force under Alexios Strategopoulos, reconnoitering near the capital while the Venetian fleet was away, discovered that the city was weakly held and entered it on 25 July. Baldwin II fled. Michael VIII entered Constantinople in triumph on 15 August and was crowned again in Hagia Sophia. In December 1261 he had the boy John IV blinded, an act that provoked the patriarch Arsenios and led to a long schism among Greek clergy and laypeople called the Arsenite movement.

The key distinction is that the Byzantine Empire was restored, but the pre-1204 Byzantine world was not. The territory of the new empire consisted of Thrace, much of Macedonia, western Anatolia, parts of the Peloponnese, and many Aegean islands, but Epirus, Trebizond, much of Greece, and Venetian possessions in Crete and the Aegean remained outside. The city of Constantinople itself had been damaged by fire and pillage, its population reduced, and its treasury drained. Its economy now operated in a Mediterranean where Genoa and Venice held major commercial positions. The emperor was a reconstituted center of a smaller polity surrounded by Latin states, Slavic kingdoms, and Turkish and Mongol neighbors. The Palaiologan dynasty, which Michael founded, would rule until 1453, and this fact, rather than the restoration itself, shaped the century and a half to come.

Section 41.6

The Palaiologan Empire: Restoration without Recovery

The restored empire faced a structural problem: it was expected to defend a more exposed and more fragmented territory with fewer resources. Michael VIII's reign (1259 to 1282) was dominated by the fear of a western counter-crusade. Charles of Anjou, brother of Louis IX, became king of Sicily in 1266 and in 1267, in the Treaty of Viterbo, agreed with the exiled Baldwin II to pursue the recovery of Constantinople. Michael's response was diplomatic. He negotiated with Pope Gregory X and at the Second Council of Lyons in 1274 accepted union of the churches on the pope's terms, including recognition of papal primacy and the Latin teaching on the procession of the Holy Spirit, known as the filioque. The Union of Lyons was largely a political instrument to deter Charles, and it was widely hated among the Greek clergy and populace, who saw it as imposed by an emperor seeking foreign favor. Michael punished opponents harshly. When Pope Martin IV, a supporter of Charles, excommunicated Michael in 1281, the union effectively collapsed.

Charles's plans were then disrupted by the Sicilian Vespers, the rebellion that began in Palermo in March 1282 and ended Angevin rule on the island. Historians debate how much Michael VIII contributed; he subsidized Charles's opponents, and he certainly benefited, but the revolt arose primarily from Sicilian grievances. Michael died in December 1282, and his son Andronikos II (1282 to 1328) repudiated the union. The episode reveals a central dilemma of the restored empire: western military support, when it was available, came with demands for ecclesiastical submission that the Greek church and many Greek laypeople refused, as Section 41.13 will show.

Meanwhile the empire's economic and military foundations weakened. The most serious problem was Anatolia. Under the Laskarids, the frontier had been defended by local troops, often settled and exempted from taxes, and by the emperor's presence. After the restoration, the political center of gravity moved to Constantinople, imperial attention shifted toward Europe, and frontier troops were allowed to decline. In 1302 an Ottoman force under Osman defeated a Byzantine army at Bapheus near Nicomedia, and over the following decades Turkish principalities, called beyliks, took most of the Anatolian territory that Nicaea had ruled. The fall of Bursa in 1326, Nicaea in 1331, and Nicomedia in 1337 to the Ottomans marked the end of Byzantine control of northwestern Anatolia. Philadelphia, in western Anatolia, held out as a Byzantine outpost until about 1390.

Fiscal and naval problems also mounted. Andronikos II reduced the navy, relying on Genoese ships instead, and attempts to hire mercenary forces proved dangerous: the Catalan Company, hired in 1303 to fight the Turks, turned on its employers, ravaged Thrace and Macedonia, and in 1311 seized the Duchy of Athens. The gold coinage, the hyperpyron, was repeatedly debased, and by the mid fourteenth century the gold coinage had largely stopped being minted, replaced by silver coins such as the stavraton introduced later in the century. Italian merchants controlled the carrying trade of the Black Sea and Aegean. Genoa held the settlement of Galata across the Golden Horn from Constantinople, a base that, according to the historian Nikephoros Gregoras, collected far more customs revenue than the imperial city itself by the middle of the fourteenth century. Such claims need to be read with caution, as Gregoras had political reasons to dramatize the situation, but the broad point is supported by Genoese records. Pronoia grants, originally conditional, became increasingly hereditary in some cases in the later period, which could reduce direct imperial control over land and revenue, though the effect varied by region and period.

A simplistic narrative of decadence is misleading. Byzantine society did not become incompetent. The empire faced several simultaneous pressures: the loss of Anatolia's revenue and manpower, competition from better organized Italian merchants, repeated western threats, the cost of mercenary armies, fiscal limits on its ability to maintain a navy, and civil conflicts, examined below, that destroyed what remained of its resources. Each pressure might have been manageable alone. Together they produced a state that survived by diplomacy and by the skill of its administrators, and one that remained capable of considerable cultural achievement, as the mosaics of the Chora monastery, completed in the early fourteenth century under the patronage of the official Theodore Metochites, still testify.

Section 41.7

A New Balkan Order: Bulgaria and Serbia

Chapter 39 deliberately stopped before the high medieval expansion of Serbia and left the restoration of Bulgaria for later. Here the Balkan story resumes, and its emphasis is regional transformation after Byzantine fragmentation, not separate national histories. The Second Bulgarian Empire grew from the revolt of Peter and Asen in 1185 and 1186. Their brother Kaloyan (1197 to 1207) expanded Bulgarian power at Byzantine and Latin expense and, in 1204, negotiated with Pope Innocent III, receiving a royal crown from a papal legate in an arrangement that recognized papal primacy but that Kaloyan used mainly to secure legitimacy. He was the victor at Adrianople in 1205, and his death, in 1207, led to a period of instability. Ivan Asen II (1218 to 1241) restored Bulgarian power, defeated Theodore of Epirus at Klokotnitsa in 1230, and ruled much of Thrace, Macedonia, and Albania for a time. In 1235 he secured recognition of a Bulgarian patriarchate seated at Tarnovo from the Greek patriarch in exile at Nicaea, in an alliance with Vatatzes, though his relationship with Nicaea soon shifted.

The Mongol raids of 1241 and 1242 and the later influence of the Golden Horde weakened Bulgarian power. After Ivan Asen II's death, a succession of rulers struggled against internal noble rivalry, Byzantine and Hungarian pressure, and Tatar raids. Bulgaria experienced a cultural revival under Ivan Alexander (1331 to 1371), a period of notable religious and literary activity at Tarnovo, but political fragmentation developed during his reign. Ivan Sratsimir ruled Vidin with effective autonomy from about 1356, while Dobruja also emerged as an increasingly autonomous principality. Ivan Alexander's death in 1371 made the division still sharper, and by the 1370s Bulgarian lands were split among rival princes at Tarnovo, Vidin, and Dobruja. Tarnovo fell to the Ottomans in 1393 and Vidin in 1396. The Tarnovo literary school, associated with Patriarch Euthymius, left an important Slavic literary legacy that would continue in Serbia, Wallachia, Moldavia, and Russia.

Serbia's rise followed a different pattern. Stefan Nemanja, the grand župan who had asserted independence from Byzantium in the late twelfth century, founded a dynasty that endured for two centuries. His son Stefan, called the First-Crowned, obtained a royal crown from the papacy in 1217 and then, through his brother Sava, an independent Serbian archbishopric from the Greek patriarch at Nicaea in 1219, creating an Orthodox autocephalous church. Under Uroš I (1243 to 1276), silver and other mines, worked in part by Saxon miners from central Europe, brought rising revenue. Stefan Uroš II Milutin (1282 to 1321) pushed south into Macedonia, taking Skopje and neighboring regions from the weakened Byzantines, and his reign was a period of great church building, including monasteries decorated with wall paintings that show strong Byzantine influence. His successor Stefan Dečanski won a decisive victory over Bulgaria at Velbazhd in 1330. Serbia's expansion was a result of Byzantine weakness, mining wealth, Hungarian and Bulgarian rivalry, and the abilities of its rulers, not of a prior national destiny.

These Balkan polities were multiethnic and multilingual, with Greek, Slavic, Albanian, Vlach, and other communities, and with Orthodox, Latin Catholic, and, in Bosnia, distinct local church structures. Commercial and political relations extended across the Adriatic through Ragusa (Dubrovnik) and Kotor, to Hungary, and to Venice. To the north, new political formations were also emerging, including Wallachia, where Basarab defeated King Charles I of Hungary at Posada in 1330, and, in Bosnia, the kingdom of Tvrtko I, crowned in 1377. Students should therefore see the fourteenth century Balkans as a regional system of competing states whose elites often married, allied, and fought across boundaries that were fluid and not national.

Section 41.8

Stefan Dušan and the Serbian Empire

Stefan Dušan (reigned 1331 to 1355) briefly transformed the balance of the Balkans. He came to power in 1331 after deposing his father Stefan Dečanski, and he inherited a Serbian state strengthened by mining revenue, Byzantine administrative models, and military success against Bulgaria. His great opportunity came from the Byzantine civil wars that began in 1341, examined in Section 41.9. By allying alternately with rival Byzantine claimants and by exploiting the exhaustion of the empire, Dušan conquered most of Macedonia, including Serres in 1345, and later seized Epirus and Thessaly, by 1348. In these years his realm extended from the Danube region to the Gulf of Corinth, excluding Constantinople itself, Thrace, and some coastal places held by Byzantium and Venice.

At Easter 1346, at Skopje, Dušan was crowned as "emperor of the Serbs and Romans," a title often rendered in modern scholarship as "emperor of the Serbs and Greeks," and the Serbian archbishop was elevated to patriarch. These steps were deliberately imperial, claiming a share in Roman imperial tradition, and they were condemned by the patriarch of Constantinople, who excommunicated Dušan and the Serbian church. The ban was lifted only in 1375, after Dušan's empire had collapsed. Historians debate whether Dušan intended to conquer Constantinople itself or to create a joint Serbian and Greek empire in which he would be the dominant partner; the evidence does not establish a firm plan, and he did not attempt the capture of the city. He did, however, take Greek bureaucratic titles, employ Greek administrators, and patronize Mount Athos, where he visited in 1347.

Dušan's Code, issued in 1349 and expanded in 1354, is among the most important legal monuments of medieval southeastern Europe. It combined Byzantine legal traditions, including material drawn from Byzantine law collections, with Serbian custom, and it regulated relations among the emperor, the nobility, the church, the free and dependent peasantry, and other groups, as well as courts and procedures. Because the Code survives in later manuscripts, historians disagree about aspects of its original form. It shows, nevertheless, that Dušan sought not merely conquest but the institutional consolidation of a large, diverse realm.

The empire was fragile. It depended on Dušan's personal authority, on the loyalty of regional nobles who held large territories, and on weak neighbors. Dušan died suddenly in December 1355, leaving his son Uroš V, who lacked the strength to hold the realm together. Regional nobles quickly asserted independence. The result was the fragmentation of Dušan's conquests into smaller principalities led by figures such as Vukašin Mrnjavčević, Lazar Hrebeljanović, and others, which were soon unable to resist Ottoman advances. Students should not read medieval Serbia as an early modern Serbian nation-state. It was a dynastic, multiethnic, Orthodox realm whose rulers used Greek and Serbian languages and titles, and whose later memory in Serbian national history reflects nineteenth and twentieth century concerns as much as medieval realities.

Section 41.9

Civil War, Plague, and the Contraction of Byzantium

The Byzantine state of the fourteenth century was increasingly unable to control events within its own borders. Dynastic conflict was a major reason. Andronikos II was challenged by his grandson Andronikos III in a civil war from 1321 to 1328, which ended with Andronikos III's victory and further weakened imperial finances and defenses. After Andronikos III died in 1341, leaving his nine year old son John V, a regency was disputed between the empress mother Anna of Savoy, the patriarch John XIV Kalekas, and the great domestic John VI Kantakouzenos, Andronikos III's close friend and chief minister. Kantakouzenos had himself proclaimed emperor in October 1341, and a bitter civil war followed from 1341 to 1347.

The war had social as well as dynastic dimensions. In Thessalonica a movement known as the Zealots seized power in 1342 and ruled the city for several years, in conflict with the local aristocracy, and the episode has led historians to debate how far it expressed class conflict, local autonomy, or political factionalism. Meanwhile outsiders exploited the conflict. Serbia under Dušan took advantage of the disorder to conquer territory, and Bulgarian rulers intervened. Kantakouzenos hired Turkish troops, above all from Umur of Aydın and later from the Ottoman ruler Orhan, whom Kantakouzenos bound to himself by marrying his daughter Theodora to Orhan in 1346. The result was that Turkish forces, as mercenaries and allies, entered Thrace as a normal element of Byzantine politics, a decisive precedent examined in Section 41.11.

The Black Death reached Constantinople in 1347, carried, like so many outbreaks, along Mediterranean and Black Sea trade routes by ships. Contemporary Byzantine authors, including Kantakouzenos, described the plague, often with models from ancient historians such as Thucydides, though no reliable population figures survive. Plague, as Chapter 50 explains in a European context, struck urban populations especially severely and recurred in later decades. For Byzantium it reduced the tax base and manpower of an already shrinking state. The empire's finances had been worsened by the war, by the loss of land to Serbia, by the mercenaries' demands, and by the commercial advantage of Italian rivals. In 1348 and 1349 a war with Genoa over customs and Galata ended in defeat for the Byzantines, further confirming Italian commercial dominance. Kantakouzenos finally resigned and became a monk in 1354, leaving John V as emperor, but the empire's territory was by then a fraction of what it had been.

Yet contraction did not mean cultural collapse. This was the era of the Palaiologan renaissance in art and learning. Church mosaics and frescoes in Constantinople, Thessalonica, and especially Mistra in the Peloponnese reached extraordinary sophistication. Monasteries copied manuscripts, and the spiritual movement known as hesychasm, a tradition of contemplative prayer defended in theological debates by Gregory Palamas, shaped Orthodox thought and was formally affirmed in church councils in the 1340s and 1350s. Scholars such as Demetrios Kydones, who translated Latin theology into Greek, and later George Gemistos Plethon and Bessarion kept Byzantine intellectual life connected to Italy. Manuel Chrysoloras's teaching of Greek in Florence beginning in 1397 would become important for Italian humanism, a subject for Chapter 57. The fourteenth century Byzantine state was therefore politically weak but culturally productive, a combination that complicates simple stories of rise and fall.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Changing Regimes: Peasants, Townspeople, and Minorities

Most inhabitants of the eastern Mediterranean and Balkans in this period were peasants whose daily lives were governed by the seasons and by obligations to landlords, monasteries, and rulers. Political change often changed who received their rents and taxes more than how they farmed. In the Latin lordships of the Peloponnese and central Greece, Orthodox Greek peasants worked under Frankish lords, and the Chronicle of the Morea, which survives in several versions with differing sympathies, shows how Franks and Greeks cooperated, intermarried, and distrusted one another. Latin hierarchies replaced many Greek bishops, though practical compromises often left Greek parish priests in place. In Serbia and Bulgaria, dependent peasants, free villagers, and pastoralists such as the Vlachs lived under legal categories described in charters and in Dušan's Code, and monasteries held vast estates and the labor attached to them.

Cities in the region were centers of commerce and diversity. Constantinople, Thessalonica, and Ragusa housed Greek, Italian, Slavic, Armenian, and other merchants. Jewish communities, among them Greek-speaking Romaniote Jews, lived in Constantinople, Thessalonica, and elsewhere, often concentrated in particular districts and engaged in trades such as dyeing, leatherworking, and commerce; their legal status shifted with the regime that ruled the city. Roma communities, whom Byzantine and Venetian sources begin to document in southern Greece in the fourteenth century, appear in records as itinerant metalworkers and entertainers. The Black Death, warfare, and loss of Anatolia produced refugees, and Turkish expansion also brought pastoral and settler groups into Thrace and Macedonia. Monastic life, especially on Mount Athos, continued under Byzantine, Serbian, and eventually Ottoman patrons.

Most of this social history must be reconstructed from fragmentary evidence, including monastic archives, Venetian and Genoese notarial acts, tax registers, charters, hagiographies, and archaeology, and historians should be cautious about generalizing from the best documented regions, such as Mount Athos or Italian trading colonies, to the whole population.

Section 41.10

From Frontier Principality to Ottoman State

The Ottomans must now be introduced in depth, but the chapter's question is how one frontier principality became unusually successful, not why the Ottomans were destined to conquer Byzantium. The setting was western Anatolia after the Seljuk sultanate of Rum was defeated by the Mongols at Köse Dağ in 1243. The sultanate survived under Mongol overlordship but lost real authority, and by about 1300 Anatolia was divided among numerous Turkish principalities, the beyliks, many of them established on the frontier with the weakened Byzantine territories. Their populations included Turkmen pastoralists, settled farmers, Sufi dervishes, and Greek Christians, and their leaders drew followers from many backgrounds.

Osman, from whom the dynasty takes its name, led a small group in the region around Söğüt in northwestern Anatolia near the Byzantine lands. Almost nothing reliable is known about his life, because contemporary sources are scarce. A Byzantine historian, George Pachymeres, reports the battle of Bapheus in 1302, which is the earliest securely dated event in Ottoman history. The traditional Ottoman narratives, such as the chronicles of Aşıkpaşazade and Neşri, were written in the later fifteenth century, well over a century after Osman, and they include legends such as Osman's dream of a tree growing from his body to cover the world. Such traditions tell us how the dynasty later wished to remember its beginnings but cannot be treated as direct evidence of what happened c. 1300.

Under Orhan (c. 1324 to 1362), the principality took major urban centers. Bursa fell in 1326 after a long blockade and became the first Ottoman capital, Nicaea surrendered in 1331, and Nicomedia in 1337. In the 1340s, Orhan absorbed the neighboring beylik of Karasi, giving him access to the Dardanelles coast and to Turkish naval and warrior resources. The Ottomans built mosques, a Sufi lodge network, and administrative institutions in the captured cities, drawing on Seljuk, Mongol, and Byzantine practice. They minted coins and adopted local tax arrangements, and their armies included Turkmen horsemen, Muslim volunteers, Christian converts, and Christian allies. Contemporary sources note that some local Byzantine lords joined the Ottomans, sometimes as converts and sometimes as vassals who kept their religion.

Several factors contributed to Ottoman success. The principality's location opposite the Byzantine capital gave it easy access both to the declining Byzantine lands and, after 1345 and the absorption of Karasi, to Europe. Its rulers were successful at combining plunder with patronage, at attracting warriors from other regions, and at integrating newly conquered territories through flexible arrangements instead of wholesale destruction. They were adept diplomats who allied with, married into, and bargained with Byzantine, Genoese, Serbian, and other powers. Their dynasty also proved remarkably durable: although succession disputes occurred, a single family monopolized rule, a feature that contrasted with the repeated fragmentation of rival beyliks. None of this made conquest inevitable. For decades the Ottomans were only one of several Anatolian powers, and in 1402 the principality came close to disappearing.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghaza (Gaza)

Ghaza, from an Arabic term for raid or campaign, was used in Turkish and Persian texts for warfare against non-Muslim neighbors, often with religious justification, and the warriors who took part are called ghazis. A frontier lord might be described in later sources as a "ghazi of ghazis." The term appears in Ottoman inscriptions and chronicles, but its meaning in early Ottoman history is disputed. Some scholars have treated it as a driving ideology of holy war; others treat it as a flexible label for raiding, patronage, and legitimacy that could include Muslims and Christians together.

The term matters to this chapter because it frames the debate on why the Ottomans rose. Students should avoid the misleading modern equivalent of "jihad" as a single organized religious campaign. The historical question is how much religious ideology explained early Ottoman behavior, and how much was shaped by the practical politics of the frontier and by the dynastic ideology of later periods.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Ottomans Rise?

The question: How should historians explain the success of a small frontier principality in northwestern Anatolia, and how much weight should they give to religious ideology, tribal organization, frontier society, and administrative continuity?

Interpretation A: The ghaza thesis: In the 1930s Paul Wittek argued that the early Ottomans were a state of ghazis, warriors of the faith whose shared ideology of holy war against Christians drove expansion and unified diverse followers. This thesis gave a clear explanation and held great influence for decades, especially because Ottoman chronicles supported it.
Interpretation B: Frontier society, tribal politics, and continuity: Later scholars criticized the ghaza thesis. Rudi Lindner emphasized tribal and nomadic politics, in which pastoralists sought pasture and booty and a successful leader attracted followers regardless of religion. Cemal Kafadar emphasized a fluid frontier society in which Muslims and Christians cooperated, in which religious boundaries were permeable, and in which the Ottomans were in competition with other beyliks. Others, including Colin Imber and Heath Lowry, argued that the ghaza language in later sources reflected the dynasty's later legitimating needs and that the early state's practices drew on Byzantine and Seljuk administrative models. Many scholars now combine elements of these views.

Evidence: The question is difficult because the earliest Ottoman sources are later than the events, because Byzantine sources are hostile or distant, and because inscriptions, coins, and a few early documents are scarce. Historians compare Byzantine narratives, later Ottoman chronicles, the practices of neighboring beyliks, archaeological evidence, and later administrative records, treating each with attention to its date and purpose.

Why it matters: If religious war was the engine of expansion, Ottoman conquest looks like a continuation of a religious clash between Christendom and Islam. If frontier politics, patronage, and administrative adaptation mattered more, the Ottoman rise appears as part of the political competition among many Balkan and Anatolian powers, and the Ottoman state as a successor in important respects to Byzantine and Balkan institutions.

Section 41.11

The Ottomans Cross into Europe

The Ottoman entry into Europe was a result of Byzantine politics as much as Ottoman strategy. During the civil war Kantakouzenos used Turkish allies, and in 1352 Ottoman forces occupied the fortress of Tzympe on the European shore of the Dardanelles during the conflict between Kantakouzenos and John V. In March 1354 an earthquake destroyed the walls of Gallipoli, and Orhan's son Süleyman occupied and resettled the town, creating a permanent European base. Kantakouzenos's resignation in December 1354 and the Byzantine inability to dislodge the Ottomans meant that Turkish control of the strategic peninsula persisted. Over the following decades, Ottoman commanders, often called marcher lords, raided and then settled throughout Thrace.

Under Murad I (1362 to 1389), the Ottomans took Adrianople, known in Turkish as Edirne, at a date placed by historians between the early 1360s and about 1369, and made it their principal European base. The capture cut Constantinople from its hinterland. In 1371, at the Maritsa river near Chernomen, an Ottoman force defeated the Serbian rulers Vukašin and Uglješa Mrnjavčević, both of whom were killed. The result was the submission of many Macedonian and Thracian lords as vassals. John V Palaiologos, too, accepted a subordinate status, paying tribute and providing military service. When Andronikos IV and Murad's own son Savcı jointly revolted against their fathers in 1373, Murad suppressed the Ottoman side of the rebellion and forced John V to punish his son, a sign of Ottoman leverage over the imperial dynasty.

Expansion was rarely simple conquest. It combined raids and sieges with negotiated surrender, vassalage, tribute, marriage alliances, hostage taking, and gradual incorporation. A conquered lord might be left in place as a vassal who paid tribute and supplied troops, and later, if he proved unreliable or died, his territory might be annexed and assigned to Ottoman cavalrymen holding revenue grants called timars. Christian soldiers fought in Ottoman armies, some Christian landholders retained their lands, and local legal and tax practices were often kept. Population movements also mattered: Turkish pastoralists and settlers moved into Thrace and Macedonia, and captives and levies, including, in a system whose origins are debated, forced recruitment of Christian youths, supplied the sultan's household troops, the later Janissaries. The mixture of coercion and accommodation helps explain why Ottoman rule spread faster than the resistance of divided Balkan states could contain.

The battle of Kosovo on 15 June 1389 (Julian calendar) illustrates the need for care. Prince Lazar Hrebeljanović, the most powerful Serbian lord after Dušan's empire's collapse, led a coalition that included contingents from the Branković family and from Bosnia. The Ottoman army under Sultan Murad I met them at Kosovo Polje, near modern Priština. Murad was killed, and so was Lazar, who was captured and executed. Contemporary sources are brief and contradictory, and it is unclear who won in a purely tactical sense, though the Ottomans clearly gained strategically. Murad's son Bayezid I took the throne, and Lazar's son Stefan Lazarević became an Ottoman vassal, bound to supply troops. Lazar's principality entered Ottoman vassalage under Stefan Lazarević, while other Serbian lords followed different paths, some already vassals and others independent or aligned with Hungary. Ottoman leverage over the Serbian principalities deepened, and the Serbian Despotate ruled by Stefan and later by Đurađ Branković survived, under varying degrees of Ottoman suzerainty, until 1459.

The battle's historical consequences must be distinguished from its mythology. Over succeeding centuries Serbian poetry, church tradition, and later national movements transformed Kosovo into a story of sacrifice, betrayal, and spiritual choice, associated with the legend of Miloš Obilić's killing of the sultan and with the commemoration of Vidovdan. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the story became central to Serbian nationalism, and the six hundredth anniversary in 1989 was used for political mobilization, a subject returned to in Chapter 135. Historians separate the battle, which was important but not the end of Serbia, from this memory, and also note that many Balkan communities, including Albanians, Bosnians, Vlachs, and Bulgarians, participated in or were affected by Ottoman expansion. Bulgaria suffered the more complete early incorporation: Tarnovo fell in 1393, Vidin in 1396, and by the end of the century most Bulgarian lands were under direct Ottoman administration.

Section 41.12

Crusade, Ankara, and the Ottoman Recovery

Ottoman advances in the Balkans drew a western response, but the response was limited, late, and poorly coordinated. This section uses Nicopolis and Varna to explain Byzantine and Balkan survival and Ottoman expansion; Chapter 47 treats crusading as a wider European institution and ideology. Bayezid I (1389 to 1402) consolidated Ottoman power in the Balkans and Anatolia and, from about 1394, blockaded Constantinople. King Sigismund of Hungary, whose kingdom now bordered Ottoman territory, appealed for aid, and in 1396 a crusading army, drawn from Hungary, France, Burgundy, and other lands, with Venetian and Genoese naval support, marched down the Danube. At Nicopolis on 25 September 1396 it was crushed. Western knights, impatient and confident, charged ahead against Sigismund's advice, and Bayezid's forces, aided by Serbian vassals under Stefan Lazarević, won a decisive victory. Many prisoners were killed, and others were ransomed, among them the Burgundian leader John of Nevers.

Constantinople's survival then depended on an accident from Central Asia. In 1399 Manuel II Palaiologos traveled to western Europe to seek aid, visiting Venice, Paris, and London, while a small French force under Marshal Boucicaut briefly relieved the city. But the decisive help came from the east. The Turco-Mongol conqueror Timur invaded Anatolia, and at Ankara on 28 July 1402 he defeated and captured Bayezid, who died in captivity in 1403. The Ottoman state disintegrated into civil war among Bayezid's sons, the Ottoman Interregnum (1402 to 1413). Byzantium gained a reprieve: Süleyman, who ruled the European provinces, returned Thessalonica and other territory to the empire in 1403. Mehmed I eventually won the struggle in 1413, reunified the state, and maintained peaceful relations with Manuel II.

The recovery was substantial. Murad II (1421 to 1444 and 1446 to 1451) resumed pressure on the Balkans and in 1422 besieged Constantinople, though without success. In 1423 Despot Andronikos Palaiologos, son of Manuel II and ruler of Thessalonica, ceded the city to Venice in the hope that the republic could defend it, but in 1430 Murad's forces stormed it, an event described by the eyewitness John Anagnostes as a catastrophe in which many inhabitants were killed or enslaved. Serbia's ruler Đurađ Branković, who rebuilt his state as a vassal of the sultan, also played a double game with Hungary and the Ottomans. Albanian resistance under Skanderbeg later complicated Ottoman control of the western Balkans.

The last major crusade was that of 1443 and 1444. John Hunyadi, the Hungarian commander, led a successful winter campaign in 1443, and the Ottomans agreed to a truce in 1444. After a complicated sequence of peace negotiations and oaths in 1444, in which King Władysław III of Poland and Hungary and the papal legate Cardinal Julian Cesarini played prominent parts, the crusading leadership repudiated the settlement and resumed the campaign toward the Black Sea coast. Historians still debate the details and the force of the agreement usually called the Peace of Szeged. At Varna on 10 November 1444 Murad II, who had briefly abdicated in favor of his young son Mehmed and returned to command, decisively defeated them, and Władysław was killed. Hunyadi's later defeat at the second battle of Kosovo in 1448 confirmed the pattern. By the late 1440s there was no organized western force able to prevent Ottoman power from reaching Constantinople.

Section 41.13

Union with Rome and Byzantium's Last Diplomacy

Chapter 40 makes this section possible: the theological and institutional divisions discussed there now collided directly with geopolitics. Byzantine emperors had long understood that substantial western military aid required church union. The precedent of the Union of Lyons in 1274, forced by Michael VIII and abandoned after his death, showed both the attractions and the dangers. In the fifteenth century the situation of the western church had changed. The Western Schism, discussed in Chapter 53, had been resolved by the Council of Constance, and Pope Eugenius IV, locked in a conflict with the Council of Basel over whether councils or popes held supreme authority, saw an opportunity in negotiating with the Greeks.

Emperor John VIII Palaiologos, accompanied by his brother Demetrios, the patriarch Joseph II, and a large delegation of bishops and scholars, traveled to Italy in 1437 and 1438 on Venetian and papal ships. The council opened at Ferrara in 1438 and, because of plague and financial reasons, moved to Florence in 1439. The principal issues were the filioque, papal primacy, the use of leavened or unleavened bread in the Eucharist, and teaching on purgatory. Delegates debated at length; the Greeks included strong advocates of union such as Bessarion of Nicaea and Isidore of Kiev, as well as the determined opponent Mark Eugenikos of Ephesus. On 6 July 1439 the union decree Laetentur caeli was proclaimed in Florence, signed by the Greek delegation with Mark Eugenikos refusing and Joseph II having died during the council.

Back in Constantinople, the union met widespread resistance among clergy, monks, and laypeople. Some opposed it on theological grounds: they believed that the Latins had added the filioque to the creed without ecumenical agreement and that papal claims were inconsistent with the Orthodox understanding of the church as governed by councils of bishops. Others opposed it because the union appeared to have been bought by the empire's need for aid, because the memory of 1204 and 1274 made Latin intentions suspect, and because it threatened the identity of a community that increasingly defined Orthodoxy as its protection against absorption. Some who favored union, such as Bessarion, believed it was essential for the survival of Greek Christianity and culture. Others, including George Scholarios, later the first Ottoman era patriarch Gennadios, changed their positions over time. Seen in this light, Orthodox opponents were not irrational obstructionists: they weighed ecclesiastical submission against a hope of military assistance that, as Varna showed, was uncertain.

The limits of western assistance were also political. Western powers had their own preoccupations: the Hundred Years' War between England and France, treated in Chapter 51, did not end until 1453, and the struggle between Pope Eugenius IV and the Council of Basel divided western Christendom. Venice and Genoa were pursuing commercial interests that required careful relations with the Ottomans. Hungary bore the main burden along the Danube. In Moscow, after Isidore, metropolitan of Kiev and all Rus', proclaimed the Florentine union in 1441, Grand Prince Vasili II rejected it and had him deposed and imprisoned, and Isidore later escaped, a development that belongs to Russian history but that illustrates the wide Orthodox rejection. Not until 12 December 1452, with the Ottoman threat imminent, did Constantine XI Palaiologos, who had become emperor in 1449 after the death of John VIII, allow the union to be proclaimed in Hagia Sophia by the papal legate, Cardinal Isidore. The ceremony met intense popular hostility and was rejected or boycotted by many anti-unionists, and it brought only a small force of papal galleys and a few hundred troops. Theological division and geopolitics were thus directly linked, but in the fatal context of a city already under threat.

Section 41.14

Constantinople, 1453: Conquest and the End of the Byzantine Empire

Mehmed II became sultan for the second time in February 1451, after the death of Murad II. Young, ambitious, and well educated, he quickly turned to the capture of Constantinople, whose existence cut the Ottoman state in two and offered a bridgehead for hostile powers. In 1452 he built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the European shore of the Bosporus, opposite the fortress that Bayezid I had built on the Asian side, giving the Ottomans control of the straits and cutting the city's access to the Black Sea grain supply. The Ottoman army and navy were mobilized during the winter. Mehmed also secured the services of Urban, a gunfounder, often called Hungarian, who had first offered his services to the Byzantines but had not been paid enough by the empire. Urban cast a very large bombard, among a number of artillery pieces used in the siege, and its destructive effect, although real, has sometimes been exaggerated.

The defenders were few. Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos ruled little beyond the city and the Morea, which his brothers held. According to the Byzantine historian Sphrantzes, Constantine had a census taken that counted fewer than five thousand Greek defenders and a few hundred foreigners, though many modern estimates of the total defenders, including foreign contingents, are about seven to eight thousand, and no figure is certain. The foreign contingents included Venetian and Genoese volunteers, such as the experienced Genoese soldier Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, who led several hundred men and took command of a key sector of the walls. Ottoman estimates are even more uncertain, because contemporary sources give very large figures, often above two hundred thousand, but modern historians generally propose a range of perhaps fifty to eighty thousand or more, including irregular troops and a naval force. Christian vassals, including Serbian troops sent by Đurađ Branković, also served in Mehmed's army.

The siege began in early April 1453. The Theodosian Walls, built in the fifth century, stretched across the peninsula on the landward side and had withstood many sieges. The defenders also held a chain across the mouth of the Golden Horn, protecting the northern shore. Ottoman artillery bombarded the walls for weeks, and troops attacked repeatedly, but the defenders repaired damage each night, and Ottoman assaults were repelled. On 20 April a small group of Genoese and Byzantine ships succeeded in breaking through the Ottoman fleet and entering the Golden Horn, raising defenders' morale. Mehmed responded by transporting a number of ships overland on greased logs from the Bosporus to the Golden Horn, which was accomplished on 22 April. Ottoman forces now threatened the sea walls along the Golden Horn, forcing the defenders to thin their forces. Reports of the failure of Venetian relief to arrive and of strains among the defenders, including disputes between Venetians and Genoese, spread through the city.

Mehmed ordered a general assault before dawn on 29 May 1453. Irregular troops attacked first, followed by Anatolian forces and then by the sultan's household troops, the Janissaries. Giustiniani was wounded and left his post, a development that sources describe as demoralizing. Ottoman forces broke through at a sector of the walls, and, according to one tradition, a minor gate was found open. Constantine XI fought and died in the final fighting, though the circumstances and the identification of his body are uncertain. Mehmed's forces took the city. Pillage, killing, and enslavement followed for some days, with tens of thousands of inhabitants enslaved or ransomed, according to varying accounts. Mehmed ended the looting and entered Hagia Sophia, which was converted into a mosque. The sultan soon began to repopulate and rebuild the city, bringing in Muslims, Christians, and Jews, and he appointed Gennadios (George Scholarios) as Orthodox patriarch in 1454.

The military explanation should be balanced. Artillery was important, but so were overwhelming manpower, naval control of the Bosporus, the blockade, logistics, repeated assaults that exhausted the small garrison, the demographic weakness of the city, and its isolation from western aid. The walls were damaged but not decisively broken by cannon alone; the final success came from a coordinated attack on a thinly stretched defense. Chapter 55 returns to gunpowder and the transition to early modern warfare.

What actually ended in 1453? The Roman imperial government centered on Constantinople, which had existed in one form or another for over eleven centuries, ended. But the Orthodox Church survived under a patriarch whom the sultan appointed and recognized; Greek language and scholarship continued; Byzantine legal and administrative traditions influenced the Ottoman state; monastic institutions and many local elites persisted. The conquest did not literally extinguish every polity descended from Byzantium: the Despotate of the Morea survived until 1460 and the Empire of Trebizond until 1461, and other Greek and Genoese outposts lasted still longer. Mehmed II cultivated an imperial image as conqueror of the Roman capital and was associated with the title Kayser-i Rûm ("Caesar of Rome"), although the frequency and formal use of that title in his own official documents are debated. In Latin Europe the news produced shock and appeals for a crusade, and Greek scholars and manuscripts moved to Italy in larger numbers, continuing a movement discussed in Chapter 57. The consequences for the European state system are examined in Chapter 79.

The Siege and Fall of Constantinople, 1453

A Greek resident of Constantinople: Many residents were poor, lived in a city whose population had shrunk and whose districts had become semi rural, and had seen decades of Ottoman blockade and plague. Janissaries and officers served the dynasty, while irregular troops sought plunder. For Mehmed II and his court, the conquest legitimated a young ruler, ended the strategic division of his realm, and claimed an imperial heritage.
A Genoese or Venetian merchant or volunteer: Italian communities were divided between loyalty to Christendom and commercial interest. After the conquest, Italian trading privileges were renegotiated under Ottoman rule.
An Orthodox cleric or monk: Opponents of union saw the siege through the lens of faith and the memory of 1204, and some interpreted the fall as divine judgment, with competing explanations for whose sins were responsible. Continuity and Change Over Time

What Ended in 1453?

What Changed

  • The Roman imperial government centered on Constantinople ended, as did the empire's role as a diplomatic center of Orthodox Christianity
  • The Ottoman dynasty gained the great capital and began to rule as an imperial power in southeastern Europe
  • The Balkans shifted from a field of competing Christian states to a region largely under Ottoman rule or suzerainty
  • Italian merchants lost or renegotiated some privileges as the Ottomans controlled the straits

What Stayed the Same

  • Orthodox Christianity, its monasteries, and much of its parish life continued under a patriarch and new political conditions
  • Greek language, learning, and many local elites survived, and Byzantine legal and administrative practices influenced Ottoman governance
  • Constantinople remained a great commercial and imperial city, reshaped as the Ottoman capital
  • Most peasants continued to work the land, paying dues to new masters

Who experienced change most directly? The imperial court, the urban population of Constantinople, and Latin merchants and officials faced the greatest disruption. Who experienced continuity? Many rural populations and church communities experienced more continuity, though under new taxation and authority. Why the pattern matters: It prevents students from equating the end of a state with the end of a society.

Cause and Effect

Why Constantinople Fell in 1453

Long-term causes

Fragmentation after 1204, the loss of Anatolia, fiscal weakness, Italian commercial dominance, civil wars, plague, Balkan disunity, and the rise of a durable Ottoman dynasty.

Short-term causes

Mehmed II's determination, preparation of fortifications and fleet, use of artillery, the failure of Varna, the divisions caused by the union of 1452, and the small size of the defending force.

The event

A siege of about seven weeks ending in a general assault on 29 May 1453 and the death of Constantine XI.

Immediate effects

Pillage and enslavement, conversion of Hagia Sophia to a mosque, repopulation of the city, the establishment of an Orthodox patriarch under the sultan, and shock in Latin Europe.

Long-term consequences

Consolidation of Ottoman rule in southeastern Europe, continued conquest of the Morea, Trebizond, and Balkan lands, new Mediterranean power relations, and migration of Greek scholars to Italy.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Cannon, manpower, naval strategy, Byzantine weakness, western inaction, and Ottoman organization interacted, and earlier contingencies such as Ankara in 1402 might have changed the timeline.

Historical complexity: Students should resist the temptation to name a single decisive cause, whether the great cannon, the failure of the West, or the decline of Byzantium. Different weights can be assigned to each factor, but the outcome emerged from their interaction.

Chapter Summary

This chapter traced the transformation of southeastern Europe and the eastern Mediterranean between 1204 and 1453. Its central argument is that the region moved from a system dominated by the Byzantine Empire to a fragmented field of Byzantine successor states, Latin possessions, Bulgarian and Serbian powers, Italian commercial interests, and Turkish principalities, and that the Ottoman state emerged from this competitive environment to build a new imperial order. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 was the culmination of long processes and not the inevitable collapse of a civilization.

The Byzantine Empire of the late twelfth century was weakened by dynastic instability, provincial fragmentation, fiscal strain, and growing Italian commercial influence, but it remained a rich and formidable state. The Fourth Crusade reached Constantinople through a contingent sequence: a debt to Venice, the capture of Zara, the offer of the exiled Alexios Angelos, the failure of his promises, a Byzantine palace revolution, and the final assault. Crusaders, Venetians, Byzantine claimants, and the papacy had different motives, and the older theory of a premeditated plot has been largely abandoned.

The sack of 1204 killed and dispossessed many inhabitants, destroyed or dispersed cultural treasures, and transferred relics and artworks to western Europe. Greek and Latin sources describe it differently and must be read critically. The sack also hardened the division between Latin and Orthodox Christians far more deeply than the events of 1054 had, because it turned theological and institutional disagreement into a memory of conquest and desecration.

After 1204 the Byzantine world divided. The Latin Empire of Constantinople, the Greek Empires of Nicaea and Trebizond, the Despotate of Epirus, Venetian colonies, and Frankish lordships in Greece competed for territory and legitimacy, while Bulgaria under Kaloyan and Ivan Asen II became a major power. Nicaea eventually prevailed because of its secure base, capable rulers, control of the church, and the weakness of the Latin Empire, but that outcome was not predetermined.

The restoration of 1261 returned Constantinople to a Greek emperor but not the old empire. The Palaiologan state was smaller, poorer, and more exposed, forced to rely on Genoese and Venetian commerce and naval power, to seek western aid at the price of church union, and to hire mercenaries who sometimes turned on it. The loss of Anatolia to Turkish principalities removed key revenue and manpower. Yet Palaiologan Byzantium produced notable art, scholarship, and theology.

In the Balkans, the Second Bulgarian Empire and the Serbian kingdom under the Nemanjić dynasty took advantage of Byzantine weakness. Stefan Dušan created a large Serbian empire in the 1340s and 1350s, claimed Roman imperial titles, and issued a law code that drew on Byzantine and Serbian traditions, but his realm fragmented after his death in 1355. These polities were multiethnic and Orthodox, and they should not be read as early national states.

The fourteenth century saw Byzantium lose control of events. Civil wars from 1321 to 1328 and 1341 to 1347, the intervention of Serbs, Bulgarians, and Turkish mercenaries, the Black Death, and the war with Genoa reduced the empire to a small territory dependent on others. Cultural vitality persisted in monasteries, manuscripts, and art.

The Ottomans emerged from the post-Seljuk frontier of northwestern Anatolia. Historians debate the roles of ghaza ideology, tribal politics, frontier society, and administrative continuity, and the late date of the earliest Ottoman narratives limits what can be known about Osman. Under Orhan and Murad I, the Ottomans took Bursa, Gallipoli, and Adrianople, defeated Serbian princes at the Maritsa in 1371, and fought at Kosovo in 1389. Expansion combined warfare, vassalage, tribute, and incorporation, and the battle of Kosovo must be distinguished from its later national mythology.

Western Europe's responses failed. The crusades of Nicopolis in 1396 and Varna in 1444 were defeated, and the Council of Ferrara-Florence achieved a union of 1439 that most Orthodox Christians rejected, partly for theological reasons and partly because of the memory of 1204 and the suspicion that union was the price of aid that might not arrive. Timur's victory over Bayezid at Ankara in 1402 gave Byzantium a reprieve, but the Ottomans recovered.

In 1453 Mehmed II conquered Constantinople after a siege in which artillery, manpower, naval strategy, and the city's isolation all mattered. The Roman imperial government centered on Constantinople ended, but Orthodox Christianity, Greek learning, Byzantine administrative traditions, and many local communities continued, and the Morea and Trebizond survived for a few years more. The conquest shaped the Ottoman Empire's imperial identity, Latin Europe's perceptions of the east, and the movement of Greek scholars to Italy.

For Part VI, the chapter completes the account begun in Chapter 36: the Byzantine state, Orthodox Christianity, and the Slavic communities of the Balkans and eastern Europe were central to medieval Europe, and their transformation was not marginal to European history. For Volume II, it shows that medieval Europe's political and religious unity was never complete, and that the competitive field of states and faiths in the east formed part of the same medieval world as the western kingdoms discussed in neighboring chapters.

A Turkish principality in Anatolia, ruled by a bey, that emerged after the decline of Seljuk authority.
Ghaza
A term for frontier warfare used in Turkish and Persian sources, whose role in early Ottoman expansion is debated.
Timar
An Ottoman grant of revenue from land assigned to a cavalryman in return for military service.
Sipahi
An Ottoman cavalryman, often holding a timar.
Janissary
A member of the Ottoman sultan's household infantry, originally recruited largely from captives and later from levies of Christian youths.
Vassalage (Ottoman)
A relationship in which a Christian or Muslim ruler retained territory while paying tribute and providing troops to the sultan.
Theodosian Walls
The fifth century land fortifications of Constantinople that protected the city for centuries.
Stavraton
A silver coin introduced in the later fourteenth century as Byzantine gold coinage largely ceased.
Conciliarism
The view that general councils held authority superior to or independent of the pope, which shaped western church politics during the Ferrara-Florence negotiations.

The period of civil war among Bayezid I's sons between 1402 and 1413.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Accounts of the Fall of Constantinople in 1453

This project asks you to compare how contemporary and near contemporary authors described the siege and fall of Constantinople, and to evaluate what each source can and cannot establish.

  1. Select sources. Using the MGU Library and published translations, choose at least four accounts from different perspectives, such as Greek (Sphrantzes, Doukas, Kritoboulos, Chalkokondyles), Italian (for example the journal attributed to the Venetian Nicolò Barbaro or the letters of Leonard of Chios), and Ottoman or Turkish accounts from later in the fifteenth century.
  2. Identify each author. Note when and where each source was written, whether the author was an eyewitness, and for whom the account was written.
  3. Compare key points. Examine descriptions of the numbers of defenders and attackers, the role of artillery, the death of Constantine XI, and the sack of the city, and note where sources agree and disagree.
  4. Evaluate reliability. Consider bias, distance from events, rhetorical models, and religious or political purpose.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4 to 6 page essay, or equivalent presentation, explain what can be established with confidence, what remains uncertain, and how later memory has shaped popular accounts. Do not invent quotations; use paraphrase or verified translations.

Library resources to use: published translations of Byzantine, Italian, and Ottoman sources, historical atlases of the eastern Mediterranean, scholarly surveys of late Byzantine and early Ottoman history, and museum collections of Byzantine and Ottoman material culture.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a major medieval state was transformed over more than two centuries without being simply destroyed at a single moment. The fragmentation of 1204, the partial restoration of 1261, the rise of Balkan rivals, the weakening effects of civil war and plague, and the growth of the Ottoman state were distinct processes that interacted. Understanding them separately and together resists both the story of inevitable Byzantine decline and the story of inevitable Ottoman triumph.

The chapter also reveals how closely religion, commerce, and politics were connected in the medieval world. The Fourth Crusade shows how papal ideals, Venetian finance, and dynastic ambition could produce an outcome none of the participants had intended at the outset. The union negotiations show how theology could be inseparable from military and diplomatic choices. The Ottoman expansion shows how frontier societies, commerce, vassalage, and religious language could combine in a political order that was not reducible to a clash of faiths.

For the history of Europe, these events complicate the idea that medieval Europe was essentially Latin and western. The Byzantine, Bulgarian, Serbian, and Ottoman worlds were central to the history of the continent, and their experiences shaped later developments: the movement of Greek scholars to Italy, discussed in Chapter 57, the position of Orthodox populations in an Ottoman and later contested Balkans, the Ottoman role in the European state system examined in Chapter 79, and the contested memories that surfaced again in the twentieth century, including in Chapter 135.

Chapter 41 closes Part VI, and Chapter 42 returns to the agricultural and demographic expansion of the High Middle Ages. Students should carry forward the recognition that the medieval world was a field of overlapping states, churches, and economies, and that its eastern and southeastern parts were neither the periphery of a western story nor a separate civilization. Their history is part of the argument of Volume II, that medieval Europe was diverse, interconnected, and shaped by contingency.
