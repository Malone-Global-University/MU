Paleolithic Europe and the First Europeans

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Deep inside a limestone cave in the Ardèche gorge of southern France, a painter, working by flickering torchlight some thirty six thousand years ago, drew a panel of lions, rhinoceroses, and horses with a confidence of line that startled the modern researchers who rediscovered the cave in 1994. No written word, no name, and no recoverable record of language survives from the world that produced this image, though the people who made it almost certainly spoke languages of their own, languages whose relationships to one another are simply lost to time rather than absent. The image itself survives, a durable trace of a human mind fully capable of observation, memory, symbolism, and deliberate artistic choice, made tens of thousands of years before any historically recoverable named language, political state, or concept of "Europe" existed on this landmass.

Chapter 2 described the physical stage on which European history unfolds, its mountains, rivers, plains, seas, and shifting Ice Age climate. This chapter introduces the first actors to walk onto that stage, populations of hominins, and eventually of anatomically modern humans, whose presence on the continent stretches back not merely thousands but well over a million years, long before agriculture, writing, metal tools, or anything resembling a state. These populations were not "Europeans" in any cultural or civilizational sense discussed in Chapter 1. They belonged to bands, not nations, and their tools, hearths, and occasional burials or painted caves are, for nearly the entire span this chapter covers, the only evidence historians and archaeologists have of how they lived, thought, and understood their world.

Consider the scale of time involved. If the whole of this textbook's one hundred and forty four chapters were compressed into a single day, the material examined in this chapter, the roughly two million years separating the earliest currently proposed evidence of hominin presence in Europe from the beginning of farming discussed in Chapter 4, would occupy the overwhelming majority of that day, while the entirety of Greek civilization, Roman history, medieval Christendom, the Renaissance, the Reformation, the era of overseas empire, industrialization, the world wars, and the contemporary European order together would occupy only its final minutes. This proportion is not a curiosity to be mentioned and then set aside. It should reshape how students understand what "the beginning" of European history actually means, and it should caution against treating the Paleolithic as a brief prelude to the "real" history that follows.

At the same time, this chapter must resist a temptation in the opposite direction: projecting later categories, ethnic, national, or even simply "human" in an unreflective modern sense, onto populations who would not have recognized them. Neanderthals were not "cavemen" in any of the crude senses popular culture has attached to that word, nor were they simply inferior early drafts of modern humans. Anatomically modern humans who reached Europe by around forty five thousand years ago were fully human in every biological and cognitive sense relevant to this textbook, but they lived in small, mobile bands whose social and economic life bore no resemblance to any later European society examined in this book. Historians and archaeologists working on this period must therefore constantly guard against two opposite distortions: flattening Ice Age populations into an undifferentiated, timeless "prehistoric" mass, and reading modern assumptions about progress, race, or civilization backward into evidence that supports no such reading.

This chapter also introduces, for the first time in this textbook, the distinctive evidentiary challenges of prehistoric archaeology. There are no chronicles, no inscriptions, no administrative records, and no named individuals to anchor this narrative, only stone tools, animal bones, hearths, occasional human remains, and, from a certain point onward, deliberately created images and objects. Reconstructing Paleolithic life from this evidence requires methods entirely different from those historians use for later, textually documented periods: stratigraphy, radiometric dating, faunal analysis, use-wear analysis of stone tools, ancient DNA analysis, and comparative study of modern hunter-gatherer societies used cautiously as analogy rather than direct evidence. Students encountering this chapter should understand that archaeological interpretation is not less rigorous than textual history, but it operates under different constraints and, in many cases, greater uncertainty, particularly regarding social organization, belief, and language, which leave far less direct trace than tools and bones.

Finally, this chapter situates European Paleolithic populations within a far larger human story that did not begin on this continent. Anatomically modern humans evolved in Africa and dispersed from there across the wider world, arriving in Europe as one region among several reached by this dispersal, and Neanderthals themselves descended from earlier hominin populations with a long history across Africa and western Eurasia before Europe became their primary, though not exclusive, homeland. Understanding Paleolithic Europe therefore requires understanding Europe's place within a broader Eurasian and African story of hominin evolution and migration, a theme consistent with this textbook's broader commitment, established in Chapter 1, to treating Europe as connected to, rather than isolated from, the wider world.

By the end of this chapter, students should understand not only what is known about Paleolithic Europe but also how fragile, partial, and continually revised that knowledge is, as new archaeological discoveries, improved dating techniques, and ancient DNA research have repeatedly overturned confident earlier conclusions. The chapters that follow, on the Neolithic transformation, the Bronze and Iron Ages, and the earliest historically documented societies, all build on the world this chapter describes, a world inhabited for immensely long stretches of time by resourceful, adaptive, symbolically capable human and hominin populations who left behind no names but an unmistakable record of ingenuity.

  4. Trace the arrival and spread of anatomically modern humans across the European landmass and describe the period of overlap with Neanderthal populations.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 established that the physical landscape of Europe was reshaped repeatedly across the Pleistocene epoch by the advance and retreat of continental ice sheets, and that the Pleistocene, lasting from roughly 2.58 million years ago until the beginning of the Holocene, encompasses essentially the entire span of hominin presence on the continent. This chapter now turns to the populations who inhabited that changing landscape, a story that must be understood within the much larger context of human evolution across Africa and Eurasia.

Modern paleoanthropology holds that the broader hominin lineage, including the genus Homo, originated and diversified principally in Africa over several million years, with successive hominin species dispersing out of Africa at different times and by different routes. Europe was never the origin point of this story. It was, instead, one of several regions reached by successive waves of hominin dispersal, beginning, on current evidence, with early Homo populations reaching parts of Eurasia, including areas at the edge of Europe, by roughly 1.8 million years ago, and reaching sites within the conventional geographic boundary of Europe discussed in Chapter 1 by dates that current evidence places close to two million years ago, depending on the type of evidence considered, ranging from possible activity evidence at Grăunceanu in Romania at roughly 1.95 million years ago to securely dated stone tools at Korolevo in Ukraine at roughly 1.42 million years ago and fossil remains at Atapuerca in Spain from roughly 1.4 to 1.1 million years ago. These early dates remain subjects of active research and occasional revision as new sites are excavated and existing evidence is re-dated using improved techniques.

Historians using this material should understand that paleoanthropology is a rapidly changing field. Conclusions confidently stated in textbooks a generation ago have sometimes been substantially revised, or even reversed, by subsequent discoveries, more precise dating methods such as improved argon-argon and uranium-series dating, and, especially since the early twenty first century, the extraction and analysis of ancient DNA from fossil remains, including, remarkably, DNA recovered from Neanderthal and Denisovan fossils and, in a few cases, from sediment itself. This chapter presents current, broadly accepted scholarly understanding while flagging, where appropriate, the genuine uncertainty and ongoing debate that characterize this field far more than most of the later periods this textbook examines.

The chronological framework historians and archaeologists use for this period differs from the framework used elsewhere in this textbook. Rather than centuries and named rulers, specialists divide the European Paleolithic into the Lower Paleolithic, extending from the earliest evidence of hominin presence to roughly 300,000 years ago; the Middle Paleolithic, extending from roughly 300,000 to 40,000 years ago and associated principally, though not exclusively, with Neanderthal populations; and the Upper Paleolithic, extending from roughly 45,000 to 11,700 years ago, the period associated with anatomically modern humans and with the flourishing of the symbolic behavior, art, and technological innovation examined later in this chapter. These period boundaries are approximate, vary somewhat by region, and are defined primarily by changes in stone tool technology, since stone tools, unlike organic materials, survive in the archaeological record in very large numbers and across the full span of this chapter's chronology.

This chapter's evidence base is therefore fundamentally different from evidence used in later chapters. Where a medieval historian might draw on a charter, a chronicle, or a saint's biography, a Paleolithic archaeologist draws on stratified sediment layers, stone tool assemblages classified by manufacturing technique, faunal remains indicating diet and hunting strategy, occasional hearths and structural remains, human skeletal remains subjected to morphological and, increasingly, genetic analysis, and, from the Upper Paleolithic onward, portable and cave art along with personal ornaments. Each of these evidence types carries its own interpretive challenges, discussed at relevant points throughout this chapter.

Section 3.1

Before "Europeans": Hominins on a Changing Landmass

As Chapter 1 emphasized, no population examined in this chapter would have understood itself as living in "Europe," and this textbook is careful not to describe them as such. What they inhabited, however, is the same physical landmass whose geography, climate, and ecological zones Chapter 2 described, a landmass that, across the immense span of time this chapter covers, looked and felt very different from the continent familiar today. For most of the period examined here, glacial and interglacial cycles repeatedly transformed coastlines, sea levels, vegetation, and the animal populations that hominins depended on for food, so that no single description of "the Paleolithic environment" applies evenly across the nearly two million years this chapter surveys.

Historians and archaeologists studying this period must therefore think in terms of successive, sometimes dramatically different environments rather than a single static backdrop. During colder glacial phases, much of northern Europe lay under ice or in a treeless, tundra-like periglacial zone, while southern Europe retained more temperate conditions and functioned, as discussed further in Section 3.9, as a refuge to which population density concentrated during the coldest intervals. During warmer interglacial phases, forests expanded, temperate and even some subtropical species extended their range further north than at present, and the human and hominin populations that could exploit this changing landscape shifted their subsistence strategies accordingly. This chapter's account of hominin and human life in Europe must accordingly be read alongside Chapter 2's account of the continent's changing physical geography, since neither can be fully understood in isolation from the other.

It is equally important to state plainly what this chapter does not claim. It does not claim that any single, continuous "population" occupied Europe across this entire span. Archaeological and genetic evidence increasingly suggests repeated episodes of population arrival, local adaptation, decline, local extinction, and later replacement or admixture by new arrivals, rather than a single unbroken line of continuous occupation. Nor does this chapter claim that any of the populations examined here possessed anything resembling a unified culture, language, or identity spanning the entire landmass at any point in this long history. The evidence instead points to regionally varied, locally adapted populations whose material culture differed across both space and time, connected loosely, if at all, across the vast distances separating, for example, Iberia from the Russian plain.

Section 3.2

The Lower and Middle Paleolithic and the Question of the Earliest Arrivals

The question of when hominins first reached the lands later called Europe has been repeatedly revised as new sites have been discovered and existing sites redated with improved scientific techniques, and this remains one of the most actively moving areas of research discussed anywhere in this textbook. For much of the twentieth century, many specialists assumed a relatively late arrival, perhaps around 500,000 years ago. Excavations since the 1990s, particularly at the Atapuerca sites in northern Spain, at Korolevo in Ukraine, at Grăunceanu in Romania, and at Dmanisi in Georgia, a site at the conventional eastern edge of the continent discussed in Chapter 1, have pushed the proposed date of hominin presence back considerably, though the precise date depends heavily on what kind of evidence is being considered.

Historians should distinguish carefully among different categories of evidence for early presence. A 2025 study reported cut-marked animal bones from Grăunceanu, Romania, interpreted as evidence of hominin butchery activity by at least roughly 1.95 million years ago, though this evidence consists of modified bone rather than stone tools or hominin fossils themselves, and its interpretation continues to be evaluated by specialists. Securely dated stone tools at Korolevo in Ukraine, by contrast, place confirmed lithic technology in Europe by roughly 1.42 million years ago. Fossil hominin remains, the most direct form of evidence, are documented at the Atapuerca sites in Spain from roughly 1.4 to 1.1 million years ago. Taken together, this evidence indicates hominin presence in parts of Europe by somewhere close to two million years ago, with the precise date depending on whether one requires bone modification, tool manufacture, or actual skeletal remains as proof, a distinction with real consequences for how confidently any single date can be stated.

The fossil and archaeological record of this early period remains sparse, consisting of a comparatively small number of sites yielding stone tools, occasional animal bones bearing cut marks indicating butchery, and, more rarely, hominin skeletal remains. Fossil material from the Atapuerca sites, including remains sometimes classified by researchers as belonging to the species Homo antecessor, provides one of the longest and most carefully studied sequences anywhere in Europe, spanning several hundred thousand years and offering insight into the physical characteristics and diet of these early populations. Cut-marked and fractured hominin bones at Atapuerca have been interpreted by most researchers as evidence of nutritional cannibalism, meaning the consumption of human remains for food, with some researchers proposing that this practice occurred in the context of conflict between distinct groups rather than within a single community; current taphonomic analysis of the bone damage patterns provides little support for a specifically ritual interpretation, and this textbook accordingly treats nutritional consumption, possibly linked to intergroup aggression, as the better-supported reading of this evidence.

By perhaps 600,000 to 400,000 years ago, fossil evidence from several European sites, including Sima de los Huesos at Atapuerca and sites in Germany and England, indicates the presence of hominin populations that many researchers have classified as Homo heidelbergensis. This designation, and the population's precise relationship to later hominins, remains taxonomically disputed rather than settled. Genetic evidence recovered from the Sima de los Huesos remains indicates that this particular population already sat on the evolutionary lineage leading toward the Neanderthals specifically, rather than representing a straightforward common ancestor shared equally by both later Neanderthals and modern humans in Africa, a more complex picture than older textbook accounts sometimes suggested. These populations manufactured increasingly sophisticated stone tools. Across the Lower Paleolithic more broadly, European stone technology included earlier core-and-flake industries, such as the Mode 1 assemblage documented at Korolevo, and later, in many regions, the large handaxes associated with the more developed Acheulean technological tradition; the populations present by roughly 600,000 to 400,000 years ago participated in this later and regionally varied technological landscape rather than in the earliest core-and-flake stage alone. Evidence for hunting weapons, including wooden implements recovered from a waterlogged site at Schöningen in Germany, indicates organized hunting of large game at some point within this broad period; the Schöningen finds were long dated to roughly 300,000 years ago, but their chronology remains under active reassessment, with a 2025 study proposing a younger age closer to 200,000 years ago and other analyses supporting an older date, so this textbook treats the Schöningen weapons as securely Middle Pleistocene in age without asserting a single precise date.

The transition from the Lower to the Middle Paleolithic, conventionally placed around 300,000 years ago, is defined primarily by a shift in stone tool manufacturing technique, from the large handaxes of the Acheulean tradition toward more standardized flake-based tool production, particularly the technique known to archaeologists as the Levallois technique, in which a stone core was carefully prepared in advance so that a single, controlled strike would detach a flake of predetermined size and shape. This technological shift, associated with the archaeological tradition archaeologists call Mousterian, corresponds broadly with the period in which the distinct Neanderthal lineage, examined in the next section, becomes clearly identifiable in the European fossil record.

Section 3.3

The Neanderthals

For several hundred thousand years, from perhaps 400,000 years ago until their disappearance from the fossil record around 40,000 years ago, Neanderthals were the dominant hominin population across Europe and parts of western Asia, adapting successfully to environments ranging from the temperate interglacial forests of some periods to the harsh, cold, open landscapes of glacial phases. Popular culture has long portrayed Neanderthals through a series of unflattering stereotypes, as hulking, stooped, unintelligent "cavemen" incapable of complex thought or behavior. Modern paleoanthropology has substantially revised this picture, though genuine scientific debate continues regarding the degree and nature of Neanderthal cognitive and behavioral sophistication relative to contemporary and later modern human populations.

Physically, Neanderthals were robustly built, with a stocky frame, pronounced brow ridges, a receding forehead, and a chest and limb proportions that some researchers interpret as adaptations to cold climates, though this interpretation, like much in this field, remains debated. Their brain volume was, on average, comparable to or in some measurements slightly larger than that of modern humans, though brain volume alone provides limited insight into cognitive capacity, since brain organization and structure matter at least as much as raw size, a point this chapter emphasizes to avoid a simplistic equation between brain measurement and intelligence.

Archaeological evidence increasingly demonstrates behavioral sophistication among Neanderthal populations. Excavations at multiple sites indicate that Neanderthals hunted large and dangerous game, including mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, and cave bear, with strong evidence for close-range thrusting and probably hand-cast spears used at close quarters, a strategy requiring considerable coordination, courage, and knowledge of animal behavior. The full range and diversity of Neanderthal projectile use remains debated among specialists, and this textbook does not claim that longer-range throwing was entirely absent from their hunting repertoire. Evidence from several sites indicates the controlled use of fire for warmth, cooking, and tool manufacture, including the production of birch-bark tar or adhesive, apparently made through a heating or dry-distillation process whose exact method continues to be debated by experimental archaeologists, used for hafting stone tools onto wooden handles, a genuinely sophisticated technological achievement. Some sites have yielded evidence interpreted by various researchers as indicating the construction of simple shelters, the use of manganese dioxide or ochre pigments possibly for symbolic or practical purposes, and, at a small number of sites, the deliberate arrangement of bodies in a manner some archaeologists interpret as intentional burial, examined further in Section 3.8.

Neanderthal effective population sizes appear generally to have been relatively small, based on genetic evidence, although population size, isolation, and connectivity varied considerably across regions and periods rather than following a single uniform pattern across the population's entire range from Iberia to Siberia. Some regional Neanderthal groups show evidence of greater isolation and inbreeding than others, a circumstance some researchers have connected to the population's eventual disappearance, a question examined directly in the Historical Debate feature later in this chapter. This chapter does not treat the Neanderthals as an inferior or failed evolutionary experiment. Rather, they represent a distinct, long-lived, and behaviorally capable hominin lineage whose several hundred thousand years of successful adaptation to European environments deserve serious historical attention in their own right, not merely as a backdrop to the arrival of modern humans.

Section 3.4

The Arrival of Homo Sapiens and the Question of Overlap

Anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, evolved in Africa, with the oldest currently known fossil evidence for the species dating to roughly 300,000 years ago at sites in North Africa, though the fuller emergence of the full suite of anatomical and behavioral characteristics associated with modern humans appears to have developed over a longer and more complex period than earlier, simpler models suggested. Modern humans dispersed out of Africa in one or more waves, reaching different parts of Asia at various times. Current evidence indicates that Homo sapiens was securely present in Europe by more than 45,000 years ago, with evidence from several sites suggesting arrivals across a broader range of roughly 50,000 to 45,000 years ago; securely dated remains at Ranis in Germany, for example, place modern humans at higher European latitudes by around 45,000 years ago, while material from Bacho Kiro in Bulgaria and Zlatý kůň in the Czech lands points to early populations within this same general interval. A much earlier and highly disputed claim comes from the Apidima 1 fossil in Greece, dated to more than 210,000 years ago and interpreted by some researchers as representing Homo sapiens, though both its taxonomic attribution and its implications for an early, apparently unsuccessful dispersal into Europe remain contested among specialists. A proposed, more contested occupation at Grotte Mandrin in southern France has been argued by some researchers to indicate a modern human presence as early as roughly 54,000 years ago, though this claim, and its implications for the timing of overlap with Neanderthal populations, remains actively debated.

The arrival of modern humans in Europe did not immediately displace the existing Neanderthal population. Instead, current evidence indicates a period of overlap lasting several thousand years, during which both populations inhabited the same broad landmass, and in some regions apparently the same general territories, though direct evidence of specific encounters between the two populations remains extremely rare in the archaeological record. This period of overlap is of considerable scientific and historical interest, since it raises questions about interaction, competition for resources, and, as ancient DNA research has now firmly established, biological interbreeding between the two populations.

Ancient DNA analysis, developed and refined particularly since the sequencing of a substantial portion of the Neanderthal genome beginning in 2010, has demonstrated conclusively that most present-day human populations with substantial ancestry outside Africa carry roughly one to three percent Neanderthal-derived DNA, a figure that continues to be refined through ongoing research. Genomic work has also shown that smaller amounts of Neanderthal-derived ancestry occur in some African populations as well, largely as a result of later back-migration of Eurasian populations into Africa, a finding that complicates any simple picture of Neanderthal ancestry as confined to non-African populations alone. This finding represents one of the most significant scientific developments affecting the study of this period in recent decades, since it demonstrates that Neanderthals and modern humans interbred at some point or points during their period of overlap in western Asia or Europe, overturning an earlier scholarly consensus that had generally assumed no significant biological mixing between the two populations. This chapter's account of Neanderthal "disappearance," discussed further in the Historical Debate feature below, must therefore be understood carefully: Neanderthals as a physically and behaviorally distinct population ceased to exist as an identifiable, separate lineage, but a portion of their genetic legacy persists within later and modern human populations.

The Aurignacian became one of the major, widespread early Upper Paleolithic culture-complexes associated with Homo sapiens in Europe, characterized by distinctive blade-based stone tool technology, worked bone and antler tools, and, at several sites, some of the earliest confidently dated examples of figurative art and personal ornamentation within Europe, examined further in Section 3.7. The earliest securely documented modern humans in Europe, however, are associated with several different transitional and Initial Upper Paleolithic technocomplexes rather than with the Aurignacian alone: the Ranis material in Germany, for example, is linked to a distinct technocomplex known to archaeologists as the Lincombian-Ranisian-Jerzmanowician, or LRJ, while the Bacho Kiro material in Bulgaria represents a separate Initial Upper Paleolithic industry. The relationship between these various early technocomplexes, the later and more widespread Aurignacian, and the biological arrival of modern humans remains an active area of research, and some scholars have debated whether certain transitional stone tool industries found at a small number of sites might represent Neanderthal populations adopting or independently developing more sophisticated technologies during the period of overlap, a question connected to the larger debate over relative Neanderthal cognitive capacity addressed in Section 3.3.

Section 3.5

Ice Age Life: Subsistence, Technology, and Adaptation

Whether Neanderthal or modern human, Paleolithic populations across Europe lived as hunter-gatherers, meaning that they obtained food entirely through hunting wild animals, fishing where opportunity allowed, and gathering wild plant foods, nuts, roots, and other edible materials, rather than through cultivated agriculture or domesticated livestock, developments examined in Chapter 4. This mode of subsistence required extensive and detailed knowledge of the local environment, including the seasonal availability of plant foods, the migration patterns and behavior of game animals, and the location of essential resources such as workable stone, water, and shelter.

Faunal remains recovered from Paleolithic sites, examined by archaeologists through the specialized discipline of zooarchaeology, indicate that Ice Age European populations hunted a wide range of large mammals depending on region, period, and local availability, including mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, reindeer, wild horse, bison, red deer, and, in some periods and regions, more dangerous prey such as cave bear and cave lion. Hunting strategies varied considerably: some evidence suggests communal hunting drives in which game animals were funneled toward natural traps such as cliffs or bogs, while other evidence indicates more individualized or small-group hunting using spears, and, from a certain point in the Upper Paleolithic onward, the spear-thrower, a lever device that substantially increased the force and range of a thrown spear. Some researchers have argued, on the basis of small, standardized stone points from Grotte Mandrin in France, that mechanically propelled projectile technology, possibly an early form of the bow and arrow, may have been in use by around 54,000 years ago, though this interpretation, and its association with an early modern human presence at the site, remains debated. More broadly accepted evidence for the bow and arrow becomes clearer toward the very end of the period covered by this chapter and more fully in the following Mesolithic period.

Stone tool technology, examined by archaeologists through the study of lithic industries, provides the single most abundant and continuously documented category of Paleolithic evidence, since stone, unlike bone, wood, or plant fiber, survives in the archaeological record across the entire span of this chapter's chronology. Successive technological traditions, from the large handaxes of the Lower Paleolithic Acheulean tradition, through the prepared-core Levallois technique associated with the Middle Paleolithic Mousterian tradition, to the standardized blade production and increasingly specialized tool types of the Upper Paleolithic Aurignacian, Gravettian, Solutrean, and Magdalenian traditions, allow archaeologists to trace technological change, regional variation, and, indirectly, changing patterns of social organization and knowledge transmission across immense stretches of time. The term knapping refers to the technique of shaping stone tools by controlled percussion or pressure, a skill requiring considerable practice, knowledge of stone fracture mechanics, and, in many cases, transmission of technique across generations, evidence in itself of sustained social learning.

Upper Paleolithic populations also developed increasingly sophisticated tools made from bone, antler, and ivory, materials that, unlike stone, could be carved, shaped, and worked into needles, harpoon points, spear points, and other specialized implements. The invention of the eyed needle, evidenced at several Upper Paleolithic sites, indicates the production of fitted, sewn clothing, a technological development of considerable importance for survival in the cold environments of the later Paleolithic examined in Section 3.9. Evidence for constructed shelters, including sites where mammoth bones were apparently used as structural elements for dwellings on the open steppe of what is now Ukraine and the East European Plain discussed in Chapter 2, demonstrates that Upper Paleolithic populations were capable of substantial engineering achievement using the materials available to them.

Section 3.6

Social Life, Language, and Symbolic Behavior

Reconstructing Paleolithic social organization presents historians and archaeologists with some of the most difficult interpretive challenges in this entire chapter, since social structures, kinship systems, and language leave far less direct physical trace than tools, bones, or art. What can be inferred comes largely from indirect evidence: the size and organization of campsites, the distances over which certain raw materials, such as particular types of flint or marine shells, were transported, and comparative study, undertaken cautiously, of modern and historically documented hunter-gatherer societies, used as a source of possible analogy rather than direct evidence about specific Paleolithic populations.

Most archaeologists believe Paleolithic populations lived in small, mobile bands, likely numbering from a few dozen to perhaps a hundred or more individuals depending on season, region, and resource availability, moving across the landscape in patterns shaped by the seasonal availability of game and plant foods. Evidence for larger, temporary aggregations of multiple bands at certain times and places, possibly for communal hunting, exchange of marriage partners, or ritual purposes, has been proposed at some Upper Paleolithic sites, though the scale and regularity of such aggregations remain debated among specialists.

The question of language is particularly difficult, since language itself leaves no direct archaeological trace. Most researchers today believe that some capacity for complex language was likely present in anatomically modern humans from a relatively early period, and increasing numbers of researchers argue that at least some capacity for complex communication, and possibly language in a fuller sense, may also have been present among Neanderthal populations, based on anatomical evidence including the structure of the hyoid bone and inner ear, and on the behavioral sophistication discussed in Section 3.3. This remains, however, a genuinely contested area of research rather than a settled conclusion, and this textbook presents it as such rather than asserting a definitive answer.

The evidence for what archaeologists broadly call symbolic behavior, the capacity to create and use objects or marks that carry meaning beyond immediate practical function, becomes considerably more abundant and less ambiguous from the Upper Paleolithic onward, though some evidence for possible symbolic activity among Neanderthal populations, including the use of pigments and the deliberate collection of unusual objects such as eagle talons apparently used as ornaments at certain sites, has prompted vigorous scholarly debate about whether symbolic thought was exclusive to modern humans or shared, at least in some degree, with Neanderthal populations. This debate connects directly to the larger and continuing reassessment of Neanderthal cognitive capacity discussed throughout this chapter.

Section 3.7

Cave Art, Portable Art, and the Question of Meaning

Among the most striking and historically significant achievements of Upper Paleolithic populations in Europe is the creation of cave art, technically termed parietal art by archaeologists to distinguish it from portable art created on movable objects, and personal ornamentation. Painted and engraved caves, found predominantly though not exclusively in southwestern France and northern Spain, depict animals, principally large herbivores and predators such as horses, bison, aurochs, mammoth, deer, lions, and rhinoceros, along with a smaller number of abstract signs, hand stencils, and, comparatively rarely, human or human-like figures, often rendered less naturalistically than the animal subjects.

Among the oldest securely dated figurative cave paintings associated with Upper Paleolithic Homo sapiens in Europe are those of Chauvet Cave in the Ardèche region of southern France, discovered in 1994 and dated by radiocarbon analysis to approximately 36,000 years ago, though the dating and interpretation of some of the cave's phases remain subjects of ongoing scientific discussion, and older, more disputed markings have been proposed at some Iberian cave sites, in certain cases associated by some researchers with Neanderthal rather than modern human artists, an attribution that remains contested. Considered within Europe specifically, the sophistication of the Chauvet paintings, which include convincing perspective, shading, and dynamic representation of animal movement, surprised many specialists who had previously assumed that European artistic technique developed gradually and linearly over tens of thousands of years, since Chauvet demonstrates a highly developed artistic capability at a very early point in the regional Upper Paleolithic sequence. This textbook does not claim that Chauvet represents the world's earliest sophisticated figurative art; research published since the 2010s on cave sites in Sulawesi, Indonesia, has documented securely dated figurative and narrative art considerably older than Chauvet, including a hand stencil with a minimum age of roughly 67,800 years and narrative figurative scenes dated to more than 51,000 years ago, findings that place the deepest origins of figurative art outside Europe and caution against treating any single European site as representing humanity's earliest artistic achievement. Later painted caves within Europe, including the renowned site of Lascaux in southwestern France, dated to roughly 17,000 years ago, and Altamira in northern Spain, whose paintings span multiple periods, demonstrate the continuation and further development of this regional artistic tradition across many thousands of years.

Portable art, including carved figurines, engraved bone and antler objects, and perforated shells and teeth apparently used as beads or pendants, is found across a much wider geographic range than cave painting, extending from Iberia across central Europe into Russia and Siberia. Among the most widely discussed categories of portable art are the so-called Venus figurines, small carved representations, generally interpreted as depicting stylized female forms with emphasized reproductive features, found at sites across this vast geographic range and dated across much of the Upper Paleolithic, from examples at Hohle Fels in Germany dated to roughly 40,000 years ago through numerous later examples. The consistency, and also the considerable variation, among these figurines across such a wide geographic and chronological range has generated extensive scholarly discussion regarding their possible meaning and function.

This chapter must be candid about a central interpretive difficulty: the meaning of Paleolithic art cannot be recovered with confidence, since no textual or oral tradition survives to explain it, and any interpretation necessarily involves inference rather than direct evidence. Proposed interpretations of cave art over the past century have included hunting magic intended to ensure success in the hunt, shamanistic or ritual practice connected to altered states of consciousness, initiation rites, territorial or group-identity marking, and, more recently, interpretations emphasizing the art's connection to specific animal behaviors, seasonal cycles, or star patterns, though this last category of interpretation, sometimes called archaeoastronomical interpretation, remains particularly speculative and contested. No single interpretation commands universal scholarly agreement, and this textbook presents these possibilities as competing hypotheses rather than established fact, a point developed further in the Historical Debate feature of this chapter's companion discussion of Neanderthal extinction and reinforced throughout this section.

Section 3.8

Burial, Belief, and the Treatment of the Dead

Evidence for the deliberate burial of the dead provides some of the most emotionally resonant, and interpretively difficult, evidence available for this period. A small number of Middle Paleolithic sites, including several associated with Neanderthal populations such as La Chapelle-aux-Saints and La Ferrassie in France, have yielded human remains found in positions and contexts that some archaeologists interpret as evidence of deliberate burial, potentially indicating some form of concern for the dead extending beyond mere practical disposal. This interpretation is not universally accepted; other researchers have argued that some or all of these cases may reflect natural burial processes, such as sediment accumulation or accidental cave-in, rather than deliberate human action, and the debate over how confidently to interpret specific sites as intentional burials continues among specialists.

Evidence for burial becomes considerably more abundant, and generally less ambiguous, in the Upper Paleolithic, associated with modern human populations. A number of Upper Paleolithic burials include grave goods, objects placed with the deceased such as personal ornaments, tools, or, in some notably elaborate cases, large quantities of perforated beads apparently sewn onto clothing, as documented at the Sungir site in Russia, where multiple burials dated to roughly 34,000 years ago included thousands of ivory beads along with other objects. The presence of grave goods, and the evident labor invested in some burials, has led many archaeologists to interpret at least some Upper Paleolithic burial practice as reflecting belief in some form of continued existence or significance of the dead, though the specific content of any such belief, whether involving an afterlife, ancestor reverence, or some other framework entirely foreign to later religious categories, cannot be recovered with any confidence from the physical evidence alone.

Historians must resist the temptation to read later religious concepts, whether drawn from historically documented polytheism, monotheism, or modern anthropological categories such as "shamanism," directly back onto Paleolithic burial practice. What the evidence supports is more limited but still significant: some Paleolithic populations, both Neanderthal and modern human, in at least some times and places, treated the bodies of the dead with deliberate care extending beyond simple disposal, a pattern of behavior that, whatever its underlying beliefs, connects these populations to the much larger and continuing human practice, examined at many later points throughout this textbook, of marking death as an event of social and, in many though not all interpretations, spiritual significance.

Section 3.9

Surviving the Last Glacial Maximum and the Late Glacial World

As Chapter 2 explained, the Last Glacial Maximum, reaching its coldest and most extensive phase roughly between 26,500 and 19,000 years ago, subjected the European landmass to its most severe environmental conditions of the entire span this chapter covers. Continental ice sheets covered Scandinavia and much of the British Isles, while a broad zone of cold, dry, largely treeless periglacial steppe-tundra extended across much of central and eastern Europe, supporting herds of cold-adapted large mammals such as mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, and reindeer, but offering few of the resources, timber, shelter, and diverse plant foods, that had supported denser human populations during milder periods.

Archaeological evidence indicates that human population density and geographic distribution across Europe contracted significantly during the coldest phase of the Last Glacial Maximum, with populations apparently concentrating in a smaller number of more hospitable proposed refugia, regions of Iberia, southern France, Italy, and the Balkans, where somewhat milder conditions, greater availability of plant foods and firewood, and continued access to diverse game allowed continued, if reduced, human occupation. These regions should not be treated as a single uniform refuge system, however; paleogenomic evidence indicates that southwestern Europe shows relatively strong population continuity across the Last Glacial Maximum, while Italy and parts of southern and eastern Europe show more substantial genetic turnover, suggesting that different refugial zones had distinct demographic histories rather than functioning identically. Regions further north and east, including much of the North European Plain and the British Isles, appear to have been largely or entirely abandoned by human populations during the most severe phase of glacial conditions, though the precise extent and timing of this abandonment varied by region and remains a subject of ongoing archaeological investigation.

The archaeological culture-complex most closely associated with the period immediately preceding and during the early stages of the Last Glacial Maximum in western Europe is generally termed the Solutrean, known for distinctive and technically sophisticated stone tool production, including finely worked, leaf-shaped stone points achieved through a specialized pressure-flaking technique. Following the Last Glacial Maximum, as climate began to ameliorate from roughly 19,000 years ago onward, the Magdalenian culture-complex, associated with sophisticated bone and antler tool technology, including barbed harpoon points, and with some of the most elaborate cave art of the entire Upper Paleolithic, including the paintings at Lascaux, spread across much of western and central Europe, apparently reflecting population recovery and, eventually, gradual resettlement of previously abandoned northern territories as retreating ice sheets and changing vegetation opened new territory to human and animal populations alike.

The Late Glacial period, extending from roughly 19,000 to 11,700 years ago, was not a simple, steady warming trend. Climate proxy evidence, including ice cores and pollen records discussed in Chapter 2, indicates significant short-term fluctuations, including a pronounced return to near-glacial conditions during an interval called the Younger Dryas, dated to approximately 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, which appears to have interrupted the broader warming trend and required renewed adaptation by human populations who had, in some regions, already begun to resettle northern territories and adjust their subsistence strategies to changing plant and animal resources. The end of the Younger Dryas and the onset of the fully interglacial conditions of the Holocene epoch, discussed in Chapter 2, mark the conventional close of the Paleolithic across most of Europe and open the transitional Mesolithic period, during which continued hunting, fishing, and gathering populations adapted to increasingly forested, temperate post-glacial landscapes in the millennia immediately preceding the agricultural transformations examined in Chapter 4.

Section 3.10

From Ice Age Hunters to the Threshold of a New World

By the close of the Paleolithic, the population examined throughout this chapter, anatomically and cognitively modern humans, had inhabited the European landmass for tens of thousands of years, adapting successfully to environments ranging from the depths of the Last Glacial Maximum to the warming conditions of the early Holocene. They had developed sophisticated stone, bone, and antler tool technologies; hunted large and often dangerous game using increasingly effective weapons and strategies; created cave and portable art of considerable technical and, by any reasonable assessment, aesthetic sophistication; buried at least some of their dead with evident care and, in some cases, considerable material investment; and organized themselves into mobile bands whose precise social structure remains only partially recoverable from the archaeological evidence.

None of these populations lived in permanent settlements, cultivated crops, kept domesticated livestock beyond, in some late contexts, a tentative and still debated relationship with wolves that would eventually lead to the domestication of the dog, or organized themselves into anything resembling the political structures examined in later chapters of this textbook. The transformations examined in Chapter 4, the shift toward agriculture, permanent settlement, and, eventually, more complex forms of social and political organization, represent a genuinely new departure in human history, not a simple or inevitable continuation of Paleolithic patterns. At the same time, the technological, cognitive, and social foundations established across the immense span of time covered by this chapter, tool manufacture, cooperative hunting, symbolic expression, and care for the dead, provided the human capacities upon which all subsequent European history, examined throughout the remaining one hundred and forty one chapters of this textbook, would ultimately depend.

This chapter closes, therefore, not with a decisive ending but with a threshold. The post-glacial Mesolithic populations who inhabited Europe's warming, increasingly forested landscape in the millennia after 11,700 years ago continued to hunt, fish, and gather using technologies and strategies adapted to the new environment, examined briefly at the opening of Chapter 4, until a different kind of transformation, the deliberate cultivation of plants and management of domesticated animals, began to spread into parts of the continent from societies further east and south, inaugurating the Neolithic period and the subject of the next chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Hominin, Hominid, and Human

The term hominin refers, in current scientific usage, to the group of species more closely related to modern humans than to chimpanzees, including modern Homo sapiens, extinct species of the genus Homo such as Homo neanderthalensis and Homo heidelbergensis, and earlier bipedal ancestral species. The broader term hominid is used somewhat differently across scientific traditions and sometimes includes great apes more broadly, so this textbook uses "hominin" as the more precise term for the human lineage discussed throughout this chapter.

This chapter uses the term human, without qualification, only for anatomically modern Homo sapiens. Neanderthals and earlier hominin populations are referred to by their specific names rather than described simply as "early humans," both because this terminology reflects current scientific practice and because it avoids implying that these populations were simply an earlier, lesser stage of modern humanity rather than distinct populations deserving evaluation on their own terms.

Historical Vocabulary
Lithic Industries and Culture-Complexes

Archaeologists use the term lithic industry to describe a recognizable, recurring pattern of stone tool manufacture, defined by characteristic techniques, tool forms, and manufacturing sequences. Because organic materials such as wood, plant fiber, and, in many contexts, bone survive far less often than stone in the archaeological record, lithic industries provide the primary evidence archaeologists use to define and compare Paleolithic populations across time and space.

The term culture-complex, used for groupings such as the Aurignacian, Gravettian, Solutrean, and Magdalenian, describes a broader pattern of material culture, including stone and bone tools, art, and sometimes burial practice, that recurs across multiple sites within a given period and region. These culture-complexes should not be mistaken for ethnic groups, languages, or political communities in the sense used elsewhere in this textbook. They are archaeological classifications based on shared material culture, not evidence of shared identity, language, or political organization in any sense comparable to later historical societies.

Primary Evidence Window

The Painted Caves of Chauvet, Lascaux, and Altamira

Type of evidence: Parietal (cave) art, created by Upper Paleolithic populations · Approximate dates: Chauvet, c. 36,000 years ago; Lascaux, c. 17,000 years ago; Altamira, spanning multiple phases · Discovery: Altamira in 1879, Lascaux in 1940, Chauvet in 1994

Context: Because no textual record survives from this period, cave art functions as a form of primary evidence unlike any other examined in this textbook: it is a direct, deliberately created artifact of Upper Paleolithic thought and skill, but it comes with no accompanying explanation, label, or context beyond what archaeologists can infer from the images themselves, their placement within the cave, and their relationship to other evidence found at the same sites.

Across these and other painted caves of southwestern Europe, artists most frequently depicted large herbivores and predators, horses, bison, aurochs, mammoth, deer, and lions, rendered with considerable attention to anatomical proportion, movement, and, in a number of panels, techniques suggesting depth and perspective. Human figures appear far less frequently and are generally rendered less naturalistically than the animals. Abstract signs, dots, and hand stencils, created by placing a hand against the rock and blowing pigment around it, also recur across multiple sites and periods.

Analysis: This evidence demonstrates conclusively that Upper Paleolithic populations possessed sophisticated observational skill, technical control of pigment and technique, and some system, or systems, of symbolic representation whose specific meaning cannot be recovered with confidence. Altamira's initial discovery in 1879 was met with considerable scholarly skepticism, since many contemporary specialists doubted that Ice Age populations could have produced work of such quality, a skepticism largely overcome only by the early twentieth century as further painted caves were discovered and dated. This history of skepticism is itself instructive, illustrating how prior assumptions about "primitive" capability can distort the interpretation of evidence until additional discoveries force reconsideration.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Neanderthals Disappear?

The question: Neanderthal populations, present across Europe for several hundred thousand years, disappear from the fossil and archaeological record by approximately 40,000 years ago, not long after the arrival of anatomically modern humans. What explains this disappearance, and what role, if any, did modern humans play in it?

Interpretation A — Competitive replacement by modern humans: Some researchers argue that anatomically modern humans possessed subtle cognitive, social, or technological advantages, perhaps related to more extensive social networks, more flexible subsistence strategies, or, at some sites, distinctive projectile technologies, that allowed them to outcompete Neanderthal populations for shared resources over an extended period, gradually reducing Neanderthal population size and territory until the population became unsustainable. Some technological innovations sometimes cited in this connection, such as the spear-thrower, are securely documented in Europe only well after the disappearance of Neanderthals, roughly 31,000 years ago at Maisières-Canal in Belgium, and should not be treated as demonstrated advantages during the actual period of overlap.
Interpretation B — Climate stress, small population size, and absorption through interbreeding: Other researchers emphasize that Neanderthal populations were already small and fragmented before the arrival of modern humans, and argue that climatic instability during the period of overlap, combined with inherently low population numbers and genetic evidence indicating some degree of inbreeding within isolated Neanderthal groups, made the population vulnerable to decline independent of direct competition. This interpretation is often combined with the observation, confirmed by ancient DNA analysis, that some Neanderthal populations were partially absorbed into modern human populations through interbreeding rather than eliminated outright, meaning that Neanderthals did not simply vanish without genetic trace.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping evidence, including the chronological overlap between the two populations, comparative analysis of stone tool technology and subsistence strategies, genetic evidence of interbreeding, and analysis of Neanderthal population genetics indicating small and sometimes isolated group sizes, but they differ in how much weight to give direct competition, replacement, and gradual absorption as complementary or competing explanations.

Why it matters: How historians and scientists resolve this question shapes broader understanding of the relationship between different hominin populations, the role of modern human behavioral or technological advantage in this specific case, and the extent to which Neanderthal "disappearance" should be understood as extinction, absorption, or some combination of both.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence that could support each interpretation, and explain why many researchers now favor multifactorial explanations, involving demography, climate, competition, migration, and admixture together, rather than a single, decisive cause.

Multiple Perspectives

Imagining Ice Age Lives: What the Evidence Can and Cannot Tell Us

Note: because no individual voice survives from this period, the perspectives below are analytical reconstructions grounded in archaeological evidence about different roles and experiences within Paleolithic bands, not quotations or invented statements attributed to specific named people.

The toolmaker's perspective, inferred from lithic evidence: Producing a well-made stone tool required extensive practiced skill, knowledge of how different stone types fracture, and, in many cases, years of apprenticeship, suggesting that skilled toolmaking was a valued and likely specialized capacity within Paleolithic bands, transmitted through direct observation and practice rather than any written instruction.
The hunter's perspective, inferred from faunal remains and weapon technology: Successfully hunting large, dangerous game such as mammoth or cave bear required coordinated group effort, detailed knowledge of animal behavior and seasonal movement, and acceptance of considerable physical risk, a set of demands that likely shaped social cooperation and the transmission of ecological knowledge across generations.
The artist's perspective, inferred from cave and portable art: Creating a cave painting deep within a dark cave system, reachable in some cases only after a difficult journey underground by torchlight, required planning, sustained physical effort, and command of technique, suggesting that image-making carried significance substantial enough to justify considerable investment of time and risk.
The perspective of those who cared for the dead, inferred from burial evidence: The evident labor invested in some Upper Paleolithic burials, including the sewing of thousands of beads onto burial clothing at sites such as Sungir, suggests that at least some individuals within Paleolithic communities held sufficient social importance, or were mourned with sufficient intensity, to justify an extraordinary expenditure of collective time and material resources.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Earliest Arrivals to Modern Humans

What Changed

  • Stone tool technology developed from simple large handaxes through prepared-core techniques to specialized, standardized blade and bone tool industries
  • The dominant hominin population shifted from archaic Homo populations, to Neanderthals, to anatomically modern humans, with periods of overlap and, in the final transition, interbreeding rather than a single clean replacement
  • Symbolic behavior, including figurative art, personal ornamentation, and elaborate burial, becomes considerably more abundant and less ambiguous from the Upper Paleolithic onward
  • Population distribution across the continent contracted and expanded repeatedly in response to glacial and interglacial climate cycles

What Stayed the Same

  • All populations examined in this chapter lived as mobile hunter-gatherers, without agriculture, permanent settlement, or the political structures examined in later chapters
  • Successful adaptation to a changing and often harsh environment remained the central challenge facing every population across this chapter's chronology
  • Stone tool manufacture remained the most consistently documented technology across the entire span of this chapter
  • Small band-level social organization appears to have persisted, in some form, across the entire period, even as symbolic behavior became more abundant and archaeologically visible in the Upper Paleolithic

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents treating the Paleolithic as an undifferentiated, static "prehistory" and instead reveals a long and genuinely dynamic history of adaptation, population change, and increasingly abundant and diverse, rather than simply more "advanced," symbolic behavior.

Cause and Effect

Climate, Migration, and Survival in Ice Age Europe

Long-term causes

Repeated glacial and interglacial cycles across the Pleistocene epoch, discussed in Chapter 2, produced dramatic and recurring shifts in temperature, vegetation, and the distribution of game animals across the European landmass.

Short-term causes

The onset of the Last Glacial Maximum around 26,500 years ago sharply reduced the habitability of much of northern and central Europe within a comparatively short span of time on a geological, though not a human, timescale.

Effect

Human population contracted into southern refugia in Iberia, southern France, Italy, and the Balkans, abandoning much of northern and central Europe until warming conditions after roughly 19,000 years ago allowed gradual resettlement.

This chapter's caution

Population contraction and resettlement were not instantaneous or uniform processes; the Younger Dryas cold interval demonstrates that recovery could itself be interrupted, and archaeological evidence for the pace and route of resettlement remains incomplete and subject to revision.

Historical complexity: Climate change alone does not fully explain the disappearance of Neanderthal populations discussed in the Historical Debate feature above, since Neanderthals had already survived multiple earlier glacial cycles successfully; climate stress in combination with small population size and the arrival of a competing population appears to offer a more complete explanation than climate alone.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Survival in a Hunter-Gatherer Band

Life within a Paleolithic band centered on the recurring, demanding work of securing food, maintaining tools, preparing hides and clothing, and moving camp in response to the seasonal availability of game and plant resources. Evidence for fitted, sewn clothing, made possible by the eyed needle discussed in Section 3.5, suggests considerable time invested in hide preparation and sewing, likely undertaken, based on comparative ethnographic evidence used cautiously as analogy, by particular members of the band with specialized skill in this craft, though the specific division of labor by age or sex within Paleolithic bands cannot be established with confidence from the archaeological evidence alone.

Children within Paleolithic bands would have learned essential skills, toolmaking, food gathering, and knowledge of the local environment, through direct observation and practice within the band rather than through any institution resembling later education. Evidence for the transport of certain raw materials, particularly marine shells and high-quality flint, across distances of many dozens or even hundreds of kilometers at some Upper Paleolithic sites suggests networks of exchange, or at least indirect transmission, extending well beyond the immediate territory of any single band, hinting at forms of social connection across the landscape that the archaeological evidence can only partially illuminate. Understanding these ordinary rhythms of survival, craft, and knowledge transmission is essential to appreciating that the populations examined in this chapter, though leaving behind no names and no written record, lived lives organized around the same fundamental human needs, food, shelter, social bonds, and the care of the young and the dead, that recur throughout every subsequent chapter of this textbook.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the immensely long history of hominin and human presence on the European landmass, from the earliest currently proposed evidence of hominin activity, close to two million years ago, through the close of the Paleolithic at the beginning of the Holocene epoch roughly 11,700 years ago. Across this span, several distinct hominin populations successively inhabited the continent, including archaic Homo populations associated with the Lower Paleolithic Acheulean tradition, the long-lived Neanderthal population associated with the Middle Paleolithic Mousterian tradition, and, from more than 45,000 years ago onward, anatomically modern humans associated with the Upper Paleolithic Aurignacian, Gravettian, Solutrean, and Magdalenian culture-complexes.

The chapter examined the Neanderthals in detail, presenting current scholarly understanding of their physical characteristics, technological capability, and increasingly recognized behavioral sophistication, while cautioning against outdated stereotypes that portrayed them as simple or unintelligent. It examined the arrival of anatomically modern humans and the resulting period of overlap with Neanderthal populations, emphasizing the significance of ancient DNA evidence demonstrating interbreeding between the two populations, a finding that has substantially revised scholarly understanding of Neanderthal "disappearance" as involving partial biological absorption rather than simple extinction alone.

The chapter surveyed Paleolithic subsistence strategies and technology, including stone, bone, and antler tool industries, hunting strategies for large and often dangerous game, and the development of fitted clothing and constructed shelters that allowed survival in demanding Ice Age environments. It examined, with appropriate caution regarding the limits of the evidence, what can and cannot be inferred about Paleolithic social organization, language, and symbolic behavior, and it surveyed the remarkable evidence of Upper Paleolithic cave and portable art, including the paintings of Chauvet, Lascaux, and Altamira and the widely distributed corpus of Venus figurines, while emphasizing that the meaning of this art remains genuinely uncertain rather than confidently established.

The chapter also examined evidence for the deliberate treatment of the dead, from contested possible Neanderthal burials to the more abundant and often elaborate burial practices of Upper Paleolithic modern humans, and it traced how human populations survived, retreated from, and eventually resettled the continent across the extreme conditions of the Last Glacial Maximum and the subsequent, non-uniform warming of the Late Glacial period, including the interruption represented by the Younger Dryas cold interval.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized the distinctive evidentiary challenges of prehistoric archaeology, a field that relies on stone tools, faunal remains, skeletal evidence, art, and increasingly on ancient DNA analysis rather than the textual sources historians rely on for later periods, and a field whose conclusions have been repeatedly and substantially revised by new discoveries and improved scientific techniques. The Historical Debate feature on Neanderthal extinction illustrated this point concretely, showing how a question once treated as settled has been substantially reframed by genetic evidence unavailable to earlier generations of researchers.

These populations, though leaving behind no political institutions, no written language, and no historical memory in any sense recoverable through documentary evidence, established the technological, cognitive, and social foundations, tool manufacture, cooperative subsistence, symbolic expression, and care for the dead, upon which the transformations examined in Chapter 4 and throughout the remainder of this textbook would ultimately depend. Chapter 4 now turns to the Neolithic transformation, the spread of agriculture and permanent settlement into Europe, a development that would, over several millennia, fundamentally reshape the way of life this chapter has described.

Key Terms

Hominin
The group of species more closely related to modern humans than to chimpanzees, including modern Homo sapiens and extinct related species such as Neanderthals.
Paleoanthropology
The scientific study of human evolution and prehistory, drawing on fossil evidence, archaeology, and, increasingly, ancient DNA analysis.
Lower Paleolithic
The earliest division of the Paleolithic, extending from the earliest hominin presence in Europe to roughly 300,000 years ago, including early core-and-flake industries and, later in many regions, Acheulean handaxe traditions.
Middle Paleolithic
The period from roughly 300,000 to 40,000 years ago, associated principally with Neanderthal populations and Mousterian stone tool technology.
Upper Paleolithic
The period from roughly 45,000 to 11,700 years ago, associated with anatomically modern humans, standardized blade technology, and the flourishing of art and symbolic behavior.
Mesolithic
The transitional period following the Paleolithic, during which hunter-gatherer populations adapted to increasingly forested, temperate postglacial environments prior to the spread of agriculture.
Neanderthal
Homo neanderthalensis, a distinct hominin population present across Europe and parts of western Asia from roughly 400,000 to 40,000 years ago.
Homo sapiens
The species of anatomically modern humans, which evolved in Africa and was securely present in Europe by more than 45,000 years ago, with proposed arrivals across a roughly 50,000-to-45,000-year range.
Interbreeding
Biological reproduction between Neanderthal and modern human populations, confirmed by ancient DNA analysis; most present-day populations with substantial ancestry outside Africa carry Neanderthal-derived DNA, and smaller amounts also occur in some African populations largely through later Eurasian back-migration.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from fossil remains or, in some cases, sediment, used to study the relationships, population history, and interbreeding of ancient hominin populations.
Mousterian
The Middle Paleolithic stone tool tradition associated principally with Neanderthal populations, characterized by the prepared-core Levallois technique.
Aurignacian
A major, widespread early Upper Paleolithic culture-complex associated with Homo sapiens in Europe, characterized by blade-based tools and early figurative art, though not the only technocomplex associated with the earliest modern human presence on the continent.
Gravettian
An Upper Paleolithic culture-complex following the Aurignacian, associated with widespread portable art including Venus figurines.
Solutrean
An Upper Paleolithic culture-complex associated with technically sophisticated pressure-flaked stone points, corresponding roughly to the period surrounding the Last Glacial Maximum in parts of western Europe.
Magdalenian
A later Upper Paleolithic culture-complex following the Last Glacial Maximum, associated with sophisticated bone and antler technology and elaborate cave art including Lascaux.
Lithic industry
A recognizable, recurring pattern of stone tool manufacture defined by characteristic techniques and tool forms.
Knapping
The technique of shaping stone tools through controlled percussion or pressure.
Hafting
The process of attaching a stone or bone tool to a wooden or bone handle, sometimes using adhesives such as birch-bark tar produced through heating or dry-distillation processes.
Faunal assemblage
The collection of animal bone remains recovered from an archaeological site, analyzed to reconstruct diet and hunting strategy.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered sediment deposits at an archaeological site, used to establish relative chronology.
Radiometric dating
A family of scientific dating techniques, including radiocarbon dating, that measure the decay of radioactive isotopes to estimate the age of organic or mineral material.
Glacial refugium
A region that retained more hospitable environmental conditions during a glacial period, allowing continued occupation by plant, animal, or human populations.
Parietal art
Art created on the fixed walls or ceilings of caves or rock shelters, as distinguished from portable art.
Portable art
Art created on movable objects, including carved figurines, engraved bone and antler, and personal ornaments.
Grave goods
Objects deliberately placed with a deceased individual at burial, interpreted by many archaeologists as evidence of belief regarding death or the status of the deceased.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to think seriously about a span of time so long that it resists ordinary historical intuition, well over a million years during which successive hominin populations inhabited the European landmass, adapting to environments that shifted repeatedly between bitter cold and relative warmth, long before anything resembling a village, a written language, or a political community existed anywhere on the continent. Understanding this chapter's subject matter honestly requires resisting two opposite temptations: dismissing this immense period as an undifferentiated "prehistoric" backdrop unworthy of the same careful attention given to later, textually documented history, and, in the opposite direction, projecting later categories of civilization, ethnicity, or nationhood onto populations who would not have recognized them.

The populations examined in this chapter, Neanderthals and modern humans alike, were neither simple nor primitive by any fair standard. They manufactured increasingly sophisticated tools, hunted large and dangerous game through coordinated effort, adapted their technology and behavior to dramatically shifting environmental conditions, created art of genuine technical and aesthetic accomplishment, and, in at least some times and places, treated their dead with evident care. That their social structures, languages, and beliefs remain only partially recoverable from the physical evidence they left behind is a limitation of the evidence, not a reflection of their capacities.

This chapter has also modeled a way of engaging with genuinely uncertain evidence that will recur throughout this textbook, though rarely again with quite this degree of uncertainty. Where the evidence supports confident conclusions, such as the existence of interbreeding between Neanderthals and modern humans demonstrated by ancient DNA, this chapter has stated so plainly. Where the evidence remains genuinely contested, such as the ultimate cause of Neanderthal disappearance or the meaning of Paleolithic art, this chapter has presented competing interpretations honestly rather than manufacturing false certainty.

Chapter 4 now turns to a transformation of a different order: the deliberate cultivation of plants and management of domesticated animals that began to spread into Europe from societies further east and south, beginning a process that would, over several thousand years, fundamentally reshape settlement, diet, social organization, and, eventually, nearly every dimension of human life on the continent. The hunter-gatherer populations examined in this chapter did not disappear overnight, and in some regions their way of life persisted for many centuries alongside or ahead of the spread of farming. Understanding the world they inhabited, and the human capacities for adaptation, technology, and symbolic expression they developed across more than a million years, provides the essential foundation for everything that follows in this textbook.
