Migration, Invasion, and the Transformation of the West

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

The western Roman world was not overrun by nations marching out of the forests. It was transformed by armed communities that crossed, served, bargained with, fought, and eventually settled within an empire whose own governments had spent generations recruiting, paying, and negotiating with them.

In the summer of 376 CE, according to the Roman historian Ammianus Marcellinus, a large and desperate crowd gathered on the northern bank of the lower Danube River. Its members were Gothic men, women, and children who had been driven from their homes by the westward advance of a people the Romans called Huns. Ammianus reports that they begged the emperor Valens for permission to cross into Roman territory, that Roman officials ferried them over the river by boats, rafts, and hollowed tree trunks, and that many drowned in the attempt. Ammianus was an experienced soldier, a Greek speaker writing in Latin, and a witness to much of the period he described, but he was also a Roman elite writer with firm assumptions about outsiders. His account is the starting point for this chapter, and it is also its first lesson: the movement that historians once called the "barbarian invasions" began, in this case, as a request that the Roman state granted.

This chapter examines what happened over the following eighty years, from the Danube crisis to the Vandal sack of Rome in 455. Its central problem is how migration, frontier warfare, Roman civil conflict, imperial diplomacy, and negotiated settlement transformed the western Roman Empire, and how mobile military coalitions became territorial political powers within the Roman world. The chapter follows Goths, Huns, Vandals, Alans, Suebi, Burgundians, and Franks, but it treats none of them as a fixed nation with a timeless identity. These were shifting political and military communities whose membership, leadership, and self-understanding changed as they moved, fought, and made agreements. They formed part of a Roman world that had long depended on recruits and allies from beyond its frontiers.

The chapter also insists on a distinction of subject. Chapter 26 examined the Christianization of the empire and the doctrinal conflicts that divided its churches. The present chapter takes up the parallel history of population movement, military service, and settlement. Chapter 28 will ask why the western imperial government itself ceased to function as an imperial regime, and Chapter 29 will examine the mature post-Roman kingdoms. The chronology of these chapters therefore overlaps, but their questions differ. Here the focus is on how the conditions arose from which those kingdoms would emerge, and the chapter deliberately stops around 455, before the deposition of the last western emperor in 476.

The evidence for the period is unusually rich in narrative and unusually treacherous in detail. Historians rely on Roman historians and chroniclers, on Christian bishops who wrote letters, sermons, and polemics in the midst of crisis, on imperial laws preserved in the Theodosian Code, on court poetry, on administrative lists such as the Notitia Dignitatum, on coins and hoards, and on archaeology of settlements, cemeteries, and fortifications. Almost all the surviving narrative sources were written by Romans or by writers in Roman traditions, and many were composed long after the events they describe. The Gothic, Vandal, Hunnic, and Frankish participants left few writings of their own in this period, so their perspectives must often be inferred from Roman accounts written with other purposes.

The transformation examined here mattered for every later part of the textbook. Groups that were still mobile armies in the 410s and 420s were, by the 450s, settled in southwestern Gaul, in Iberia, in North Africa, and along the Rhine. The western imperial court had moved to Ravenna, the Mediterranean grain supply of the west was under threat, and Britain had slipped beyond effective imperial administration. At the same time, Roman law, Roman aristocracies, Roman cities, and Roman bishops remained central to the lives of the people who lived through these decades. The story is neither one of simple destruction nor of painless assimilation, and it should be read as the history of a political landscape being rebuilt in the presence of violence, negotiation, and profound uncertainty.

Within Part IV, this chapter supplies the military and demographic side of the transformation of Rome, between the religious and institutional developments of Chapter 26 and the fragmentation of imperial authority in Chapter 28. Within Volume I, it stands near the end of a long ancient narrative that began with prehistoric Europe, and it prepares the transition to the medieval world of Volume II. Students should not read it as the moment "antiquity ended," since the eastern Roman Empire continued, as Chapter 36 will show, and Roman institutions persisted across the west, but as the period in which the political geography of the western Mediterranean and Atlantic Europe was decisively reorganized.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the late Roman frontiers should be understood as zones of interaction, recruitment, and diplomacy rather than impermeable walls.
  2. Trace the sequence from the Danube crisis of 376 through Adrianople, the settlement of 382, and the careers of Alaric and Stilicho, distinguishing Roman decisions from Gothic ones.
  3. Analyze the causes of the Gothic revolt of 376 to 378, including Hunnic expansion, Roman administrative failure, and the actions of Gothic leaders.
  4. Assess the military and political significance of Adrianople without accepting the older claim that it marked the beginning of the fall of Rome.
  5. Evaluate the concept of Roman accommodation, including the uncertain terms of the 382 settlement and the problems of interpreting foederati, hospitalitas, and land division.
  6. Interpret the sack of Rome in 410 as both a limited physical event and an enormous symbolic shock, using contemporary Christian and pagan reactions.
  7. Trace the movements of Vandals, Alans, and Suebi from the Rhine to Iberia and North Africa, and explain why the loss of Carthage in 439 mattered to the western government.
  8. Compare the trajectories of Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, Franks, and Huns as different outcomes of interaction with Roman power.
  9. Contextualize the careers of Stilicho and Aetius to show why "Roman versus barbarian" is an inadequate description of fifth century politics.
  10. Describe how migration, warfare, and settlement affected civilians, including captives, refugees, landholders, and city populations.
  11. Distinguish among migration, invasion, and transformation as historical interpretations and evaluate how historians combine them.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did long standing Roman practices of recruitment, subsidy, and settlement shape what happened when large groups of outsiders entered the empire after 376?
  2. Was the crisis of 376 to 378 primarily a barbarian invasion, a refugee crisis, or a failure of Roman administration, and what evidence supports each description?
  3. How did Roman civil wars and competition among commanders make it harder for imperial governments to control armed groups inside the empire?
  4. Why did some armed communities, such as the Visigoths and Vandals, become durable territorial powers while others, such as the Huns, did not?
  5. What do the careers of Stilicho and Aetius reveal about the relationship between ethnic background and political allegiance in the late Roman world?
  6. How do historians use sources written by Roman elites, Christian bishops, and later chroniclers to reconstruct the actions and identities of groups that left few records of their own?
  7. How did ordinary provincial residents experience billeting, taxation, refugee movement, capture, ransom, and changing military authority?
  8. In what sense were the successor powers of c. 455 products of Roman institutions as well as challengers to Roman rule?

Historical Context

By the mid fourth century, the Roman Empire had been reorganized by the reforms examined in Chapter 25. Diocletian and Constantine had created a larger, more bureaucratic state with separate civil and military hierarchies, a tetrarchic tradition of shared rule, and, after 330, a second imperial capital at Constantinople. The empire was normally governed by more than one emperor, and by the 360s the division of the imperial college between eastern and western halves had become a regular administrative arrangement, though the empire was still conceived as legally one. When Valentinian I took power in 364, he appointed his brother Valens to rule the east, and the pattern of a western emperor and an eastern emperor, with their own courts, armies, and priorities, became the operating reality of Roman government. Chapter 24 explained how the third century crisis had strained the empire's finances and military structure. The fourth century had restored order, but at the price of heavy taxation, an expanded army, and an increasingly powerful cadre of military officers.

The army was central to this new order. It was divided broadly between frontier troops, who garrisoned the Rhine and Danube limits, and mobile field armies under commanders called magistri militum, "masters of the soldiers," who followed the emperor or were stationed at strategic points. Recruits came from the empire's own provinces, but also, increasingly, from peoples beyond the frontiers or from communities settled within them. This was not a new development. Rome had recruited auxiliary soldiers from outside its formal citizen body for centuries, and by the fourth century soldiers and officers of Frankish, Alamannic, Gothic, Sarmatian, and other origin held high commands. The empire's dependence on such manpower was a structural feature of late Roman government and not a sign of its decline.

Beyond the Rhine and Danube lay societies that the Romans knew through trade, diplomacy, and periodic war. North of the lower Danube, communities associated with the archaeological Sântana de Mureș and Chernyakhov cultures practiced farming and craft production and maintained substantial contact with the Roman provinces. Roman writers referred to Gothic groups there, and Christianity was already spreading among them, including through the missionary bishop Wulfila, who translated portions of Scripture into Gothic in the fourth century. Along the Rhine, Frankish and Alamannic communities traded with, raided, and served Rome. To the east and north of these zones the world beyond the frontier extended across the steppe corridor described in Chapter 2, from which, in the later fourth century, the Huns emerged into Roman awareness. Chapter 6 examined the diversity of Iron Age peoples in these regions and cautioned against projecting later national identities backward. That caution applies even more forcefully to the fourth and fifth centuries, when the political communities that Roman writers named were actively changing.

Religion added a further dimension. The Christianization of the empire, examined in Chapters 23 through 26, had produced serious doctrinal divisions. Valens supported the Homoian party, which rejected the language of the Council of Nicaea, whereas Theodosius I, who succeeded in the east after Adrianople, would embrace Nicene orthodoxy. Gothic communities converted through Roman channels in the fourth century received the Homoian form of Christianity then dominant in the eastern court, and this religious difference later became a marker of identity separating some successor rulers from their Nicene subjects. Christian bishops, meanwhile, became prominent figures in local crisis management, and the ransoming of captives, the feeding of refugees, and the negotiation with military commanders became part of episcopal life.

This chapter relies on evidence quite different from that used in the geographical and prehistoric chapters of Part I. The principal narrative source for the years 376 to 378 is Ammianus Marcellinus, whose surviving history ends with Adrianople. For the fifth century, historians draw on Olympiodorus of Thebes, preserved in fragments, on the pagan historian Zosimus, on the Christian historian Orosius, on chroniclers such as Prosper of Aquitaine and Hydatius, on the fragmentary history of Priscus of Panium, who visited Attila's court, and on the sixth century Gothic history of Jordanes, who used earlier material now lost. Bishops such as Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Salvian, and Victor of Vita provide sermons, letters, and polemic. Imperial laws survive in the Theodosian Code, compiled in 438. Archaeology, including coin distribution, fortifications, burials, and settlement patterns, offers independent evidence, though it seldom identifies an ethnic community with certainty. Every one of these sources requires careful attention to its author, audience, and date.

Section 27.1

Rome and the Peoples Beyond the Frontiers

The older narrative of the "barbarian invasions" imagined a sharp line between Roman civilization and the peoples beyond it, a line that collapsed under external pressure. Modern scholarship has replaced that image with a picture of frontier zones, meaning broad regions on both sides of the Rhine and Danube in which soldiers, merchants, diplomats, migrants, captives, refugees, and information moved constantly. The Latin word limes is often translated as "frontier," and its ancient usage ranged across several senses, from a surveyed boundary line or road to a fortified military zone, senses that developed and shifted over centuries of Roman administration; modern historians frequently use it more broadly still, to describe an entire frontier system of forts, roads, and supervised crossing points rather than a single fixed line. By the fourth century, the fortified river frontiers were sites of exchange as well as defense. Roman coins, pottery, and metalwork appear well beyond the frontier, and objects and practices from beyond it appear within the provinces. Chapter 2 emphasized that the Rhine and Danube were both barriers and connectors, and the fourth century shows this dual function very clearly.

Rome's relations with outside communities took many forms. Emperors paid subsidies to allied rulers, granted Roman titles, exchanged hostages, and supervised markets at which cross frontier trade was permitted. They recruited soldiers from beyond the frontier and settled defeated or allied groups inside the provinces. Earlier emperors had settled Marcomanni, Sarmatians, and others within the empire, and the emperor Julian, in 358, allowed a Frankish group called the Salians to remain in territory in the Rhine delta region in return for obligations. Communities of laeti, whose precise legal status is debated, were settled in parts of Gaul and Italy with military duties. In 369, Valens negotiated a treaty with the Gothic leader Athanaric, reportedly meeting him on boats in the middle of the Danube, apparently because Athanaric had sworn never to set foot on Roman soil. The image is a useful one: the two leaders treated as diplomatic equals in a space that belonged to neither.

What Roman authors did with these relationships was often rhetorical. The Latin word barbarus and its Greek equivalent carried a range of meanings, from simple foreignness to moral and cultural inferiority. Writers inherited ethnographic conventions from earlier authors, notably Tacitus, whose Germania of about 98 CE imagined the peoples beyond the Rhine as rugged, warlike, and simple. Late Roman writers used such categories to praise or condemn, to explain Roman defeats, and to reassure readers about the boundaries of civilization. The vocabulary box below explains the terms most likely to mislead students. It is essential to recognize that these categories describe how Romans wrote about outsiders, not how outsiders described themselves.

North of the lower Danube, Roman authors identified major Gothic political groupings, chiefly the Tervingi and the Greuthungi. The relationship of these fourth century groups to the later Visigoths and Ostrogoths is complicated and should not be reduced to simple equations. The kingdoms that historians eventually call Visigothic and Ostrogothic were formed by later political processes, in which survivors of different groups, Roman military office, new leadership, and shared success in war all played a part. Along the Rhine, Frankish and Alamannic groups likewise formed and reformed around leaders, shared traditions, and opportunities for pay and plunder. Migration period identities, in short, were made as well as inherited, a principle that will recur through this chapter and through Chapters 28 to 30.

Historical Vocabulary
"Barbarian," "Germanic," Tribe, and People

Barbarus (Latin) and its Greek equivalent barbaros named outsiders, applied to communities with very different languages, religions, and political systems. Neither word described a single race or civilization, and both were often used as terms of contrast, to define Roman or Greco-Roman order by what it excluded. Some Romans, including senior army officers, were themselves of "barbarian" descent and were understood as fully Roman in office. This textbook uses the word only when discussing how Roman and Greek authors thought and wrote.

Germanic is a modern linguistic and historical convenience. It groups languages descended from a common ancestor, but it does not imply that Goths, Vandals, Franks, Burgundians, and others shared a political identity, a religion, or a sense of common kinship. Applying "Germanic" to political communities as though it named a unified people encourages the error that "the Germans" invaded Rome.

Tribe is a similarly misleading word. It suggests a timeless, biologically descended community, whereas the groups discussed here often formed around military leaders, dynastic prestige, shared traditions, success in war, and the incorporation of outsiders. This textbook uses "people," "group," "community," or "coalition," and it explains the basis of a group's cohesion when the evidence allows. Phrases such as "the Germanic tribes poured into Rome" should be avoided altogether.

Section 27.2

Goths, Huns, and the Danube Crisis of 376

The immediate trigger of the crisis of 376 was the westward movement of the Huns. Ammianus offers a vivid, hostile description of them and reports that they attacked the Alans and then defeated the Gothic ruler Ermanaric, whose polity north of the Black Sea broke apart. The Hunnic origins and their relationship to earlier steppe peoples are debated, and the archaeological evidence for the Huns of this period is thin, so much of what is known about them comes from Roman writers who described them in the language of alarm. What is clear is that Hunnic military strength, based on mounted archery and steppe mobility, disrupted the political order north of the Danube. It is also clear that the Huns were not a single, unified horde: the sources show groups that fought, allied, and negotiated in changing combinations.

Gothic responses differed. Athanaric, the leader of some Tervingi, tried to resist and then withdrew into the mountains. Others, under leaders Ammianus names as Fritigern and Alavivus, approached the Danube and petitioned Valens, who was at Antioch dealing with the Persian frontier, for permission to enter. Ammianus reports that they promised military service, and that the emperor's advisers saw advantages, including a potential supply of recruits and the possibility of collecting money in lieu of recruits from the provincial population. Valens agreed to admit at least part of the group, and Roman officials organized a crossing that Ammianus describes as chaotic. A separate Gothic group, the Greuthungi under Alatheus and Saphrax, also appeared at the river, and Ammianus indicates that they crossed without permission.

The next stage was a Roman administrative failure. Ammianus states that the Goths were supposed to be disarmed, but that officials accepted bribes and allowed many to retain weapons. Supplies were inadequate, and the Roman commanders at the Danube, Lupicinus and Maximus, are portrayed as profiting from the newcomers' hunger, selling them food at exploitative prices, and exchanging dogs for Gothic children as slaves, according to Ammianus's report. Such details come from a hostile Roman source, and they may exaggerate, but the underlying picture of exploitation is plausible and consistent with the wider evidence of corruption in fourth century military supply. The crisis came to a head at Marcianople, where Lupicinus invited Gothic leaders to a banquet and, when a disturbance broke out in the city, attempted to kill their escort. Fritigern escaped, and the Goths revolted.

This sequence should shape how students think about 376. The Gothic group led by Fritigern and Alavivus did not begin as invaders. They were refugees seeking admission under negotiated conditions, and Rome had the capacity to accept them. The Greuthungi under Alatheus and Saphrax present a somewhat different case, since Ammianus indicates that they crossed the river without Roman authorization once the precedent had been set, a distinction worth preserving rather than treating "the Goths" as a single undifferentiated group making a single negotiated request. What turned a manageable movement into a military crisis was a combination of scale, poor supervision, the greed of local officials, the emperor's absence, and the retention of weapons and independent leadership by the groups now inside Roman territory. That does not remove Gothic agency, since Fritigern and other leaders chose to revolt and to press their advantage. It does show that the decisive early events were substantially shaped by Roman decisions, and that the empire's response to migration depended heavily on the honesty and competence of its officials.

Religion was present in the background. The Gothic Christians associated with Wulfila had received their faith in the Homoian form favored at the eastern court, and Fritigern's followers, according to later church historians, accepted Valens's version of Christianity in the context of the negotiation. The evidence is thin, and the extent of conversion is uncertain, but the connection anticipates a pattern that will matter later. Gothic elites who took their Christianity from the imperial court of the 370s retained it after the doctrinal settlement of the 380s, as Chapter 26 explained, and Homoian Christianity became a distinguishing feature of several successor elites.

Section 27.3

Adrianople, 378, and the Limits of Roman Military Power

After the revolt at Marcianople, fighting spread across Thrace and the Balkan provinces. In 377 Roman forces fought an indecisive battle at a place called Ad Salices, and the Goths, joined by Alans, Huns, and dissatisfied Roman subjects including some miners and slaves, ravaged large areas. Roman forces could contain but not destroy them. Valens, having concluded an arrangement with Persia, returned to Constantinople in 378 and prepared to lead an army against the Goths. His nephew, the western emperor Gratian, marched to help but was delayed by campaigning on the Rhine. Valens, according to Ammianus, was concerned that Gratian would receive credit for a victory, and was misled by a report of the Gothic numbers.

On 9 August 378, near Adrianople in Thrace, the Roman army attacked Gothic forces during the heat of the day, after marching some distance. The battle, as Ammianus tells it, was confused and disastrous. Gothic cavalry, whose return to the battlefield contributed to the collapse of the Roman left wing, joined the fighting, and the Roman infantry was compressed and slaughtered. Valens was killed, and his body was not found, according to Ammianus, who compared the disaster to Cannae. Ancient sources give figures suggesting that a very large portion of the army was lost, and Ammianus says that about two thirds perished. Modern estimates of the size of Valens's army are considerably more modest than older totals, and the precise numbers remain uncertain. What is clear is that a major eastern field army and many of its officers were destroyed.

Older textbooks described Adrianople as the beginning of the end of Rome, and as the moment when cavalry replaced infantry. Both claims are misleading. The Roman Empire continued to field large armies after 378, and it defeated significant enemies in the following decades. The eastern empire survived for more than a thousand years, as Chapters 36 to 41 will show. Adrianople mattered because it destroyed an army and killed an emperor, because it revealed the danger of large armed groups within imperial territory, because the Roman response to it produced the settlement of 382, and because it deepened the government's dependence on military recruitment and on the political management of armed groups. It is best understood as a serious defeat with important consequences rather than as a single cause of the empire's transformation.

The aftermath included further violence and, in some places, atrocity. Ammianus reports that Roman commanders in Asia Minor, alarmed at the number of Gothic soldiers stationed in their cities, ordered a mass killing of them. The Gothic wars devastated the Balkan provinces and produced a wave of captives. The bishop Ambrose of Milan, according to his own writings, broke up church plate to ransom prisoners, an act that anticipates the role of Christian communities in the later violence of the fifth century. In January 379, Gratian appointed Theodosius, an experienced commander from Spain, as emperor of the east. Theodosius spent the next years attempting to contain the Gothic crisis by a combination of force and diplomacy.

Primary Source Window

Ammianus Marcellinus, Res Gestae, Book 31, on the Gothic Crisis of 376 to 378

Creator: Ammianus Marcellinus, a Greek speaking Roman officer and historian who wrote in Latin, composing his history in the late fourth century, probably completing it in the 390s · Type: Narrative history, the fullest contemporary account of the crisis

Context: Ammianus's Res Gestae continued the history of Rome from the emperor Nerva, and the surviving books cover the years 353 to 378. Book 31 begins with the arrival of the Huns and ends with Adrianople. Ammianus had served in the army earlier in his life and had access to officers and eyewitnesses, though he was not present at the crossing or at Adrianople itself. He wrote as a Roman elite author steeped in classical models, with strong views about the moral and military qualities of soldiers and emperors.

Paraphrase of Ammianus, Res Gestae 31.3 to 31.4 and 31.12 to 31.13 Ammianus reports that the Goths, terrified by the Huns, appealed to Valens for admission and promised to provide soldiers. He says that the emperor's courtiers welcomed the prospect, imagining a large new army and additional revenue, and that officials found the count of arrivals impossible to complete. He then describes the corruption of the Roman commanders, their exploitation of Gothic hunger, and the disorder that led to war. Ammianus's narrative of Adrianople stresses Valens's impatience, poor scouting, and the collapse of the Roman line, and he closes his history with a call to later writers to continue the story. This is a paraphrase and synthesis, not a quotation.

Analysis: Ammianus reveals how a Roman insider explained the disaster. He blames Roman officials, and he criticizes Valens's decisions, while also depicting the Goths and Huns with familiar ethnographic hostility. His account is invaluable for chronology and for the sequence of events, and it shows the Roman government's ambivalence, which mixed opportunism, contempt, and improvisation. Its limitations are equally important. Ammianus did not speak with Gothic leaders, so the Gothic reasons for revolt, and the internal politics of Fritigern's followers, are presented only through Roman eyes. His numbers are approximate and rhetorical, and his narrative shape, which follows a tragic arc from moral failure to catastrophe, may impose more coherence than the events had at the time. The voices of the women, children, and dependents who crossed the river are almost entirely missing.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Ammianus say about the causes of the crisis, and whom does he blame?
  2. Who created this source, and for what possible purposes might he have written it?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Roman attitudes toward migration and toward the Goths?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, and what kinds of additional evidence would help?
Section 27.4

The Settlement of 382 and Roman Accommodation

Theodosius could not immediately defeat the Goths in the field. Over several years he combined limited military successes, diplomacy, and inducements. Some Gothic groups were settled in Pannonia under Gratian's authority around 380, according to later sources. In 381 Athanaric, who had earlier resisted Roman entry, came to Constantinople, was received with honors, and died soon afterward, and his people were folded into Roman service. On 3 October 382, according to a chronicle record, an agreement was concluded with Gothic forces in the Balkan provinces. Themistius, an orator and philosopher at the eastern court, delivered a speech in which he presented the settlement as a triumph of Roman generosity and statecraft, a piece of imperial propaganda that deserves careful reading alongside the events it celebrates.

The exact terms of the 382 agreement are poorly documented, and students should not assume more detail than the sources support. The Goths were settled within the empire, apparently in the provinces south of the Danube, and were expected to provide military service. They retained a degree of internal cohesion and leadership that distinguished them from smaller groups absorbed into the regular army. Historians debate how exceptional the arrangement was, whether it created formally autonomous Gothic communities, whether it involved land grants or fiscal arrangements, and whether contemporaries regarded it as a novelty or as an extension of earlier practice. Older textbooks sometimes presented 382 as the creation of a Gothic "state within the state," but the evidence does not support that degree of clarity.

The broader significance lies in what the settlement reveals about Roman strategy. Roman governments did not merely fight or submit. They recruited, settled, paid, granted titles, recognized leaders, exchanged hostages, played one group against another, and integrated commanders into imperial politics. The 382 arrangement was an attempt to turn dangerous armed migrants into useful imperial manpower. Gothic soldiers served Theodosius in his civil wars, notably at the Frigidus River in 394, where he defeated the western usurper Eugenius and his Frankish commander Arbogast. At that battle the two armies were both multiethnic, which is a reminder that the neat contrast between Romans and non-Romans in the sources often collapses when we examine who actually served and fought. Later tradition associates the young Alaric with Gothic forces serving Theodosius at the Frigidus; whatever his exact position there, he emerged soon afterward as the leader of a substantial Gothic army in the Balkans.

What became increasingly consequential over the following decades was not the principle of settlement but the scale and cohesion of some settled forces, and the weakness of the political structure that was supposed to control them. When emperors were strong, such forces were useful. When emperors died, when civil wars broke out, or when court factions competed, armed groups with their own leaders and expectations could act as autonomous political players. That vulnerability, rather than the mere presence of outsiders, is the key to the transformation examined in the rest of this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Foederati

Foederati (singular foederatus), from the Latin foedus, "treaty," describes allies bound to Rome by agreement. In earlier centuries the term was applied to independent allied communities. In the fourth and fifth centuries it came to be used for groups of soldiers, usually under their own leaders, who served Rome under negotiated arrangements, receiving pay, supplies, or land in return. The precise meaning of the word varied by decade and by author, and it should not be treated as a single, rigid institutional category.

Students should avoid the simplified picture in which barbarians sign a treaty, receive a third of Roman land, and become a kingdom. The mechanisms of settlement differed from place to place and from decade to decade. Federate forces were not "foreign armies" fully independent of Rome, since they operated within Roman politics, sought Roman titles, and were often used by Roman commanders against other Romans. Yet they also retained leaders and loyalties of their own, and the tension between those two facts produced much of the political drama of the fifth century.

Section 27.5

Alaric, Stilicho, and Roman Civil Politics

Theodosius I died in 395, dividing the empire between his young sons, Arcadius in the east and Honorius in the west. Real power passed to court officials and generals, and the western government came to be dominated by Flavius Stilicho, a senior commander whose father, according to Roman sources, was of Vandal origin and who had married into the Theodosian dynasty. Stilicho's career itself undermines any simple contrast between Romans and outsiders. He held the highest Roman military office, commanded armies drawn from many backgrounds, was regent for the young Honorius, and presented himself, and was largely accepted, as a defender of Roman order.

In this same period, Alaric's demands recurred with a striking consistency across a decade and a half: official Roman military rank, regular pay and supplies for his soldiers, and a secure place of settlement. He moved between eastern and western territory, sometimes negotiating with Constantinople, sometimes with Ravenna, and sometimes fighting against Roman forces when negotiations failed. Around 397, Alaric appears to have secured a Roman military command in Illyricum from the eastern court, an appointment that, if the sources are read correctly, gave his Gothic army formal standing within the Roman military hierarchy. His invasion of Italy in 401 to 402 was met by Stilicho, and further campaigning and negotiation followed for years, with Alaric's price rising and falling according to the balance of power between the eastern and western courts and within the western court itself.

This pattern should not be read simply as extortion by an external enemy. Alaric's army needed to be paid and fed like any other Roman force, and his repeated requests for office and settlement suggest that his goal was inclusion within the Roman system on favorable terms rather than its destruction. At the same time, his willingness to use force when negotiations stalled shows that he commanded genuine independent power. The relationship between Alaric and the western court was therefore neither simple loyalty nor simple hostility, but a continuing negotiation conducted, at times, at the point of a sword.

The crisis deepened after 405 to 406, when a large mixed force under a Gothic leader named Radagaisus invaded Italy and was defeated by Stilicho, who incorporated many of the survivors into his own forces. Stilicho's political position, however, was increasingly precarious. He faced opposition from a faction at the western court that resented his power and his negotiations with Alaric, and that exploited widespread suspicion of soldiers of non-Roman background. In 408, following the death of Arcadius in the east and rising court intrigue, Stilicho was arrested and executed on the orders of Honorius. In the aftermath, Roman soldiers in Italy massacred the families of federate soldiers serving under Stilicho's command, according to the historian Zosimus, and many of the surviving soldiers defected to Alaric. The execution of Stilicho removed the one figure capable of managing the relationship with Alaric and triggered a further collapse of Roman military cohesion in Italy.

Section 27.6

The Sack of Rome, 410

With Stilicho dead and his own army swollen by defectors, Alaric marched on Rome itself, besieging the city three times between 408 and 410. During the negotiations he proposed, and the Roman Senate briefly accepted, a rival emperor, Priscus Attalus, in an attempt to force Honorius, secure in the marshes of Ravenna, to grant Alaric's demands. When negotiations with Honorius's court again failed, Alaric's forces entered Rome on 24 August 410, apparently admitted through the Salarian Gate, and sacked the city for about three days.

The sack involved looting of private and public property, the burning of some buildings, the killing of some inhabitants, and the taking of captives, including, according to later tradition, members of the imperial family such as Galla Placidia, the half sister of Honorius, who was later married to Alaric's successor Athaulf. Ancient accounts differ in their emphasis. Some Christian writers noted that churches were respected as places of sanctuary, at least in part, because Alaric's Goths were themselves Christians, of the Homoian confession. The physical damage, while real, was limited compared to later, more destructive sacks of cities in this period, and Rome's population and institutions continued to function afterward. The city was not the seat of imperial government at the time, since emperors resided chiefly at Milan, and after 402, at Ravenna, so the sack did not paralyze imperial administration in the way a capture of Ravenna or Milan might have.

The event's importance was chiefly symbolic and psychological. Rome had not been captured by a foreign force since the Gauls under Brennus, according to Roman tradition, centuries earlier, and its fall, even briefly and incompletely, shocked contemporaries across the Mediterranean world. Jerome, then living in Bethlehem, wrote of his grief and of interrupted work, describing the city that had conquered the world as itself conquered. Pagan critics argued that the abandonment of the old gods had brought divine punishment upon Rome, a charge that prompted Augustine of Hippo to begin composing his enormous work The City of God, in which he argued that the eternal city of God was distinct from any earthly city, including Rome, and that Rome's history included calamity long before Christianity. The debate over the meaning of 410 extended the Christian intellectual and social transformations examined in Chapter 26 into the fifth century.

Alaric died later in 410, and his successor Athaulf led the Gothic army out of Italy into southern Gaul and then into Iberia, still seeking a durable settlement within the Roman system. His marriage to Galla Placidia in 414, and reports that he initially hoped to replace the Roman Empire with a Gothic one before concluding that Gothic strength could better preserve than destroy Roman institutions, are recorded by the historian Orosius, writing shortly afterward, though his account should be read as reflecting Orosius's own providential interpretation of history as well as any statement Athaulf may have made. After further fighting and negotiation, the Gothic forces, under Athaulf's eventual successor Wallia, reached an agreement with the western government in 418 and were settled in Aquitaine in southwestern Gaul, a territorial base from which the kingdom later called Visigothic would develop, a story that Chapter 29 will continue.

Section 27.7

The Rhine Crossing, Gaul, and Britain

While the Gothic drama unfolded in Italy, a separate and equally consequential crisis developed on the Rhine. On the traditional date of 31 December 406, though the precise date has been questioned by some historians, groups identified by Roman sources as Vandals, Alans, and Suebi crossed the Rhine into Gaul, apparently near Mainz. The old image of a frozen river crossed by a wall of invaders is not securely established in the sources and should be treated with caution. The crossing occurred amid weakened Rhine defenses, since Stilicho had withdrawn troops for campaigns in Italy against Alaric and against Radagaisus, and it took advantage of political instability rather than a single dramatic natural event.

The movement through Gaul was neither a single column nor an orderly migration. Roman sources describe fighting, plundering, and negotiation as the groups moved through the Gallic provinces over the following two years, encountering Frankish forces allied with Rome near the Rhine and eventually reaching the Pyrenees. Meanwhile, Roman civil order in the northwest collapsed further. In 407 the army in Britain proclaimed a soldier named Constantine as emperor, a common pattern of usurpation encouraged by frontier crisis and imperial weakness. Constantine III crossed to Gaul, established control over much of the region, and was recognized for a time by Honorius before being defeated and executed in 411.

Britain's experience in these years illustrates the broader pattern of the chapter more cautiously than a sudden invasion narrative would suggest. With troops removed for continental campaigns and usurpations, and with the western government preoccupied by the crises in Italy and Gaul, Roman administration in Britain weakened considerably in this period. A passage in the historian Zosimus reports that around 410 the emperor Honorius, in a communication concerning defense, told certain communities to look to their own protection, though historians disagree over whether this passage actually concerns Britain, over its precise meaning, and over the broader chronology of the island's separation from imperial government. By the early fifth century, direct imperial administration in Britain had sharply weakened, and the island ceased to be securely integrated into the western imperial system, but the precise chronology and character of that break remain debated, and the island's subsequent transformation, involving migration, settlement, and the emergence of new political communities, belongs to Chapter 29 rather than to the present chapter.

The Rhine crossing also affected Gaul's internal life in ways the chapter's later section on everyday life will examine further. Landholders faced billeting, requisition, and, in places, the breakdown of tax collection. A movement of rural insurgents known as the Bagaudae, active intermittently in Gaul and Iberia across the fourth and fifth centuries, has sometimes been interpreted as a peasant uprising against oppressive taxation and landlord power, though the evidence is fragmentary and historians debate how organized or ideologically coherent these movements were. Their recurrence in this period nonetheless suggests that provincial society was under considerable strain from war, taxation, and administrative breakdown, independent of any single ethnic migration.

Section 27.8

Vandals, Alans, and Suebi: Gaul, Iberia, North Africa

In late 409, amid the civil war between Constantine III, Gerontius, and other Roman claimants, the Vandal, Alan, and Suebic groups that had crossed the Rhine entered Iberia through Pyrenean defenses that had broken down. Ancient and modern accounts differ over whether Roman commanders facilitated their entry, but the surviving evidence does not securely establish a formal invitation by Gerontius, the Roman commander who had rebelled against Constantine III in the region. By about 411, according to the chronicler Hydatius, the groups had divided areas of the peninsula's provinces among themselves, with the Suebi and one Vandal group, the Hasdingi, taking the northwest, the Alans taking the central provinces, and another Vandal group, the Silingi, taking the south.

The Roman government at Ravenna, once it had settled the Gothic forces in Aquitaine in 418, used its new Gothic allies to campaign against the groups in Iberia. Gothic forces under Wallia campaigned in Iberia for the western government between 416 and 418, inflicting severe defeats on the Silingi Vandals and the Alans; by 418 the Alan position had been shattered and survivors attached themselves to the Hasdingi Vandal king. Under a new leader, Gaiseric, who came to power in 428, the combined Vandal and Alan forces then moved south. In 429, Gaiseric led them across the Strait of Gibraltar into North Africa, a decision that transformed the trajectory of the group entirely. Roman Africa, comprising the wealthy provinces around Carthage, was one of the most valuable regions of the western empire, supplying grain to Rome and substantial tax revenue to the imperial treasury.

Roman resistance in Africa was hampered by the ongoing rivalry between the western court and its senior commanders, notably a conflict between the general Boniface, who governed Africa, and Aetius, the rising commander at Ravenna. Later sources accused Boniface of inviting the Vandals into Africa to strengthen his position against Aetius, but the charge is disputed among historians and may reflect a hostile later reconstruction of the political crisis surrounding Boniface and the western court rather than a securely documented fact. Gaiseric's forces besieged and captured several cities, including Hippo Regius, where the bishop Augustine died during the siege in 430, an event that closed one of the most significant intellectual careers of the age at the very moment the western Mediterranean's political order was being reshaped. In 435 a treaty recognized Vandal control of substantial African territory, but Gaiseric broke it in 439 and captured Carthage itself, the second city of the western empire and the administrative and commercial center of Roman Africa.

The capture of Carthage removed some of the western government's richest taxable territory and placed a crucial grain-producing region and its distribution networks under Vandal control. Gaiseric also built a substantial fleet, using Carthage's harbor and shipyards, which allowed Vandal forces to raid Sicily, Sardinia, and the Italian coast, and which culminated, as later sections of this chapter and Chapter 28 will discuss, in the Vandal sack of Rome itself in 455. In 441 to 442, an eastern Roman expeditionary force assembled in Sicily for an intended attack on Vandal-held North Africa, but Hunnic pressure in the Balkans forced the eastern government to redirect its forces, and the operation was abandoned. A treaty in 442 then recognized Vandal control of the richest African provinces while restoring some less valuable territory to Roman administration. The Vandal kingdom in Africa proved to be one of the most durable successor powers of the fifth century western Mediterranean and survived until the eastern Roman reconquest of 533 to 534; its mature history lies beyond this chapter's main chronology.

The word "vandalism," meaning wanton destruction, entered European languages many centuries later, coined in the era of the French Revolution to describe destruction of art and monuments, and it drew on a reputation for the Vandals as uniquely destructive that owes more to later polemic than to a careful reading of the fifth century evidence. The sack of Carthage and, later, of Rome in 455 did involve plunder, and Vandal rule in Africa included episodes of coercion and persecution directed against Nicene clergy, but the modern stereotype of senseless vandalism should be recognized as a later construction rather than an accurate description of Gaiseric's calculated pursuit of territory, resources, and naval power.

Section 27.9

Franks, Burgundians, and Northern Gaul

The Franks illustrate accommodation more clearly than sudden invasion. Frankish communities along the lower and middle Rhine had interacted with Rome for generations, through trade, raiding, and, especially, military recruitment. Frankish officers served at the highest levels of the Roman army in the fourth century, including Bauto and Arbogast, the latter a dominant figure at the western court in the early 390s who supported the usurper Eugenius against Theodosius and was defeated at the Frigidus in 394, discussed above. This career, like Stilicho's, shows that Frankish origin was compatible with the highest Roman command, and that Roman civil war, not simple ethnic hostility, often decided which side a commander of any background supported.

Through the early fifth century, Frankish groups along the Rhine expanded their territory and independence as Roman authority in the region weakened, particularly after the 406 to 407 crisis drew Roman attention and resources elsewhere. Some Frankish leaders continued to hold Roman military titles and to cooperate with Roman commanders against other threats, including, at times, against the Vandals, Alans, and Suebi during their passage through Gaul. Other Frankish groups fought against Roman forces or against each other. By the middle of the fifth century, Frankish power was becoming an increasingly important regional factor in northern Gaul, though it had not yet produced the unified kingdom that would emerge under Clovis in the last decades of the century, a story reserved for Chapter 30.

The Burgundians present a related but distinct case. Settled originally in the Rhine borderland, a Burgundian kingdom under a king named Gundahar attempted to expand its territory westward in the 430s, an ambition that brought it into direct conflict with Aetius, the dominant western commander of the period. In 436, Aetius employed Hunnic mercenaries to crush the Burgundian kingdom, an episode of such severity that it later contributed, through a long process of legendary transformation, to the medieval Germanic epic tradition, including elements later reflected in the Nibelung stories, although that later legendary material should not be mistaken for a historical account of the events themselves. Following this destruction, Roman authorities resettled Burgundian survivors around 443 in the region of Sapaudia, in what is now western Switzerland and eastern France, where they were expected to assist in the defense of the area and where a new, more durable Burgundian kingdom developed.

The Burgundian case demonstrates a recurring pattern in this chapter: Roman power did not simply confront or accept outside groups, but used force, alliance, and resettlement together, often within a period of only a few years, to manage the same community. A group that Rome destroyed as a military threat in 436 became, only seven years later, a resettled ally intended to help secure Roman territory. This flexibility reflects both the western government's declining capacity to control its frontier zones directly and its continued ability to use diplomacy and negotiated settlement as tools of governance, even in a period of severe fiscal and military strain.

Section 27.10

Huns, Aetius, and the Politics of Imperial Survival

The Huns, whose westward expansion had helped trigger the crisis of 376, occupied a shifting position in Roman politics across the following decades. In the earlier fifth century, Hunnic groups sometimes served as Roman allies and mercenaries, most notably in the service of Aetius, who as a young man had spent time as a hostage among the Huns and who maintained close personal and diplomatic ties with their leadership throughout his career. Aetius used Hunnic forces to support his own political position in western civil conflicts during the 420s and 430s, and, as already noted, to destroy the Burgundian kingdom on the Rhine in 436. This dependence illustrates, once again, that the boundary between Roman ally and Roman adversary was porous and shifted according to immediate political interest rather than fixed ethnic loyalty.

Under the leadership of Attila, who became sole ruler of the Huns after eliminating his brother Bleda around 445, Hunnic power reached its greatest extent, dominating a large and diverse coalition of subject and allied peoples across the plains of central Europe, extracting substantial annual tribute from the eastern Roman Empire, and conducting devastating campaigns in the Balkans in the early 440s. The historian Priscus of Panium, who traveled with an eastern Roman embassy to Attila's court around 449, left a detailed account, preserved in fragments, describing Attila's court, his diplomatic dealings, and the mixture of Hunnic, Gothic, and other elements present among his followers and subjects. Priscus's account is one of the most valuable sources for this period precisely because it offers a rare eyewitness description of a Hunnic ruler's court from within, though it remains the perspective of a Roman diplomat rather than a Hunnic voice.

Attila's relationship with the western empire shifted from a working alliance to open conflict in the late 440s, for reasons that remain disputed, including a claimed marriage proposal involving Honoria, the sister of the western emperor Valentinian III, which Attila apparently used as a pretext for intervention in western affairs. In 451, Attila led a large force into Gaul, prompting Aetius to assemble a coalition that included Visigothic forces under King Theodoric I, settled in Aquitaine since 418, along with Burgundians, Franks, and other Roman allies. The resulting engagement, conventionally called the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains, was a large and costly encounter in which both sides fielded multiethnic coalitions, Theodoric was killed, and Attila withdrew from Gaul without achieving a decisive victory. Older accounts describing this battle as the engagement that "saved Western civilization" overstate its significance and misrepresent its character as a clash between Rome and an external enemy, when in fact it was a coalition battle in which Goths and other former migrants fought on the Roman side against a Hunnic army that itself included subject Germanic contingents.

In 452, Attila invaded Italy instead, sacking several cities including Aquileia, though he withdrew before reaching Rome, for reasons that ancient sources attribute variously to disease among his troops, a shortage of supplies, and a delegation that reportedly included Pope Leo I, whose role in the episode has often been exaggerated in later Christian tradition. Attila died in 453, reportedly on the night of a wedding celebration, and his death precipitated the rapid disintegration of the Hunnic coalition, which had depended heavily on his personal authority and the tribute and plunder that sustained his subordinate leaders' loyalty. Within a short period, subject peoples rebelled successfully against Hunnic rule, and the Hunnic political system that had dominated central European politics for a generation largely dissolved, its remnants dispersing or being absorbed into other groups. Students should note that the Huns of this period should not be identified with the much later Magyars or Hungarians, despite the similarity of the modern name, since the historical and linguistic connections between the two are not established.

Section 27.11

Migration, Settlement, Violence, and Everyday Life

The political and military narrative traced above should not obscure the human costs and adaptations experienced by ordinary people across this period. Warfare, requisitioning, taxation, and the movement of armies affected rural and urban populations throughout Gaul, Iberia, Italy, and Africa, whether or not a given locality was ever directly fought over. Roman law codes from the period, including material later compiled into the Theodosian Code of 438, contain repeated provisions concerning billeting of soldiers in civilian households, the supply obligations placed on provincial populations, and penalties for officials who abused these systems, evidence that such burdens were widespread and often resented.

Captivity and enslavement were pervasive features of the period's violence. Communities captured during sieges, such as those experienced during Alaric's campaigns in Italy or Gaiseric's in Africa, faced enslavement, forced relocation, or, for those with wealthy connections, the possibility of ransom. Christian bishops frequently took responsibility for organizing ransoms, using church funds or melting down church plate, as Ambrose had done after Adrianople, a practice that continued through the fifth century and connected the pastoral and charitable functions of the church, discussed in Chapter 26, to the immediate emergencies produced by warfare and migration. Salvian of Marseille, a Christian writer of the mid fifth century, argued in his work On the Governance of God that Roman provincials sometimes preferred life under the new settled peoples to the oppressive taxation and corrupt administration of Roman officials, a claim that should be read as polemic aimed at criticizing Roman moral failings rather than as a straightforward sociological survey, but one that nonetheless suggests that the experience of Roman rule was not uniformly positive for the rural poor.

Landholding elites experienced the transformation in varied ways depending on their region and their willingness to negotiate with new authorities. Some aristocratic families in Gaul and elsewhere retained their estates and local influence by coming to terms with settled Gothic, Burgundian, or Frankish leaderships, continuing to serve as local administrators, tax collectors, or bishops under the new arrangements. Sidonius Apollinaris, a Gallo-Roman aristocrat and later bishop whose letters and poems survive from the middle and later fifth century, illustrates this adaptation, moving between imperial service, literary culture, and eventual accommodation with Visigothic power in southern Gaul, a case examined further in Chapter 29. Other landholders lost estates to confiscation, taxation, or the general disruption of agriculture and trade during periods of active warfare.

Everyday Life and Social History

Provincial Life amid Military Settlement, c. 400–450 CE

Historians cannot recover the experience of any single named household from this period in full detail, and this feature avoids constructing one. Instead, the surviving legal, ecclesiastical, and literary evidence allows several documented categories of pressure to be identified, categories that affected landholding and rural populations across Gaul, Iberia, Italy, and Africa in varying combinations depending on region and period. Billeting: provisions in Roman law, later compiled into the Theodosian Code, regulated the quartering of soldiers in civilian households, evidence that the practice was widespread enough to require legal management. Requisition and taxation: provincial populations supplied food, transport, and other resources to both Roman and settled federate forces, and law codes penalize officials for abusing these systems, suggesting the burden was often heavy and unevenly enforced. Captivity: sieges and campaigns produced captives who faced enslavement, forced relocation, or ransom, a pattern documented from Ambrose's ransoming efforts after Adrianople through the fifth century. Disrupted commerce: chroniclers and letters describe unsafe roads and interrupted river transport during active campaigning, though the extent and duration of such disruption varied greatly by locality and year.

Ransom and charity: bishops organized the ransom of captives using church funds or plate, connecting ecclesiastical institutions directly to the emergencies produced by warfare, as Chapter 26 discussed for the church's charitable functions more broadly. Local institutional continuity: the letters of Sidonius Apollinaris and similar evidence show aristocratic families in Gaul retaining local influence by negotiating with newly settled authorities, continuing to serve as administrators, tax collectors, or bishops rather than by resisting outright. Taken together, this evidence resists a single uniform narrative. Some regions and years saw severe disruption; others saw agricultural and civic life continue with comparatively little interruption. The documented categories above, rather than an invented composite family, are what the surviving evidence actually supports.

Multiple Perspectives

One Transformation, Seven Experiences

Roman frontier commanders in the 370s faced newcomers at the river as both opportunity and danger: potential recruits and taxpayers if the crossing was managed honestly, but a serious threat to order if supply and discipline broke down. Ammianus indicates that some officials, under pressure to extract profit from the situation, chose corruption over careful administration.
Gothic families admitted across the Danube in 376 sought protection from Hunnic expansion and had reason to expect the food and settlement that Roman negotiators had promised. According to Ammianus, the failure of Roman supply and the exploitation practiced by local commanders instead left many in severe insecurity, a grievance that helped drive the revolt that followed.
Gallo-Roman landholders in the 410s experienced billeting, requisition, and shifting military authority in ways that often mattered more for daily life than which army, Roman or Gothic, controlled a given district. Sources such as the letters of Sidonius Apollinaris suggest that continued local influence depended chiefly on successful negotiation with whichever power held effective control.
Gothic soldiers under Alaric are represented in the sources as seeking the pay, rank, and secure settlement that Roman military service traditionally offered. Alaric's repeated negotiations, and his resort to force when negotiations failed, are consistent with an army pursuing inclusion on favorable terms rather than the empire's destruction.
Roman elite writers in the city during August of 410 registered profound shock. Jerome's letters describe grief and interrupted work at the news that the city which had conquered the world had itself been taken, while pagan critics and Augustine's response show contemporaries debating whether the sack signaled divine punishment or a more limited, if still traumatic, military and political event.
Merchants and townspeople in Carthage after 439 lived under new Vandal rulers and, for Nicene Christians, altered religious standing, but archaeological and documentary evidence suggests that harbor trade and urban commercial life continued, so that a merchant's livelihood likely depended more on the safety of Mediterranean sea lanes than on which authority collected harbor taxes.
Frankish commanders in Roman service in the 440s held Roman military rank while leading men whose ancestry lay beyond the Rhine, a combination with ample precedent in commanders such as Bauto and Arbogast earlier in the century. Historical Debate

Migration, Invasion, or Transformation?

The question: How should historians characterize the processes examined in this chapter, and how much weight should be given to population movement, to military conquest, and to Roman institutional accommodation in explaining the reorganization of the western Roman world between 376 and 455?

The migration emphasis: Some historians, particularly working from archaeological evidence of settlement change, burial practice, and material culture, argue that substantial population movement did occur and mattered historically, even if the numbers involved were smaller and the processes slower than older, more dramatic narratives once assumed.
The military-political emphasis: Other historians stress that relatively small, cohesive armed groups could acquire disproportionate territorial power because Roman civil wars, court rivalries, and fiscal weakness undermined the state's capacity to respond, so that political and military factors internal to the Roman system explain the outcome more than the scale of migration itself.
The accommodation and transformation emphasis: A third body of scholarship emphasizes that Roman governments deliberately settled, employed, and negotiated with many of these groups, and that the successor kingdoms which emerged, examined further in Chapter 29, drew heavily on Roman law, administration, and aristocratic cooperation, so that the process is better described as a transformation of the Roman world from within than as its destruction from without.

Evidence: All three emphases draw on overlapping evidence, including narrative histories, chronicles, legal codes, letters, and archaeology, but they weigh that evidence differently and ask different questions of it.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes whether the period is understood chiefly as catastrophe, as continuity, or as a genuine and consequential reorganization that included elements of all three, and it shapes how the successor kingdoms of Chapter 29 should be understood in relation to the Roman world that preceded them.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Armed Migrant Groups Become Territorial Powers inside the Western Empire?

Long-term causes

Centuries of Roman recruitment, subsidy, and diplomatic engagement across the Rhine and Danube frontiers had already made armed outside groups a familiar and structurally necessary part of the late Roman military system.

Short-term causes

Hunnic expansion displaced Gothic communities in the 370s, and recurring Roman civil wars, court rivalries, and the deaths of dominant commanders such as Stilicho and Aetius repeatedly weakened central control over settled and allied forces.

Event or transformation

Groups such as the Visigoths in Aquitaine, the Vandals in North Africa, and the Burgundians in Sapaudia acquired durable territorial bases, secured through a mixture of warfare, negotiation, and formal Roman settlement.

Immediate effects

The western government lost direct control over major tax producing and grain producing regions, faced a shrinking pool of reliable military manpower, and increasingly depended on the very groups whose settlement it had negotiated.

Long-term consequences

Durable successor powers emerged in Aquitaine, North Africa, Sapaudia, and northern Gaul; direct western imperial control and revenues contracted; and new rulers increasingly combined military authority with Roman fiscal, legal, aristocratic, and ecclesiastical institutions. These developments created the political landscape inherited by Chapters 28 and 29.

Historical complexity: Not every armed coalition produced the same political outcome. Attila's realm fragmented rapidly after 453 as succession struggles among his heirs and revolts by subject peoples, culminating in the defeat associated with the Nedao, broke the political coalition he had built. Visigothic, Vandal, and Burgundian regimes followed different paths, consolidating territorial rule through varying combinations of military force, settlement, taxation, Roman administrative precedent, and cooperation or conflict with provincial elites. The contrast should not be reduced to a simple opposition between nomadic instability and settled permanence.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Western Roman World, c. 375 and c. 455

What Changed

  • Large, cohesive armed groups under their own leaders, rather than dispersed individual recruits, had become settled territorial powers in Aquitaine, North Africa, and along the Rhine
  • The western government relied increasingly on large federate contingents commanded through negotiated relationships and on powerful generals such as Stilicho and Aetius, even though mixed recruitment from imperial provinces and beyond the frontiers had long characterized Roman armies
  • The western imperial court had relocated from Milan to the more defensible city of Ravenna
  • North Africa, including Carthage, had passed from direct imperial administration to Vandal rule, with major fiscal and grain-supply consequences
  • Britain had, in practice, ceased to receive direct imperial administration

What Stayed the Same

  • A Roman emperor still ruled in the west, and Roman law and legal practice, exemplified by the Theodosian Code of 438, continued to structure life both in territories under direct imperial government and, in adapted forms, under several emerging successor powers
  • Roman aristocratic families, exemplified by figures such as Sidonius Apollinaris, continued to hold local influence, often by negotiating with new settled powers rather than by resisting them
  • Christian bishops and church institutions continued to function, to provide charity, and to ransom captives, as they had during and after the crisis of 376 to 378
  • Cities, markets, and agricultural production continued in many regions despite warfare and disruption, although the degree of continuity varied sharply by locality and year
  • Many migrant military leaders and successor rulers sought Roman office, titles, treaties, or recognition, showing that political advancement often continued to operate through Roman institutions even as direct imperial control weakened

Why the pattern matters: By 455, Roman imperial rule in the west had been profoundly weakened, but Roman society, law, religion, and aristocratic culture had not disappeared. The successor powers considered in this chapter had, in most cases, been built through negotiation with Roman institutions rather than through their wholesale destruction, a pattern that Chapter 29 will examine in far greater detail for the mature kingdoms of Ostrogothic Italy, Visigothic Iberia, Anglo-Saxon Britain, and Frankish Gaul.

Section 27.12

From Imperial Allies to Successor Powers

By the middle years of the 450s, the political landscape of the western Roman world looked very different from what it had been eighty years earlier. Aetius, who had dominated western politics for two decades through his combination of military command, diplomatic skill, and personal ties to Hunnic and other allied forces, was murdered by the emperor Valentinian III in 454, reportedly out of fear of Aetius's growing power, and Valentinian himself was assassinated the following year by followers of Aetius seeking revenge. This double killing removed, within a matter of months, both the dominant military figure and the last member of the Theodosian dynasty to rule in the west, a political vacuum that invited further crisis.

It was in this unstable context that Gaiseric's Vandal fleet sailed from Carthage to Rome in June 455. According to later Christian tradition, Pope Leo I again negotiated with an approaching commander, as he reportedly had with Attila in 452, and secured an agreement that the city would not be burned or its population indiscriminately massacred, though extensive looting followed over a period of about two weeks, considerably longer than Alaric's sack forty-five years earlier. Gaiseric's forces also removed a substantial quantity of treasure, some of it originally taken from Jerusalem by the emperor Titus centuries before and displayed in Rome ever since, according to later report, along with captives who reportedly included members of the imperial family. This second sack of Rome within living memory of the first underscored, for contemporaries, how far the balance of power in the western Mediterranean had shifted.

Yet the western Roman Empire had not ceased to exist. A Roman emperor, though of increasingly uncertain authority, would continue to be recognized at Ravenna for another two decades, until 476, an event examined in Chapter 28. Roman senators, bishops, provincial governors, and tax collectors continued to operate across much of Gaul, Italy, and parts of Iberia. The successor powers surveyed in this chapter, the Visigoths in Aquitaine, the Vandals in North Africa, the Burgundians in Sapaudia, and the increasingly autonomous Frankish leaders along the Rhine, had each achieved their positions through a combination of warfare, negotiation, and formal or informal Roman recognition, and each continued to operate within a political and legal framework substantially shaped by Roman precedent, even as their rulers exercised increasingly independent authority.

The central lesson of this chapter, then, is not that foreign nations replaced the Roman Empire in the west, but that the political landscape of Roman territory had been reorganized through eighty years of migration, warfare, and negotiated settlement into a patchwork of Roman and non-Roman authorities whose relationships to one another, and to the shrinking imperial center at Ravenna, remained unresolved. The critical question facing the western Roman world in 455 was no longer whether outside peoples could be kept entirely beyond the frontiers, since Rome had never completely sealed those frontiers and by the fifth century increasingly relied on a mixture of military defense, negotiated admission, settlement, alliance, and coercion. The question was whether a western emperor could still command the armies, revenues, provinces, and political loyalties necessary to govern this transformed world at all. That is the question Chapter 28 will now take up.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the western Roman Empire between roughly 350 and 455 CE through the intertwined processes of migration, warfare, and negotiated settlement. It began by establishing that the Rhine and Danube frontiers were zones of ongoing interaction, recruitment, and diplomacy rather than fixed barriers, and that Roman governments had recruited, subsidized, and settled outside groups for centuries before the crisis examined here. It then followed the sequence of events beginning with the Gothic appeal to cross the Danube in 376, a request Rome granted but then mismanaged so severely, through corruption, poor supply, and administrative failure at Marcianople, that negotiated admission collapsed into open revolt.

The chapter examined the Battle of Adrianople in 378 as a serious military defeat that killed an emperor and destroyed a major field army, while cautioning against the older claim that it single-handedly began the fall of Rome. It then traced the settlement of 382 as a major example of Roman accommodation, in which dangerous armed migrants were converted, imperfectly and with poorly documented terms, into a source of imperial military manpower, an arrangement whose precise legal character, including the meaning of terms such as foederati and hospitalitas, remains debated among historians.

The careers of Alaric and Stilicho illustrated how Gothic and Roman politics became entangled through the 390s and 400s. Alaric's repeated pursuit of Roman rank, pay, and settlement, and Stilicho's position as a Roman commander of partly Vandal ancestry at the summit of western power, together showed that ethnic origin did not determine political allegiance in this period. The execution of Stilicho in 408 removed the figure best positioned to manage the relationship with Alaric's army and contributed directly to the sack of Rome by Alaric's forces in 410, an event of limited physical scale but enormous symbolic and psychological impact across the Christian and pagan Mediterranean world, as the reactions of Jerome and Augustine of Hippo made clear.

A parallel narrative followed the Rhine crossing of 406 to 407 by Vandals, Alans, and Suebi, the resulting usurpations in Britain and Gaul under figures such as Constantine III, and the gradual withdrawal of effective imperial administration from Britain, a withdrawal produced by crises elsewhere rather than by a single catastrophic invasion of the island itself.

The chapter then traced the movement of Vandal, Alan, and Suebic groups from Gaul into Iberia by about 411, their subsequent defeat in the years 416 to 418 at the hands of Gothic forces campaigning for Rome under Wallia, and, under Gaiseric after 429, their move into North Africa, culminating in the capture of Carthage in 439. That capture removed some of the western government's richest taxable territory and placed a crucial grain-producing region and its distribution networks under Vandal control, without ending the agricultural and commercial life of the region itself, and it created a durable Vandal naval and territorial power that would outlast the western empire.

The Frankish and Burgundian cases showed accommodation operating through different mechanisms. Long-standing Frankish service in the Roman military, exemplified by commanders such as Bauto and Arbogast, contrasted with the more dramatic Burgundian experience of military destruction by Aetius's Hunnic allies in 436 followed by deliberate Roman resettlement in Sapaudia around 443, a sequence that showed Rome using both force and negotiated settlement toward the same community within a single decade.

The chapter gave substantial attention to the Huns, whose westward expansion in the 370s had helped trigger the original crisis and whose later empire under Attila, sustained through tribute, alliance, and personal leadership, dominated central European politics in the 440s and early 450s. That coalition fragmented rapidly after Attila's death in 453, as succession struggles among his heirs and revolts by subject peoples, culminating in the defeat associated with the Nedao, broke apart a political system that had depended on personal authority and the flow of tribute and plunder rather than on the kind of territorial settlement that sustained the Visigothic, Vandal, and Burgundian kingdoms.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized the human costs of this transformation for ordinary provincial populations, including billeting, taxation, captivity, ransom organized through Christian bishops, and the adaptive strategies of landholding families, such as Sidonius Apollinaris in Gaul, who negotiated with new settled authorities rather than simply resisting them. It closed by examining the assassination of Aetius and Valentinian III in 454 and 455 and the second Vandal sack of Rome in June 455, arguing that these events, while marking a further weakening of western imperial authority, did not yet mark its end. A Roman emperor still ruled at Ravenna, Roman law and legal practice continued to structure life across much of the west, and the successor powers examined in this chapter had each been built through a combination of conquest, negotiation, and engagement with Roman institutions rather than through the wholesale destruction of Roman society.

The chapter's recurring historiographical theme, developed most fully in the Historical Debate feature, is that migration, military-political weakness, and Roman institutional accommodation each contributed to the outcomes examined here, and that no single explanatory framework adequately captures the complexity of the period on its own. This theme, along with the caution against treating migration-era peoples as fixed nations and against equating any single battle or sack with the fall of Rome, will recur in Chapter 28's examination of the final unraveling of western imperial authority and in Chapter 29's account of the mature post-Roman kingdoms that grew from the foundations laid here.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Barbarus
Latin barbarus, corresponding to Greek barbaros, was a category for outsiders, carrying moral and cultural connotations rather than describing a single race or civilization.
Limes
A Latin term whose meanings included boundary lines, roads, and frontier zones; modern historians often use it more broadly for Roman frontier systems of forts, roads, and controlled crossing points.
Tervingi and Greuthungi
Fourth century Gothic political groupings north of the Danube whose relationship to the later Visigoths and Ostrogoths is complex and should not be treated as a simple equation.
Foederati
Allied soldiers, usually under their own leaders, serving Rome under negotiated arrangements involving pay, supplies, or land; the term's precise meaning varied across the period.
Hospitalitas
A Roman legal mechanism, originally for billeting soldiers or officials on civilian households, that some historians believe was adapted to organize the settlement of federate forces, though its exact application to land or revenue remains debated.
Magister militum
"Master of the soldiers," a senior Roman military command, securely held in this period by figures including Stilicho and Aetius. Alaric also obtained Roman military rank, commonly reconstructed as a similar command in Illyricum around 397, although the precise office is debated.
Comitatenses
Mobile field army units of the late Roman military system, distinguished from frontier garrison troops.
Laeti
Communities of settlers, often of non-Roman origin, established within the empire under military obligations, with legal status that remains only partly understood.
Usurper
A claimant to imperial power not recognized through the normal succession process, such as Constantine III, proclaimed by the army in Britain in 407.
Ethnogenesis
The scholarly concept describing how migration-era group identities, such as those of the Visigoths or Ostrogoths, were formed and reformed through political and military processes rather than simply inherited from earlier fixed peoples; the mature "Visigothic" and "Ostrogothic" identities took shape only after the events of this chapter, through later political consolidation examined in Chapter 29.
Homoian Christianity
A form of Christian theology rejecting the Nicene formula, dominant at points in the fourth century eastern court and adopted by several Gothic and other elites, later distinguishing them from Nicene Roman populations.
Subsidy
Regular payments made by Roman governments to allied or potentially hostile rulers and groups, a standard tool of frontier diplomacy.
Tribute
Payments extracted by a dominant power, such as the annual payments the eastern Roman Empire made to Attila's Hunnic coalition in the 440s.
Praetorian prefect
A senior civil administrative official responsible for large regions of the empire, distinct from military commanders, though the two offices sometimes overlapped in influence.
Bagaudae
Rural insurgent movements active intermittently in Gaul and Iberia in this period, of debated scale and organization, suggesting broader social strain from taxation and warfare.
Sapaudia
The region, roughly corresponding to parts of modern western Switzerland and eastern France, where Roman authorities resettled Burgundian survivors around 443.
Ransom
The payment, often organized by Christian bishops using church funds or plate, to secure the release of captives taken during warfare and sieges.
Notitia Dignitatum
A late Roman administrative document listing military and civil offices, used by historians to reconstruct the late Roman military and bureaucratic structure.
Migration Period
A modern historical label for the era of population movement and political reorganization examined in this chapter, understood as an analytical convenience rather than a self-evident, uniform process.
Sack
The forcible entry, plunder, and often partial destruction of a city by an armed force, as occurred at Rome in 410 and 455.
Successor state
A modern term for a political entity, such as the Visigothic or Vandal kingdom, that emerged from territory formerly under direct Roman imperial administration.
Vandalism
A much later word, coined in the era of the French Revolution, drawing on a reputation for the Vandals that owes more to later polemic than to a careful reading of Gaiseric's calculated fifth century policies.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced eighty years in which the western Roman world was reorganized, not by a single invasion, but by a long, uneven, and often improvised process of migration, warfare, and negotiated settlement. Goths, Vandals, Alans, Suebi, Burgundians, Franks, and Huns each followed distinct paths through this period, shaped as much by Roman civil conflict, administrative failure, and diplomatic strategy as by their own military strength or political ambition. Understanding this complexity, rather than reducing it to a single story of barbarian conquest, is essential preparation for the chapters that follow.

At the same time, this chapter has insisted throughout that Roman institutions did not simply vanish. Roman law, Roman aristocracies, Roman bishops, and, until 476, Roman emperors continued to operate across most of the territories discussed here, even as their authority became increasingly contested and their resources increasingly diminished. The successor powers that emerged by 455, in Aquitaine, in North Africa, in Sapaudia, and along the Rhine, were built through engagement with Roman institutions as much as through opposition to them, a pattern that Chapter 29 will examine in far greater depth.

This chapter has also emphasized that the human costs of this transformation, captivity, displacement, taxation, and the disruption of ordinary agricultural and urban life, fell most heavily on people who left little record of their own experience. Recognizing this absence of evidence, rather than filling it with invented detail, is itself a lesson in historical method that students should carry forward into every subsequent chapter of this textbook.

Chapter 28 now turns to the specific question of how western imperial government itself unraveled between the 450s and 476, even as the Roman institutions and successor kingdoms described here continued to function. Understanding the migration, warfare, and accommodation traced in this chapter is the necessary foundation for understanding why the collapse of imperial authority in the west did not mean the disappearance of Roman society, a distinction that will shape this textbook's treatment of the medieval world throughout Volume II.
