The Habsburgs, Spain, and Dynastic Empire

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 25 October 1555, in the palace at Brussels, the Emperor Charles V gave up the government of the Netherlands to his son Philip. In the weeks that followed he surrendered the crowns of Spain as well. The man who had ruled more European territories than any ruler since Charlemagne left behind not a single empire but a bundle of kingdoms, duchies, and lordships, each with its own laws, each with its own bargains between ruler and ruled.

According to the reports that survive, Charles spoke to the assembled deputies of the Netherlandish provinces about the long journeys of his reign and about his weariness, and he is said to have wept as he presented his son to them. Historians must treat these accounts with some care, since the speech was reported by several observers and the versions differ in detail. What the occasion makes unmistakable is the shape of the problem this chapter examines. The Emperor was not handing over a state. He was dividing a dynastic inheritance among relatives and servants who would each have to negotiate afresh with the political communities that composed it. The Netherlands received Philip as its lord according to its own customs. Castile, Aragon, Naples, Sicily, Milan, and the American territories would each receive him on different terms. The imperial title, the oldest and most prestigious of Charles's dignities, would pass in a different direction, to his brother Ferdinand.

This chapter studies the political system that Philip II inherited, expanded, and defended, and that his successors Philip III, Philip IV, and Charles II governed until the last Spanish Habsburg died in November 1700. Historians often call it the Spanish monarchy, the Monarchy of Spain, or, less satisfactorily, the Spanish Empire. Each label carries risks. The monarchy was not Spain in the modern national sense, for it included the Netherlands, much of Italy, Portugal for sixty years, and vast overseas territories in the Americas and Asia, while the Iberian territories at its core were themselves legally separate kingdoms. It was not an empire in the sense of a single sovereign state that ruled subject peoples through uniform institutions. It was what scholars now call a composite monarchy, a collection of territories held by one dynasty, bound together by a common ruler, a common Catholic identity, a shared apparatus of councils and viceroys, and a flow of money, men, and information that never fully overcame the legal and political differences among the parts.

The central question the chapter pursues is how the Habsburgs governed such a collection, financed their commitments, and responded to the strains that accumulated between roughly 1516 and 1700. The answer cannot be reduced to the character of any monarch. Charles V's ambitions, Philip II's meticulous paperwork, Philip III's reliance on a favorite, Philip IV's ministerial reform program, and Charles II's long minority and uncertain health all mattered. But the system's constraints mattered as much as its rulers. Castile, the richest and most politically accessible of the Iberian kingdoms, carried a disproportionate fiscal burden. Bankers, above all those of Genoa, supplied the credit that allowed the crown to pay armies that could not wait for taxes to be collected. American silver strengthened the monarchy's credit but never closed the gap between its commitments and its immediately available funds. Religion supplied legitimacy, and also created enemies and fractured populations.

The chapter also tries to avoid two persistent errors. One treats the Habsburg monarchy as a centralized absolutist state that grew steadily more powerful until it collapsed under the weight of its own ambition. The other treats the seventeenth century as an unbroken slide into decline. The evidence supports neither. The monarchy remained politically resilient and could still mobilize formidable resources long after its peak of military dominance. It lost territory and military advantage in the seventeenth century, yet it also survived revolts that might have broken it apart, recovered Catalonia, and in 1700 passed intact, in most of its territories, to a new dynasty. Its history is a story of negotiation, adaptation, and limits, not simply of grandeur and failure.

Historians reconstruct this history from an unusually rich body of sources. The archives at Simancas, which Philip II ordered to be organized as a central repository of state papers, preserve the correspondence and consultations of the councils. The Archivo General de Indias in Seville holds the records of the Council of the Indies and the Casa de la Contratación. Notarial contracts, tax registers, and the records of the Genoese banking houses allow scholars to reconstruct credit and revenue. The proceedings of the Cortes of Castile and the representative assemblies of Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia reveal how rulers bargained. Inquisitorial trials, municipal records, and the petitions of communities offer glimpses of lives rarely visible in royal papers. Against these stand pamphlets, ambassadors' reports, and the polemics of enemies, among them the Dutch and English propaganda that produced the "Black Legend" of Spanish cruelty, a literature that must be used with caution.

This chapter belongs to Part XII, which examines the development of stronger fiscal and military states, dynastic competition, constitutional alternatives, and a diplomatic system based increasingly on the balance of power. Chapter 74 introduced the general mechanisms of early modern state formation. The present chapter shows how those mechanisms operated within the most geographically extensive of the European dynastic systems. It also stands at an important point in the argument of Volume III. The Reformation, maritime expansion, and religious conflict examined in Chapters 62 through 73 all converge on the Habsburg monarchy, which was at once the principal champion of Catholic power, the principal European beneficiary of the conquest of the Americas, and the principal target of Protestant, Ottoman, and French opposition. The chapters that follow pick up the story from other vantage points: Bourbon France in Chapter 76, the Dutch and British constitutional experiences in Chapter 77, the Austrian Habsburg lands and Prussia in Chapter 78, and the general balance of power in Chapter 80.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Spanish Habsburg monarchy was assembled through marriage, inheritance, and negotiation rather than through the administrative unification of Spain.
  2. Distinguish Charles V's authority as Holy Roman Emperor from his sovereignty over particular inherited kingdoms, and the Spanish branch of the dynasty from the Austrian branch after 1556.
  3. Trace the principal military and diplomatic commitments of Charles V and Philip II in Italy, the Mediterranean, the Netherlands, England, Portugal, and France.
  4. Analyze the council system, viceroyalties, and secretarial government as a polycentric method of ruling dispersed territories.
  5. Describe Castilian taxation, American silver, asientos, juros, and royal payment suspensions, and distinguish revenue, credit, liquidity, and debt.
  6. Assess the roles of the Inquisition, religious exclusion, and the expulsion of the Moriscos in Habsburg confessional politics and their consequences for society.
  7. Compare the governing styles and political problems of Charles V, Philip II, Philip III, Philip IV, and Charles II, including government through favorites and ministers.
  8. Compare the causes and outcomes of the Catalan and Portuguese revolts of 1640 and explain why one territory returned to Habsburg rule and the other did not.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of the monarchy as a centralized imperial state or a negotiated composite monarchy, and of the causes of seventeenth century Spanish weakness.
  10. Interpret primary sources concerning abdication, finance, expulsion, reform, rebellion, and succession, attending to authorship, purpose, and omission.
  11. Connect Habsburg dynastic strategy to the wider European system, including the succession crisis of 1700 that opened the War of the Spanish Succession.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could a dynasty govern territories that shared a ruler but not laws, taxes, or political institutions, and what did negotiation with local elites contribute to the monarchy's survival?
  2. In what sense was Charles V's imperial title a source of power, and in what sense was it a burden that limited his freedom of action?
  3. Why did Castile come to bear so much of the monarchy's fiscal weight, and what were the political consequences of that imbalance?
  4. What does the history of asientos, juros, and payment suspensions reveal about the difference between a government that is short of cash and one that is insolvent?
  5. How did religion function as a source of legitimacy, a justification for war, and an instrument of social exclusion within the monarchy?
  6. Why did the revolts of 1640 produce such different outcomes in Catalonia and Portugal?
  7. What evidence supports, and what evidence complicates, the idea of continuous Spanish decline in the seventeenth century?
  8. How did the dynastic structure of the monarchy turn the death of a childless king in 1700 into a crisis for the whole European state system?

Historical Context

By 1469, when Isabella of Castile married Ferdinand of Aragon, the Iberian Peninsula was divided among several Christian kingdoms and one surviving Muslim polity. Castile, the largest, was a kingdom of high plateaus and wool pastures whose crown had in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries conquered most of Andalusia. Aragon was a confederation, the Crown of Aragon, whose component realms of Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia possessed their own representative assemblies, their own laws, and their own mutual rights, and whose Mediterranean ambitions had earlier brought Sicily, Sardinia, and for a time Naples under Aragonese rulers. Portugal, Navarre, and the Nasrid emirate of Granada completed the peninsular map. The long medieval process of Christian expansion in the peninsula, often called the Reconquista, was examined in Chapter 47. Its completion with the fall of Granada in 1492 gave the Catholic Monarchs prestige and fostered an ideology of religious unity that would shape the policies of their successors.

The wider European setting included the dynastic and military changes examined in Chapter 55, notably the growth of gunpowder armies, the expansion of print, and the intensification of fiscal demands. It also included the Ottoman advance into southeastern Europe described in Chapter 41, which gave the Habsburgs a permanent eastern and Mediterranean enemy. The Italian Wars that began in 1494 turned the peninsula into the principal battleground between the Valois kings of France and the Aragonese and later Habsburg rulers. The conquests of the Americas, examined in Chapters 68 and 69, began under the Catholic Monarchs, and the silver that would later transform the monarchy's credit had barely begun to flow in 1516.

Chapter 74 described the general institutional elements that this chapter takes for granted: councils, secretaries, taxation, credit, standing military forces, and the limits of centralization. Students should especially retain the idea of the composite monarchy, in which a ruler held several crowns without merging them. The Habsburg monarchy is the best known example. It is also essential to remember that the Reformation, treated in Chapters 62 through 67, supplied the religious conflicts that determined many of Charles V's and Philip II's military priorities, and that the details of the Dutch Revolt and the Thirty Years' War belong to those chapters rather than to this one.

Finally, students should recognize the demographic and environmental setting. Castile's population, by most estimates, grew through the sixteenth century to a peak of perhaps six million around 1590, though such figures are uncertain, then suffered the plague of 1596 to 1602 and later epidemics that cut deeply into the interior. Coastal regions of the Crown of Aragon and the Basque and Cantabrian north followed different demographic rhythms. The Little Ice Age, introduced in Chapter 2, brought cooler and sometimes more erratic conditions that affected harvests in parts of the peninsula, and the monarchy's wars were fought amid recurring dearth in many European regions. These material conditions shaped the capacity of ordinary households to bear the taxes on which the monarchy depended.

Dynastic Foundations and the Formation of the Habsburg Monarchy

How marriage, inheritance, and negotiation assembled a dispersed collection of territories, and why the imperial title never gave Charles unlimited authority.

Section 75.1

The Catholic Monarchs and the Dynastic Foundations of Spain

The union of Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon is traditionally treated as the birth of Spain, but the phrase misleads. Isabella and Ferdinand married in 1469. Isabella secured the Castilian throne in 1474 after a contested succession, and Ferdinand became king of Aragon in 1479. Thereafter they ruled jointly, a partnership that contemporaries and later writers celebrated as the work of the Catholic Monarchs, a title granted by Pope Alexander VI in 1496. Yet their marriage created a dynastic union, not a merged state. Castile and the Crown of Aragon kept separate laws, separate representative assemblies, separate coinage and customs frontiers, and separate administrative personnel. Ferdinand was a foreign king in Castile in some respects, and Isabella was a queen with no independent authority in Aragon.

The institutional differences were substantial. In Castile, the Cortes was a relatively weak body that granted taxes and presented petitions but could be summoned and managed by the crown, and which by the sixteenth century represented only the cities. The nobility and clergy were largely absent from it. The crown's practical authority rested on its judges, its corregidores in the towns, and its control over the military orders. In the Crown of Aragon, by contrast, the rulers had to meet separate assemblies in Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia, whose consent was required for legislation and taxation and whose constitutions, called fueros in Aragon and Valencia and constitutions in Catalonia, constrained royal action. The kingdoms of the Crown of Aragon also held, as explained below, extensive rights of resistance against royal acts judged to violate their laws.

Within these structures the Catholic Monarchs strengthened royal power in ways that suited each. In Castile they reorganized the royal councils, revived the Santa Hermandad as a police and military force, brought the great military orders under crown administration, and enforced royal justice more effectively than their predecessors. They also created the Spanish Inquisition in 1478, a tribunal under royal control that, as examined in Section 75.24, became one of the most distinctive institutions of the monarchy. The conquest of Granada in 1492 completed the long Christian advance in the peninsula. In the same year the monarchs ordered the expulsion of unconverted Jews, a decision whose human and political consequences are examined in Section 75.25.

Territorial expansion also occurred beyond the peninsula. Ferdinand's armies, led by the commander Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, conquered Naples between 1501 and 1504, and Naples remained a Spanish possession for two centuries. Navarre, south of the Pyrenees, was seized in 1512 and later incorporated into the crown of Castile while retaining its own laws. The voyages of Columbus, supported by Isabella from 1492, opened the Atlantic world, and the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494 divided the claims of Castile and Portugal outside Europe, as examined in Chapter 68.

The key point for the rest of the chapter is that this union was a dynastic and political arrangement, and its continuing legal separateness shaped what later Habsburg rulers could and could not do. Even after their deaths the Catholic Monarchs' inheritance remained a union of crowns, each of which retained the right to be governed according to its own laws. Historians continue to emphasize this point against older national narratives that projected a united Spain backward into the fifteenth century.

Section 75.2

The Habsburg Marriage Network and the Burgundian Inheritance

The extraordinary inheritance of Charles V resulted from a sequence of marriages, deaths, and accidents of survival that no one in 1469 could have predicted. The Habsburgs were in the fifteenth century a family of the Austrian lands whose head, Frederick III, held the imperial title but had limited resources. His son Maximilian, however, married in 1477 Mary, the heiress of Charles the Bold, duke of Burgundy, whose death in battle that year left a daughter to inherit a rich collection of territories. These included the Netherlandish provinces, a region of wealthy cities and cloth manufacture, and the county of Burgundy, called Franche-Comté. Louis XI of France contested the inheritance, and in the settlements that followed France retained the duchy of Burgundy itself, while the Habsburgs kept the Netherlands and Franche-Comté.

Mary died in 1482 after a riding accident. Their son Philip, called the Handsome, inherited the Burgundian lands under his father's guardianship. Maximilian then arranged a series of marriages intended to strengthen his family's position against France. In 1496 Philip married Joanna, a daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella, and Joanna's brother John, her elder sister Isabella, and Isabella's infant son Miguel all died between 1497 and 1500. The mortality of the Spanish royal family therefore transformed Joanna from a minor princess into the eventual heiress of both Castile and Aragon. Her marriage to Philip produced six children, including Charles, born at Ghent in 1500, and Ferdinand, born in Spain in 1503. Their children's marriages later extended Habsburg claims into Bohemia and Hungary, which Chapter 78 examines.

When Isabella died in 1504, Joanna succeeded to Castile. Philip claimed the right to govern as her husband, and he arrived in Spain in 1506, but he died suddenly in September of that year. Joanna's mental state, still debated by historians, led to her effective confinement in Tordesillas for the remainder of her life, although she remained nominally queen until 1555. Ferdinand of Aragon governed Castile as regent until his own death in January 1516. Charles, who had been raised in the Netherlands under the influence of his aunt Margaret of Austria and a circle of Burgundian advisers, thus inherited a vast and varied collection of titles while still a teenager and while still culturally Burgundian.

This genealogical history matters because it explains why the Habsburg monarchy had no natural center and no single founding act of union. Its parts were joined by the fortunes of marriage and mortality. Each came with its own laws, its own political class, and its own reasons for accepting, resisting, or exploiting the new dynasty. Contemporaries understood this, and the Habsburgs themselves explicitly justified their rule by dynastic right rather than by any national or imperial program. The phrase attributed to the dynasty, that others wage war while fortunate Austria marries, is a later tag of uncertain origin, but it expresses a recognizable truth about the sources of their power.

Section 75.3

Charles I of Spain and the Imperial Election of 1519

Charles was proclaimed king of Castile and Aragon in Brussels in March 1516, jointly with his mother, and he arrived in the peninsula only in September 1517. The Castilians who greeted him found a young man who spoke little Spanish, surrounded by Flemish counselors, notably Guillaume de Croÿ, lord of Chièvres, who had been his tutor and governor. The Cortes of Castile, meeting at Valladolid in 1518, extracted promises that he would learn the language, that foreigners would not hold offices, and that he would not export bullion, while granting him a substantial subsidy. He then traveled to Aragon, where he had to be accepted separately by the Cortes of the Crown of Aragon, a process that required long negotiations in Zaragoza and Barcelona. Each kingdom, in other words, received him as its own king on its own terms.

In January 1519 the death of Maximilian opened the question of the imperial title. The Holy Roman Empire, examined in Chapter 46, was an elective monarchy. Seven electors, who included the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne, the count Palatine, the duke of Saxony, the margrave of Brandenburg, and the king of Bohemia, chose the king of the Romans who would then be crowned emperor. The principal candidates were Charles and Francis I of France. Both, and also the pope and other powers, sought to influence the electors, and the contest became a matter of money. The Fugger banking house of Augsburg and other south German bankers advanced the funds with which Charles's agents secured the votes, and the electors unanimously chose him on 28 June 1519. Historians estimate the cost of the election at a very large sum, though precise totals vary by account and by the monies counted, and the debts incurred shaped his government for years.

Charles's election also required him to accept a capitulation, a written agreement that limited his actions in the Empire. He promised to respect the privileges of the princes, to employ Germans in imperial offices, to use German in the imperial administration, and to refrain from bringing foreign troops into the Empire without the consent of the electors. He was crowned at Aachen in October 1520. The capitulation highlights the point that the imperial title did not give Charles authority to command resources from Castile or Naples, and equally that his Spanish subjects had no obligation to fight his German wars. In his dynastic capacity he governed a diverse group of territories. In his imperial capacity he presided over a loose constitutional structure that depended on the cooperation of princes.

The election created a lasting problem of obligation. Charles needed Castilian silver and soldiers to maintain his imperial position, but Castile had not sought the title and many Castilians resented paying for it. Their leaders' complaints, voiced at the Cortes of 1520, were a direct cause of the revolt discussed in the next section. The problem would recur in different forms throughout the Habsburg period: a ruler's dynastic ambitions in one territory were financed by the taxpayers of another.

Section 75.4

The Comuneros, the Germanías, and Resistance to Early Habsburg Government

In the spring of 1520, Charles left Spain to be crowned in Germany, after calling the Cortes of Castile to Santiago and then to La Coruña to obtain money for the journey. The assembly, which met in an unusual location and under heavy pressure, granted a tax, but several city deputations had been instructed by their towns to refuse. Many Castilians saw the grant, the departure of the king, the appointment of the Fleming Adrian of Utrecht as regent, and the apparent export of Castilian wealth to finance a foreign title as a betrayal of their expectations. In May 1520 Toledo rose against the royal government, and other cities, among them Segovia, Salamanca, Valladolid, and Burgos, joined the movement in a series of urban revolts.

The rebels, called comuneros from the comunidades, or city communities, formed a junta at Tordesillas, claimed to act in the name of the captive queen Joanna, and issued a programme of demands. These included the exclusion of foreigners from office, the return of the monarch to Spain, restrictions on the export of money, more frequent and more effective meetings of the Cortes, and limits on taxation without the consent of the towns. Historians have debated whether the movement was a medieval defense of urban liberties, a precocious constitutional revolution, or primarily a coalition of cities with varied and sometimes contradictory aims. It was, in any case, not simply a revolt against the king. It was also a challenge to the increasing influence of foreign advisers and of the great nobility who stood to benefit.

As the revolt radicalized, with attacks on noble property and demands from the lower orders in some towns, many nobles who had initially been neutral rallied to the royal cause. Charles also made concessions, including appointing two Castilian nobles as co-regents to Adrian of Utrecht. The royalist army defeated the comunero force at Villalar on 23 April 1521, and the leaders Juan de Padilla, Juan Bravo, and Francisco Maldonado were executed the next day. Toledo held out for some months under Padilla's widow, María Pacheco, before surrendering.

A distinct but contemporary movement, the Germanías (brotherhoods), affected the Crown of Aragon. In Valencia, craft guilds armed themselves against pirate attacks in 1519 and then turned their organization against the nobility and royal officials. Their grievances combined economic hardship, resentment of aristocratic landlords, anger at the nobility's flight during an outbreak of plague, and hostility to the Muslim agricultural population, who worked noble estates. The rebels forced baptisms of many Muslims in the kingdom, an episode with long consequences for the Morisco population. A related uprising occurred in Mallorca. Royal and noble forces suppressed the revolts by 1523.

The consequences for early Habsburg governance were substantial. Charles returned to Spain in 1522, remained until 1529, and thereafter made increasing use of Castilian ministers and administrators. The crown made peace with the Castilian nobility, whose members retained their privileges and tax exemptions, and in return the aristocracy supported the monarchy. The towns lost political influence, and the Cortes of Castile became a more limited instrument. The Castilian elite also learned to see the monarchy as a partnership in which they would be rewarded for taxing their own communities. The experience of 1520 to 1521 taught Charles that his Spanish kingdoms were a base from which he could not be absent without risk, and it reinforced the importance of negotiating with local elites rather than imposing change.

Division II · Sections 75.5–75.10

Charles V and the Ambitions of Universal Monarchy

The reign that established the problems later Habsburgs inherited: dynastic ambition, continental war, confessional division, the Ottoman challenge, and the limits of governing many territories.

Section 75.5

Charles V, Imperial Government, and the Idea of Universal Monarchy

Charles came to the Empire with a conception of rulership shaped by Burgundian court ritual, Catholic piety, and a sense of dynastic duty. He believed that a Christian prince had a responsibility to defend the faith, to preserve the inheritance of his ancestors, and to maintain peace among Christian rulers so that they might face the Turks together. Contemporaries and later historians have often described this outlook as the idea of universal monarchy, but the phrase requires careful handling. It did not mean a plan to conquer Europe and rule it as a single state. The more usual sense was that the emperor, as the highest secular authority in Christendom, had a particular duty to protect the Church and to arbitrate among rulers. His enemies, notably Francis I, portrayed it as a threat of domination.

The most influential adviser in articulating this idea was Mercurino Gattinara, a Piedmontese jurist who became Charles's grand chancellor in 1518 and held the office until his death in 1530. Gattinara drew on the Roman and imperial legal traditions to argue that the emperor possessed a special responsibility for the unity of Christendom, and he encouraged Charles to see his dispersed inheritance as a basis for a more coherent policy. His memoranda urged the emperor to pursue the recovery of Italian territories claimed by the empire and to seek a reformed Church through a general council. Charles listened but did not follow him consistently. After Gattinara's death, a more varied group of counselors guided policy, including Nicolas Perrenot de Granvelle and his son Antoine, and the Castilian secretary Francisco de los Cobos, who managed the finances and correspondence of the Spanish kingdoms.

Government in this period was an itinerant business. Charles spent most of his reign traveling among his territories, and historians have counted dozens of journeys. He governed the territories he was not in through regents, usually members of the family or trusted noble servants: his aunt Margaret of Austria and later his sister Mary of Hungary in the Netherlands, his wife Isabella of Portugal and later his son Philip in Spain, and viceroys in Italy and the Indies. This personal and familial system worked when regents were loyal and well informed, but it relied on slow correspondence. A letter from Brussels to Madrid might take several weeks, and decisions often arrived after circumstances had changed.

The central structural problem was the conflict of obligations. As emperor, Charles was expected to defend the Empire against the Turks and to maintain peace among princes. As king of Spain, he was expected to protect the Mediterranean coasts and to defend Spanish interests in Italy. As duke of Burgundy and lord of the Netherlands, he had to safeguard provinces threatened by France. These demands were not always compatible. In some years he fought the Ottomans, in others France, in others German Protestants, and rarely could he concentrate his resources on a single enemy. The effort to govern a dynastic inheritance as though it were a unified imperial project exposed the limits of his power more than it enlarged them.

Section 75.6

The Italian Wars and the Rivalry with Francis I

The Italian Wars had begun in 1494, when Charles VIII of France invaded the peninsula to assert a claim to Naples. By the time Charles V became emperor, Italy had become the principal arena of competition between France and the Habsburg and Aragonese powers. Francis I, who succeeded Louis XII in 1515, won a major victory at Marignano that year and took Milan, but he was now surrounded by Habsburg territories to the north, east, and south. The Netherlands, Franche-Comté, Spain, and the Empire all bordered his kingdom, and he regarded Habsburg ascendancy as a mortal threat. Charles, for his part, claimed Milan as an imperial fief and Burgundy as his ancestral inheritance.

The wars between Charles and Francis, which are often called the Habsburg Valois conflicts, extended from 1521 to 1559 with intervals. In 1525 an imperial army defeated the French at Pavia, near Milan, on 24 February, and captured Francis I himself. He was held in Madrid and released after signing the Treaty of Madrid in 1526, which required him to renounce claims in Italy and to cede Burgundy. He repudiated the treaty once free, arguing that it had been extracted under duress, and formed with the pope, Venice, Florence, and Milan the League of Cognac against Charles. The consequences of this renewed war included one of the most shocking events of the period. In May 1527 an imperial army, mutinous after months without pay and composed of Spanish, Italian, and German troops, many of them Lutheran, sacked Rome, forced the pope to take refuge in the Castel Sant'Angelo, and committed widespread violence against the city's inhabitants.

The Sack of Rome damaged Charles's reputation, but it also strengthened his position, because Pope Clement VII ultimately came to terms. The Treaty of Cambrai in 1529, sometimes called the Ladies' Peace because it was negotiated by Charles's aunt Margaret and Francis's mother Louise of Savoy, confirmed Habsburg predominance in Italy. In 1530 Clement crowned Charles at Bologna, the last coronation of a Holy Roman Emperor by a pope. The Habsburg position in Italy was further strengthened when the death of Francesco II Sforza in 1535 ended the Sforza line in Milan, and Charles took the duchy for his son Philip, formally investing him in 1540. The Spanish presence in Milan, Naples, Sicily, and Sardinia, together with outposts on the Tuscan coast, made Spain the dominant power in Italy.

Wars continued intermittently through the 1530s and 1540s, and Francis made unconventional alliances, including with the Ottoman sultan Suleiman, which scandalized contemporary opinion. The Treaty of Crépy in 1544 brought a pause, and Henry II of France, who succeeded Francis in 1547, resumed the struggle. The eventual settlement was the Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis in 1559, which confirmed Spanish control of Milan and Naples. The significance of these wars for the monarchy was immense. They demanded large armies that had to be paid, and the sums obtained from Castile and from bankers were increasingly devoted to Italian and northern European campaigns. Italy also became the training ground and recruiting area for the monarchy's armies, and the strategic importance of its possessions shaped the monarchy's communication routes for the next century.

Section 75.7

Charles V, the Ottoman Empire, and the Mediterranean Frontier

While Charles contended with France, the Ottoman Empire under Suleiman I (r. 1520–1566) expanded in two directions that threatened the Habsburgs. In the Balkans and Hungary, Ottoman armies took Belgrade in 1521 and defeated the Hungarian army at Mohács on 29 August 1526, where King Louis II of Hungary and Bohemia died. The result was a disputed succession in which Charles's brother Ferdinand, who was married to Louis's sister Anna, claimed both crowns. He was elected king of Bohemia in 1526 and obtained a partial recognition in Hungary, which was divided among Habsburg, Ottoman, and Transylvanian authorities for generations. An Ottoman army besieged Vienna in autumn 1529 and withdrew, and in 1532 another expedition into Hungary was stopped short of the capital. These events are considered in Chapters 41 and 79 from the Ottoman side and in Chapter 78 for the Austrian monarchy.

At sea, the Ottomans and their North African corsair allies challenged Spanish and Italian coasts. The corsair captain Khayr al-Din, known to Europeans as Barbarossa, entered Ottoman service and received command of the Ottoman fleet. He captured Tunis in 1534. In response, Charles led an expedition in 1535 that retook Tunis, a prestigious success celebrated in tapestries and verse, though Spain could not hold North Africa permanently and soon pursued different goals. A large Christian fleet organized by the pope, Venice, and Charles suffered defeat at Preveza in 1538. Charles's own attack on Algiers in October 1541, undertaken late in the season, was wrecked by storms, with heavy losses of ships and men.

The Ottoman threat shaped Habsburg strategy in ways that are easily overlooked in accounts focusing on France and the Reformation. Spain's coasts needed fortification and galley fleets, which were expensive. Charles repeatedly sought contributions from the German princes for defense against the Turks, which gave the princes leverage in religious negotiations, and he occasionally agreed to concessions to German Protestants in order to secure their help against the Ottomans. Conversely, the Ottomans benefited from the Habsburg preoccupation with France and Germany. The Habsburg Ottoman conflict was also not a simple clash of civilizations. Diplomacy, trade, truces, and the movement of renegades and captives between the two spheres were constant features, and the Ottomans themselves faced a Persian frontier that restricted their western ambitions. Truce arrangements between Ferdinand and Suleiman in 1547 temporarily reduced the pressure on Hungary.

The lesson for the monarchy was strategic. A power with simultaneous commitments in the east, the west, and the Mediterranean could not defeat any enemy decisively without leaving other frontiers weak. Charles often stated, or his advisers did, that peace among Christians was necessary for resisting the Turk, but this peace was never achieved. The monarchy's geography thus produced a persistent condition of multi front warfare that its fiscal and administrative resources, as the following sections show, were never quite adequate to sustain.

Section 75.8

The Reformation, German Princes, and the Limits of Imperial Religious Authority

The religious division of Germany, which is treated in detail in Chapters 62 through 67, confronted Charles with a problem for which he had no fully effective solution. At the Diet of Worms in 1521 he heard Martin Luther, rejected his claims, and issued an edict condemning him. But enforcement depended on the princes, and many of them protected Lutheran reform for religious or political reasons. Charles was absent from Germany for nearly a decade after 1521, preoccupied with France, Spain, and Italy, and in his absence the Reformation spread widely. When he returned in 1530 for the Diet of Augsburg, Protestant princes and cities presented their confession of faith, and he failed to secure a reunion. In 1531 several of them formed the Schmalkaldic League, a defensive alliance that gave Protestant territories a collective military and political organization.

Charles's religious aim was to preserve the unity of the Church through a general council and, if necessary, to restore it by force. But his actions were constrained by his other commitments, by the position of the papacy, which feared the strengthening of imperial power and was reluctant to summon a council, and by the need to retain the support of princes against the Turks. He made concessions to Protestants at moments of foreign crisis and threatened force at others. When the truce with France in 1544 gave him freedom to act, he turned against the League. In the Schmalkaldic War of 1546 and 1547 he defeated its forces at the battle of Mühlberg in April 1547, capturing the elector John Frederick of Saxony and the landgrave Philip of Hesse.

The victory proved temporary. Charles sought to impose a religious settlement, the Augsburg Interim of 1548, which allowed limited Protestant practices while restoring Catholic ones, and which satisfied almost no one. In 1552 a coalition of Protestant princes led by Maurice of Saxony, who had earlier supported the emperor, rose against him with French support. Charles, caught in Innsbruck, narrowly escaped, and he delegated the settlement to his brother Ferdinand. The resulting Peace of Passau in 1552 and then the Peace of Augsburg in September 1555 recognized the right of Lutheran princes and cities to maintain their religion, while Catholic princes retained theirs. Calvinists and others were not included. The principle that rulers determined the religion of their territories was later summarized in the Latin phrase cuius regio, eius religio, which was formulated after the settlement and is not in its text.

The Peace of Augsburg was negotiated by Ferdinand in Charles's name, and Charles refused to be associated personally with a compromise he found distasteful. This detail highlights the limits of imperial religious authority. The emperor's legal and spiritual claims were large, but their enforcement depended on the cooperation of princes who had their own interests, and his resources were committed elsewhere. For the Spanish branch of the dynasty, Augsburg was part of a lesson. The Habsburgs could not restore confessional unity in the Empire by force, and later Spanish interventions in German affairs, as in Philip IV's reign, would be motivated by dynastic solidarity and strategic concern with the Netherlands rather than by hope of a universal religious victory.

Section 75.9

Financing Charles V's Wars and Administering His Dispersed Territories

The wars of Charles V were expensive, and their financing reveals the practical structure of his power. The principal sources of revenue were Castile, which supplied the greatest share of regular taxation and credit, the Netherlands, which provided substantial subsidies and loans, and, to a lesser extent, Naples, Sicily, and Milan. The American colonies contributed a growing but, in his reign, still modest proportion of royal income, primarily the royal fifth on mined silver and gold. After the discovery of the great silver deposits at Potosí in 1545 and Zacatecas in 1546, the flows increased greatly, but their major effects came under Philip II.

Castilian revenue came from a number of sources. The alcabala, a sales tax of in principle one tenth on commercial transactions, was the most productive, and from the 1530s the crown negotiated fixed payments with cities in place of direct collection. The servicios were extraordinary grants voted by the Cortes, and the crown also drew on the cruzada, a levy associated with papal indulgences for crusading, the subsidio from the clergy, and the revenues of the military orders. The clergy and nobility were exempt from most direct taxation, so the burden fell disproportionately on commoners. The Castilian Cortes, as the comuneros had foreseen, could not effectively refuse, but its role in voting taxes remained significant.

Because armies had to be paid before taxes arrived, Charles relied on bankers. In his early years, southern German houses, especially the Fugger and the Welser, supplied loans. Later, Genoese merchant bankers, who had links to Italian, Antwerp, and Spanish markets, became more important. Loans were arranged through asientos, contracts under which a banker advanced a sum in a specified place and currency, to be repaid from designated revenues, including future taxes or colonial treasure. Short term debts were frequently converted into juros, long term annuities secured on specific royal revenues, which were also sold to Castilian investors. By the end of the reign royal debts were large and growing, and in 1557, shortly after the abdication, Philip II suspended payments, as discussed in Section 75.23.

Administering the territories required continuous negotiation between the center and the periphery. Viceroys governed Naples, Sicily, Aragon, and the Indies, and governors or regents ruled the Netherlands. Each had specific instructions, but in the absence of rapid communication they often acted on their own judgment. Charles exchanged enormous volumes of correspondence with his deputies and ministers, and he is known to have given general instructions that left room for local adjustment. He also had to deal with the discrepancy between nominal and real resources. The empire might on paper command vast revenues, but at any moment the funds actually available in the right place and in the right currency were usually small. A great battle could be lost because payment had not reached the troops, as the mutinies and the Sack of Rome showed.

Section 75.10

The Abdications of 1555–1556 and the Division of Habsburg Power

By the early 1550s Charles was physically exhausted, suffering from gout, and politically beleaguered by the revolt of Maurice of Saxony and the failure of his religious program. He had also been preparing for succession for some time. In 1521 and 1522 he had transferred the Austrian hereditary lands to his brother Ferdinand, who had been elected king of the Romans in 1531 and who was the effective administrator of Habsburg interests in the Empire. Charles had hoped that his son Philip might eventually succeed as emperor, and in the later 1540s he promoted this idea, but Ferdinand and his son Maximilian resisted, and the German princes had no wish to see the imperial title become hereditary in a Spanish line. A compromise by 1551 left the question open.

Charles's abdications unfolded in stages. In 1554 he gave Philip the kingdom of Naples on the occasion of the latter's marriage to Mary Tudor, queen of England, so that Philip would marry as a king; Milan had already been destined for him. On 25 October 1555 in Brussels, before the assembled Estates of the Netherlands and the Knights of the Golden Fleece, Charles transferred sovereignty over the Netherlands to Philip. On 16 January 1556 he resigned the crowns of Castile and Aragon with their dependencies, including the American territories. He later arranged for the imperial dignity to pass to Ferdinand. The formal transfer was announced in 1556 and the electors accepted it in 1558. He then retired to a house adjoining the monastery of Yuste in Extremadura, where he died on 21 September 1558.

The division of power was not a division of an empire but a reorganization of dynastic responsibilities. Philip received an enormous but different inheritance from the one Charles had ruled: he held Spain, the Spanish possessions in Italy, the Netherlands, Franche-Comté, and the American territories, but he did not hold the imperial title or the Austrian lands. Ferdinand held the Austrian lands, Bohemia, the Habsburg part of Hungary, and the imperial dignity. The two branches were closely connected by blood, by marriage, and by mutual interest, and they supported each other in many ventures, but they had distinct administrations, finances, and primary concerns. This separation is crucial. Later references to the Habsburgs as a single power can blur the fact that Madrid and Vienna often pursued different priorities and had to negotiate with each other as relatives and partners.

The reign of Charles V left a legacy that was ambiguous. He had defended and enlarged his inheritance, defeated France in Italy, resisted the Ottomans, and prevented the disintegration of the Church in his territories outside Germany. But he had failed to restore religious unity, he had exhausted Castile's finances, and he had left Philip a heritage of debt and multiple wars. The fundamental problem, that of governing territories connected by lineage rather than by institutions, remained unsolved. Philip would approach it differently, by concentrating his government in Castile, but he would face the same underlying constraints.

Historical Vocabulary
Composite Monarchy

A composite monarchy is a political structure in which a single ruler holds several crowns or territories that retain their own laws, institutions, and privileges, rather than being merged into a single state. The term, developed by historians such as Helmut Koenigsberger and John Elliott (who also wrote of "composite states"), describes the Habsburg monarchy well, and it also applies to Britain after 1603 and to many other early modern dynastic structures. Unification was usually by the person of the ruler, who had to be accepted separately in each territory.

The term matters here because it replaces the misleading image of a centralized Spanish empire. Contemporaries spoke of the Monarchy, or of the several kingdoms and lordships. Modern equivalents such as "nation state" or "empire" in the sense of a single sovereign state with uniform administration are misleading. The term does not imply weakness. Composite monarchies could be very powerful, but their power depended on negotiation with local elites and could be constrained by local law.

Division III · Sections 75.11–75.17

Philip II and the Defense of Habsburg Power, 1556–1598

A reign in which the monarchy became more closely tied to Castilian institutions while retaining a remarkably dispersed imperial geography.

Section 75.11

Philip II's Inheritance and the Reorientation of Habsburg Government

Philip was born in Valladolid in 1527 and was therefore, unlike his father, raised in Castile and shaped by its language, court, and clergy. He had acted as regent in Spain during Charles's absences and had been introduced to the northern territories on a long tour in 1548 to 1551. His inheritance in 1556 was nonetheless an uneasy one. The treasury was burdened with debt. The wars with France continued. The Netherlands, which he scarcely knew, had been told that he would rule as their natural lord, although he lacked the Burgundian manner of his father. He did not receive the imperial title, and the Austrian branch under Ferdinand held the German lands. His authority was therefore centered on Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, and the Americas.

Philip's reign saw a reorientation in the geography of Habsburg government. Charles had been an itinerant ruler whose court moved among his territories. Philip remained in the Iberian Peninsula after 1559, and he never returned to the Netherlands, a decision with major consequences. He governed the Netherlands, Italy, and the Indies from Spain, through correspondence, councils, and delegates. This increased the importance of Castile, which provided the court, the secretaries, much of the revenue, and many of the soldiers and administrators. The monarchy did not become Castilian in a legal sense, but its center of gravity unmistakably shifted to the Iberian interior.

Religious legitimacy was an essential component of this system. Philip regarded himself as a Catholic king whose duty was to defend the faith within his realms and to support it abroad. This conviction was not merely personal piety. It was also a source of political authority that bound his diverse territories together and justified the exercise of royal power in ecclesiastical matters, since the crown held extensive patronage over the Spanish and American churches. The Council of Trent, concluded in 1563, was accepted in his realms with certain reservations about royal rights. Philip's religious commitments also shaped his foreign policy, though, as later sections show, he made decisions on grounds of dynastic and strategic interest as often as on religious ones.

The new king's immediate task was to end the war with France. The Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis in April 1559, in which Henry II of France abandoned his Italian claims, confirmed Spanish predominance in Italy. Philip was by then a widower, since Mary Tudor had died in November 1558, and he married the French king's daughter Elisabeth of Valois in 1559. Henry II's accidental death in a tournament in the same year introduced a period of weakness in France, which would soon descend into religious war and which allowed Philip a relatively free hand for some years. The peace did not end his military problems, but it allowed him to turn to the Mediterranean and, increasingly, to the Netherlands.

Section 75.12

Madrid, El Escorial, and the Government of the Prudent King

In 1561 Philip established his court at Madrid, a modest town in the center of the peninsula, which then became the capital in practice, though not by any formal decree. Several considerations probably influenced the choice, including the central location, the absence of an entrenched episcopal or municipal authority comparable to those in Toledo or Valladolid, and the proximity to hunting and religious sites. Madrid grew into a large city, and with the court came a great increase in the number of officials, petitioners, servants, and others dependent on royal favor. Nearby, Philip commissioned the monastery of San Lorenzo de El Escorial, begun in 1563 and substantially complete by 1584, which combined a royal palace, a monastery, a church, a college, a library, and a royal mausoleum. Its austere architecture expressed the dynasty's Catholic piety and its self-image of sober authority.

Philip is often called the Prudent King, a title that reflects his reputation for caution and deliberation, and he is also remembered as the paper king (rey papelero) for his devotion to written administration. He read large volumes of correspondence, annotated documents with marginal comments, and demanded written consultas from councils before decisions. The records survive in great quantity at Simancas, where he ordered the establishment of a state archive, and they offer an extraordinary view of the process of government. But the image of a king who personally controlled everything can mislead. The volume of business exceeded what any individual could oversee. Philip relied on secretaries such as Gonzalo Pérez, whose son Antonio Pérez later became the center of a political scandal, and on ministers who competed for influence, including Ruy Gómez de Silva, prince of Éboli, and the duke of Alba, each with a faction at court.

The method of government created advantages and disadvantages. The central advantage was information: Philip's bureaucracy produced and preserved abundant documentation, and correspondence allowed him to monitor distant viceroys. The central disadvantage was delay. A decision on a Netherlands question might require consultation in Madrid, transmission to Brussels, interpretation by the governor, and a report back, a cycle that took many weeks. Philip's habit of deliberating, his reluctance to delegate fully, and his tendency to take decisions in private with a small group of secretaries could slow response at moments when speed mattered. Historians such as Geoffrey Parker, in his studies of Philip's grand strategy, have stressed that these constraints of information and distance shaped Habsburg strategy in ways that no amount of royal diligence could entirely overcome, although how far they explain the monarchy's difficulties remains debated.

Philip's court was also a social world. Noble families competed for offices, favors, and marriages. Women of the dynasty, including his sisters, daughters, and aunts, exercised influence, and some, like his sister Juana, served as regents. Court ritual, partly derived from the Burgundian tradition that Charles had introduced, regulated access to the king. The court's cost was substantial, though it remained small compared with military expenditures, and its patronage helped bind elites to the crown.

Section 75.13

The Netherlands, Rebellion, and the Cost of Imperial Authority

The Netherlands was among the richest territories in Europe, a collection of seventeen provinces with great commercial cities, including Antwerp, the leading financial and trading center of the age. It was also administratively complex, with local privileges, provincial estates, and a tradition of consultation. Under Charles, the provinces had paid substantial taxes, and he had made them a coherent political entity through the Pragmatic Sanction of 1549. Philip, however, was an absentee king. He appointed his half sister Margaret of Parma as governor in 1559, with Antoine Perrenot de Granvelle, later a cardinal, as her principal adviser. The leading nobles of the Netherlands, among them William of Orange and the counts of Egmont and Hoorn, resented what they saw as their exclusion from power.

Several grievances converged. Philip's anti heresy policies, which included the enforcement of edicts against Protestants and the reorganization of the bishoprics in 1559, were seen as an attack on provincial privileges. The presence of Spanish troops after the peace with France was unpopular. Taxation was heavy, and economic difficulties in the 1560s increased tension. In 1566 a group of lesser nobles presented a petition to Margaret asking for moderation, and in the summer of that year waves of image breaking, mostly by Calvinist crowds, struck churches across the provinces. The government regained control, but Philip decided to send an army under the duke of Alba, who arrived in 1567 and established a special tribunal, the Council of Troubles, which executed many including Egmont and Hoorn in 1568.

Alba's severity transformed the situation. William of Orange launched military campaigns from exile, and in 1572 the capture of the port of Brielle by the rebel Sea Beggars opened a wider rebellion in Holland and Zeeland. A new tax, the Tenth Penny, which Alba attempted to impose in 1569, aroused broad resistance. Subsequent governors, Requesens, Don John of Austria, and Alexander Farnese, duke of Parma, alternated between negotiation and force. A mutiny of unpaid Spanish soldiers in Antwerp in November 1576, called the Spanish Fury, killed thousands of civilians and drove the provinces together in the Pacification of Ghent. Parma reconquered the southern provinces by diplomacy and war in the 1580s, but the northern provinces, as examined in Chapters 66 and 77, formed a separate polity.

The Netherlands revealed several limits of royal authority. Philip's decisions were made at a distance and sometimes undermined by inconsistency between his instructions and local realities. The cost of military operations was immense, since the Army of Flanders became the largest standing force of the monarchy, and it depended on remittances from Castile and Italy. The army's unpaid soldiers mutinied repeatedly, and the mutinies prolonged the war and damaged royal credibility. The conflict thus consumed resources that might have been devoted elsewhere and became the central drain on the monarchy's finances. Chapters 66 and 77 give the detailed Dutch history. For the present chapter, the crucial point is how a dispute over religion, taxation, and privilege in one territory could create fiscal and military pressures that affected all the others.

Section 75.14

Mediterranean Strategy, the Morisco Revolt, and Lepanto

The Mediterranean remained a theater of competition with the Ottoman Empire throughout Philip's reign. In 1560 a Spanish and allied expedition to the island of Djerba off Tunisia ended in a disastrous defeat, with the loss of many galleys and soldiers. In 1565 the Ottomans besieged Malta, held by the Knights of St. John, and a Spanish relief force helped to save the island. These episodes indicated the continuous nature of the contest, which depended on galley fleets, fortified bases, and the unpredictable seasons of sailing.

The war in the Mediterranean was complicated by an internal conflict. The Muslims of Granada, converted to Christianity after the conquest of 1492 and the revolt of 1499 to 1501, were called Moriscos, though many retained their language, dress, and customs. In 1567 Philip issued a decree, revived from earlier edicts, that prohibited Arabic, traditional dress, and certain customs. The Moriscos of the Alpujarras and other districts rose in December 1568, in a rebellion that spread through the mountainous areas of the former emirate and that became a bitter and violent guerrilla war. Philip's half brother, Don John of Austria, took command in 1569, and the revolt was suppressed by 1571 with considerable brutality. Many Moriscos were dispersed in 1570 from Granada to other parts of Castile, a deportation that scattered communities and added to the Morisco presence in places where it had been small.

While the Alpujarras revolt was being suppressed, the monarchy formed a Holy League with the papacy and Venice in 1571, after the Ottoman conquest of Cyprus from Venice. The allied fleet, under Don John, met the Ottoman fleet in the Gulf of Patras, near Lepanto, on 7 October 1571. The Christian fleet won a decisive tactical victory, destroying or capturing a large part of the Ottoman galleys and freeing thousands of Christian oarsmen. The victory was celebrated across Catholic Europe and in the arts, and it is one of the best known naval battles of the early modern period.

Its strategic results were, however, limited. The Ottomans rebuilt their fleet quickly during the following winter. The allies quarreled over objectives, and Venice made a separate peace with the sultan in 1573. Don John captured Tunis in 1573, but the Ottomans retook it in 1574. By 1578 and 1581, Philip and the Ottoman government concluded truces, effectively recognizing that neither side could achieve a decisive result and that both faced more pressing priorities elsewhere, Philip in the Netherlands and Portugal, the sultan on his Persian frontier. Lepanto thus exemplifies a recurring pattern: a spectacular victory that did not alter the underlying balance, and a limit to the usefulness of naval battle in a war fought chiefly by logistics and finance.

Section 75.15

England, the Spanish Armada, and the European Confessional Struggle

Philip's relationship with England had begun as a dynastic marriage. As the husband of Mary I from 1554, he had been king consort until her death in 1558, and he had hoped to maintain English friendship under Elizabeth, even proposing marriage to her. But the Protestant settlement in England, English support for the Dutch rebels, and the activity of English privateers against Spanish shipping gradually turned the relationship hostile. Francis Drake's raids on Spanish possessions in the Caribbean and Pacific in the 1570s and 1580s, the Treaty of Nonsuch in 1585 by which Elizabeth committed troops to the Netherlands, and the execution of Mary, queen of Scots, in February 1587, whom Catholics regarded as the rightful heir, moved Philip toward invasion.

The plan, devised over several years, was ambitious. A great fleet, the Armada, would sail from Lisbon to the Channel, join the army of the duke of Parma in the Netherlands, and ferry it across to England. The fleet, commanded by the duke of Medina Sidonia after the death of the experienced marquis of Santa Cruz, consisted of roughly 130 ships and, by most estimates, around 27,000 to 30,000 men, though counts vary by source and by what is included. It left in the summer of 1588 and fought running engagements with the English fleet in the Channel. The decisive moment came at Gravelines on 8 August, when fire ships had disrupted the Spanish formation. The wind prevented a return up the Channel, and the Armada sailed north around Scotland and Ireland, where storms wrecked many ships. Perhaps a third of the fleet failed to return, and many thousands died, mainly of exposure, disease, and shipwreck.

The central problem was one of coordination. The Armada's plan depended on a junction with Parma's army, but there was no deepwater port available to shelter the fleet, and the Dutch controlled the shallow waters that Parma's barges would have had to cross. Communication between the fleet and Parma was slow. Spanish gunnery and ship design were less well suited to the long range engagements that the English preferred. The expedition reflected the weakness of the monarchy's strategic planning when different theaters had to be coordinated across great distances.

The consequences, though serious, should not be exaggerated. The defeat damaged Philip's prestige, strengthened English and Protestant morale, and cost large sums. But Spain rebuilt its navy and improved its Atlantic escort system, and subsequent English attempts to exploit the victory, such as the Drake and Norris expedition to Lisbon in 1589, failed. Later Spanish armadas in 1596 and 1597 were dispersed by storms. Treasure fleets continued to cross the Atlantic with considerable success through the 1590s. The war with England continued until 1604, and it showed the monarchy's ability to sustain a long maritime struggle at high cost while failing to achieve a decisive outcome. The Armada thus belongs to a larger story of limited resources used in too many places, rather than of the sudden destruction of Spanish power.

Section 75.16

The Iberian Union of 1580 and the Expansion of Philip's Dynastic Empire

In August 1578 King Sebastian of Portugal, a young and impetuous ruler, led an expedition against Morocco and was killed at the battle of Alcácer Quibir with much of the Portuguese nobility. He left no heir, and the throne passed to his great uncle, the aged Cardinal Henry, who was himself childless. When Henry died in January 1580, the succession was contested among several claimants: António, prior of Crato, an illegitimate grandson of King Manuel I; Catherine, duchess of Braganza; and Philip II, whose mother Isabella of Portugal had been Manuel's daughter. Philip's claim through legitimate descent was strong in dynastic law, though the Portuguese political community was divided. Many nobles and clergy favored him, while the urban populace tended to support António.

Philip prepared with diplomacy, financial inducements to Portuguese notables, and military force. The duke of Alba led an army into Portugal in 1580 and defeated António's forces at Alcântara in August. Philip was acclaimed king in the Cortes of Tomar in April 1581. The settlement, which has become a central text for later discussions of Portuguese autonomy, is the key feature of the Iberian Union. Philip promised, according to the terms accepted at Tomar, that Portugal would remain a separate kingdom with its own laws, courts, currency, and institutions, that offices in Portugal and its empire would be held by Portuguese, that the Cortes would be convened in Portugal, and that Portugal's overseas empire would be administered separately. A Council of Portugal was established at the court, and a viceroy or governors ruled in Lisbon. Philip himself stayed in Portugal from 1581 to 1583.

The union therefore was a union of crowns, not an absorption. Its significance was nonetheless enormous. Philip now ruled two overseas imperial systems, the Spanish in the Americas and the Philippines and the Portuguese in Brazil, Africa, and the Indian Ocean. Their combination gave the dynasty a global presence that contemporaries called the empire on which the sun never set, a phrase that is later and metaphorical. The union also improved the strategic position of the monarchy in the Atlantic, since Lisbon was a major port. The Portuguese empire, however, became exposed to the enemies of the Habsburgs, especially the Dutch, who attacked Portuguese trading posts in Asia and Brazil from the 1590s, and the English. The Portuguese elite benefited from some Spanish markets but resented the sense that Portuguese interests were being sacrificed to Castilian wars.

Philip's successors would find it harder than he did to maintain the delicate balance, as later sections show. The union also illustrates the limits of historical labels. Contemporaries used phrases such as the Monarchy of Spain, including Portugal, while the Portuguese continued to regard their kingdom as a distinct realm with a separate ruler who happened also to hold other crowns. Historians describe the sixty years as the Iberian Union, the Dual Monarchy, or the Philippine period in Portuguese historiography, each name reflecting a different interpretive stance.

Section 75.17

Philip II's Final Wars, Strategic Overextension, and Political Legacy

In the later 1580s and 1590s Philip fought several wars at once. In France, the assassination of Henry III in 1589 left the Protestant Henry of Navarre as the heir, and the Catholic League, long supported by Spanish funds, sought to prevent his accession. Philip intervened militarily, sending Parma's army from the Netherlands to relieve Paris in 1590 and Rouen in 1592, and he pressed the claim of his daughter Isabella Clara Eugenia to the French throne. These interventions diverted resources from the Netherlands, allowed the Dutch under Maurice of Nassau to recover ground in the north, and ultimately failed when Henry converted to Catholicism in 1593 and entered Paris in 1594. France declared war on Spain in 1595. Spanish forces took Amiens in 1597 but could not hold the advantage.

Financial strain accompanied military overextension. Philip suspended payments to creditors in 1596, following earlier suspensions in 1557, 1560, and 1575, as military expenses overwhelmed revenues. The Cortes of Castile agreed in 1590 to a new consumption tax, the millones, an extraordinary grant of eight million ducats payable over six years, but collection was difficult and the burden fell heavily on commoners. Plague and harvest failures in the late 1590s deepened the strain in Castile. The costs of the war in the Netherlands, of defending the Atlantic fleets, of maintaining galley fleets, and of subsidizing the Catholic League in France created an expenditure that no combination of taxes and credit could comfortably meet.

The Peace of Vervins, signed in May 1598, restored essentially the borders of Cateau-Cambrésis, while Henry IV promulgated the Edict of Nantes in April 1598 to settle the French religious wars. In the Netherlands Philip made a significant dynastic decision. In 1598 he granted sovereignty over the southern Netherlands and Franche-Comté to Isabella, who would marry her cousin Archduke Albert, with the arrangement that the territory would revert to the Spanish crown if they had no heirs. The Archdukes, as they were called, governed the provinces until 1621 and their rule gave the Spanish Netherlands a relatively distinct political character, though Spanish influence remained strong.

Philip died on 13 September 1598 at El Escorial after a painful illness. His reign has often been characterized as a failure, for the Dutch revolt was unresolved and the Armada was lost, or as a triumph, for he held together his vast inheritance and added Portugal. Neither judgment is satisfactory. He left a monarchy that was still the strongest power in Europe by most measures, with a fleet that carried American silver safely, an army that was among the best in Europe, and a system of administration that worked effectively across distances. He also left a heavy debt, a taxation system strained at its limits, a war in the Netherlands without a clear exit, and a successor of uncertain abilities. The challenge for his heirs was less a collapse than the need to adapt a structure built for ambitious expansion to the realities of limited resources.

Governing and Financing a Dispersed Dynastic Empire

The mechanisms through which the monarchy actually operated: councils, provincial privileges, viceroyalties, taxation, and credit.

Section 75.18

The Council System, Royal Secretaries, and Polycentric Government

The Spanish Habsburg government was organized through councils, collegiate bodies of nobles, clergy, and trained lawyers who advised the king and, in some cases, exercised judicial and administrative authority. The system, often called polysynodial, did not rest on a single hierarchy but on a plurality of councils that corresponded to territories or subjects. The Council of Castile, the most important, handled the government and justice of the Castilian kingdoms. The Council of Aragon, created in the late fifteenth century, dealt with the Crown of Aragon. The Council of the Indies, established as a separate body in 1524, as the Archivo General de Indias records, managed the American territories. Later additions included the Council of Italy (1558), the Council of Portugal (1582), and the Council of Flanders (established in 1588 and later reorganized). The Council of State, the highest policy body, considered foreign affairs and war, and the Council of War oversaw military organization. The Council of Finance (Hacienda), the Council of the Inquisition, the Council of the Military Orders, and others handled specialized matters.

Councils worked through consultas, written recommendations submitted to the king, who responded in the margin. The process generated records that survive in remarkable abundance. Secretaries, who mediated between the councils and the king, therefore wielded great influence. A secretary controlled the flow of paper, shaped the presentation of options, and in some cases became a figure of political weight, as Francisco de los Cobos under Charles and Antonio Pérez under Philip II. The competition among secretaries, councils, and court factions was an enduring feature of government.

The system had strengths. Councils represented diverse territories and brought legal expertise to bear. They gave stability to government, since officials continued in office across reigns, and they provided a channel for provincial elites to present interests. The use of councils for particular territories reflected the principle that each kingdom should be governed according to its own laws by officials familiar with them. The Council of Aragon, for example, was staffed mainly by natives of the Crown of Aragon.

The weaknesses were also real. Overlapping jurisdictions created delays and disputes. Coordinated decisions on matters that crossed territorial lines, such as financing a war in the Netherlands from Castilian revenues and Italian remittances, required informal committees, called juntas, assembled for special purposes. Philip IV's government was particularly marked by the proliferation of juntas. The king could compensate for the lack of a unified executive by personal supervision, but this depended on the energy and ability of the monarch. When the king was inattentive or the favorite dominant, the councils could become instruments of faction. The term polycentric captures this character: the monarchy had many centers of decision and no single center that could impose a uniform policy.

Section 75.19

Castile, Aragon, Provincial Privileges, and the Negotiation of Authority

The relationship between the crown and its Iberian kingdoms was the foundation of the whole system, and the differences among them largely determined which territories bore the heaviest burdens. Castile, with a large population and a Cortes that was composed of procuradores from eighteen cities, was the monarchy's principal financial resource. The Cortes granted taxes in return for royal responses to petitions, and by the later sixteenth century its role as a bargaining partner was important, even though it could not refuse taxes indefinitely. The cities' representatives often attempted to extract concessions, such as limits on royal borrowing or promises of reform. The nobility and clergy were exempt from the main taxes and did not sit in the Cortes after the 1530s, so the tax burden rested heavily on commoners.

The Crown of Aragon, in contrast, had assemblies in each kingdom: the Cortes of Aragon, whose four arms (nobles, knights, clergy, and cities) had to consent to legislation and taxes; the Corts of Catalonia, with a standing deputation, the Generalitat, that managed taxes and defended the constitutions; and the Corts of Valencia. These bodies had rights to be heard before the king acted, and in Aragon the Justicia, a high judicial officer, could protect individuals against royal officials. The kings could not impose taxes without consent, and they could not station troops or issue major decrees without respecting the fueros. Navarre and the Basque provinces similarly retained their own laws and exemptions, which explains why Basque territories escaped many Castilian taxes while supplying sailors and iron.

The monarchy's relationship with these territories rested on a practice of negotiation. The kings accepted the privileges of each, and in return the assemblies voted subsidies, sometimes after protracted bargaining. Because the sums from Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia were smaller than those from Castile, their contribution to the common cause was far lower. This was a source of Castilian resentment and a motive for later reform proposals. The system also reflected a political philosophy in which the ruler's authority was legitimate when it respected the customary rights of each community.

Philip II tested these limits in 1590 to 1592. Antonio Pérez, his former secretary, fled to Aragon in 1590 to escape prosecution, and invoked the protection of Aragonese law. When royal agents tried to remove him, riots broke out in Zaragoza in 1591. Philip sent an army, the rebellion collapsed, and Juan de Lanuza, the Justicia of Aragon, was executed. But the king's response was moderate. At the Cortes of Tarazona in 1592 he introduced limited changes, including provisions allowing him to appoint a non Aragonese viceroy and to decide some cases by majority vote, but he did not abolish the fueros. The episode shows both the king's readiness to use force and the constraints that led him to preserve the system. A more thorough integration would be attempted by Olivares in the next century, with dramatic consequences.

Section 75.20

The Italian Possessions, the Spanish Netherlands, and Imperial Communications

Spain's European possessions outside Iberia were governed according to local institutions and by delegated authorities. In Italy, the kingdom of Naples and the kingdom of Sicily each had a viceroy, who ruled with local councils and parliaments in which the barons held considerable power. Sardinia had its own viceroy and parliament. The duchy of Milan was governed by a governor with a Senate staffed by Milanese jurists. The Spanish crown also held a string of coastal fortresses on the Tuscan shore, called the State of the Presidios. The viceroys combined civil, military, and judicial roles and operated within a framework of negotiation with local nobility. Pedro de Toledo, who governed Naples from 1532 to 1553, imposed strong rule and earned both respect and hatred, notably when an attempt to introduce the Spanish Inquisition in 1547 provoked riots.

Italy was valuable to the monarchy in several ways. It provided strategic bases, soldiers, taxes, and financial links with the Genoese, who owed much of their prominence to alliance with Spain. Milan was especially important as the hub of land communications. The financial burden was also real: Naples and Sicily, whose revenues were pledged to creditors, provided funds for the royal treasury, and the strain of taxation periodically provoked unrest, as in the Neapolitan and Sicilian revolts of 1647.

The Netherlands posed the most difficult problem of communication. The Spanish Road, a land route used from the 1560s to move soldiers from Lombardy to the Netherlands, ran from Milan through Savoy, Franche-Comté, and Lorraine to Luxembourg and Brussels. It was first used by the duke of Alba in 1567, and for many years afterward it carried thousands of troops. The route depended on the cooperation or tolerance of rulers along the way and could be cut by French action. When the Dutch controlled the Channel and North Sea approaches, the sea route from Spain became vulnerable, though naval squadrons based at Dunkirk and corsair raids eventually restored some capacity. A related problem was the Valtellina, an Alpine valley whose control mattered for communication between Milan and the Austrian territories, and which was contested in the 1620s and 1630s.

Financial and political burdens accumulated. Troops needed to be paid, and payments had to be made in the currency of the place where they served, which required complex credit arrangements, as the next sections explain. Governors and viceroys, such as the duke of Alba, Parma, and later Archduke Albert, also exercised considerable independence, and their loyalties and abilities influenced policy. The monarchy's European geography thus required constant management of routes, fortresses, and relationships with intermediate powers, and it is better understood as a network of corridors than as a contiguous territory.

Section 75.21

The American and Asian Possessions within Habsburg Government

The overseas territories, whose conquest was examined in Chapters 68 through 73, were formally part of the Crown of Castile, and the Council of the Indies governed them. The council, established as a separate body in 1524, drafted legislation, nominated officials, heard appeals, and supervised the colonial church under the royal patronage. In the Americas two viceroyalties were established, New Spain in 1535 with its capital at Mexico City, and Peru established by the New Laws in 1542, with its first viceroy, Blasco Núñez Vela, arriving in 1544 and governing from Lima. Beneath the viceroys were audiencias, judicial and administrative courts, and governors, corregidores, and municipal councils. The Philippines, claimed for Spain after the expedition of Miguel López de Legazpi in 1565, with Manila founded in 1571, were governed from New Spain through a governor and audiencia, and connected to the Americas by the Manila galleon, which carried Asian goods and Mexican silver across the Pacific.

Trade with the Americas was regulated through the Casa de la Contratación, founded in Seville in 1503. It licensed ships, recorded passengers, and inspected cargoes, and it served as a court for commercial disputes. From the 1560s trade was organized in convoys, the flotas, which sailed annually or at intervals from Seville to the Caribbean and Mexico, and the galeones to Tierra Firme and Peru, with the silver brought home under escort. The system was designed to protect shipments against privateers and to ensure that duties were collected. It concentrated trade in a few ports, notably Seville and later Cádiz, and gave merchants of those places a privileged position.

American revenue flowed to the crown through several channels. The royal fifth, the quinto, was a share of mined silver and gold, in practice often reduced. Other revenues included customs duties, the sale of offices, tribute from Indigenous communities, and ecclesiastical receipts. Private silver, sent home by merchants and settlers, was also taxed on arrival. The silver mines of Potosí, in the Andes, and of Zacatecas in northern New Spain depended on Indigenous labor, including a draft labor system, the mita, in Peru, and on mercury from Huancavelica and Almadén, examined in connection with the labor systems of Chapter 70. The human cost of this system was high.

The relationship between metropolitan authority and colonial reality was complicated. Royal orders traveled for months, and officials on the spot often adapted them with the formula that a law was acknowledged but not enforced, in effect negotiating its application. Colonial elites, including settlers, officials, and clergy, developed their own interests, and the sale of offices, which became more common from the later sixteenth century, gave local families a stake in the system. The Habsburgs claimed extensive authority, but effective administration depended on cooperation and compromise. In this respect, the American territories resembled the European ones, though with important differences in the legal status and treatment of Indigenous peoples, who were subjects of the crown but were subjected to systems of forced labor and tribute, as Chapters 69 and 70 explain.

Section 75.22

Castilian Taxation, Royal Revenue, and the Financial Structure of Empire

The monarchy's financial structure rested heavily on Castile. The alcabala, a tax on sales, had been established in the Middle Ages and by the sixteenth century produced the largest part of regular ordinary revenue. In the early part of the century the crown moved from direct collection to arrangements, called encabezamientos, under which towns agreed to pay fixed sums. In an age of rising prices, such fixed sums eroded in real value, and Philip II sought to raise them, with the result that conflict with the Cortes over the alcabala was recurrent. The tercias reales were a portion of the ecclesiastical tithe assigned to the crown, and customs duties on trade, including the duties on exports of wool through northern ports, produced additional income.

Extraordinary revenues were vital. The servicios, voted by the Cortes, had become regular, and the crown also drew on the papal grants known as the cruzada (a payment associated with an indulgence), the subsidio and excusado, which were ecclesiastical contributions granted by the pope, and the income of the military orders, whose masterships the crown administered. From 1590 the millones, a new consumption tax on basic foods and drink, produced substantial additional sums, though they were levied in ways that fell hardest on poorer households. The royal domains and the sale of titles, offices, and jurisdictions supplemented these sources. American treasure, though of growing importance, was not the monarchy's largest source of income, and modern estimates generally place it at a significant but not dominant share, with considerable variation by year and depending on how private silver and credit are counted.

The fiscal burden was unevenly distributed among territories and social groups. Castilian commoners paid a large share. The Crown of Aragon contributed less, in part because of its constitutional limits, and Portugal in the union period contributed modestly at first, though its payments grew. Naples, Sicily, Milan, and the Netherlands paid substantial sums, but local needs, including the defense of those territories, absorbed much of what they raised. This imbalance helps explain why Castilians frequently complained that they were paying for a monarchy whose benefits were distributed more widely. It also explains why, when Olivares sought more equal contributions in the 1620s, he met with resistance from those who feared losing their privileges.

Historians must distinguish Castilian revenue from the revenues of the monarchy as a whole. The monarchy's total resources included Castilian taxes, American revenues, the contributions of the Netherlands and Italian territories, and borrowing against future income. They did not form a unified budget. Different treasuries received different revenues and paid different obligations, and funds could be transferred between them only through credit arrangements. The monarchy might in this sense be wealthy and yet unable to pay a particular army on a given day. This distinction between total resources and available cash is central to understanding the fiscal history of the period.

Section 75.23

Asientos, Juros, Genoese Bankers, and Royal Payment Suspensions

Because military expenses arose immediately while taxes arrived slowly, the crown relied on credit. The asiento was the principal instrument for short term borrowing. Under an asiento, a banker agreed to supply a given sum at a specified place, such as Antwerp or Milan, at a given date, and the crown agreed to repay it, with interest and fees, from identified revenues, often those of the Castilian treasury or the arrival of American silver. Such contracts allowed the crown to convert Castilian revenues into payments in Flanders or Italy, a capacity no royal administration could have organized directly. The bankers were therefore not simply lenders but also providers of transfer services across a continent.

Longer term debt took the form of juros, annuities secured on specific revenues, which paid interest to holders. Short term floating debts were often consolidated into juros, so that the crown shifted obligations into the future. The juros were widely held by Castilian elites, institutions, and individuals, so that the crown's debt was not only an international banking question but a domestic investment, and its servicing mattered to many households. The sale of juros was the means by which Castilian savings were channeled into royal finance.

The Genoese bankers, including families such as the Spinola, Grimaldi, Centurione, and Lomellini, became the monarchy's principal financiers from the 1550s. Their position resulted from their command of international commercial networks, their ties to Spain through trade and Mediterranean alliance, and their capacity to organize payments across the monarchy's geography. Their relationship with the crown was a partnership of mutual dependence, and their profits were sometimes large, though their risks were also considerable. Antwerp was the main financial center before the 1570s, but after the disruptions of the Netherlands war, Genoese fairs, held at Besançon and then at Piacenza from 1579, became the principal clearing mechanism for the monarchy's credit.

When expenditures outran available credit, the crown suspended payments, a procedure that contemporaries called a bankruptcy but that was in reality a renegotiation. Suspensions are recorded in 1557, 1560, 1575, and 1596 under Philip II, and later in 1607, 1627, and 1647. The suspension of 1575, announced by the decree of Medina del Campo, led to a settlement in 1577, and a further settlement followed the suspension of 1596 in the closing years of the reign. In each case the crown suspended asiento payments, negotiated with creditors, and converted part of the debt into juros at lower interest. The bankers resisted, but they typically returned to lending after the settlement, because they could expect continued profit and because the crown, deprived of credit, could not pay troops.

Interpretations of these episodes have changed. The older view saw them as evidence of financial irresponsibility and of the monarchy's inability to manage its resources. More recent archival research, especially by Mauricio Drelichman and Hans-Joachim Voth, has argued that Philip II's finances were more sustainable than previously thought. Their work, based on records of lenders and the crown, suggests that many suspensions arose from short term liquidity problems rather than long term insolvency, and that the crown's arrangements with its creditors, including repeated renegotiations that left both sides with continuing gains, functioned as a kind of contingent credit system. The debate continues, and the figures are difficult to compare across sources. For students, the lesson is the difference between a government with inadequate liquid funds and a government without prospective resources, a distinction that the following feature illustrates.

Historical Vocabulary
Asiento and Juro

An asiento was a contract, here between the crown and a banker or merchant, for the provision of money, supplies, or services. In royal finance it normally committed the banker to deliver a sum at a given time and place, and the crown to repay it from specified revenues. A juro was a perpetual or long term annuity, secured on a particular tax, which paid its holder a fixed annual income. The crown created juros by sale or by converting floating debt.

The terms matter because they show how the monarchy converted future taxes into present spending. Modern words such as "loan" or "bond" capture parts of these instruments but not their full meaning. An asiento was also a transfer and service contract, and a juro was a claim on a particular revenue, not a general obligation of the state. A "payment suspension" affected the asientos principally, and was followed by negotiated conversion into juros.

Historical Vocabulary
Valido and the Council System

A valido (from valer, to be worth, or valimiento, favor) was a royal favorite who exercised delegated authority on behalf of the king, as the duke of Lerma did for Philip III and the count duke of Olivares for Philip IV. The council system was the structure of collegiate bodies, each responsible for a territory or subject, through which the king governed.

These terms are significant because they describe two ways of organizing authority. Councils provided institutional continuity and territorial representation, while the valido provided centralized direction that the council system lacked. The valido was not a modern prime minister. He held power only at the king's pleasure, was often criticized as an usurper of royal responsibility, and depended on the monarch's confidence and on managing patronage.

Section 75.24

Catholic Monarchy, Religious Reform, and the Spanish Inquisition

The Habsburg monarchy was a self-consciously Catholic monarchy, and its relationship with the Church shaped almost every aspect of its government. The crown held extensive rights of patronage. It nominated bishops in Castile, in Aragon, and in the American territories, subject to papal confirmation, and it administered the military orders and controlled substantial ecclesiastical revenues. Rulers regarded themselves as protectors of the faith and justified policies on religious grounds. At the same time, the relationship with Rome was not always harmonious. Popes sometimes opposed Habsburg power in Italy, as Clement VII had done before the Sack of Rome, and Philip II quarreled with several pontiffs over jurisdiction and revenues.

Catholic reform was an important aspect of this religious policy. The Council of Trent, examined in Chapter 65, concluded in 1563, and Philip ordered its decrees to be observed in his realms in 1564, with reservations protecting royal prerogatives. The crown promoted the education of the clergy, the founding of seminaries, and the reform of religious orders. Spain produced notable spiritual figures, including Teresa of Ávila, who reformed the Carmelite order, John of the Cross, and Ignatius of Loyola, whose Society of Jesus became a leading instrument of Catholic renewal. Religious life flourished in many forms, from monastic reform to lay confraternities, pilgrimage, and popular devotion, and the Church was among the monarchy's largest landowners and employers.

The Spanish Inquisition, established by the Catholic Monarchs in 1478, was a royal institution, headed by an Inquisitor General appointed by the crown, and governed through a central council, the Suprema, and a network of regional tribunals. It dealt with heresy, with offenses related to belief and practice, and, in its early decades, above all with converts from Judaism suspected of secretly retaining their former faith. Later its attention extended to Protestants, to Moriscos, to blasphemy and sexual offenses, and to the control of printed books. The Inquisition issued indexes of prohibited books, notably in 1559, and it supervised the circulation of ideas. A group of Protestant sympathizers discovered in Valladolid and Seville in 1558 to 1562 was prosecuted in a series of public ceremonies called autos de fe.

Interpreting the Inquisition has long been contested. Protestant and Enlightenment writers portrayed it as an engine of cruelty and obscurantism, and the Black Legend drew heavily on this picture. Modern archival studies have corrected many exaggerations. They show that its procedures, though harsh, included written records, defense counsel, and a considerable proportion of cases ending in acquittal, suspension, or minor penalties, and that the number of executions was far smaller than older accounts claimed. Historians continue to disagree about the numbers and the Inquisition's wider social effects, and the evidence varies by period and region. It is clear that the institution fostered fear, encouraged denunciation, shaped the intellectual climate, and was used by the crown to enforce conformity, though it also had limited jurisdiction in some territories. In the Netherlands, for example, it was not formally introduced, and Neapolitans resisted it. The tensions between spiritual and political authority persisted, as the crown, the papacy, and the Inquisition itself sometimes disagreed over jurisdiction.

Section 75.25

Conversos, Moriscos, Jews, and Religious Exclusion

Medieval Iberia had been home to substantial Jewish and Muslim populations, and the religious policies of the Catholic Monarchs and their successors sought to end this diversity. After violent anti Jewish riots in 1391 and later pressures, many Jews in Castile and Aragon converted to Christianity. These converts, called conversos or New Christians, formed a significant presence in commerce, administration, and the Church. Many were sincere Christians, others were suspected of Judaizing, and the line between suspicion and prejudice was often thin. On 31 March 1492, shortly after the conquest of Granada, the Catholic Monarchs issued the Alhambra Decree, ordering all Jews in Castile and Aragon to convert or leave by the end of July. Estimates of the number who departed vary widely, from tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand, and historians continue to debate the figures. Many went to Portugal, North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, and Italy, and a smaller number of others accepted baptism. The expulsion ended the medieval Jewish communities of the Spanish kingdoms, and it created Sephardic diasporas that preserved Iberian languages and traditions in the Mediterranean world.

The persistence of converso communities created a further problem. Statutes of limpieza de sangre, or purity of blood, appeared from the mid fifteenth century, first in Toledo in 1449 and then more widely, and barred persons of Jewish or Muslim descent from certain offices, orders, colleges, and cathedral chapters. The archbishop of Toledo adopted such a statute for his cathedral in 1547. These rules were debated, and some theologians and officials criticized them as contrary to Christian teaching on baptism, but they spread through many institutions and became part of a system of social ranking. Genealogical investigations, testimony from neighbors, and the threat of exposure shaped relationships within communities. In Portugal, after the forced conversion of Jews in 1497, a large population of New Christians remained, and the Portuguese Inquisition was established in 1536. After the union of 1580, and especially in the seventeenth century, Portuguese New Christians became prominent in finance and trade within the monarchy.

The Muslims of Castile were forced to choose baptism or exile in 1502, and the Muslims of the Crown of Aragon, including the large Valencian population, were forced to convert in 1525 and 1526, following the forced baptisms of the Germanías revolt. The converts were called Moriscos. They were a very diverse group. In Granada, the Morisco population was concentrated in the countryside and engaged in silk production and agriculture. In Valencia, they formed a large part of the rural labor force on noble estates and made up perhaps a quarter to a third of the kingdom's inhabitants. In Aragon, they were an important element in the Ebro valley, and in Castile smaller scattered communities lived in towns and villages. Their experience differed accordingly. Some were thoroughly assimilated in language and practice, while others, especially in Valencia and Granada, retained Arabic, distinctive customs, and in many cases private Muslim belief.

Religious pressure on the Moriscos intensified over the century, through preaching missions, inquisitorial investigations, and the prohibitions of 1567. Spanish officials and clergy debated whether Moriscos could ever be truly assimilated, and some argued for gentler instruction, while others favored coercion or expulsion. The Morisco revolt in Granada in 1568 convinced many that the community was a security threat, a belief reinforced by fears of cooperation with Ottoman and North African powers. Noble landlords in Valencia and Aragon, however, depended on Morisco labor and often protected them. The consequences of exclusion were thus social and political as well as religious: the Habsburg monarchy defined itself as a Catholic community of Old Christians and reduced the space available to those it classed as outsiders. The mass expulsion of the Moriscos, which came in the next reign, is treated in Section 75.31.

Section 75.26

Agriculture, Wool, Urban Production, and the Uneven Spanish Economy

The economy of the Iberian kingdoms was diverse, and generalizations about Spanish economic history should be made with caution. Castile's interior was a region of cereal agriculture, vineyards, and extensive sheep pasture, and its economy had grown in the sixteenth century with the expansion of population and markets. The wool trade was a major source of wealth. Merino sheep, moved seasonally on long routes between summer and winter pastures, were organized by the Mesta, a powerful association of sheep owners with royal privileges that protected migratory routes. The Mesta's privileges, which gave pastoral interests precedence over cultivation along the routes, brought it into conflict with farmers, and historians debate its long term effects on agriculture. Wool was exported through Burgos and the northern ports to Flanders and Italy, and Burgos merchants played a central role in international commerce.

Urban production flourished in the sixteenth century. Segovia produced fine woolen cloth, Toledo silk and weaponry, and Granada silk, while Seville, as the Atlantic gateway, grew into one of Europe's largest cities, with a mercantile community of Spanish, Genoese, Flemish, and other merchants. The great fairs of Medina del Campo were an important center of commercial and financial exchange. In the Crown of Aragon, Barcelona and Valencia were commercial and manufacturing centers, with distinctive guild structures. The Basque north supplied iron and ships. These diverse regional economies were not integrated into a single national market, and they responded differently to the monarchy's fiscal and monetary pressures.

The arrival of American silver has long been central to discussions of Spanish economic history. Earl Hamilton, in a study published in 1934, argued that the influx of silver caused a sustained rise in prices, the so called price revolution, and that high prices damaged Spanish manufacturing by making exports uncompetitive. Subsequent research has qualified this interpretation. Population growth, monetary velocity, credit, and the circulation of silver beyond Spain all contributed to inflation, and price rises in Spain were not uniquely severe. The relationship between silver and manufacturing is also debated, since some sectors declined while others adapted. Historians stress that silver reached Spain but did not stay there, passing to foreign creditors, to military expenditure abroad, and to purchases of imported goods.

Spanish economic history in this period should not be reduced to a story of uniform decline. Castile experienced economic expansion until the 1580s or 1590s, followed by serious difficulties, including the plague of 1596 to 1602, heavy taxation, and monetary disorders arising from debasement of the copper coinage, called vellón, in the early seventeenth century. Contemporary writers, called arbitristas, offered numerous proposals for reform, and some, like Martín González de Cellorigo in 1600, diagnosed a society in which wealth was ill directed. But many regions later showed signs of recovery, and the Crown of Aragon and the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts had different rhythms. Fiscal pressure on Castile, demographic shocks, and monetary instability contributed to difficulties there without making the economy a single story of collapse.

Section 75.27

Nobility, Cities, Women, Households, and Social Hierarchies

Habsburg Spain was a hierarchical society ordered by privilege. At the top stood a titled aristocracy, whose members held great estates, jurisdictions, and positions at court. Below them were the lesser nobility, or hidalgos, who enjoyed exemption from direct taxes and a status that many sought to acquire or claim. Clergy formed another privileged estate, and the Church was both a landowner and a source of employment. Commoners, who paid the main taxes, ranged from wealthy merchants and professionals to artisans, peasants, laborers, and the poor. Mobility existed, through the purchase of offices, titles, and exemptions, and through service in the Church, the army, and the colonial administration. The crown's sale of noble status and jurisdiction over villages from the later sixteenth century in effect made privilege a commodity.

Cities in Castile were governed by municipal councils, often dominated by oligarchic families who held offices for life. Corregidores, royal officials, supervised them. Municipal governments were important because they collected taxes, managed markets, supplied troops, and negotiated with the crown. In the Crown of Aragon, municipal institutions, such as the Consell de Cent in Barcelona, were more autonomous. Villages and rural communities handled local affairs through assemblies and officials, and the peasants of Castile were generally free but burdened by taxes, rents, and tithes. In Catalonia, serfdom like obligations on peasants (the remences) had been ended by the sentence of Guadalupe in 1486, but lordly rights and obligations continued. In Valencia, nobles exercised strong jurisdiction over Morisco tenants.

The household was the basic unit of production and consumption. Marriage was ordinarily arranged with attention to property and family strategy, and inheritance practices varied by region: Castilian law divided property among children, with some allowance for favoring one, while parts of the north and of Catalonia often concentrated inheritance in a single heir. Women's legal position depended on status and region. They could own and inherit property, manage households, and in widowhood sometimes exercise considerable authority. Women worked in agriculture, textile production, domestic service, trade, and nursing, but they were excluded from formal offices and universities. Convents offered an alternative to marriage for many daughters of elite families, and women like Teresa of Ávila exercised notable spiritual influence. Women were also subject to the Inquisition, to sexual regulation, and to the dangers of poverty.

Poverty was widespread, and cities hosted large numbers of beggars, vagrants, and the sick. Charitable institutions, hospitals, and confraternities offered relief, and debates arose over whether charity should be indiscriminate or restricted to the deserving poor. Soldiers and former soldiers formed an important and sometimes disruptive population. Patronage relationships linked different strata, from peasants and artisans who depended on a noble lord to nobles who depended on the court. For ordinary people, the monarchy appeared through tax collectors, recruiting officers, quartered soldiers, and parish priests, in institutions whose demands could be heavy but also negotiated. The Habsburg period thus created a society in which privilege, religion, and household economy shaped everyday life in ways that varied profoundly between Castilian villages, Andalusian ports, Catalan towns, and Basque valleys.

Section 75.28

The Spanish Golden Age: Culture, Monarchy, and Political Thought

The century and a half from about 1550 to 1680 is called the Golden Age (Siglo de Oro) of Spanish culture, a period of remarkable creativity in literature, painting, theology, and political thought. In literature, Miguel de Cervantes published the first part of Don Quixote in 1605 and the second in 1615, a work that reflected on illusion, reality, and the social world of Habsburg Spain. Lope de Vega and Pedro Calderón de la Barca transformed the theater, producing hundreds of plays for public stages (corrales) and the court. Poets such as Luis de Góngora and Francisco de Quevedo developed distinctive and sometimes rival styles, and the picaresque novel offered satirical views of social life. The achievements were not restricted to Castile, since Catalan, Portuguese, and Valencian writers also contributed, though Castilian increasingly served as a language of prestige.

In the visual arts, the court of Philip II patronized painters such as Titian and later local artists. El Greco, born in Crete and trained in Venice, settled in Toledo in the 1570s and developed a distinctive religious style. Under Philip IV, Diego Velázquez became court painter in 1623 and produced works such as the portraits of the royal family and Las Meninas, completed in 1656. Francisco de Zurbarán and Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, working in Seville, expressed Catholic devotion in monastic and popular images. Art was shaped by royal and ecclesiastical patronage, so that the monarchy's image, the Church's teaching, and artistic experiment were interwoven. Churches, monasteries, and palaces such as El Escorial and the Buen Retiro gave the dynasty a visible presence.

Political thought and theology were equally vigorous. The School of Salamanca, a group of theologians and jurists associated with the University of Salamanca, applied scholastic thought to the moral and legal problems of the age. Francisco de Vitoria (d. 1546) argued in lectures on the American conquest that Indigenous peoples possessed natural rights of property and political authority, and that the Spanish claim to dominion could not rest simply on papal grant or on the unbelief of the Indians, though he accepted other grounds under which war and intervention could be justified. Domingo de Soto, Luis de Molina, and later Francisco Suárez developed theories of law, sovereignty, and justice, discussing the limits of royal power, the legitimacy of taxation, and the morality of war. Bartolomé de las Casas, a Dominican, denounced the cruelty of the conquest and debated with Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda at Valladolid in 1550 and 1551 over the justice of the war against the Indians. Juan de Mariana, a Jesuit, wrote works defending limits on kings, including the argument that a tyrant might in extreme cases be resisted.

This intellectual and artistic achievement coexisted with the coercive institutions and economic strains described earlier. It was not a separate world. Writers and painters worked within a system of patronage, censorship, and religious orthodoxy, and many were themselves affected by the status issues of limpieza de sangre or by the pressures of seeking royal favor. The political thought of Salamanca both justified aspects of Habsburg rule and criticized abuses. A fair assessment recognizes that the Golden Age was neither a simple expression of imperial power nor an escape from it, but an arena in which the monarchy's religious, political, and social tensions were explored with exceptional intensity.

Section 75.29

Philip III, the Duke of Lerma, and Government through Favorites

Philip III came to the throne in 1598 at the age of twenty, and he was by most accounts a pious, conscientious, and retiring prince who preferred to delegate the business of government. He had been raised under his father's strict supervision and had little experience of rule. The central figure of his reign was Francisco Gómez de Sandoval y Rojas, marquis of Denia and from 1599 duke of Lerma, a Valencian noble who had been prominent in the prince's household and who quickly gained the king's confidence. Lerma became the valido, the favorite who exercised the king's authority, a role that existed informally in earlier reigns but now took on a more systematic form.

The rise of the valido reflected structural features of the monarchy. The council system required someone to coordinate decisions that crossed jurisdictions, and a king who did not follow Philip II's practice of reading and annotating papers needed a trusted intermediary. Lerma controlled access to the king, appointed officials, distributed patronage, and gave his relatives and clients important positions. The king signed documents and, by a decree of 1612, authorized Lerma's signature to carry the same force as his own in certain matters. The system delivered stability, since a single figure could direct government, but it also invited criticism that the favorite enriched himself and his family, and that royal responsibility was being usurped.

The court moved to Valladolid in 1601, apparently at Lerma's urging and for reasons that historians connect to his own interests and to the city's finance and building opportunities, and returned to Madrid in 1606. The move shows the importance of the court's location and the expense it caused. Lerma's government relied on networks of patronage, rewarding nobles with offices and pensions, which reconciled aristocratic elites to the monarchy but increased expenditure. The king's court became a place of lavish ceremony and religious devotion, and the reign saw a considerable expansion of royal household costs.

The consequences of government through favorites were mixed. On the one hand, it provided continuity and allowed the king to maintain a dignified and pious public image, while the favorite absorbed criticism. On the other, it concentrated patronage in a narrow circle, fueled factional rivalry, and made the monarchy dependent on the favorite's ability. Contemporaries and later historians have often criticized the Lerma years as a period of corruption and neglect. More recent scholarship has stressed the pragmatic achievements, such as the stabilization of foreign relations, while acknowledging the extent of favoritism. The system would be adapted by Philip IV's valido Olivares, with different aims.

Section 75.30

The Pax Hispanica and the Search for Strategic Restraint

The reign of Philip III is often associated with the Pax Hispanica, a phrase that historians use for the period of relative peace between about 1604 and 1618. The new government inherited the wars of Philip II and had to adjust them to the monarchy's resources. England's Elizabeth died in 1603, and her successor James I was willing to end the war that had lasted since 1585. The Treaty of London in 1604 brought peace between England and Spain, ending the maritime contest in a form that neither side regarded as a victory. In the Netherlands, the archdukes Albert and Isabella sought a negotiated settlement. After the capture of Ostend in 1604 by the Spanish general Ambrogio Spinola and continuing military and financial exhaustion, negotiations led to the Twelve Years' Truce, signed in April 1609, which suspended hostilities between the monarchy and the United Provinces.

The truce was important in several respects. It recognized, in practice, the Dutch Republic as a negotiating partner, though without conceding formal independence. It allowed the monarchy to reduce military expenditure in the Netherlands and gave a respite to both sides. It also revealed the divisions within the Spanish government. Some counselors regarded the truce as a humiliation and a betrayal of religious duty, while others argued that continued war was unaffordable. The decision also reflected the preferences of the archdukes, whose territory was devastated by war. The monarchy also improved relations with France, through the exchange of royal marriages in 1615, which joined Philip IV and Louis XIII to Elisabeth of France and Anne of Austria respectively.

The limits of peaceful adjustment became apparent. Peace did not mean the abandonment of military capacity. Spain maintained troops in Italy, strengthened its fleets, and continued naval operations against North African corsairs. The Spanish viceroy of Naples, the duke of Osuna, pursued an aggressive policy in the Adriatic, which brought him into conflict with Venice, and the alleged Spanish involvement in the Venetian conspiracy of 1618 remains a debated episode. The Dutch also used the truce to strengthen their commerce and prepare for a resumption of war, and attacks on Portuguese overseas holdings continued by chartered companies. The truce, in other words, was a pause rather than a settlement. Competing court factions, some favoring a more forward policy and associated with Baltasar de Zúñiga, increasingly criticized Lerma's approach.

The outbreak of the Bohemian revolt in 1618 and the subsequent Thirty Years' War, examined in Chapter 67, changed the strategic environment. The Spanish monarchy had committed itself to support the Austrian Habsburgs, a commitment strengthened by the Oñate treaty of 1617 with Archduke Ferdinand of Styria, and Spanish troops intervened in the Palatinate in 1620. The Pax Hispanica thus ended, not with a deliberate reversal, but as events elsewhere drew the monarchy into new wars. It had shown that strategic restraint was possible and that it could relieve fiscal pressure, but it also showed that the monarchy's dynastic commitments made lasting disengagement difficult.

Section 75.31

The Expulsion of the Moriscos, 1609–1614

The expulsion of the Moriscos was among the largest acts of forced displacement in early modern Europe. Its origins lay in the long failure of efforts at assimilation, in the security fears generated by the Granada revolt and by Ottoman and North African power, and in the political calculation of the government. The decision was debated in the Council of State and among advisers for some years. Advocates, such as the archbishop of Valencia Juan de Ribera, argued that the Moriscos were unconverted and dangerous. Opponents pointed to the economic damage that would result, especially for noble landlords, and to the moral difficulty of expelling baptized Christians. The government decided in 1609, in the same period as the Twelve Years' Truce, to carry out the expulsion, and some historians have suggested that the monarchy sought a demonstration of religious zeal and royal authority after the compromise with the Dutch.

The expulsion began in the kingdom of Valencia, where the largest and most concentrated Morisco population lived. A decree of September 1609 ordered the Moriscos to leave within a short period, with permission to take only the movable goods they could carry, and it provided for the confiscation of land and houses, which passed to the lords. Troops were deployed to enforce the order and escort the departing communities to ports. The expulsion then moved to Andalusia, Murcia, Castile, Aragon, and Catalonia in successive stages between 1610 and 1614. Exceptions were made for some who could prove sincere Christianity, for children in certain circumstances, and for some Old Christian relatives, though these provisions were applied unevenly, and families were frequently separated.

Responses varied. Some Moriscos accepted the order, believing that they would find a home in North Africa or the Ottoman lands where they could practice Islam openly. Others resisted. In Valencia, groups rose in the mountains of Muela de Cortes and Val de Laguar, and were suppressed by royal troops. Many who reached North Africa found hostile or difficult conditions, since some were regarded as insufficiently Muslim or were robbed on arrival, and a number migrated further to Tunisia, where they made important contributions to local society. Some remained in Spain by exemption, concealment, or return, and archival research has documented families who evaded the order or came back. The total number expelled is conventionally estimated at roughly 250,000 to 300,000 people, with higher figures sometimes given and regional estimates varying, and Valencia and Aragon bore a very large proportion.

The consequences were demographic, economic, and social. Valencia lost a large share of its population, perhaps a quarter or more in estimates, and many villages were abandoned or resettled slowly. Noble landlords lost tenants and rents, and sought compensation and resettlement. The crown benefited from the expropriation of property only to a limited degree. Historians disagree about the economic effects, some stressing severe localized damage, others arguing that the Spanish economy overall was less affected than older accounts claimed. The expulsion removed one of the last significant religious minorities from the Iberian kingdoms and strengthened the notion of a uniformly Catholic society, at the price of human suffering that contemporaries such as Cervantes alluded to with some ambivalence. It remains a painful subject and a powerful example of the use of state coercion in the pursuit of religious uniformity.

Section 75.32

Finance, Reform, and the Limits of Philip III's Settlement

Philip III's government faced the fiscal legacy of Philip II's wars. In November 1607 the crown suspended payments to its creditors again, and in 1609 a settlement was reached with the Genoese, which reduced interest and consolidated debts into juros. The truce with the Dutch reduced military outlays temporarily, but the monarchy's debt continued to burden the treasury. The Cortes of Castile, which met in various sessions, bargained for reforms in exchange for grants, particularly the continuation of the millones. It also demanded limits on the sale of offices, on royal grants, and on expenditure at court, but these demands had mixed success.

Monetary problems added to financial difficulties. The government, short of silver in the Castilian currency, issued large quantities of copper vellón coinage, which circulated at face values much above metal content. Inflation followed, and the market value of vellón fluctuated against silver. Writers criticized the practice, and the arbitristas offered numerous reform proposals, from tax changes to measures to encourage agriculture and manufacture. Some of these proposals found a hearing, and the Council of Castile in 1619 produced a famous consulta recommending reforms, among them restraint in royal expenditure and fiscal relief for agriculture, but implementation was slight.

Politically, Lerma's position was weakened by rivalry with a court faction around his own son, the duke of Uceda, and Baltasar de Zúñiga, and by criticism of corruption. He sought protection by becoming a cardinal in 1618, but in October of that year he lost the king's favor and withdrew from court. He was later subjected to legal proceedings, and he avoided the worst consequences, though he lost much of his influence. The fall of a valido illustrated the vulnerability of such figures, who rose through personal favor and could be dismissed when that favor vanished. The Bohemian crisis then shifted the monarchy toward a more active foreign policy.

Philip III died in March 1621, leaving his son Philip IV a monarchy that was still large and capable, though burdened by debt and facing the end of the truce in the Netherlands. His reign has often been dismissed as one of weak rule, but it should be understood more carefully. It brought a decade of reduced military costs, a settlement with England and the Dutch that avoided immediate defeat, a continuation of cultural flourishing, and a strategic pause. It also showed the dangers of fiscal drift, the effects of the expulsion of the Moriscos, and the inability of the monarchy to translate peace into structural reform. The next reign would attempt reform on a far more ambitious scale.

Philip IV, Olivares, and the Crisis of the Composite Monarchy

Pressures once managed through negotiation became dangerous when ministers sought to spread the costs of imperial defense more broadly.

Section 75.33

Philip IV, the Count-Duke of Olivares, and the Program of Royal Reform

Philip IV was sixteen when he succeeded in March 1621. Intelligent, cultivated, and a devoted patron of the arts, he lacked a strong inclination for the daily labor of government, and he came under the influence of Gaspar de Guzmán, count of Olivares, a Castilian noble of Andalusian family who had served in the prince's household. Olivares, who became count-duke in 1625, was a man of great energy and strong convictions. After the fall of the Lerma circle, he displaced the Zúñiga faction and, by about 1623, was the dominant minister. He ruled for twenty-two years as the king's valido, though he insisted that he was a servant of the crown and cultivated the image of a minister devoted to the king's glory.

Olivares's program began from a diagnosis: the monarchy's reputation, its standing among rulers, had declined, and its resources needed to be mobilized more effectively. His first measures were domestic. A series of juntas of reform met in 1622 and 1623 and proposed sumptuary regulations, restrictions on luxury, measures to repopulate the countryside, the creation of public banks to lower interest rates, and efforts to improve the administration of justice. Many of these proposals echoed the arbitristas' criticisms, and few were fully carried out. Olivares also sought to increase royal revenue by reforming taxation, though his approach, like his predecessors', ran into the privileges of the nobility and the cities.

More ambitious was his vision of the monarchy's structure. In a confidential memorandum to the king in December 1624, known as the Great Memorial, Olivares advised Philip to work toward a closer union of his kingdoms, so that they would share more equally in the costs and the government of the monarchy. He thought of it as bringing the other realms into conformity with Castilian laws, which he regarded as more suited to royal service, and he was careful to say that the process should proceed by gradual and legal means. The memorial's exact wording has been much quoted, and the text should be consulted in a scholarly edition. It was a statement of aims, not a plan to abolish the fueros, but its direction was clear: the composite monarchy should become more integrated.

Olivares's reform program reflected the particular pressures of his time. The monarchy faced renewed war with the Dutch after the truce expired in 1621, increasing involvement in the German war, and fiscal strain. Castile could not indefinitely carry the burden alone. His proposals were therefore dynastic strategy and domestic reform at once: he thought that an effective foreign policy required a more efficient domestic base. Historians have debated whether he was a visionary modernizer, an authoritarian centralizer, or a pragmatic minister responding to circumstances, and they have noted that the constitutional resistance he met cannot be reduced to selfishness. His program tested the composite monarchy at its limits.

Section 75.34

The Union of Arms and the Limits of Fiscal and Military Integration

The central concrete proposal of Olivares's integration policy was the Union of Arms, presented in 1625 and 1626. It envisaged a common reserve army of 140,000 men, to be raised and maintained by all the kingdoms and territories of the monarchy in proportion to their population and resources, and available for the defense of any of them. The distribution, as usually reported, assigned the largest quota to Castile, about 44,000 men, with 16,000 each for Catalonia, Portugal, and Naples, 12,000 for Flanders, 10,000 for Aragon, 8,000 for Milan, 6,000 each for Valencia and Sicily, and smaller numbers for the other territories. These figures come from the scheme as described in later scholarly accounts and should be treated as approximate.

The proposal responded to a fiscal and military reality. Castile had been providing a disproportionate part of the monarchy's military expenditure, and there was no mechanism for other territories to contribute to a general defense unless their own assemblies consented. The Union of Arms attempted to create such a mechanism. In political terms, however, it asked each realm to accept a commitment whose benefits were uncertain and whose administration would be controlled by the crown. In constitutional terms, it raised the question of whether a ruler could require subjects of one kingdom to contribute men and money for the defense of another.

Olivares presented the plan to the Cortes in 1626. Aragon and Valencia agreed to provide money in lieu of troops, though only after bargaining and with reservations. Catalonia was more resistant. The Catalan assemblies, which met under the king's own presence in Barcelona in 1626, resisted the demands, citing the constitutions and the principle that taxation required consent, and Philip left the principality, having obtained little. A further session in 1632 yielded a modest grant. In Castile, the Cortes had its own objections and the kingdom's fiscal capacities were already stretched. Portugal and Naples responded unevenly. In practice the Union of Arms was never fully implemented.

The failure is instructive. The monarchy was not a single fiscal and military state, and no minister, however determined, could transform it into one without the consent of the political communities that composed it. Resistance was not simply provincial selfishness. It reflected a coherent understanding of the monarchy as a federation of kingdoms, each with rights the king had sworn to respect. The result was a compromise: Olivares obtained additional funds from some territories through negotiation, but the structural imbalance persisted, and the attempt itself left a legacy of distrust. The political costs would become apparent when war with France increased demands on all territories after 1635.

Section 75.35

The Thirty Years' War, the Netherlands, and the Costs of Habsburg Strategy

When the Twelve Years' Truce ended in 1621, the monarchy resumed the war with the Dutch Republic. The early years brought successes: Ambrogio Spinola captured Breda in 1625, an event commemorated by Velázquez in a famous painting a decade later. But the Dutch captured the great fortress of 's Hertogenbosch in 1629, and in 1628 the Dutch admiral Piet Heyn captured a Spanish treasure fleet off Cuba, a heavy blow to the monarchy's finances. Spanish policy was also drawn into the wider German war. The monarchy supported the Austrian Habsburgs with money and troops, and the combined Habsburg armies won a major victory at Nördlingen in September 1634, a high point of cooperation between the two branches. The Cardinal Infante Ferdinand, Philip IV's brother, then governed the Spanish Netherlands.

The strategic situation changed when France entered the war openly in May 1635. Cardinal Richelieu, who had financed Habsburg enemies for years, now committed French armies against the Spanish Netherlands and Spanish Milan. For a time the Spanish army enjoyed some success, and in 1636 it invaded northern France and threatened Paris, but it could not exploit the advantage. The monarchy now faced a war on several fronts: against the Dutch in the north, against France in the Netherlands, along the Pyrenees, and in Italy. In 1639 a large Spanish fleet carrying reinforcements to Flanders was defeated by the Dutch under Maarten Tromp in the Downs, a symbolic and practical blow to the sea route, though the Spanish naval establishment remained significant.

The fiscal consequences were severe. The monarchy suspended payments in 1627, ending the dominance of the Genoese bankers in favor of Portuguese New Christian financiers who provided credit in subsequent years. Castile was taxed more heavily, and the crown resorted to debasement of the copper coinage, to sales of offices, titles, and jurisdictions, to forced loans, and to the seizure of private silver remittances. The tax burden fell especially on commoners, and provincial reluctance to contribute meant that the war effort depended heavily on Castile and on the Netherlands and Italy. The reserves that had made it possible to sustain war earlier were exhausted. Chapter 67 provides the detailed narrative of the Thirty Years' War, and the present chapter emphasizes how its costs reshaped the internal politics of the monarchy.

Olivares was convinced that the monarchy's reputation required it to persist and that a collapse in the Netherlands or against France would unravel its power. Critics, then and later, argued that he had committed the monarchy to a war it could not win. The truth is more complicated. The monarchy's commitments were genuine and had a logic, since the Netherlands and Italy were integral to dynastic security. But the means were inadequate, and the pressure for resources collided with the political structure he had hoped to reform.

Section 75.36

The Catalan Revolt of 1640 and the Crisis of Royal Authority

Catalonia became a front line of the war with France. After the French declaration of war in 1635, the principality was drawn into military operations in Roussillon and along the frontier. In 1639 the French captured the fortress of Salses, and in 1640 Spanish forces retook it, using large numbers of troops, many of them quartered in Catalan towns and villages during the winter of 1639 to 1640. The soldiers' behavior, the cost of lodging and supply, and the king's demands for additional levies provoked disputes with local authorities and peasants. Violence between soldiers and villagers, the burning of a church at Santa Coloma de Farners, and the royal government's attempts to arrest Catalan officials created a mood of crisis.

The revolt took a decisive turn on 7 June 1640, the feast of Corpus Christi, when armed harvesters, called segadors, entered Barcelona and killed the viceroy, the count of Santa Coloma. The episode, remembered in Catalan tradition as the Corpus de Sang (Corpus of Blood), turned a series of local disorders into an open rebellion. The political leadership came from the Generalitat, led by its president Pau Claris, a cleric and lawyer, and from the municipal government of Barcelona. Their aims were initially defensive, to protect Catalan constitutions and to end the quartering of troops. But when royal armies advanced on Catalonia, the Catalan leaders, fearing defeat, turned to France. In January 1641 they proclaimed a short lived Catalan republic and then recognized Louis XIII as count of Barcelona. A French and Catalan force defeated the royal army at Montjuïc, near Barcelona, on 26 January 1641.

The alliance proved uneasy. French troops, like Spanish ones, imposed burdens, and French officials sought to control the principality. The war devastated Catalonia, and epidemics in the late 1640s and 1650 caused heavy mortality. Catalan political leaders, who had sought protection of their rights, found themselves facing new demands, and divisions between supporters of France and of the king deepened. By the early 1650s, with French internal troubles in the Fronde, the Spanish army under Don John of Austria, Philip IV's illegitimate son, besieged Barcelona, which surrendered in October 1652. The restoration was accompanied by a general pardon and by Philip IV's confirmation of Catalan constitutions and institutions. The war nonetheless left the principality weakened, and in the Peace of the Pyrenees in 1659, the monarchy ceded to France the county of Roussillon and part of Cerdanya, thereby dividing historic Catalan territory.

The revolt raises interpretive questions about the character of early modern resistance. It was not a national revolution in the nineteenth century sense. Its leaders appealed to constitutional rights and legitimate resistance to royal abuse, and their goals changed under pressure. Social tensions, including those between rural and urban Catalans and between elite and popular groups, also shaped events. Because the outcome was reconciliation with Philip IV, Catalonia retained its constitutions until after the War of the Spanish Succession, a continuity that distinguishes it from the Portuguese outcome.

Section 75.37

The Portuguese Restoration of 1640 and the Dissolution of the Iberian Union

The union of crowns of 1580 had initially brought some benefits to Portugal, but by the 1630s many Portuguese perceived it as a failure. The monarchy's wars exposed Portuguese colonies to attack. The Dutch West India Company occupied the sugar producing northeast of Brazil from 1630 and seized Portuguese possessions in Africa and Asia, and the monarchy's focus on Europe left little capacity to respond effectively. Portuguese taxpayers resented increasing fiscal demands. Riots at Évora in 1637 against new taxes spread to other parts of southern Portugal. The nobility feared the effect of the Union of Arms and the loss of offices to Castilians. Many believed that the guarantees of Tomar had been eroded.

Portugal was governed by a viceroy, the duchess of Mantua, Margaret of Savoy, assisted by the secretary Miguel de Vasconcelos, who became a target of hatred. In 1640 the government planned to send Portuguese noblemen to fight in Catalonia, a measure that many perceived as a way to remove the nobility from the kingdom. A group of conspirators organized a coup. On 1 December 1640 they seized the palace in Lisbon, killed Vasconcelos, and proclaimed John, duke of Braganza, king as John IV. The Braganzas were the greatest noble family in the kingdom and had a plausible dynastic claim through descent from Manuel I. John was hesitant but accepted. The Cortes confirmed him in January 1641.

The restoration succeeded because of timing, geography, and diplomacy. The monarchy was already fighting in Catalonia, Flanders, and Italy and could not concentrate its forces on Portugal. The Portuguese made a truce with the Dutch in 1641 and sought support from France, England, and others. The war with Spain was largely a frontier conflict, with raids and sieges along the border, and it continued for twenty eight years. Spanish campaigns in the 1660s met setbacks, including defeats at Ameixial in 1663 and Montes Claros in 1665. In 1668, by the Treaty of Lisbon, mediated by England, Spain recognized the independence of Portugal under the Braganza dynasty, retaining the city of Ceuta in Morocco.

The result had lasting effects. Portugal reasserted itself as a separate kingdom with its own empire, and in the following decades it recovered Brazil, though the Dutch had permanently taken some Asian possessions. The English alliance, reinforced by the marriage of Catherine of Braganza to Charles II of England in 1662, became a lasting feature of Portuguese foreign policy. For the Spanish monarchy, the loss meant the end of the Iberian Union and the loss of a major overseas empire. The Portuguese case shows that a composite monarchy could be divided when a dynasty lost the loyalty of political elites and when external circumstances favored resistance.

Section 75.38

Olivares's Fall, Military Setbacks, and the European Settlements of 1648–1659

The revolts of 1640, the failure of the Union of Arms, and the cost of the war led to the fall of Olivares. In January 1643, after plots at court involving the queen and members of the nobility and the king's own weariness, Philip dismissed him. Olivares retired to his estates and later to Toro, where he died in 1645. His successor in royal confidence was his nephew Luis de Haro, who, though not styled a valido in the same way, directed policy until his death in 1661, in a style more cautious and less reformist. The king, at last, took more direct interest in government, though he remained dependent on ministers.

Military setbacks accompanied the political ones. At Rocroi, in the Ardennes, on 19 May 1643, a French army under the duke of Enghien, later the Great Condé, defeated a Spanish army, and the battle has long been seen as marking the end of the supposed invincibility of the Spanish infantry (the tercios). Historians note that the army was not annihilated and that the Spanish continued fighting for years, and the battle's symbolic importance outweighs its immediate strategic effect. Rebellions also broke out in Naples and Sicily in 1647, provoked by taxation and food shortages, and were suppressed with difficulty. Plague ravaged Andalusia and Valencia in 1647 to 1652.

The monarchy made a decisive strategic adjustment in the Netherlands. After lengthy negotiations, it concluded the Treaty of Münster with the United Provinces on 30 January 1648, recognizing their independence and ending the Eighty Years' War, as examined in Chapter 77. The treaty freed resources and allowed Spain to concentrate on France, which continued the war after the general settlement of Westphalia in 1648. The French civil conflict known as the Fronde, between 1648 and 1653, benefited Spain, allowing it to recover Barcelona in 1652 and to regain some ground in the Netherlands. But the Fronde ended, and French armies resumed the offensive. In 1658 a French and English army defeated the Spanish at the Battle of the Dunes, and Dunkirk passed to England.

The war ended with the Peace of the Pyrenees, signed in November 1659. Spain ceded Roussillon, part of Cerdanya, and several fortresses in the Spanish Netherlands and Luxembourg to France, and agreed to the marriage of Philip IV's daughter Maria Theresa to Louis XIV, which took place in 1660. Her renunciation of claims to the Spanish succession was linked to the payment of a large dowry, which was never paid, an omission that later gave Louis XIV a legal pretext for his claims. The peace marked a transfer of preponderance in Europe from the Spanish to the French monarchy, as Chapter 76 explains, though Spain remained a power with extensive territories. Spanish strategic priorities shifted from offensive ambitions to the defense of what remained, and the Madrid government turned to the problems of Portugal and the internal recovery of its kingdoms.

Cause and Effect

Dynastic Commitments, War, Taxation, and Rebellion

Long-term causes

A composite structure in which territories retained separate laws and fiscal obligations; dynastic commitments in Italy, the Netherlands, and the Empire; and an uneven distribution of burdens that fell most heavily on Castile.

Short-term causes

The resumption of war with the Dutch in 1621, the Union of Arms proposal of 1626, war with France after 1635, the quartering of troops in Catalonia, and the plan to send Portuguese nobles to Catalonia.

Event or transformation

Simultaneous revolts in Catalonia and Portugal in 1640, followed by revolts in Naples and Sicily in 1647, and a general reordering of the monarchy's priorities.

Immediate effects

The fall of Olivares in 1643, prolonged war in Catalonia and on the Portuguese frontier, recognition of Dutch independence in 1648, and further fiscal suspensions.

Long-term consequences

The recovery of Catalonia with its institutions intact, the loss of Portugal, the transfer of European preponderance to France, and a more defensive Spanish strategy.

Historical complexity

The same pressures produced different outcomes. Local political elites, geography, foreign support, and the timing of royal responses determined whether resistance became restoration or independence.

Why no single cause suffices: The revolts cannot be explained by fiscal demands alone, since fiscal demands also fell on loyal territories. They cannot be reduced to constitutional principle alone, since economic distress, military abuses, and the opportunity created by foreign war also mattered.

Division VIII · Sections 75.39–75.43

Charles II, Late Habsburg Adaptation, and the Succession Crisis

The political system that survived the great seventeenth century crises, and why its dynastic future became an international problem.

Section 75.39

The Later Government of Philip IV and the Survival of the Monarchy

After Olivares, the monarchy was governed more cautiously. Luis de Haro, who held the king's confidence until his death in 1661, managed finances and diplomacy without attempting constitutional change. The Cortes of Castile continued to meet in some form, and the crown negotiated with the regional assemblies of the Crown of Aragon. In Catalonia, the restoration of 1652 was followed by conciliation, and in Aragon and Valencia the existing arrangements remained. The government made practical adjustments, such as the reliance on voluntary contributions, on sales of offices, and on the collaboration of local elites in raising troops, which were less ambitious than the Union of Arms but more feasible.

The fiscal situation remained difficult. The crown suspended payments again in 1647 and 1652, and the currency suffered repeated manipulations, with vellón alternately inflated and deflated. The war with Portugal continued, as did the war with France until 1659, and the monarchy depended on increasingly narrow groups of creditors. American silver arrivals declined in the middle of the century, though historians disagree about how far this reflected reduced production, rising local use in the Americas, or increased contraband and evasion. The monarchy maintained a large navy and army by seventeenth century standards, but it faced the need to economize.

Dynastic problems added to the strain. Philip IV's first wife, Elisabeth of France, died in 1644, and his son Balthasar Charles, the heir, died in 1646 at the age of sixteen. The king married his niece Mariana of Austria in 1649, and their children died young, except Margaret Theresa, later empress, and Charles, born in November 1661. The survival of a single weak male heir created a succession problem that would dominate the next decades. Philip IV died on 17 September 1665 and left a monarchy that had lost Portugal in all but name, had ceded territory to France, and had avoided collapse.

That survival is a significant fact. The monarchy had endured two massive revolts, repeated defeats, and plague, and yet in 1665 it still held the Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, and the American empire. The explanation lies partly in the willingness of local elites to continue cooperating with the crown on negotiated terms and partly in the absence of a rival power capable of dismantling the monarchy in the short term. It lay also in the political skill of the king and his ministers after 1643 in restoring consensus. The system had been tested and found flexible enough to survive.

Section 75.40

Charles II, Regency, Court Factions, and Political Government

Charles II was a child of three when he succeeded in 1665, and he suffered throughout his life from poor health, which has been the subject of much speculation about hereditary disorders arising from the dynasty's repeated intermarriage. His physical and mental limitations were real, though contemporary and later accounts exaggerated them. His mother, Mariana of Austria, served as regent under a governing junta set up by Philip IV's will, and she relied heavily on her Jesuit confessor, the Austrian Juan Everardo Nithard, who became a minister and inquisitor general. Nithard's foreignness and his influence caused resentment among the Spanish nobility, and in 1669 he was forced from office.

The central political rivalry was between the regent's circle and Don John of Austria, Philip IV's illegitimate son, who commanded armies and attracted support from nobles and cities. After Charles formally came of age in 1675 at fourteen, Don John pressed his claim to political leadership, and in 1677, with military backing, he forced the queen mother from court and became first minister, ruling until his death in 1679. His government attempted reforms in finance and administration, but it was short. His successors, the duke of Medinaceli and then the count of Oropesa, continued the effort to restructure the monarchy's finances. Charles's marriage in 1679 to Marie Louise of Orléans, a French princess, and after her death in 1689, in 1690 to Mariana of Neuburg, a German princess, did not produce an heir, intensifying competition among the powers.

The king's weakness has often been presented as evidence of national collapse, but government continued through institutions and factions. The councils functioned, the viceroys governed, and noble families managed estates and offices. Court factions, whose rivalry reflected differences over policy toward France and the Empire and over patronage, competed through petitions and intrigue. The crown also resumed negotiations with the regional assemblies. The Cortes of Aragon met in 1677–1678, and Charles also convened the Cortes of Navarre in 1677. Valencian representatives sought a new meeting, but their petition did not result in a Cortes: the kingdom’s last such assembly had met in 1645. The Catalan Cortes had last been convened in 1626 and resumed without agreement in 1632; they did not meet again during Charles II’s reign. The Castilian Cortes were not reconvened after 1665, and the crown instead negotiated tax renewals directly with the voting towns. These differences reveal that continued bargaining did not require every representative assembly to meet.

Reforms did occur. The government of Medinaceli carried out a major monetary stabilization in 1680, which reduced the value of the copper coinage and ended decades of inflation, though it caused short term disruption. Oropesa's government in the later 1680s reduced royal expenditure, restructured debt, and attempted to improve the collection of taxes. Historians have increasingly emphasized that the Caroline period was not a time of simple drift, and that it experimented with administrative and fiscal remedies. Their success was limited by war, by the unpredictability of court politics, and by the monarchy's structural constraints.

Section 75.41

Economic Adjustment, Regional Recovery, and the Debate over Spanish Decline

The economic history of late seventeenth century Spain requires regional analysis. Castile's interior, devastated by plague, emigration, heavy taxation, and rural indebtedness, suffered depopulation and stagnation in many areas, and some towns declined dramatically. Seville, which had been the center of the American trade, lost its position to Cádiz, where the merchants of other European nations increasingly operated through Spanish intermediaries. Foreign merchants, especially Dutch, English, French, and Genoese, supplied manufactured goods to the American market and took much of the silver, a situation that Spanish observers deplored but that reflected the structure of trade and the limited competitiveness of some Spanish manufactures.

But elsewhere the picture was different. The Mediterranean periphery of the Crown of Aragon showed signs of recovery in the later seventeenth century. Catalonia, despite war and devastation, saw rising population and commercial growth, with exports of wine, brandy, and textiles, and a growing urban and rural economy that would accelerate in the following century. Valencia, though depopulated by the expulsion, recovered by resettlement and agricultural investment, with silk and fruit production. The Basque and Cantabrian regions maintained commercial and maritime activity, and Galicia and the north benefited from the spread of maize in agriculture. Population in some regions began to rise by the 1680s and 1690s.

Fiscal and monetary changes also shaped recovery. The stabilization of 1680 and later measures restored a more predictable currency, and the crown's reduction in the burden of juros and of some taxes after 1680 relieved pressure. Smuggling and contraband, in the Americas and Iberia, meant that official records understated trade. Meanwhile, the collapse of silver imports from official channels did not mean the collapse of the Atlantic economy, since silver continued to circulate in the colonies and through private networks. The evidence is complex, and different indicators sometimes point in different directions.

The idea of uninterrupted Spanish decline emerged from contemporary criticism, from writers of the Enlightenment, and from nationalist reflections on lost greatness, and it has been qualified in modern research. Historians such as John Elliott and Henry Kamen, while differing in emphasis, have stressed the need to distinguish political and military decline relative to France, demographic crisis in Castile, and economic change. The seventeenth century saw real setbacks, but also adaptation and, in some regions, recovery. A more accurate description is of a monarchy whose relative power declined, whose center of gravity shifted, and whose economy was restructured and uneven, rather than one that experienced continuous collapse. The Historical Debate that follows explores the causes of weakness.

Section 75.42

The Defense of Spanish Possessions and the Growing Pressure of Louis XIV

After 1659 the principal external threat to the monarchy was Louis XIV's France, whose resources, army, and ambitions grew rapidly. Louis married Maria Theresa in 1660 and, citing the unpaid dowry, argued that her renunciation of inheritance claims was void. When Philip IV died in 1665, Louis advanced a claim, based on a local law called devolution, to parts of the Spanish Netherlands, and in the War of Devolution of 1667 and 1668 French armies overran Flemish territory and Franche-Comté. The Triple Alliance of England, the Dutch Republic, and Sweden forced Louis to settle, and under the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1668 France retained a series of fortified towns in Flanders, including Lille, while returning Franche-Comté.

In the Dutch War from 1672, Spain joined the Dutch against France in 1673, and the Spanish Netherlands and Franche-Comté again became theaters of war. The Treaties of Nijmegen in 1678 and 1679 ended the conflict, with Spain ceding Franche-Comté and a number of Flemish towns to France. Louis then pursued the policy of reunions, using legal arguments and courts to claim territories alleged to have been dependent on lands he had acquired, and in 1683 and 1684 he fought a short war with Spain, taking Luxembourg. The truce of Ratisbon in 1684 recognized his gains for twenty years. The monarchy was repeatedly forced to accept territorial losses because it could not defend all its possessions.

The Nine Years' War, from 1688 to 1697, saw Spain in the Grand Alliance against France, together with the Dutch Republic, England, and the Austrian Habsburgs. French armies invaded Catalonia and in 1697 captured Barcelona, but Louis, seeking to improve his position for the coming succession question, returned Catalonia and other conquests in the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697. The restoration of Spanish territory was an indication that Louis viewed the monarchy as a prize to be secured intact rather than dismembered. The war also saw the revolt of the barretines, a peasant uprising in Catalonia in 1687 to 1689, directed against the quartering of troops and the impositions of the military.

Throughout these wars, Spanish strategy was defensive and coalition based. It relied on the Dutch and English for naval and financial support, on the Austrians for military help in the Empire, and on its own diminished resources. The monarchy also defended its overseas possessions against raiders, buccaneers, and foreign commercial penetration. By the 1690s the balance of power had changed fundamentally. France, with a large population and an efficient fiscal system, was dominant, while Spain and its possessions had become, in the eyes of other powers, the object of a future contest. The broader war narratives belong to Chapter 80, and the importance here lies in the way French pressure and Spanish weakness made the question of the succession a European issue.

Section 75.43

The Death of Charles II, the Succession of 1700, and the Habsburg Legacy

By the 1690s it was clear that Charles II would die without a child. The question of who should inherit his territories was of immense importance to every European power. The principal claimants were two. Louis XIV's son, the Dauphin, had claims through two Spanish princesses: his father's wife Maria Theresa, Charles's half sister, who had renounced her rights at marriage in exchange for a dowry that was never paid, and his grandmother Anne of Austria, a daughter of Philip III. The Austrian Habsburgs, represented by Emperor Leopold I, claimed through his mother Maria Anna, another daughter of Philip III, and through his first wife Margaret Theresa, a daughter of Philip IV. A third candidate, Joseph Ferdinand of Bavaria, the young grandson of Leopold and Margaret Theresa, emerged as a possible compromise.

The great powers, anxious to avoid a union of the Spanish possessions with either France or Austria, negotiated partition treaties. The first, in 1698, assigned the bulk of the monarchy to Joseph Ferdinand, with Naples, Sicily, and some other territories going to France and Milan to the Austrian archduke Charles. Charles II, informed of the plan, was indignant that his dominions were being divided without his consent, and he named Joseph Ferdinand his universal heir. But the prince died in February 1699, and a second partition treaty, concluded in 1700, assigned Spain, the Netherlands, and the Indies to Archduke Charles, the younger son of Leopold, while giving France Naples, Sicily, and other territories. Spaniards of the court, and Charles himself, disliked the prospect of dismemberment, though the Austrian faction remained influential.

In his final months, Charles faced intense pressure from rival court groups and foreign ambassadors. The Council of State and leading ministers, including Cardinal Portocarrero, archbishop of Toledo, concluded that the integrity of the monarchy was best preserved by naming a French candidate, whose grandfather had the resources to defend it. The pope was consulted and reportedly encouraged the same course. On 2 October 1700 Charles signed a will naming Philip, duke of Anjou, the second grandson of Louis XIV, as universal heir to all his dominions, on the condition that the crowns of Spain and France never be united, and establishing a contingent succession that placed Philip’s brother, the duke of Berry, before Archduke Charles, followed by the duke of Savoy, if the preceding heirs could not succeed. Charles died on 1 November 1700. Louis XIV, after some days of consideration, accepted the will, and on 16 November presented his grandson to the court at Versailles. Philip arrived in Madrid in February 1701.

The succession was therefore a legal and diplomatic question as well as a dynastic one, a decision made in an environment of foreign pressure. It also demonstrated that the monarchy still held great value. Its territorial extent, its American revenues, and its strategic position made it a prize. The Habsburg legacy was complex. The dynasty had created a system that mobilized remarkable resources and held together diverse territories for nearly two centuries, but it had not integrated them, and the system's survival depended on negotiated acceptance. The new Bourbon dynasty would try different methods, provoking resistance in the Crown of Aragon during the War of the Spanish Succession, a conflict examined in Chapter 80. The war itself does not belong to this chapter.

Historical Debate 1

Was Habsburg Spain a Centralized Imperial State or a Negotiated Composite Monarchy?

The question: Did the Spanish Habsburg monarchy function as a centralized and increasingly absolutist state directed from Madrid, or was it a collection of territories governed through negotiation and respect for local law?

Interpretation A — A centralizing imperial state: Some historians, especially in older nationalist and institutional histories, emphasized the growth of royal power, the council system, the Inquisition, the shift of the court to Madrid, and Olivares's integration plans as evidence of a state moving toward absolutism and Castilian predominance.
Interpretation B — A negotiated composite monarchy: Other historians, notably John Elliott and Helmut Koenigsberger, stressed that the territories retained their laws, assemblies, and tax systems, that rulers needed to bargain, and that attempts at integration, such as the Union of Arms, were resisted. In this view, the monarchy was held together by the ruler, religion, patronage, and negotiated cooperation.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same sources: royal decrees, council records, proceedings of assemblies, and political writings. They differ in whether they treat Castilian dominance and royal ambition as the essential pattern, or the persistence of local constitutional rights and the pragmatic limits of royal power.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the revolts of 1640, the later survival of the monarchy, and the comparison with France and Britain. It also guards against reading the modern Spanish state backward.

Historical Debate 2

What Weakened Spanish Power in the Seventeenth Century?

The question: Was the seventeenth century weakening of Spanish power primarily caused by imperial overextension, fiscal and institutional structures, domestic economic problems, or external competition?

Overextension and structure: One tradition emphasizes the monarchy's multiple commitments and the mismatch between dynastic ambitions and resources, together with institutional features such as a polycentric government and fiscal dependence on Castile.
Domestic economy and demography: Another stresses Castile's demographic and agrarian difficulties, plague, and monetary disorders, arguing that the economic base weakened. Critics reply that regional recovery and contraband complicate the claim.
External competition: A third emphasizes the rise of France, the Dutch Republic, and England, with greater fiscal and commercial efficiency, so that relative decline reflected rivals' strength as much as Spanish weakness.

Evidence: Revenue and expenditure records, demographic data, trade registers, diplomatic correspondence, and the outcomes of wars. Each type has gaps and is read differently.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians describe the period, either as decline, as relative change, or as adaptation. Most current scholarship treats several factors as interacting rather than choosing one.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Monarchy under Charles V, Philip II, Philip IV, and Charles II

What Changed

  • The ruler's style: from the itinerant Charles to the court bound Philip II, to government through favorites under Philip III and Philip IV, to a regency and ministerial government under Charles II
  • The balance of military power: from Habsburg predominance in the sixteenth century to French predominance after 1659
  • The territorial extent: the addition of Portugal in 1580, its loss in 1640, and cessions to France in 1659, 1668, and 1678
  • The financial system: from German and Genoese banking to Portuguese financiers and finally to a reduced dependence on foreign credit

What Stayed the Same

  • The composite structure, with separate laws and assemblies in the constituent kingdoms
  • The council system, the use of viceroys, and the reliance on written consultation
  • The Catholic identity of the monarchy and its use of religion for legitimacy
  • The heavy fiscal dependence on Castile and the recurrent use of credit and payment suspensions

Who experienced change most directly: Castilian taxpayers, soldiers, and the populations of Catalonia, Portugal, and the Netherlands experienced the most direct effects of war and fiscal pressure. Who experienced greater continuity: many communities in the Iberian interior, in the Americas outside the mining zones, and in Italy experienced governance by familiar local institutions. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the monarchy's stability rested on institutional continuity beneath changes in policy, and that its relative decline was not a sudden fall.

Section 75.44

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The Spanish Habsburg monarchy between 1516 and 1700 was a political structure of exceptional scale and resilience. It was assembled by marriage and inheritance, enlarged by conquest and succession, and maintained by a combination of dynastic loyalty, religious legitimacy, patronage, credit, and negotiation. Its rulers followed distinct styles. Charles V traveled and fought across Europe, and bequeathed problems of obligation and finance. Philip II governed through paper from Madrid and El Escorial, defended the dynasty's interests in the Netherlands, the Mediterranean, England, and Portugal, and faced repeated fiscal crises. Philip III and Lerma adjusted commitments and expelled the Moriscos. Philip IV and Olivares attempted a more integrated monarchy and met the revolts of 1640. Charles II's government preserved the monarchy until a dynastic succession opened a new European war.

The institutions of the monarchy, councils, viceroys, secretaries, assemblies, tax systems, and credit contracts, are best understood as the means by which a dispersed dynasty negotiated with the communities that composed it. The council system gave territories a voice but made coordination difficult. Castile bore much of the fiscal load because its Cortes could be managed and its resources were accessible, while other territories defended their privileges. Asientos and juros allowed the monarchy to convert future revenue into immediate military payments, and suspensions represented negotiated renegotiations rather than simple collapse. American silver strengthened credit but never closed the gap between obligations and available funds.

The monarchy was also a society and a culture. Its religious institutions, including the Inquisition and statutes of purity of blood, shaped lives and excluded communities, culminating in the expulsion of the Moriscos. Its economy was regionally varied, with expansion and crisis in different places and times. Its culture produced Cervantes, Velázquez, and the School of Salamanca, whose moral and legal reflections on conquest and sovereignty form part of the monarchy's history. A balanced assessment must hold these achievements and coercions together.

The monarchy was thus both a powerful political structure and a collection of territories whose integration remained fundamentally limited. Its power was real, in armies, fleets, finances, and administration, and its limits were equally real, in constitutional privileges, slow communications, and fiscal constraints. The revolts of 1640 show how these limits could produce crisis, and the divergent outcomes in Catalonia and Portugal show how contingent such crises were. The decline of Spanish power relative to France, though evident by 1659, was neither uniform nor uninterrupted, and the monarchy that passed to Philip V in 1700 still held vast territories. How that inheritance would be reorganized, and how the other powers would respond, is the subject of the chapters that follow, beginning with the Bourbon monarchy of Louis XIV in Chapter 76.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Spanish Habsburg monarchy between 1516 and 1700 as a functioning dynastic political system, neither a unified Spanish nation state nor a loose collection of territories destined to disintegrate. It began with the dynastic foundations: the union of Castile and Aragon under the Catholic Monarchs, the Burgundian inheritance acquired through Maximilian's marriage, and the accidents of death that brought Charles of Ghent to the Spanish crowns in 1516 and to the imperial title in 1519. Each territory received him on its own terms, and his imperial office did not give him authority over his hereditary kingdoms.

The reign of Charles V established the monarchy's central problems. Habsburg Valois rivalry in Italy, Ottoman pressure in Hungary and the Mediterranean, and the confessional division of Germany all demanded resources that came principally from Castile and from bankers. The Comuneros and Germanías showed that early Habsburg government could provoke serious resistance, and the Peace of Augsburg and the abdications of 1555 and 1556 showed the limits of imperial religious authority and the practical necessity of dividing responsibilities between Spanish and Austrian branches.

Philip II shifted the monarchy's center of gravity to Castile, establishing his court at Madrid and governing through correspondence and councils. His reign saw the Dutch Revolt, the Morisco revolt in Granada, the victory at Lepanto, the acquisition of Portugal, the failure of the Armada, and intervention in France. These wars should not be read simply as the work of a fanatical or incompetent ruler. They reflected genuine dynastic and strategic commitments, pursued with limited information and with resources that could not be concentrated in any one theater.

The mechanisms of government and finance explain how the monarchy operated. The council system gave territories institutional representation but made coordinated decision making difficult. The Iberian kingdoms retained distinct privileges, and the monarchy's other possessions were governed by viceroys and governors who negotiated with local elites. Castilian taxation, American silver, asientos, juros, and Genoese bankers formed a financial system in which payment suspensions were negotiated renegotiations. Archival research has shown that many suspensions reflected liquidity problems rather than simple insolvency, a distinction essential to understanding the monarchy's resilience.

Religion, society, and culture shaped the monarchy's identity. The Catholic Church and the Inquisition were instruments of legitimacy and control, and statutes of purity of blood and the treatment of conversos and Moriscos defined communities of inclusion and exclusion. The economy varied by region, and the Golden Age produced major achievements in literature, painting, and political and legal thought alongside coercion and inequality. The expulsion of the Moriscos between 1609 and 1614 displaced roughly a quarter million to 300,000 people, an act of state coercion with profound human and local consequences.

The reign of Philip III revealed an important change in the form of royal power, the rise of the valido, and a period of strategic restraint known as the Pax Hispanica. The Truce of 1609 with the Dutch and the Treaty of London in 1604 relieved military pressure, but the monarchy could not turn the pause into structural reform, and the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War drew it back into continental conflict.

Under Philip IV, Olivares attempted to distribute the burdens of defense more equally through the Union of Arms. The attempt met constitutional resistance, and the pressure of war with France after 1635 contributed to the revolts of 1640. Catalonia, after a long war and French intervention, returned to Philip IV with its institutions confirmed, while Portugal re-established an independent monarchy under the Braganzas, recognized by Spain in 1668. The two outcomes show that the same structural strains could produce different results depending on political leadership, geography, and foreign support. The treaties of 1648 and 1659 reordered the strategic position of the monarchy and transferred preponderance to France.

The reign of Charles II demonstrated the monarchy's capacity to survive under weak kingship through factional government, monetary reform, and negotiation with regional elites. Economic evidence points to regional recovery, particularly on the Mediterranean periphery, alongside continuing difficulties in the Castilian interior. The simple narrative of uninterrupted decline obscures political, military, demographic, and economic developments that moved at different rates. The pressure of Louis XIV, however, steadily reduced the monarchy's territories in the Low Countries, and the childlessness of Charles II turned the monarchy itself into a European question.

The succession of 1700, in which the dying king named Philip of Anjou his universal heir on condition that the crowns remain separate, ended the Spanish Habsburg line and opened the War of the Spanish Succession, treated in Chapter 80. The legacy of the Habsburgs was a monarchy of remarkable scale that mobilized extraordinary wealth and military power, yet struggled to integrate its territories, control its fiscal obligations, and reconcile dynastic ambition with the rights and interests of its constituent kingdoms. That tension, more than any single ruler's failing, is the chapter's central historical argument.

Composite monarchy
A political structure in which one ruler holds several kingdoms or territories that keep their own laws, institutions, and privileges.
Catholic Monarchs
Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, whose marriage created a dynastic union of crowns without merging their institutions.
Burgundian inheritance
The Netherlandish provinces and Franche-Comté that passed to the Habsburgs through Maximilian's marriage to Mary of Burgundy.
Comuneros
Participants in the urban revolt in Castile of 1520 and 1521 against foreign advisers, taxation, and the king's absence.
Germanías
Armed brotherhoods of craftsmen in Valencia and Mallorca whose revolt of 1519 to 1523 combined social, economic, and religious grievances.

A contemporary idea of the emperor's special responsibility for the unity and defense of Christendom, often feared by rivals as a bid for domination.
Habsburg Valois wars
The contest between the Habsburgs and the French crown, chiefly in Italy, from 1521 to 1559.
Peace of Augsburg
The 1555 settlement recognizing Lutheran and Catholic rulers' right to determine religion in their territories within the Empire.
Council system
The structure of collegiate bodies, such as the Councils of Castile, Aragon, State, and the Indies, through which the Habsburgs governed.
Valido
A royal favorite who exercised delegated royal authority, as Lerma and Olivares did.
Cortes
Representative assemblies of the Iberian kingdoms that voted taxes and presented petitions, differing greatly in power between Castile and the Crown of Aragon.
Fueros
Legal privileges and constitutional rights of kingdoms and communities, which constrained royal action, especially in the Crown of Aragon and Navarre.
Viceroy
A royal representative governing a kingdom or large territory in the king's name, as in Naples, Sicily, New Spain, and Peru.
Alcabala
A Castilian sales tax that was the largest regular source of crown revenue, often collected through fixed agreements with towns.
Millones
A Castilian consumption tax introduced in 1590, voted by the Cortes in large sums and levied on basic goods.
Asiento
A contract under which a banker or merchant provided money or services to the crown, repaid from designated revenues.
Juro
A long term annuity secured on a specific royal revenue, used to consolidate royal debt.

The crown's halt of payments on certain debts, followed by negotiated settlement, a renegotiation rather than a permanent default.
Spanish Road
The overland military route from Milan through Savoy, Franche-Comté, and Lorraine to the Netherlands.
Iberian Union
The union of the crowns of Portugal and Spain under one ruler from 1580 to 1640, in which Portugal retained its own institutions.
Cortes of Tomar
The Portuguese assembly of 1581 that accepted Philip II as king on terms preserving Portuguese laws and offices.
Inquisition
A royal tribunal, established in 1478, that investigated heresy and religious offenses and supervised books and belief.
Limpieza de sangre
Statutes of purity of blood excluding descendants of converted Jews and Muslims from certain offices and institutions.
Moriscos
Descendants of Muslims forced to convert to Christianity in the Iberian kingdoms, expelled between 1609 and 1614.
Conversos
Converts from Judaism to Christianity and their descendants, often called New Christians.
Mesta
The privileged association of Castilian sheep owners that managed migratory flocks and their routes.
Arbitristas
Writers who proposed remedies for the monarchy's fiscal, economic, and demographic problems.
Pax Hispanica
The period of relative peace and strategic restraint associated with the reign of Philip III, roughly 1604 to 1618.

Olivares's 1626 proposal for a common reserve army to which all the monarchy's territories would contribute.
Corpus de Sang
The killing of the viceroy in Barcelona on 7 June 1640, which opened open revolt in Catalonia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows early modern state formation in its most demanding setting. Chapter 74 described the instruments by which rulers raised money, armies, and obedience. The Spanish Habsburgs used all of them, yet their monarchy reveals how far those instruments could be applied before they met the limits set by law, distance, and the political rights of the governed. The monarchy's history is therefore a case study in the negotiated character of early modern power, as well as in the extraordinary reach of a dynasty with Atlantic and Mediterranean commitments.

The chapter also reveals processes that shaped later developments. The fiscal and military strain of the seventeenth century contributed to the transfer of European preponderance to France, whose monarchy is the subject of Chapter 76. The Dutch republic that emerged from the monarchy's wars, treated in Chapter 77, became a model of an alternative political and commercial order. The separation of the Spanish and Austrian branches shaped the Austrian Habsburg state treated in Chapter 78, and the succession crisis of 1700 opened the contest examined in Chapter 80. The pattern of dynastic accumulation, negotiated governance, and credit financed warfare did not disappear in 1700, and it continued to characterize other European monarchies.

Continuities also survived the Habsburg era. The Bourbon dynasty inherited a monarchy of separate kingdoms, though it would reduce the privileges of the Crown of Aragon in the following years. The Catholic identity that the Habsburgs cultivated, and the exclusion of religious minorities that accompanied it, shaped Iberian and colonial society long afterward. The administrative structures of the Americas, developed in the Habsburg period, continued to organize colonial government until the late eighteenth century, and the memory of the monarchy and its Black Legend influenced how later generations understood Spain.

Finally, the chapter contributes to Volume III's argument that Europe's early modern states were shaped by religious conflict, overseas expansion, and the pressures of war. The Spanish Habsburg monarchy combined all three on an unmatched scale, and its experience shows that power in this era depended less on a single ruler's will than on the continuing negotiation among crown, elites, creditors, and communities. Students should carry this idea into the next chapters, where other states met similar pressures with very different solutions.
