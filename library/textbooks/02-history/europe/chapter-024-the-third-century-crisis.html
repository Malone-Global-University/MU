The Third-Century Crisis

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 260 CE, a reigning Roman emperor was captured by a foreign king and did not return. Valerian's captivity, and the decades of upheaval surrounding it, mark one of the most instructive episodes in Roman imperial history, not because Rome collapsed, but because it did not.

Picture the city of Antioch in the autumn of 260 CE, a great Roman metropolis on the Orontes River, still recovering from a Persian sack only a few years earlier, its markets, colonnades, and baths bearing the scars of a war that had reached deep into what Romans regarded as secure imperial territory. Word had just arrived, carried by frightened refugees and disbelieving officials, that Valerian had been captured by Shapur I near Edessa. Shapur's own royal inscription presents the capture as the result of a major battle, while later Roman traditions sometimes attributed it to negotiations or treachery, and the precise circumstances remain disputed among historians working from these divergent accounts. What is not in doubt is the outcome. For the first time in the history of the Roman Empire, a living emperor, wearing the purple that symbolized the majesty of Rome itself, had become a prisoner of a foreign monarch. He would never return, and later Roman writers, angry and ashamed, would remember his fate as one of the deepest humiliations the empire had ever suffered.

This chapter studies the roughly half century between 235 and 284 CE, a period Roman historians conventionally call the Third-Century Crisis, during which the confident imperial order described in Chapter 22 came under extraordinary strain. In these decades, the Roman Empire endured the murder of an emperor by his own soldiers, a rapid and at times bewildering succession of rival claimants to the throne, invasions and raids along the Rhine, the Danube, and the coasts of the Aegean and Black Seas, a newly assertive Sasanian monarchy that repeatedly defeated Roman forces and ultimately captured a reigning emperor, a severe and poorly understood epidemic remembered by contemporaries as the Plague of Cyprian, a currency that lost most of its silver content within a single generation, and a prolonged western fragmentation beginning in 260 and, by the early 270s, a shorter period in which central Roman, Gallic, and Palmyrene centers of imperial authority coexisted: the central regime under the reigning emperor, the Gallic regime in the western provinces, and Palmyrene power centered on Syria.

It would be easy, looking only at this list, to conclude that the Roman Empire nearly ended in the third century, and that its eventual survival was a matter of luck rather than institutional strength. This chapter argues for a more careful and, in the end, more historically interesting conclusion. The pressures of these decades were real, severe, and mutually reinforcing, but they did not affect every province, every city, or every household in the same way or to the same degree, and the empire that Diocletian inherited in 284 CE, while transformed in important respects, remained recognizably the same political, legal, and military system that had governed the Mediterranean world for centuries. Understanding both the severity of the crisis and the resilience of the imperial system it tested is the central task of this chapter.

Several distinct kinds of pressure converged in these decades, and part of this chapter's work is to keep them analytically separate even as it shows how they interacted. Political pressure arose because the Roman system had never developed a reliable, universally accepted procedure for transferring imperial power, leaving succession vulnerable to the ambitions of army commanders and the loyalties of the soldiers under their command. Military pressure arose because Rome's frontiers, already extensive, faced simultaneous challenges from newly assertive peoples along the Rhine and Danube and from a reorganized and increasingly confident Persian monarchy in the east. Economic pressure arose because the enormous cost of paying and supplying multiple competing armies strained a monetary and taxation system that had not been designed for such demands. Demographic pressure arose, in ways historians can trace only imperfectly, from a serious epidemic that struck the empire in the middle decades of the century. None of these pressures alone would likely have threatened the empire's survival. Together, and reinforcing one another across a period of roughly fifty years, they produced a political crisis of a severity Rome had not experienced since the civil wars that ended the Republic three centuries earlier.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about a word that appears constantly in accounts of this period: crisis. Historians continue to debate how uniform, how continuous, and how severe the difficulties of the third century actually were across the whole of the Roman world, and this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines that question directly. Some regions, particularly along the Rhine and Danube frontiers and in parts of the Balkans, experienced repeated invasion, requisition, and insecurity. Other regions, including much of North Africa, Egypt, and parts of Anatolia, experienced far less direct disruption, and their cities, farms, and trade networks continued to function with a considerable degree of ordinary continuity throughout the period. A single word applied uniformly to the entire Roman world for fifty years risks flattening this regional variation, even as it usefully signals that something unusual and historically significant was happening to the imperial system as a whole.

Finally, this chapter is deliberately bounded. It begins with the murder of the emperor Severus Alexander in 235 CE, a conventional but useful starting point, and it ends with the accession of Diocletian in 284 CE, whose sweeping reforms are the subject of Chapter 25. The Third-Century Crisis is not, in this textbook, a rehearsal for the fall of the western empire two centuries later, a narrative arc examined in its own right in Chapter 28. It is instead a distinct historical episode with its own causes, its own regional variations, and its own resolution, one that reshaped the relationship between emperor, army, and empire well before anyone could have known what the fourth or fifth centuries would eventually bring.

Learning Objectives

  2. Analyze the structural relationship between imperial succession, army loyalty, and civil warfare during this period.
  3. Trace the major military pressures facing Rome simultaneously on the Rhine, Danube, and eastern frontiers.
  4. Describe the rise of the Sasanian Empire under Ardashir I and Shapur I and its consequences for Roman power in the east, including the capture of Valerian.
  5. Explain why civil war among Roman claimants weakened frontier defense even as external threats intensified.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for the Plague of Cyprian and the limits of what historians can responsibly claim about its mortality and mechanisms.
  7. Analyze the causes and regional unevenness of monetary debasement, inflation, and fiscal disruption during the crisis.
  8. Distinguish the Gallic Empire and Palmyrene power from later national independence movements, and explain why both regimes remained fundamentally Roman in form.
  9. Assess Aurelian's role in reunifying the empire without treating his achievement as a purely personal or miraculous rescue.
  10. Describe how soldiers, farmers, merchants, and city dwellers experienced the demands of frontier warfare differently depending on region.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of how severe, uniform, and continuous the "crisis" actually was.
  12. Explain why the Roman imperial system survived largely intact into the reign of Diocletian despite decades of severe disruption.

Historical Context

Chapter 22 described the Roman Empire at the height of its territorial extent and institutional confidence, a vast, integrated Mediterranean system bound together by law, roads, provincial administration, and a shared, if unevenly distributed, prosperity under the long peace historians call the Pax Romana. Chapter 23 turned to a different subject entirely, tracing the emergence of Christianity within this Roman world and the empire's evolving, often uneven, religious responses to it. This chapter returns to the political and military history of the empire itself, but at a moment when the confident order of Chapter 22 began to strain in ways its architects had not anticipated.

Several long-term vulnerabilities, present well before 235 CE, help explain why the empire proved so susceptible to the pressures of the third century. First, Rome had never developed a settled constitutional procedure for transferring imperial power. Since Augustus, succession had depended on some combination of dynastic inheritance, adoption, Senate confirmation, and, crucially, the acceptance of the army, particularly the Praetorian Guard at Rome and the legions stationed on the frontiers. When these sources of legitimacy pointed in different directions, as they increasingly did during the third century, the empire lacked any agreed mechanism for resolving the conflict short of force.

Second, the same military establishment that made Rome secure also made it politically volatile. Legions stationed for years along the Rhine, the Danube, and the eastern frontier could rally around commanders who provided effective leadership, secured supplies and pay, and represented their immediate regional military interests. A commander who met these expectations controlled, in effect, an army capable of making him emperor, whether or not he had sought the position, and whether or not the reigning emperor consented.

Third, the strategic environment facing Rome had already begun to shift by the early third century. In the east, the Arsacid Parthian monarchy that Rome had confronted for nearly three centuries was overthrown around 224 CE by Ardashir I, founder of a new Sasanian dynasty that proved considerably more assertive in contesting Roman control of Syria, Mesopotamia, and Armenia, a development introduced in Chapter 22 and developed further in this chapter. Along the Rhine and Danube, meanwhile, Roman sources increasingly applied collective labels, including Goths, Alamanni, and Franks, to populations and political groupings whose internal cohesion varied considerably and should not be assumed to represent fixed ancient nations or uniformly organized confederations; whatever their precise political organization, these groupings placed new pressure on long-established frontier arrangements.

Fourth, the fiscal and military demands of the Severan dynasty, examined in Chapter 22, had already strained imperial finances before 235. Septimius Severus and his successors had raised military pay substantially to secure army loyalty, a policy that improved short-term political stability while increasing the ongoing cost of maintaining the empire's roughly thirty legions and their auxiliary forces. When the political crises of the mid-third century multiplied the number of armies requiring pay and supply, often simultaneously and in competition with one another, this underlying fiscal pressure became acute.

Historians reconstruct this period from a body of evidence considerably thinner and more uneven than that available for the high imperial centuries examined in Chapter 22. Literary sources are fragmentary, hostile to many of the emperors they describe, and often written well after the events they narrate; the notoriously unreliable collection of imperial biographies known as the Historia Augusta, compiled probably in the late fourth century, must be used with particular caution for this period. Papyri from Egypt, inscriptions, coin hoards, and archaeological evidence of destruction layers, fortification building, and settlement change provide independent and often more reliable evidence, though this evidence, too, is unevenly distributed across the empire's provinces, richer for some regions than for others. This unevenness of evidence is itself part of the reason historians debate how uniform the crisis actually was, a question this chapter's Historical Debate feature addresses directly.

Section 24.1

From Severan Stability to Imperial Crisis, 235 CE

Historians conventionally date the beginning of the Third-Century Crisis to 235 CE, when the emperor Severus Alexander, the last ruler of the Severan dynasty examined in Chapter 22, was murdered by his own soldiers while campaigning on the Rhine frontier. His death is a useful marker, not because it caused everything that followed, but because it removed the last vestige of Severan dynastic continuity and opened the imperial throne to a pattern of military selection that would recur, with only partial interruption, for the next half century.

The soldiers who killed Severus Alexander were reportedly dissatisfied with his handling of Rhine frontier defense and, according to some accounts, with his mother Julia Mamaea's continued influence over imperial policy. In his place, the army raised Maximinus Thrax, a career officer of relatively humble provincial origin who had never held senatorial rank. Maximinus was, in this sense, something genuinely new: the first Roman emperor to reach the throne entirely through military service rather than through senatorial standing, dynastic connection, or Praetorian Guard politics at Rome. His accession signaled, more clearly than any single event before it, that military backing from an army willing to acclaim a commander emperor could now override other traditional sources of imperial legitimacy.

It would be a mistake, however, to imagine that Roman government changed overnight in 235, or that the political system had been entirely stable before that date. As Chapter 22 explained, imperial succession had never rested on a settled constitutional procedure, and the Severan dynasty itself had experienced repeated internal violence, including the murder of Caracalla and a series of contested successions. What changed after 235 was not the underlying structural vulnerability of Roman succession, which had existed since Augustus, but the frequency and intensity with which that vulnerability was exposed, as multiple candidates, often commanding different frontier armies, found themselves simultaneously capable of claiming the throne by force.

Several other conditions converged with this succession crisis to make the following decades unusually difficult. In the east, the Sasanian dynasty founded by Ardashir I around 224 CE was actively consolidating power and probing Roman defenses in Mesopotamia and Armenia, a development this chapter examines further in Section 24.4. Along the Rhine and Danube, Roman sources increasingly applied collective labels such as Goths, Alamanni, and Franks to changing populations and political groupings beyond the frontier; historians debate how cohesive these groupings actually were and caution against imagining them as fixed ethnic nations or uniformly organized confederations, a process examined further in Section 24.3. And the fiscal burden of the enlarged, better-paid army established under the Severans, discussed in Chapter 22, meant that any interruption to orderly taxation and supply could quickly translate into military unrest, since unpaid or poorly supplied soldiers were considerably more likely to turn against their commanding emperor.

This chapter therefore treats 235 as a conventional and useful chronological boundary rather than a single cause. The murder of Severus Alexander did not, by itself, create the pressures examined in this chapter. It did, however, remove the dynastic continuity that had provided at least a partial check on military politics for much of the Severan period, and it inaugurated a pattern, examined in the next section, in which imperial power increasingly depended on the support and acclamation of specific armies rather than on any broader institutional consensus.

Section 24.2

Military Emperors and the Problem of Succession

To understand why the following decades produced so many short-lived emperors, students must grasp a structural mechanism rather than simply memorize a list of names. That mechanism can be summarized in a chain of linked steps. Roman emperors depended fundamentally on the army, since no other institution possessed comparable coercive force, and frontier armies could rally around commanders who provided effective leadership, secured supplies and pay, distributed rewards, and represented their immediate regional military interests, rather than around commanders who had simply won some diffuse personal devotion. Frontier armies faced immediate, often urgent, military threats requiring rapid decision making, decisions that a distant emperor at Rome could not always make quickly enough to satisfy soldiers facing an active enemy. Successful frontier commanders who had earned their soldiers' confidence and whose troops expected continued leadership, pay, donatives, and military success possessed both the armed force and the local prestige necessary to be proclaimed emperor by their own soldiers, whether or not they had sought the position. When soldiers in one region proclaimed their commander emperor, and soldiers elsewhere remained loyal to a different claimant, or proclaimed a rival of their own, the empire acquired more than one simultaneous claimant to supreme authority. Rival claimants then had no institutional mechanism for resolving their competing claims short of armed conflict, and Roman armies consequently fought one another rather than facing external enemies. This civil warfare diverted troops, supplies, and commanders away from frontier defense precisely when external pressures were intensifying, and the resulting frontier defeats or failures further damaged the reigning emperor's legitimacy and prestige, encouraging yet another round of usurpation.

This is best understood as a self-reinforcing feedback loop rather than a simple chain of unfortunate individual failures. Civil war weakened frontier defense; frontier weakness produced defeats; defeats damaged legitimacy; damaged legitimacy invited usurpation; and usurpation produced further civil war. Breaking this cycle required more than simply replacing one emperor with a supposedly better one, since the underlying structural problem, the absence of any agreed mechanism for selecting and confirming an emperor's authority across the whole empire, remained in place regardless of who currently held the throne. This structural understanding is more historically important than memorizing the names of every individual claimant who rose and fell during these decades, and it is the central political explanation this chapter asks students to carry forward into later chapters, including the administrative reforms of Chapter 25.

The Year of the Six Emperors in 238 CE illustrates this instability vividly without requiring detailed treatment of every figure involved. In that single year, the aged proconsul Gordian I and his son Gordian II were raised in rebellion against Maximinus Thrax in North Africa, both died within weeks, the Senate then elevated two of its own members, Pupienus and Balbinus, to joint rule, and finally, following the Praetorian Guard's murder of both men, the young Gordian III, grandson of Gordian I, was proclaimed emperor. The episode matters not because students need to remember each name in sequence, but because it demonstrates, within the compass of a single year, how many distinct sources of potential legitimacy, provincial military command, senatorial election, and Praetorian Guard acclamation, could each produce a rival claimant, and how quickly force could displace each of them in turn.

This political instability should not obscure an important qualification developed further in later sections of this chapter: even amid frequent changes at the very top of the imperial hierarchy, much of the machinery of Roman government, provincial administration, taxation, law courts, and local city councils, continued to function with considerable continuity in many regions. The crisis was concentrated most severely at the level of supreme political authority and frontier military command; it did not necessarily mean that ordinary provincial administration collapsed everywhere at once, a distinction this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines directly.

Section 24.3

An Empire under Pressure on Several Frontiers

Rome's frontiers had always required defense, but the mid-third century brought simultaneous pressure on three widely separated fronts, a strategic problem considerably more difficult than the intermittent, sequential frontier conflicts of earlier centuries examined in Chapter 22. Along the Rhine, Roman sources increasingly used the name Alamanni for changing groups beyond the frontier. The name has traditionally been interpreted as meaning roughly "all men," but its precise derivation and the political cohesion of those labeled Alamanni remain debated, and specialists caution against imagining a single, uniformly organized confederation. Along the lower Rhine, groups called Franks similarly tested Roman defenses; here too, the name most plausibly denotes a shifting coalition of smaller groups rather than a unified nation. These names should not be read as timeless ethnic labels; they describe evolving third-century political formations, whose internal cohesion and organization Roman authors observed and named without necessarily understanding, or caring to record, their internal complexity.

Along the Danube and around the shores of the Black Sea, groups Roman sources call Goths conducted increasingly ambitious raids, at times striking far into the Balkans and even, by sea, into the Aegean, threatening cities in Greece and Asia Minor that had experienced little direct warfare for generations. The death of the emperor Decius in battle against Gothic forces at Abrittus in 251 CE, the first documented instance of a Roman emperor dying at the hands of a foreign enemy in open battle, demonstrated the seriousness of this threat with unusual clarity. Decius's defeat also removed, at a stroke, an experienced military leader from a system already struggling to coordinate defense across widely separated frontiers.

In the east, meanwhile, the newly assertive Sasanian monarchy, examined in the next section, posed a still more serious challenge, since it commanded resources, organization, and ambitions comparable to Rome's own, unlike the generally less centralized frontier groups and shifting coalitions along the northern frontiers. The strategic problem facing any third-century emperor was therefore not simply that Rome faced dangerous enemies, since Rome had always faced dangerous enemies, but that it increasingly faced dangerous enemies on multiple fronts simultaneously, at precisely the moments when internal political division made it hardest to concentrate military resources where they were most needed. An emperor who marched east to confront Persia risked leaving the Rhine or Danube dangerously exposed, and a commander left in charge of an exposed frontier possessed, as Section 24.2 explained, both the incentive and the capacity to make himself emperor rather than wait for reinforcement that might never arrive.

Students should resist the common older habit of describing every frontier crossing during this period as an "invasion" in the modern sense of an organized war of conquest. The evidence suggests considerable variety: opportunistic raiding aimed at plunder rather than territorial control; larger seasonal incursions; occasional negotiated settlement of groups within or near Roman territory, sometimes in exchange for military service; and, especially along the Danube, genuinely large and destructive military expeditions capable of defeating Roman field armies. Distinguishing these different kinds of frontier interaction, rather than collapsing them into an undifferentiated narrative of "barbarian invasion," gives a more historically accurate and more analytically useful picture of what Roman frontier commanders and provincial populations actually faced.

Section 24.4

The Sasanian Challenge in the East

Of all the pressures examined in this chapter, the rise of the Sasanian Empire arguably posed the most serious strategic challenge to Rome, because it confronted the empire not with less centralized frontier groups and coalitions but with a rival state capable of fielding large, organized armies, besieging major cities, and, on one occasion, capturing a reigning Roman emperor. As Chapter 22 noted, the Arsacid Parthian monarchy that Rome had confronted for nearly three centuries was overthrown around 224 CE by Ardashir I, a regional ruler from Persis in southwestern Iran who founded the Sasanian dynasty. Ardashir and his successors strengthened royal authority and promoted a renewed Zoroastrian religious ideology closely tied to kingship, though, as this textbook cautioned in Chapter 22, the new dynasty also retained significant continuities with Arsacid institutions and depended on the cooperation of powerful aristocratic families, and historians increasingly resist older claims that Sasanian government was simply and straightforwardly more centralized than its Parthian predecessor.

Ardashir's son and successor, Shapur I, who reigned from roughly 240 to 270 CE, pursued an assertive policy toward Rome's eastern provinces, contesting control of Mesopotamia, Armenia, and Syria across a series of campaigns. Roman emperors responded with campaigns of their own, with mixed results. The emperor Gordian III died during a campaign against Shapur in 244 CE, under circumstances Roman and Persian sources describe differently. Shapur's forces captured and sacked the great city of Antioch, one of the largest and wealthiest cities of the eastern Mediterranean, on at least one occasion during this period, a shock to Roman confidence that anticipated the far greater humiliation to come.

That greater humiliation arrived in 260 CE. The emperor Valerian marched east with a substantial army to relieve Roman forces near Edessa, and there, in circumstances the surviving sources describe differently, he fell into Shapur's hands. Shapur's own inscription presents the capture as the outcome of a major battle; later Roman literary tradition instead emphasized negotiation, ambush, or betrayal. The precise sequence of events cannot be securely reconstructed from these divergent and interested accounts, but the outcome is not in doubt: the Roman emperor spent the remainder of his life as a Persian captive. No earlier reigning Roman emperor had suffered this fate, making Valerian's capture unprecedented in Roman imperial history to that point. Later Roman writers, deeply embarrassed by the episode, sometimes embellished or moralized the story of Valerian's captivity, and modern historians must therefore separate the securely attested fact of his capture from later legendary elaboration about his specific treatment, some of which likely reflects Roman anxiety and propaganda rather than verified Persian practice.

Valerian's capture reverberated through the rest of the empire in ways this chapter traces in subsequent sections. It exposed the eastern provinces to further Persian pressure at a moment when Gallienus and the central imperial government could not immediately coordinate an effective defense across the region. It opened space for regional military leaders, most importantly Odaenathus of Palmyra, examined in Section 24.9, to assume responsibility for eastern defense on Rome's behalf, initially, and eventually to build independent regional power. And it damaged imperial prestige so severely that Gallienus, Valerian's son and surviving co-emperor, faced renewed challenges to his authority from multiple directions at once. Understanding Valerian's capture as a single, dramatic turning point, rather than as merely one item on a long list of third-century difficulties, helps students see how a single military and diplomatic disaster could cascade into political fragmentation across an entire half of the empire.

Section 24.5

Civil War, Usurpation, and the Fragmentation of Command

Section 24.2 explained the structural mechanism linking succession crisis to civil war. This section examines how that mechanism actually operated across the middle decades of the third century, and why the resulting fragmentation of military command proved so damaging to frontier security.

Contemporary and later sources apply the label "usurper" to a great many third-century claimants, but students should approach this term with some historical caution, a caution this chapter's vocabulary feature examines more fully. The label typically reflects the perspective of whichever claimant ultimately succeeded and whose regime therefore shaped the surviving historical record. A general proclaimed emperor by an entire frontier army, commanding the same legal and religious symbols of imperial authority as any other emperor, and governing his portion of Roman territory through the same provincial and fiscal institutions, might have appeared, to his own soldiers and to the provincials under his authority, every bit as legitimate as his rival at Rome. Only the eventual outcome of the conflict, not any inherent quality of the claim itself, generally determined which contender historical tradition remembers as the rightful emperor and which it dismisses as a usurper.

The practical consequence of this repeated fragmentation of command was severe. Each new claim to the throne typically required military force to establish or defend, meaning that experienced legions and their commanders, urgently needed on the frontiers, were instead drawn toward internal confrontations with rival Roman forces. Gallienus, who ruled for much of the 250s and 260s, faced an unusually large number of such challenges, sometimes fighting several rival claimants in different provinces within the same few years, even as he attempted to respond to Gothic pressure on the Danube and the aftermath of his father Valerian's capture in the east. Some modern historians believe that Gallienus, or officers serving under him, began experimenting with military reforms during this period, including a greater reliance on cavalry forces capable of moving quickly between threatened sectors and a reduced role for senators in direct military command in favor of experienced equestrian and professional officers. These developments should be described carefully rather than presented as a single deliberate reform program; the evidence for exactly how systematic or intentional these changes were remains debated, and historians differ over how directly they anticipated the more thoroughly reorganized field armies of the following century, examined in Chapter 25.

The cumulative effect of this period of internal conflict was a Roman military system increasingly organized around rapid response to whichever threat, internal or external, appeared most immediately dangerous, rather than around the more methodical, long-term frontier planning that had characterized earlier centuries. This shift, born of necessity rather than deliberate design, would prove to have lasting consequences for how the Roman army was organized and deployed in the centuries that followed.

Section 24.6

Disease, Population, and the Plague of Cyprian

Alongside political and military disruption, the Roman Empire faced a severe epidemic during the middle decades of the third century, conventionally dated to roughly 249 through 270 CE, though its precise timing and geographic extent varied by region. Historians call this outbreak the Plague of Cyprian, after Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, whose surviving writings, particularly a treatise called De Mortalitate, describe severe symptoms, high mortality, and profound social disruption in his North African city.

Students should approach this evidence with the same careful, source-critical attitude this textbook applies throughout. Cyprian was not a medical observer providing a clinical case description for later scientific analysis; he was a Christian bishop writing a pastoral and theological work intended to comfort and instruct his congregation amid a terrifying crisis, and to interpret the epidemic within a framework of Christian meaning that emphasized the fleeting nature of earthly suffering and the promise of a better world to come. His description of symptoms, including violent intestinal illness, is nonetheless one of the fuller surviving contemporary accounts of the epidemic's effects, and other, more fragmentary references appear in additional Christian and pagan sources describing high mortality in Rome, Alexandria, and elsewhere.

Modern historians and scientists have not been able to identify the responsible pathogen with confidence. Suggested candidates have included smallpox, measles, and various viral hemorrhagic diseases, but the surviving descriptions are not detailed or systematic enough to support a firm diagnosis, and no ancient DNA evidence comparable to that used for some later epidemics has yet provided a definitive answer for this outbreak. Similarly, no reliable overall mortality figure can be established. Older estimates, sometimes citing extremely high death tolls in specific cities, generally rest on rhetorical or impressionistic ancient statements rather than on the kind of demographic evidence that would allow a defensible quantitative estimate.

What historians can say with somewhat greater confidence is that a serious epidemic, whatever its precise nature, coincided with and likely intensified several of the other pressures examined in this chapter. Disease affecting soldiers concentrated in frontier camps could weaken military manpower and readiness at precisely the moments when frontier defense was most urgently needed. Disease affecting farmers and agricultural laborers could reduce food production and tax revenue. Disease affecting urban populations could disrupt commerce, civic administration, and religious life, the last a subject of particular importance for the confrontation between Christian communities and Roman civic religion examined in Chapter 23. The Plague of Cyprian should therefore be understood as one significant, though scientifically uncertain, stressor operating alongside political, military, and economic pressures, not as an independent catastrophe capable of explaining the crisis by itself, and certainly not as an event whose precise human toll this textbook can respectably quantify.

Section 24.7

Money, Taxation, Supply, and Economic Disruption

No aspect of the Third-Century Crisis has attracted more historical oversimplification than its economic dimension, and this section asks students to resist the tempting but misleading narrative chain that runs directly from monetary debasement to inflation to the collapse of trade to the collapse of cities. The actual picture, as historians have reconstructed it from coin hoards, papyri, and archaeological evidence, is considerably more uneven and more regionally variable.

The clearest and best-documented development is the debasement of Roman silver coinage. The antoninianus, a double-denarius coin introduced under Caracalla, contained a steadily declining proportion of actual silver across the middle decades of the third century, so that by the 260s and 270s coins that still bore a silvery appearance were, in reality, composed overwhelmingly of base metal with only a thin silver wash. This debasement reflected the enormous and recurring need to pay multiple competing armies, sometimes simultaneously, from a treasury that could not easily be expanded through ordinary taxation alone, particularly when tax collection itself was disrupted by warfare, civil conflict, or regional political fragmentation.

Repeated debasement, accompanied by a greatly increased volume of coinage in circulation, contributed to monetary instability and substantial price increases during the third century. The relationship was not simply one in which a coin's purchasing power mechanically followed its silver content; nominal valuation, public confidence, the overall money supply, taxation practice, and regional patterns of circulation all mattered, and historians continue to debate exactly how these factors combined. Surviving Egyptian papyri, which provide unusually detailed price evidence for grain and other commodities, do show price increases across this period. However, historians debate how directly and uniformly this monetary evidence translates into a single empire-wide inflationary narrative, since prices, wages, and the practical use of coinage varied considerably from province to province, and the surviving price evidence is heavily weighted toward Egypt, whose experience cannot simply be generalized to every other region without further evidence.

Fiscal pressure extended beyond coinage into the practical mechanics of supplying Roman armies. Governments increasingly relied on requisition, the direct seizure or compulsory purchase of grain, animals, and transport services from provincial populations, alongside more traditional monetary taxation, particularly in regions where armies were actively campaigning or where monetary tax collection had become unreliable. This shift toward requisition and supply in kind placed a heavy and unevenly distributed burden on communities near active frontiers or major military routes, a burden examined further in this chapter's Everyday Life feature.

What the evidence does not support is a simple narrative of universal economic collapse. Trade did not cease across the Mediterranean world; some networks contracted or reorganized, particularly those most exposed to frontier disruption, while others, especially in regions relatively insulated from direct warfare such as much of North Africa and Egypt, continued functioning with considerable continuity. Agricultural production likewise varied enormously by region, shaped by local security conditions, soil, and administrative circumstances rather than by any single empire-wide economic trend. Some cities suffered severely from warfare, requisition, or reduced civic expenditure; others show little archaeological evidence of disruption during this period at all. The historically responsible conclusion, and the one this chapter asks students to carry forward, is that the third century brought serious and significant economic disruption and reorganization in many regions, driven above all by the fiscal demands of sustained and multiplied military conflict, rather than a single, uniform economic collapse affecting the entire Roman world equally.

Section 24.8

The Gallic Empire and Regional Government in the West

In the aftermath of Valerian's capture in 260 CE, a Roman commander named Postumus, then in charge of forces along the Rhine, was proclaimed emperor by his troops and established a regime that controlled Gaul, Britain, and, at various points, parts of Hispania, independently of the central imperial government based first at Rome and later, under Gallienus, wherever the emperor happened to be campaigning. This regime, which modern historians call the Gallic Empire, persisted under Postumus and a succession of his own claimants and successors until Aurelian's reconquest in 274 CE.

It is essential, and consistent with this textbook's broader caution against projecting modern national categories backward, that students not describe this episode as an early instance of Gallic or French national independence. Postumus and his successors made no claim to represent a distinct Gallic nation separate from Rome. On the contrary, they adopted the full apparatus of Roman imperial legitimacy: they took traditional imperial titles, struck coinage in standard Roman style bearing their own portraits and traditional Roman imperial imagery, appointed consuls and other offices modeled on those at Rome, and presented themselves, to their own subjects and soldiers, as legitimate defenders of Roman order in a region the central government had, in their view, failed to protect adequately. Their central practical justification was frontier defense: the Rhine required constant, immediately responsive military attention that a distant emperor, preoccupied with crises in the Balkans or the east, often could not provide in time.

The Gallic Empire is therefore best understood as a regional Roman imperial government operating during a prolonged breakdown of unified central authority, rather than as a secessionist or nationalist movement in any modern sense. Its existence illustrates an important dimension of the Third-Century Crisis: political fragmentation did not necessarily mean the disappearance of Roman institutions or Roman identity from the fragmented regions. Provincial administration, taxation, law, and military organization within the Gallic Empire's territory continued to operate along recognizably Roman lines throughout its existence, even as its rulers exercised authority independently of Rome's central government.

This does not mean the Gallic Empire was without internal instability of its own; Postumus was eventually murdered by his own soldiers, in a pattern that should by now be familiar to students of this chapter, and his several successors held power only briefly before Aurelian's reunification campaign. The episode as a whole illustrates, in miniature, the same structural vulnerability examined throughout this chapter: a Roman political and military system fully capable of local, competent governance, but lacking any reliable mechanism for resolving competing claims to supreme authority once that authority had fractured along regional lines.

Section 24.9

Palmyra and the Roman East

The eastern counterpart to the Gallic Empire developed more gradually, and students should resist the common oversimplification that Zenobia of Palmyra simply declared an independent empire in a single dramatic act. The actual sequence of events shows a much more gradual escalation from Roman-aligned regional command toward outright imperial rivalry.

Palmyra was a wealthy caravan city in the Syrian desert whose merchants had long profited from controlling trade routes linking the Roman Mediterranean world to Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf, and, beyond them, to India and Central Asia. Following Valerian's capture in 260 CE, a prominent Palmyrene leader named Odaenathus, who already held recognized Roman civic and military rank, assumed responsibility for organizing eastern Roman defense against continued Sasanian pressure. Odaenathus campaigned successfully against Persian forces, recovering territory and prestige for Rome at a moment when no reigning emperor was available to do so personally, and Gallienus, the surviving co-emperor, granted him extensive military authority over the Roman east in recognition of these services. During this period, Odaenathus operated firmly within a Roman framework, presenting his authority as delegated from, and exercised on behalf of, the legitimate Roman emperor rather than as an independent claim to imperial power in its own right.

Following Odaenathus's assassination around 267 CE, under circumstances the sources describe only vaguely, his wife Zenobia assumed effective control of Palmyrene power, ruling in the name of their young son Vaballathus. Under Zenobia's direction, Palmyrene forces extended their control significantly beyond Syria, into Egypt, a critically important grain-producing province, and into parts of Asia Minor. Exactly when this expansion shifted from continued, if increasingly assertive, service to Rome into open imperial rivalry remains a matter of some historical debate, since Palmyrene coinage and titulature during this period show a gradual, rather than sudden, transition from acknowledging Roman imperial authority to claiming independent imperial status for Vaballathus and eventually for Zenobia herself.

This gradual escalation matters historically because it illustrates how the collapse of unified central authority created space for ambitious and capable regional leaders to expand their power incrementally, testing at each stage how far the weakened center could or would respond, rather than through a single decisive act of rebellion. Only when Palmyrene ambitions had grown to encompass major provinces including Egypt, and only when Palmyrene coinage began to omit the reigning Roman emperor's name and image entirely, did the conflict with Rome become an unmistakable and irreconcilable contest for imperial authority in its own right, a contest that Aurelian would resolve through military reconquest, examined in the next section.

Section 24.10

Aurelian and the Reunification of the Empire

The empire's gradual military recovery began before Aurelian's reign; the emperor Claudius II Gothicus won an important victory over Gothic forces at Naissus in 269 CE, a success that eased, though it did not eliminate, pressure on the Danube frontier and that later Roman tradition, for reasons connected to fourth-century dynastic politics discussed in Chapter 25, would associate with the origins of the family of Constantine. When Claudius died of the ongoing epidemic examined in Section 24.6, his general Aurelian succeeded him and undertook, in a reign of only five years, an extraordinary campaign of reunification.

After first confronting serious military emergencies in Italy and the Danubian region, including incursions by the Juthungi and other Alamannic groups that reached alarmingly close to Rome itself, Aurelian turned east in 272, defeating Palmyrene forces in a series of campaigns and capturing Zenobia herself, and reincorporating Syria, Egypt, and Asia Minor into unified Roman administration. He then turned west, where in 274 he defeated the Gallic regime under its final ruler, Tetricus, at the battle of Châlons and reincorporated its territories, restoring nominal unity to the entire Roman world for the first time since 260. Later literary tradition claimed that Tetricus had secretly arranged his own surrender to Aurelian in advance, but the reliability and details of that story remain uncertain, and surviving coinage suggests the Gallic regime remained a functioning and reasonably vigorous government up to the very end. Within Italy itself, facing the psychological shock of enemy raids that had penetrated dangerously close to Rome earlier in the crisis, Aurelian ordered the construction of a substantial new circuit of defensive walls around the city, the Aurelian Walls, whose scale signaled a striking departure from the confident assumption, characteristic of the high imperial centuries examined in Chapter 22, that Rome itself required no fortification against external attack. Aurelian also pursued monetary reform, attempting, with only partial and temporary success, to stabilize the debased coinage examined in Section 24.7, and elevated the cult of Sol Invictus, the "Unconquered Sun," establishing a major temple and priestly college at Rome and associating solar imagery closely with imperial rule; historians have often interpreted this as part of a broader effort to strengthen imperial religious cohesion, a religious development this textbook revisits in connection with Constantine's later religious policy in Chapter 25.

Aurelian's achievements were genuinely remarkable, and later Roman tradition, not without justification, honored him with the title Restitutor Orbis, "Restorer of the World." Students should nonetheless resist the temptation to present his reign as a single-handed personal rescue of a nearly extinguished empire. Aurelian's successes depended on foundations laid by his immediate predecessors, including Claudius II's Danubian victory; on the continued functioning, in many provinces, of Roman taxation, recruitment, and supply systems despite decades of political fragmentation; on experienced professional officers and armies whose basic military effectiveness had never disappeared, whatever emperor they happened to be serving; and on the relative exhaustion, by the early 270s, of several of the empire's most pressing external and internal rivals. Aurelian's reign demonstrates the empire's underlying institutional resilience as clearly as it demonstrates his own considerable military skill, and this chapter treats his reunification as evidence for that resilience rather than as an isolated act of individual heroism.

Aurelian's own assassination in 275 CE, by officers acting on a fabricated rumor of his displeasure, is a final, almost ironic illustration of the persistent structural vulnerability examined throughout this chapter: even the emperor who had done more than any other individual to restore imperial unity remained, in the end, as exposed to the political dynamics of military loyalty and betrayal as any of his less successful predecessors.

Section 24.11

Soldiers, Civilians, Cities, and Everyday Life in a Time of Crisis

The political and military narrative traced in this chapter's earlier sections risks obscuring an important truth: the Third-Century Crisis was experienced very differently depending on where a person lived, what work they did, and how directly their community lay along a contested frontier or a major military route. This section, together with the Everyday Life feature that follows, asks students to consider these varied experiences directly.

Soldiers themselves experienced the period as one of both danger and opportunity. Frontier campaigning against Persian, Gothic, Alamannic, or Frankish forces carried obvious risk, and disease within crowded military camps, discussed in Section 24.6, compounded the ordinary hazards of ancient warfare. At the same time, soldiers whose loyalty was actively courted by rival claimants to the throne, discussed in Section 24.2, could find their pay increased, their donatives multiplied, and their political influence considerably enhanced compared to the more settled conditions of earlier centuries, a dynamic that helps explain why usurpation remained such a persistent temptation for ambitious commanders throughout the period.

Provincial civilians near active frontiers faced a different set of pressures: the danger of raiding or invasion, certainly, but also the burden of requisition, discussed in Section 24.7, as passing armies, whether loyal to the reigning emperor or to a rival claimant, demanded grain, transport animals, and lodging from local communities regardless of which government those communities considered legitimate. A farming household near the Rhine or Danube might find itself supplying, in rapid succession, the requisitions of a legitimate frontier garrison, a usurper's forces passing through, and then an invading raiding party, with little practical ability to distinguish among these demands or to resist any of them.

Civilians in regions further removed from active campaigning, by contrast, particularly in much of North Africa, southern Hispania, and Egypt, experienced considerably greater continuity. Cities in these regions continued to function, local elites continued to compete for civic office and status, markets continued to operate, and the epidemic discussed in Section 24.6 likely posed a more immediate and universally shared danger than warfare or political instability did for most of their inhabitants. This unevenness of experience is precisely why this chapter's Historical Debate feature cautions against treating "crisis" as a single, uniform condition affecting the entire Roman world identically for fifty consecutive years.

Merchants and traders, meanwhile, had to adapt to shifting political boundaries, debased currency, and disrupted transport networks in some regions, while continuing established commercial patterns in others. A merchant trading within Egypt or across the relatively stable waters of the eastern Mediterranean south of the main conflict zones might experience the crisis chiefly through inflation and currency uncertainty, discussed in Section 24.7, rather than through direct physical danger, while a merchant whose livelihood depended on routes through Gaul or the Balkans faced far more direct disruption to the physical safety and predictability of trade itself.

Section 24.12

Crisis, Resilience, and the Empire Diocletian Inherited

Following Aurelian's assassination in 275 CE, a brief and less eventful period of transitional rule followed under Tacitus, Florianus, and then Probus, whose reign from 276 to 282 continued the pattern of vigorous frontier campaigning against Rhine and Danube peoples while consolidating the reunification Aurelian had achieved. Probus, too, was eventually murdered by his own soldiers, a final, almost formulaic illustration of the persistent structural vulnerability this chapter has traced throughout. His successor Carus ruled from 282 to 283, followed briefly by his sons Carinus, who governed the west, and Numerian, who campaigned in the east. Numerian's death in 284, under circumstances contemporaries found suspicious, led the assembled eastern army to proclaim Diocletian, a commander of relatively humble Illyrian background, as the new emperor. Carinus, however, still controlled the western provinces and did not immediately accept Diocletian's claim; he would remain a rival emperor until his own defeat and death in 285, a final contest for undisputed authority that belongs properly to the opening of Chapter 25 rather than to this chapter's narrative.

By 284, the Roman Empire that Diocletian inherited had survived an extraordinary sequence of difficulties: repeated imperial assassination and usurpation; simultaneous military pressure on the Rhine, Danube, and eastern frontiers; the capture and death in captivity of a reigning emperor; a severe and prolonged epidemic of uncertain but evidently substantial mortality; a currency debased to a small fraction of its former silver content; and a prolonged western fragmentation that, by the early 270s, briefly produced three coexisting centers of imperial authority. Any one of these difficulties, by itself, might have severely damaged a less institutionally robust state. Together, sustained across roughly half a century, they represent one of the most severe internal challenges the Roman imperial system faced before the very different circumstances of the fifth century examined in Chapter 28.

And yet the empire Diocletian inherited in 284 remained, in its fundamental outlines, the same political and legal system that had governed the Mediterranean world for nearly three centuries. Roman law continued to be administered; Roman taxation, however strained and however increasingly supplemented by requisition, continued to be collected; Roman cities, with significant regional variation in their fortunes, continued to function as centers of local government, commerce, and civic life; and the Roman army, whatever its internal political turbulence, remained a formidable and largely intact military institution capable, as Aurelian had demonstrated, of reasserting unified control over a fragmented empire within a matter of years rather than generations. This underlying continuity, alongside the genuine severity of the disruptions this chapter has traced, is the paired conclusion this chapter asks students to hold together.

What the crisis had exposed, unmistakably, was that the political and administrative arrangements inherited from Augustus and refined by the Severans, examined in Chapter 22, were no longer adequate to manage an empire facing simultaneous multi-front military pressure, a fiscal system strained by the sustained demands of competing armies, and a succession process with no reliable mechanism for preventing repeated civil war. Diocletian, whose accession closes this chapter, would spend much of the following two decades attempting a systematic redesign of imperial government intended to address precisely these structural weaknesses, a story this textbook takes up directly in Chapter 25.

Historical Vocabulary
The Third-Century Crisis

The Third-Century Crisis is a modern historical label, not a term contemporaries used, applied to the period of roughly 235 to 284 CE, during which the Roman Empire experienced unusually frequent changes of emperor, severe and simultaneous frontier pressure, a serious epidemic, monetary debasement, and the temporary political fragmentation of imperial authority into separate regional regimes. The term is useful shorthand, but, as this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines directly, historians increasingly caution against treating it as describing a single, continuous, empire-wide catastrophe of uniform severity. Effects varied considerably by region and by decade, and much of the ordinary machinery of Roman government continued functioning throughout the period in many provinces.

Historical Vocabulary
Usurper and Imperial Legitimacy

A usurper is, in ordinary usage, someone who seizes political power illegitimately. Students should use this term with some caution when studying the third century, however, since the label very often reflects the perspective of whichever claimant eventually won and whose version of events shaped the surviving historical tradition. A general proclaimed emperor by an entire Roman army, wielding the same imperial symbols, coinage, and administrative institutions as any other emperor, might have appeared entirely legitimate to his own soldiers and provincial subjects at the time. This textbook uses "usurper" only where it reflects a genuinely contested and ultimately unsuccessful claim, and encourages students to ask, whenever the term appears in a source, whose perspective that judgment represents.

Context: Shapur I commissioned this monumental inscription to commemorate his military victories, including his campaigns against Rome. Unlike nearly every other source examined in this chapter, this inscription allows students to view the crisis from outside the Roman imperial tradition entirely, through the eyes of Rome's most formidable third-century rival.

Paraphrase of the Res Gestae Divi Saporis In the inscription, Shapur recounts, in the voice of royal triumph, his victories over successive Roman emperors: he describes defeating and killing Gordian III in battle, compelling the emperor Philip the Arab to sue for peace and pay tribute, and, in a climactic passage, describes surrounding and capturing the emperor Valerian along with a large Roman army near Edessa, after which, Shapur states, the captured Romans were resettled within Persian territory. This is a paraphrase and synthesis of the inscription's Roman-related passages rather than a translation of its full continuous text.

Analysis: This inscription is valuable precisely because it is not neutral reporting; it is a royal victory monument designed to project Sasanian power and legitimacy, both to Shapur's own subjects and to posterity. Its listing of three successive Roman emperors defeated in some fashion, culminating in Valerian's capture, constructs a deliberate narrative of escalating triumph. The inscription's central claim that Valerian was captured is independently secure, corroborated by Roman sources that had every reason to minimize such a humiliation rather than invent it. Its presentation of Gordian III's death and of Philip's peace settlement as a form of tribute must be compared more cautiously with Roman evidence, whose accounts of those two episodes differ from Shapur's triumphalist version in ways that remain difficult to fully reconcile.

  2. What political and religious purposes might this inscription have served for Shapur's own Persian subjects?

  4. How might a Roman account of the same events, written for a Roman audience, differ in emphasis and tone from Shapur's inscription?
Historical Debate

Was There Really a "Third-Century Crisis"?

The question: Does the conventional label "Third-Century Crisis" accurately describe the Roman Empire's experience between 235 and 284 CE, or does it overstate the severity, uniformity, and continuity of the period's difficulties?

Interpretation A — The period constituted a genuine, empire-wide crisis: Historians emphasizing this view point to the sheer accumulation of difficulties within a single half century: an unprecedented rate of imperial turnover and civil war, simultaneous military pressure on multiple frontiers, the unique humiliation of an emperor's capture, a severe epidemic, currency debased to a small fraction of its former value, and, by the early 270s, the outright political division of the empire into three rival centers of authority. Taken together, this interpretation holds, these pressures produced a systemic crisis of governance affecting the empire as a whole, distinguishing this period sharply from the stability of the second century examined in Chapter 22.
Interpretation B — The label flattens significant regional and chronological variation: Other historians argue that the word "crisis," applied uniformly across fifty years and the entire Mediterranean world, obscures more than it reveals. Archaeological and papyrological evidence shows many cities, particularly in North Africa, Egypt, and parts of Anatolia, continuing to function with considerable ordinary continuity throughout the period. Roman administration, taxation, and law persisted even within the supposedly "breakaway" Gallic and Palmyrene regimes, which remained thoroughly Roman in structure and self-presentation. The most severe disruptions were concentrated in specific regions, especially frontier zones, and in specific shorter episodes, such as the years immediately following Valerian's capture, rather than continuously across the entire half century.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying body of literary, papyrological, numismatic, and archaeological evidence examined throughout this chapter, but differ in how much weight to give the most dramatic political and military episodes relative to the quieter continuities visible in regions less directly affected by warfare.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how the following chapter, on Diocletian's reforms, should be understood: as a desperate rescue of a nearly collapsed system, or as a systematic redesign of institutions that had, in fact, already demonstrated considerable resilience under severe but regionally uneven strain.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, then explain which combination of "crisis" and "continuity" language you find most historically accurate, and why an entirely one-sided description, in either direction, would misrepresent the evidence.

Living through Fragmented Authority

From the perspective of a legionary soldier on the Rhine frontier: Loyalty is owed, above all, to the commander who ensures pay, supply, and competent leadership in battle; whether that commander wears the purple by Senate decree, by inheritance, or by the army's own acclamation matters far less than whether he can be trusted to keep his soldiers alive and adequately provisioned.
From the perspective of a farming family near the Danube frontier: Armies passing through, whether loyal to the reigning emperor, to a rival claimant, or hostile raiders from across the river, all make the same basic demands for grain, animals, and shelter, and a household's survival often depends less on which side eventually wins than on avoiding the attention of any of them.
From the perspective of a municipal council member in a North African city: Distant political upheavals at Rome or on the Rhine seem, from this vantage point, less pressing than the ordinary business of civic administration, tax collection, and public building, which continues largely as before, interrupted more by the epidemic than by any change of emperor.
From the perspective of a Palmyrene merchant family: Palmyra's wealth has always depended on carefully balanced relationships among Rome, Persia, and the desert trade routes between them; as Roman central authority weakens, expanded Palmyrene military and political power protects trade and prestige, even if it eventually brings the city into direct and dangerous conflict with Rome itself.
From the perspective of a Sasanian court official: Rome is not the unchallenged center of the world but a rival western empire, presently divided and militarily embarrassed, whose emperor's capture near Edessa provides fitting material for royal inscriptions proclaiming the King of Kings' triumph over a formerly arrogant neighbor.

  • The eastern strategic environment was permanently altered by the more assertive Sasanian monarchy

What Stayed the Same

  • The basic territorial extent of the empire remained substantially intact, with the major provinces reunified by 284
  • Roman law, provincial administration, and civic institutions continued to operate, with significant regional variation, throughout the period
  • The Roman army remained a formidable and largely intact institution, capable of reunifying a fragmented empire within a matter of years
  • Latin and Greek remained the empire's administrative and cultural languages, and traditional Roman civic and religious symbols, including imperial titulature, persisted even within regional breakaway regimes

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both real transformation and underlying continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating the Third-Century Crisis as a near-collapse indistinguishable from the later end of the western empire, or minimizing the genuine and lasting changes it produced in how Roman government and the Roman military actually operated.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Imperial Rule Become So Unstable after 235?

Long-term causes

The absence of any settled constitutional procedure for imperial succession, the army's central political role since Augustus, extensive and costly frontier commitments, and the changing strategic environment produced by Sasanian consolidation in the east.

Short-term causes

The murder of Severus Alexander in 235, the accession of Maximinus Thrax through military rank alone, renewed and simultaneous frontier emergencies on the Rhine, Danube, and eastern frontiers, and repeated challenges to reigning emperors by rival commanders.

Transformation

Imperial authority became repeatedly contested through military acclamation, producing recurring civil war among Roman forces themselves rather than sustained, coordinated frontier defense.

Immediate effects

Frequent changes of emperor, diversion of armies from frontier defense to internal conflict, monetary debasement to fund competing forces, and, following Valerian's capture, the temporary division of the empire into three regional imperial centers.

Long-term consequences

Precedents for a more mobile, professionally commanded military, a fortified imperial capital, and an urgent recognition, inherited directly by Diocletian, that imperial government required systematic administrative and military redesign.

Historical complexity

No single factor, whether succession politics, external invasion, epidemic disease, or monetary failure, explains the crisis by itself; each pressure intensified the others, and the empire's regions experienced this interaction with markedly different severity.

Historical complexity: External enemies did not simply overwhelm a previously healthy empire, and internal politics alone did not create the Sasanian and frontier threats; economic and demographic stress interacted continuously with military and political instability throughout the period.

Everyday Life and Social History

Supplying a Frontier Army, c. 260 CE

This composite reconstruction, drawing on evidence from papyri, inscriptions, and archaeological findings across several frontier provinces, follows the ordinary chain of people whose labor kept a Roman frontier army in the field during the crisis. A farmer along the middle Danube produces grain across the growing season, aware that a portion beyond ordinary taxation may be requisitioned outright if a nearby army requires supply, regardless of whether that army serves the reigning emperor or a rival claimant passing through the region. A town official in a nearby settlement is responsible for organizing this requisition, balancing the competing demands of imperial authority, local subsistence, and, at times, conflicting orders from more than one claimant to legitimate command.

A merchant with carts and draft animals is compelled, under the Roman practice of compulsory transport service, to carry requisitioned grain toward the frontier camp, a service that disrupts his own ordinary trade but for which he may receive only partial or delayed compensation, paid, if at all, in increasingly debased coinage. At the camp itself, a soldier receives his rations and irregular pay, aware that the silver content of his wages has declined noticeably within his own years of service, and aware, too, that his own continued loyalty, and that of his comrades, may determine whether the current emperor remains in power at all. A household near the frontier, meanwhile, may lose sons to military levy, face billeted soldiers demanding lodging, and live with the ever-present possibility that the same road carrying friendly supply wagons might, in a different season, carry an enemy raiding party instead.

This chain illustrates the chapter's central argument in concrete, human terms: maintaining Roman armies during the Third-Century Crisis was never merely a matter of military strategy. It reorganized taxation, transportation, local government, household economy, and the daily rhythms of provincial life for the ordinary people whose labor, whether willing or compelled, ultimately sustained every emperor, legitimate or contested, who claimed the Roman purple during these turbulent decades.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Roman Empire's experience of severe political, military, economic, and demographic disruption between the murder of Severus Alexander in 235 CE and the accession of Diocletian in 284 CE, a period conventionally, though not uncritically, called the Third-Century Crisis. It has argued that this crisis is best understood not as a single catastrophe but as the interaction of several distinct, mutually reinforcing pressures whose severity and continuity varied considerably by region.

The chapter began by explaining why the Roman system of imperial succession, which had never rested on a settled constitutional procedure, left the empire vulnerable to a self-reinforcing cycle in which civil war weakened frontier defense, frontier defeat damaged imperial legitimacy, and damaged legitimacy invited further usurpation. It then examined the simultaneous military pressures facing Rome on the Rhine, Danube, and eastern frontiers, giving particular attention to the rise of the more assertive Sasanian monarchy under Ardashir I and Shapur I, whose campaigns culminated in the unique humiliation of Valerian's capture in 260 CE.

The chapter then turned to the demographic and economic dimensions of the crisis, examining the Plague of Cyprian with appropriate scientific caution about its precise mortality and mechanisms, and examining the debasement of Roman coinage and the shift toward requisition as responses to the enormous fiscal demands of sustained and multiplied military conflict, while cautioning against a simplistic narrative of universal economic collapse, since the evidence for prices, trade, and monetary confidence varied considerably from province to province.

A substantial portion of the chapter examined the Gallic Empire under Postumus and the gradual escalation of Palmyrene power under Odaenathus and Zenobia as two regional Roman imperial regimes that emerged from the breakdown of central authority without abandoning fundamentally Roman institutions and self-presentation. Both regimes struck Roman-style coinage, maintained Roman offices, and, at least initially, framed their authority as continuing or defending Roman order rather than as founding a separate nation or people, a distinction essential to understanding this period without projecting later ideas of national independence backward onto the third century.

The chapter's later sections traced the empire's gradual military recovery, beginning with Claudius II's victory at Naissus and culminating in Aurelian's remarkable five-year reign, during which the Gallic and Palmyrene regimes were reincorporated into a reunified empire, Rome itself was newly fortified, and important, if only partially successful, monetary and religious reforms were undertaken. The chapter cautioned throughout against crediting any single emperor with single-handedly rescuing the empire, emphasizing instead the underlying institutional resilience, in taxation, law, provincial administration, and military organization, that made such recovery possible.

Social and regional history received sustained attention throughout, with particular focus on how soldiers, frontier farming households, provincial merchants, and urban civic elites experienced these decades in markedly different ways depending on their proximity to active conflict, a variation the chapter's Everyday Life and Multiple Perspectives features developed in concrete, human detail. Regions removed from the Rhine, Danube, and eastern battle zones, particularly much of North Africa and Egypt, generally experienced far greater continuity in ordinary civic and economic life than the more dramatic political narrative of usurpation and reconquest might otherwise suggest.

The chapter's Historical Debate feature examined directly the question of how uniform, severe, and continuous the crisis actually was across the whole Roman world, weighing an interpretation emphasizing systemic, empire-wide breakdown against an interpretation emphasizing significant regional continuity, and concluding that the conventional label remains useful but requires careful qualification rather than uncritical acceptance.

Finally, the chapter closed by describing the empire Diocletian inherited in 284: a state that had survived assassination, civil war, foreign capture of a reigning emperor, epidemic disease, monetary collapse, and temporary political fragmentation, while retaining the essential institutional structures, law, administration, and military organization, that had governed the Mediterranean world for nearly three centuries. This paired conclusion, genuine crisis alongside genuine resilience, sets up the central question of Chapter 25: what systematic changes would be necessary to prevent the same underlying vulnerabilities from producing the same instability again.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Third-Century Crisis
A modern historical label for the period roughly 235 to 284 CE, marked by frequent imperial turnover, simultaneous frontier pressure, epidemic disease, monetary debasement, and temporary political fragmentation, whose uniformity and severity historians continue to debate.
Military emperor
An emperor whose claim to power rested primarily on the acclamation and continued loyalty of an army, rather than on senatorial confirmation or dynastic inheritance, exemplified by Maximinus Thrax.
Usurper
A term applied to an unsuccessful claimant to imperial power, generally reflecting the perspective of the eventual victor rather than any inherent illegitimacy in the original claim.
Acclamation
The formal proclamation of a new emperor by soldiers, the Senate, or other bodies, a process that during this period increasingly bypassed dynastic or senatorial procedure in favor of direct military action.
Sasanian Empire
The Persian dynasty founded by Ardashir I around 224 CE, replacing the Arsacid Parthian monarchy and pursuing a more assertive policy toward Roman territory in the east.
Shapur I
Sasanian king who defeated several Roman emperors, sacked Antioch, and captured the emperor Valerian in 260 CE, commemorating these victories in the Res Gestae Divi Saporis.
Antoninianus
A double-denarius Roman silver coin introduced under Caracalla whose silver content declined dramatically during the third century.
Debasement
The reduction of precious metal content in coinage while maintaining or altering its face value, used by Roman governments to fund the enormous costs of competing armies.
Requisition
The compulsory seizure or purchase of grain, animals, transport, or labor by military or governmental authority, increasingly used alongside monetary taxation during this period.
Plague of Cyprian
A severe epidemic afflicting the Roman Empire from roughly 249 to 270 CE, described by the bishop Cyprian of Carthage, whose precise pathogen and mortality remain scientifically uncertain.
Gallic Empire
A regional Roman imperial regime governing Gaul, Britain, and at times parts of Hispania from 260 to 274 CE, established by Postumus and maintained by his successors using thoroughly Roman institutions and titulature.
Postumus
Roman commander proclaimed emperor by Rhine forces following Valerian's capture, founder of the Gallic Empire.
Palmyra
A wealthy Syrian caravan city whose leaders, Odaenathus and later Zenobia, assumed responsibility for eastern Roman defense and gradually expanded into a rival imperial power before Aurelian's reconquest.
Odaenathus
Palmyrene leader who assumed extensive Roman-delegated military authority over the east following Valerian's capture, campaigning successfully against Sasanian Persia.
Zenobia
Regent of Palmyra following Odaenathus's death, under whose rule Palmyrene power expanded into Syria, Egypt, and Asia Minor before her defeat by Aurelian.
Claudius II Gothicus
Emperor whose victory over Gothic forces at Naissus in 269 CE marked an important stage in the empire's military recovery before Aurelian's reign.
Aurelian
Emperor from 270 to 275 CE who reunified the fragmented empire, defeating Palmyra and the Gallic regime, fortified Rome, and pursued monetary and religious reform, honored by later tradition as "Restorer of the World."
Aurelian Walls
A substantial new defensive circuit built around Rome under Aurelian, reflecting a departure from earlier confidence that the city required no fortification.
Sol Invictus
The "Unconquered Sun," a solar deity whose cult Aurelian elevated with a major temple and priesthood at Rome, closely associating solar imagery with imperial rule; historians often interpret this as an effort to strengthen religious cohesion, though the precise motive remains an interpretation rather than a directly documented fact.
Year of the Six Emperors
238 CE, during which multiple rival claimants, including Gordian I, Gordian II, Pupienus, Balbinus, and Gordian III, held or contested imperial power in rapid succession.
Frontier confederation
A cautious modern label for collective names such as Alamanni and Franks, which Roman sources applied to shifting populations beyond the Rhine and Danube; historians debate how politically cohesive these groupings actually were and warn against treating the label as evidence of a fixed, uniformly organized ancient nation.
Historia Augusta
A late Roman collection of imperial biographies covering this period, notoriously unreliable in many details and requiring careful, critical use by historians.
Res Gestae Divi Saporis
The trilingual royal inscription of Shapur I on the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht at Naqsh-e Rustam, commemorating his victories over Rome, including the capture of Valerian.
Field army
A mobile military force capable of rapid deployment between threatened frontiers, whose origins some historians trace, with appropriate caution, to military adaptations beginning under Gallienus.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two seemingly contradictory ideas together at once: that the Roman Empire endured, between 235 and 284 CE, one of the most severe internal challenges in its long history, and that the same empire emerged from this period still fundamentally intact, still governed by recognizably Roman law and institutions, and still capable, under Aurelian, of restoring its own unity within a matter of years rather than generations. Holding both ideas together, rather than collapsing the chapter into either an uncomplicated story of catastrophe or an uncomplicated story of resilience, is the central historical skill this chapter has tried to develop.

The structural mechanism at the heart of this chapter, the feedback loop linking uncertain succession, army loyalty, civil war, and frontier weakness, will recur, in modified forms, throughout the remainder of this textbook's treatment of Roman and post-Roman political history. Diocletian's reforms, examined in Chapter 25, respond directly to the vulnerabilities this chapter has traced, and the eventual transformation of the western Roman world, examined in Part IV's later chapters, cannot be understood without appreciating both the severity of third-century instability and the genuine, if partial, success with which Diocletian and his successors addressed it.

This chapter has also modeled an important historiographical lesson: that a convenient label, such as "the Third-Century Crisis," can be simultaneously useful and potentially misleading, depending on how carefully historians qualify it. Students who leave this chapter understanding both why the label captures something historically real and why it requires careful regional and chronological qualification are well prepared to apply the same critical caution to other sweeping historical labels they will encounter throughout the remainder of this textbook, from "the fall of Rome" in Chapter 28 to "the Renaissance," "the Enlightenment," and "the Cold War" in much later volumes.

Chapter 25 now turns to Diocletian, whose accession in 284 CE closes this chapter's narrative and opens a new and deliberate chapter in the history of Roman imperial government, one that would transform the empire's administrative structure, its military organization, and, eventually and unexpectedly, its relationship with the Christian communities first introduced in Chapter 23.
