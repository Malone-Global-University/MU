Augustus and the Creation of the Principate

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Augustus did not abolish the Roman Republic, and he did not restore it. He built, over more than four decades, a system in which the offices, assemblies, Senate, and vocabulary of the Republic continued to exist while one man held a permanent predominance that no republican office had ever allowed. The achievement was not simply that he ruled for a long time. It was that the system he constructed survived his death.

In the years after his death in 14 CE, the Romans caused a long account of his career to be inscribed on bronze pillars set up beside his Mausoleum in Rome. Copies were later carved in stone in the provinces, and one, in both Latin and Greek, survives in remarkable condition on the walls of a temple in Ancyra, the modern Ankara in Turkey. The text is known as the Res Gestae Divi Augusti, "the deeds of the deified Augustus." It lists offices declined, money spent on soldiers and citizens, temples built, wars concluded, and honors refused. It also contains a carefully worded claim about the most important question in Roman politics: how much power did he actually hold? The answer he gave, that he surpassed everyone in prestige while holding no more formal power than any colleague in office, is one of the most consequential and most contested statements in ancient political history.

That statement makes a useful entry point into the chapter's central historical problem. Chapter 20 ended with Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus, the adopted heir of Julius Caesar, standing without a military rival in the eastern Mediterranean in 30 BCE. Chapters 19 and 20 showed how a century of competition among commanders, armies whose soldiers' fortunes were bound to successful generals, and the collapse of restraint in political conflict had destroyed the republican settlement. Octavian therefore faced a problem that Caesar had not solved. He needed to hold overwhelming power without repeating the mistakes that led to Caesar's assassination in 44 BCE, and he needed to persuade a Roman elite that had spent generations denouncing kingship that they were living in a restored commonwealth rather than a monarchy.

This chapter examines how that problem was solved. It does not treat the year 27 BCE as the moment when "the Roman Empire began," a formulation that appears in many popular accounts but misrepresents how the Principate emerged. Instead, it traces a sequence of settlements, powers, honors, provincial commands, military reforms, religious restorations, family arrangements, and political experiments that together produced a durable system. Historians conventionally call this system the Principate, from princeps, "first" or "leading" citizen. Romans did not enact a constitution formally named the Principate, although the Latin principatus came to be used for the ruler's position or reign.

The chapter is organized around the ways that one man's dominance was made permanent and acceptable. It begins with the immediate aftermath of civil war and the settlements of 27 and 23 BCE, then turns to the conceptual heart of the problem: how republican forms coexisted with monarchical reality. It then examines administration, the permanent army, war and diplomacy on the frontiers, religion and imagery, family and moral legislation, the transformation of the city of Rome, and the problem of succession that ultimately tested the entire arrangement. Throughout, it asks what Romans of different positions, senators, soldiers, provincials, women of the elite, and residents of the capital, experienced under the new order.

The evidence is unusually rich but unusually shaped by the regime itself. Augustus's own inscription, coins, monuments, and the poetry of Virgil, Horace, and Ovid were produced under his rule or in dialogue with it. The major narrative histories that survive, by Velleius Paterculus, Suetonius, Tacitus, and Cassius Dio, were written decades or centuries later, sometimes with hostile or retrospective attitudes toward monarchy. Livy, whose history of Rome was one of the great works of the age, survives only in part. Historians must therefore weigh official representation against later interpretation, and this chapter repeatedly asks whose voices are recorded and whose are missing.

This chapter belongs to Part III, Rome and the Mediterranean World, and it marks the transition from the competitive Republic examined in Chapters 15 through 20 to the imperial order examined in Chapter 22. It also belongs to the larger argument of Volume I: Rome's institutions, languages, law, and infrastructure would profoundly shape later European societies, but those legacies took forms that neither Augustus nor his contemporaries could have predicted. Chapter 22 examines how the Augustan system operated across the Roman world once it had outlived its founder, and later chapters follow the fortunes of the empire into Late Antiquity.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Octavian's victory in the civil wars did not by itself create a stable political order, and what problems he needed to solve after 30 BCE.
  2. Trace the settlements of 27 and 23 BCE and explain why the Principate is better understood as a process than as a single founding event.
  3. Distinguish among imperium, potestas, auctoritas, tribunicia potestas, princeps, Augustus, and imperator, and avoid translating them mechanically as "emperor" or "power."
  4. Analyze how republican institutions continued to operate while political competition was transformed by Augustus's resources and prestige.
  5. Describe the administrative arrangements of the Augustan period, including senatorial and imperial provinces, equestrian officials, Egypt's special status, and the census, without anachronistically attributing a modern bureaucracy to Rome.
  6. Assess how Augustus reorganized the Roman army into a permanent professional force and why that reorganization addressed the late Republic's problem of soldiers' fortunes becoming tied to successful commanders.
  7. Evaluate Augustan foreign policy as a mixture of conquest, negotiation, client kingship, and limits, using Spain, the Alps, the Danube, Parthia, and Germania as examples.
  8. Interpret Augustan religion, monuments, coinage, and literature as political communication rather than simple propaganda.
  9. Analyze the marriage and adultery legislation, the role of elite women such as Livia and Julia, and the household as a political institution.
  10. Explain why succession was the central weakness of the Principate and how Augustus arranged for Tiberius to succeed him in 14 CE.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Augustan settlement and use the Res Gestae critically as evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Augustus create a political system in which one man held overwhelming and enduring power while insisting that the Republic had been restored?
  2. Why did Romans of the senatorial class accept, and in many cases actively support, a system that limited their own political competition?
  3. What was the difference between legal powers, such as imperium and tribunicia potestas, and informal authority, such as auctoritas, and why did that difference matter?
  4. How did Augustus address the late Republican problem in which soldiers' fortunes could become tied to successful commanders, and what problems remained?
  5. In what sense was Augustan foreign policy expansionist, and in what sense was it limited or improvised?
  6. How did religion, monuments, literature, and imagery help create acceptance of the new order, and what do they conceal?
  7. Why did Augustus intervene in marriage, adultery, and reproduction, and what does the gap between legislation and elite behavior reveal?
  8. If Augustus was not officially a king, how could his position pass to another person, and why was this problem never fully solved?
  9. How did the experience of the Augustan order differ for senators, soldiers, provincials, women, and residents of Rome?

Historical Context

By the 30s BCE, the Roman state ruled an enormous Mediterranean territory acquired through the conquests examined in Chapters 16 and 17. It was governed, in theory, through the institutions examined in Chapter 15: annually elected magistrates, a Senate composed chiefly of former magistrates, and popular assemblies that voted on laws and elections. These institutions had been designed for a city state and adapted, imperfectly, to a Mediterranean empire. Provincial governors held command for limited terms and often enriched themselves and their supporters. Armies were recruited from citizens, and increasingly from poor citizens, who looked to their commanders for pay and, at discharge, for land.

Chapter 19 traced how this arrangement broke down. Competition for office and command grew more intense as the rewards of empire increased. The reforms attributed to Gaius Marius, the Gracchi's attempts at land reform, the violence of the Sullan period, and the extraordinary commands granted to Pompey and Caesar showed how prolonged commands and expectations of booty, land, and discharge rewards could bind soldiers' fortunes to successful commanders. Chapter 20 followed that logic through Caesar's civil war, his dictatorship, his assassination on the Ides of March in 44 BCE, and the wars of the Second Triumvirate that ended with Octavian's victory over Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII at Actium in 31 BCE and his entry into Alexandria in 30 BCE. Those chapters also recorded the enormous human cost of the wars: proscriptions that killed senators and equestrians, confiscations of land to settle veterans, and the destruction of communities across Italy and the provinces.

By 30 BCE, Octavian controlled the largest concentration of armed force in the Roman world, some sixty legions by many estimates at the peak of the wars, and the treasury of Egypt, which he had absorbed after the death of Cleopatra. He was thirty two years old, the adopted son of a murdered dictator, and identified in the minds of many Romans with the proscriptions and violence of the triumviral years. He had risen through the triumviral commission, whose legal term had expired or become contestable, and his position now rested on repeated consulships, military supremacy, and accumulated authority. The victory had solved the military question. It had not solved the constitutional, financial, or social ones.

The political problem had two sides. First, the senatorial elite had lost its ability to function as a competitive governing class, and many of its members had died or been ruined. Yet Roman political culture retained a powerful hostility toward kingship, a tradition traced to the expulsion of the kings and refreshed by the assassination of Caesar. Any settlement that appeared to restore monarchy would risk the same fate. Second, the army and the Italian and provincial populations who had suffered through two decades of war wanted stability, land, and security. A settlement had to address both audiences: it had to reassure the elite that the Republic remained, and reassure the soldiers and the people that the wars were over and that promised rewards would be paid.

The evidence for how Augustus addressed these problems is a distinctive mixture. Official and semi official sources include the Res Gestae, inscriptions recording laws and honors, coins, and public monuments. Contemporary poets, including Virgil, Horace, Propertius, and Ovid, wrote in a cultural world shaped by Augustan patronage, though their attitudes varied from enthusiastic to ironic. Later historians wrote from a distance. Velleius Paterculus wrote under Tiberius and was generally favorable. Tacitus, writing in the early second century CE, opens his Annals with a famously pointed account of how Augustus drew the offices of the state into his own hands. Suetonius, a biographer of the early second century, preserved anecdotes, some reliable and some doubtful, from the imperial household. Cassius Dio, writing in the early third century in Greek, provides the fullest surviving chronological narrative, but by his time monarchy was the accepted form of Roman government and his interpretation reflects that fact.

Section 21.1

Victory without a Constitution, 30–28 BCE

Octavian's first tasks after Alexandria were practical. He had to pay and discharge a vast army, settle veterans on land without repeating the confiscations of the triumviral years, restore financial confidence, and reassure the Senate and people. The size of the military problem is suggested by the fact that the legions stood at something like sixty at their peak during the civil wars, while the peacetime state could not sustain a force of that size. Over the following years the army was reduced to roughly twenty eight legions, a process examined more fully in Section 21.6. Veterans were settled in colonies in Italy and the provinces. In the Res Gestae, Augustus claimed to have paid for land purchases from his own resources rather than confiscating from existing owners, and he gave figures running to hundreds of millions of sesterces. Those figures are his own claims, but the broad policy of purchase in place of confiscation is consistent with the political need to distance the new order from the proscriptions.

The seizure of Egypt provided the means. Cleopatra's Egypt had been the richest kingdom in the eastern Mediterranean, with a tradition of centralized taxation and a grain surplus of enormous importance to the city of Rome. Octavian did not make it an ordinary province. He appointed an equestrian prefect and forbade senators from entering it without his permission, a measure that reflected both its wealth and the danger that a rival could use it as a base. This decision, discussed further in Section 21.5, was an early sign of a principle that would define the Principate: the princeps would control the resources on which military and political power depended.

At the same time Octavian took care to present himself as a restorer rather than a conqueror of his own city. In 29 BCE he celebrated three triumphs over Illyrian peoples, Actium, and Egypt, but he declined some of the more extravagant honors offered to him. The gates of the temple of Janus, closed only in times of universal peace, were shut, marking symbolically that the wars had ended. In 28 BCE he began a program of restoring dilapidated temples, which the Res Gestae counts as eighty two in a single year, and dedicated a temple to Apollo on the Palatine adjacent to his own house. He also carried out, with Agrippa, a census and a revision of the Senate's membership. The Senate had swollen to perhaps a thousand members under Caesar and the triumvirs, including many men of dubious background, and its membership was reduced, ultimately to about six hundred. The reduction improved the Senate's dignity and gave Octavian a chance to reward his supporters and remove opponents while presenting the process as moral reform.

Octavian held the consulship annually from 31 BCE through 23 BCE, and it was through this republican office, together with his military supremacy and accumulated authority, that he governed in these years. The arrangement was legal, but it presented an obvious problem. It concentrated a repeated magistracy in one man and blocked the ordinary circulation of offices among the elite. It resembled the reliance of Marius, Sulla, and Caesar on repeated or extraordinary offices, precedents that Romans knew and feared. Whatever the intentions of the young leader, the arrangement was unlikely to reassure the aristocracy indefinitely.

Section 21.1 therefore shows a leader who had won a war and was carefully constructing a peace. What he lacked was a stable legal and political basis for his position. The response, in 27 BCE, was a carefully staged constitutional performance whose meaning has been debated ever since.

Section 21.2

The First Settlement, 27 BCE

In January 27 BCE, in a session of the Senate whose choreography was probably planned in advance, Octavian declared that he was returning control of the state to the Senate and Roman people. The Senate responded by pressing him to retain responsibility for the provinces where armed force was still needed. A few days later it voted him the honorific name Augustus, a term with religious associations of reverence and sanctity, and a laurel wreath and other honors for his house. Octavian had reportedly considered the name Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome, but abandoned it because it carried an association with kingship. The choice of Augustus is telling. It expressed distinction and sacred prestige without any constitutional content.

The practical core of the settlement was a division of provinces. Augustus accepted, for a term of ten years, a large provincial command consisting of Spain, Gaul, Syria, and other regions where military force was most needed, and where most of the legions were stationed. The remaining provinces, generally those that were long pacified and lay in the interior of the Mediterranean world, such as Africa and Asia, were left under the traditional system of governors selected by the Senate. He governed his own provinces through deputies, called legates, whom he appointed and who acted in his name, rather than in person. The imperial provinces, as they came to be called, were thus the frontier zones. The senatorial provinces, in ordinary conditions, held few or no legions.

The division should not be presented as a permanent constitutional structure. The provinces changed category over the reign as circumstances required, Cyprus and Gallia Narbonensis among them, and Egypt was always exceptional. Augustus's provincial command was renewed in successive periods, initially for ten years and then for five or ten year terms, in a repeated ritual of apparent reluctance and public gratitude. The division mattered because it gave the princeps command over most of the army while leaving the Senate with the appearance and some of the substance of governing the rest.

Why did the Senate cooperate? The evidence suggests that many senators, exhausted by decades of civil war and its risks, welcomed a settlement that guaranteed order and returned to them some of the offices and dignities of the Republic. Others, whose fortunes depended on Augustus's favor or whose families had been decimated, had little alternative. The elite was also divided by generation and background. Many members of the Senate had been created by Caesar, the triumvirs, or Augustus himself. A settlement that preserved senatorial offices and prestige, even at the cost of real political independence, was attractive compared to the alternative of renewed violence.

The year 27 BCE is important as the moment when a new political vocabulary was publicly established, but it is not a magical founding date. Augustus's position in 27 remained legally dependent on a consulship held year after year and on a provincial command granted for a term. The powers of 27 were adjusted, extended, and redesigned over the following years, and the first major restructuring occurred within four years.

Section 21.3

The Second Settlement and the Powers of the Princeps, 23 BCE

The reasons for the second settlement lay in a crisis in 23 BCE. Augustus fell seriously ill, so much so that he expected to die. According to the later tradition, he handed his official papers to the consul and gave his signet ring to Agrippa, a gesture read by contemporaries and modern historians as a sign of whom he trusted to manage the state, although the accounts vary in detail. His young nephew and son in law Marcellus, whom some Romans had seen as a potential heir, died in the same year. There is also evidence, of uncertain date and detail, of a conspiracy against Augustus involving senators around this time. Whether or not the accounts fully agree, the events of 23 BCE exposed the fragility of a system that depended on one man holding an annually renewed consulship.

Augustus recovered and reorganized his position. He resigned the consulship, which freed one of the two annual places for other senators and answered complaints about monopolizing a magistracy. In its place he received a set of powers that did not require an annual election. The most important of these was tribunicia potestas, the power associated with the tribunes of the plebs, magistrates traditionally charged with protecting ordinary citizens and empowered to veto measures, propose legislation, and convene the Senate. It also conferred a special inviolability. Augustus did not become a tribune. His personal sacrosanctity had already been established earlier, and from 23 BCE tribunician power became the principal public marker of his position. He held it for life, while inscriptions numbered its years annually. It became the most persistent legal foundation of his position and a model for later emperors, who received it as part of their accession.

The second major element was a form of imperium that allowed him to retain command over his provinces without holding the consulship and extended his authority in a way that other governors could not match. Ancient sources and modern scholars disagree about the exact form and date of this superior imperium. Some scholars argue that a grant of authority greater than that of other provincial governors was made in 23 BCE, while others place it in later years, and the terminology used in ancient sources is often unclear. What is clear is that, by the end of the 20s BCE, Augustus's authority no longer depended on the consulship, that his command could be exercised in Rome and in the provinces, and that it was superior in practice to that of the regular governors and magistrates.

Further additions followed. Dio reports that in 19 BCE Augustus received consular privileges or powers, including the use of consular insignia, though the exact constitutional nature of the grant is debated. In 12 BCE, after the death of Lepidus, he became pontifex maximus. In 2 BCE he accepted the title pater patriae, "father of the fatherland." The important point is accumulation. There was no single constitutional text that established the Principate. The regime was a bundle of powers, honors, and precedents assembled over time, each individually defensible in republican terms, but collectively creating a position without republican parallel.

This pattern of accumulation helps explain why Roman writers themselves struggled to describe it. Augustus was not a king, since he did not use the title. He was not a dictator, an office he had refused. He was not simply a consul, since he held powers no consul possessed. The label most commonly used, princeps, is deliberately vague. It identified a first among equals in a way that could be interpreted, according to the observer, as either modest or ominous.

Section 21.4

Republic in Form, Monarchy in Practice

The conceptual heart of the Augustan system was the relationship between republican institutions and the concentration of power. Students should avoid two easy formulas. The first says that Augustus abolished the Republic and created an empire. The second says that because the institutions continued and Augustus claimed to have restored the Republic, the Republic essentially continued. Both miss the way in which the institutions changed.

The republican offices persisted. Each year two consuls were elected, along with praetors, quaestors, aediles, and tribunes. The Senate met, debated, and issued decrees, and its role in administration expanded rather than contracted. The popular assemblies continued to meet and to enact laws, at least in the earlier part of the reign. Magistrates continued to hold command in the provinces designated to the Senate and to preside over courts. Augustus himself presented his role as consistent with tradition, and the historian Tacitus, writing a century later, observed that the old names of the magistracies remained in use.

What changed was the political ecology in which these offices operated. Competition among aristocrats for office and command, the engine of the Republic, was no longer open-ended, because the ultimate rewards, command of major armies, victory and triumph, and access to the most important provinces, were now controlled by the princeps. Triumphs, once the crowning distinction of a republican career, were increasingly reserved to the princeps and his family. Successful military commanders who were not members of the imperial house received honors such as triumphal ornaments rather than full triumphs. Augustus influenced elections through his recommendation of candidates, his patronage, and the weight of his prestige. In 5 CE, a system in which senators and equestrians voted in preliminary groups named in honor of Augustus's grandsons Gaius and Lucius helped structure the selection of consuls and praetors, and in 14 CE Tiberius moved elections into the Senate, according to Tacitus, effectively ending the meaningful role of the popular assemblies in choosing magistrates.

Augustus's power rested on several overlapping foundations. First, he commanded the largest military force and controlled the provinces where it was stationed. Second, he held legal powers, imperium and tribunicia potestas, that allowed him to act in Rome and in the provinces, to summon the Senate, and to veto the actions of others. Third, he possessed immense wealth, from his family inheritance, from Egypt, and from bequests, which allowed him to reward soldiers, fund building, pay for games and grain distributions, and support the treasury. Fourth, he was surrounded by a network of patronage: senators, equestrians, provincial elites, and cities in Italy and the provinces owed offices, honors, or benefits to him. Fifth, he possessed auctoritas, the prestige that made his opinion difficult to ignore.

The senatorial class, for its part, was not merely a passive victim. Senators still held governorships, commands, priesthoods, and honors, and the empire could not be governed without them. What they lost was independence. The republican aristocracy had competed by building networks of clients, seeking military glory, and outbidding rivals in popular favor. In the new order, advancement depended on remaining in favor with the princeps. Some accepted this bargain gladly, since it offered security. Others resented it, as later writers such as Tacitus suggest, though Tacitus was writing under a different regime and with hindsight.

It would be a mistake, however, to describe Augustus as an absolute monarch. He could dominate Roman politics but he did not possess a modern state apparatus capable of commanding every outcome. He needed senators to command legions and govern provinces, equestrians to manage finances and lead auxiliary units, city councils across the empire to collect taxes and maintain order locally, and soldiers who remained loyal. He needed the Roman people to accept the settlement, and he spent lavishly to secure their support. He needed to sustain a republican vocabulary in which his power could be described without offending traditional sensibilities. His dominance was therefore extraordinary but structured by dependencies, and those dependencies help explain why the system proved stable.

The modern label "republic in form, monarchy in practice" is a useful shorthand but should be qualified. Romans themselves did not think in terms of modern constitutional categories. The Principate looked like a monarchy to some observers and like a restored republic to others, and this ambiguity was a deliberate and functional feature of the regime.

Historical Vocabulary
Princeps, Augustus, and Imperator

Princeps means "first" or "leading" person. In republican usage it could describe the most distinguished senator or any leading man in the state, and the Senate had a traditional member called the princeps senatus, who spoke first in debate. Augustus adopted the term as a description of his position because it implied precedence without office. It was not initially a formal title equivalent to modern "emperor," and it gave rise to the conventional term Principate for the political system of the early empire.

Augustus was an honorific voted to Octavian in 27 BCE. It suggested reverence and sacred authority and carried no defined legal powers. It later became a formal title of later emperors and, by extension, an imperial name.

Imperator in the Republic was a title acclaimed by soldiers to a victorious commander. Augustus used it as a personal name (praenomen), and it became associated with the supreme commander, but Roman writers did not use it consistently in the sense of "emperor," and students should avoid translating it that way mechanically. The English word "emperor" derives from it, which is one reason for confusion.

Historical Vocabulary
Auctoritas, Potestas, and Imperium

Imperium was the legal authority to command, held by consuls, praetors, and provincial governors, and was the basis of military and judicial power. It was granted for a defined term and area, and the distinction between imperium exercised within the city and outside it was significant in republican law.

Potestas means "power" in the sense of the formal capacity attached to a magistracy. Tribunicia potestas was the specific power of a tribune, including the right of veto, protection of citizens, and personal inviolability.

Auctoritas is harder to translate. It refers to prestige, standing, and the weight of a person's opinion, deriving from achievement, family, and reputation rather than office. It was not a legal power. The Augustan claim to surpass others in auctoritas while holding no more potestas than colleagues, examined in the Primary Source Window, depends on the distinction between them. The three terms are not synonyms, and confusing them obscures the Augustan system.

Section 21.5

Governing the Roman World

Augustus inherited an empire governed through a mixture of senatorial governors, tax contractors, local elites, and client rulers. He did not replace it with a centralized bureaucracy. What he did was to add layers of officials chosen and controlled by himself, establish more regular procedures, and improve the collection of information. Historians speak of development rather than a fully formed imperial civil service, and students should be wary of modern analogies.

At the top of the system stood the princeps and his household. Augustus relied on his own family, freedmen, and slaves, who managed his private property and, increasingly, tasks that blurred private and public administration. His personal wealth was enormous, and the boundary between his resources and the resources of the state was not clearly drawn. Alongside the traditional senatorial treasury, the aerarium Saturni, an imperial fiscal apparatus gradually took shape, and the military treasury created in 6 CE, discussed in the next section, was a major innovation.

In the provinces, the distinction between senatorial and imperial provinces produced different types of governors. Senatorial provinces were governed by proconsuls chosen by lot from former magistrates for annual terms. The imperial provinces were governed by legates chosen by Augustus from among senators, who held command for longer periods and reported to him, in effect acting as his deputies. Some regions, such as Egypt, were governed by equestrian prefects. Others, particularly smaller or more difficult provinces, were placed under equestrian governors, and financial officers called procurators, who might be equestrians or imperial freedmen, managed revenues on the princeps's behalf and increasingly across the provinces. The role of equestrians expanded as the reign advanced, giving a wealthy but non senatorial elite a career in imperial service that depended on the princeps.

Egypt occupied an exceptional position within the imperial system. It was governed by an equestrian prefect directly accountable to Augustus, and senators and prominent equestrians required permission to enter. This arrangement partly reflected the province's exceptional wealth and grain supply, which was essential to feeding Rome, and partly the danger of leaving such a base in senatorial hands. The first prefect, Cornelius Gallus, fell into disgrace and died around 26 BCE, an episode which showed the dangers of overreaching and the seriousness with which Augustus treated Egypt.

Rome continued to rely extensively on local elites. In Italy and the provinces, cities managed their own affairs through councils and magistrates drawn from local property holders, who collected taxes, maintained order, and organized public life. The imperial government intervened relatively little in daily affairs. Roman provincial administration was thin by modern standards, with a small number of governors and officials supervising hundreds of communities, and it depended on cooperation with local elites who were offered status, security, and, over time, Roman citizenship in return. The role of these local elites, and the broader dynamics of provincial life, will be examined in Chapter 22.

Augustus improved the collection of information and revenue. Censuses were held in 28 BCE, 8 BCE, and 14 CE, and the Res Gestae gives figures of about 4,063,000, 4,233,000, and 4,937,000 Roman citizens respectively. Historians debate what these figures count, whether all citizens or only adult males, and how the sharp increase should be explained, since it may reflect changes in the method of counting or in the extension of citizenship as well as demographic growth. Provincial censuses and surveys assessed populations and property for taxation. Such exercises were sometimes resented, and they provoked resistance in some regions, but they created a basis for a more regular system of tribute. Augustus also established an official postal and messenger system, the cursus publicus, in a limited form, to improve communications along the roads.

The overall effect was a gradual, uneven strengthening of central oversight rather than the establishment of a centralized state. The princeps stood at the center of a web of relationships, and government worked through cooperation, patronage, and delegation.

Section 21.6

Creating a Permanent Military System

The late Republic had revealed a recurring military and political problem: prolonged commands and expectations of booty, land, and discharge rewards could bind soldiers' fortunes to successful commanders. Augustus's solution was not to separate armies from politics but to make the princeps the dominant source of command, pay, promotion, and veteran reward.

The first step was demobilization. After Actium the legions numbered around sixty, and the reduction to approximately twenty eight, and later, after 9 CE, to twenty five, required discharging large numbers of soldiers and finding land or money for them. Veterans were settled in colonies, for example in Italy, southern Gaul, Spain, and the East, at the princeps's expense. The colonies also expanded Roman citizen communities, landholding networks, and military connections in the provinces, and provided a reserve of loyal citizens.

The second step was to establish a permanent standing army. Under the Republic, armies had been raised for particular wars. Under Augustus, the legions became permanent units with continuous existence, numbers, and identities, stationed for long periods in provinces. Service terms were regularized at sixteen years in 13 BCE and extended to twenty years in 5 CE, with an additional reserve period. Legionary pay was about 225 denarii annually, with more for centurions and for the Praetorian Guard, and in 5 CE the standard discharge grant for legionaries was fixed at 3,000 denarii, a substantial sum, although land was also used in some settlements. Officers and soldiers swore an oath of loyalty to the princeps, linking their obligation personally to him.

The financing of discharge benefits was a long term problem. In 6 CE Augustus created the aerarium militare, a military treasury with an initial endowment from his own funds, supplemented by new taxes, notably a tax on inheritances among citizens and a tax on sales at auction. The new taxes proved unpopular, but they reflected the recognition that the regime needed a regular means to support veterans if it were to avoid the recurrent land confiscations of the civil war years. By linking veteran benefits to a central treasury under the princeps, the treasury also strengthened the bond between soldiers and the ruler.

Alongside the legions were the auxilia, units of non citizen soldiers recruited from provincial populations, serving as infantry and cavalry, often specialized in skills such as archery. Auxiliary service later became standardized at about twenty five years, and citizenship on discharge is securely documented from the Julio-Claudian period onward; the precise Augustan arrangements are less clear. Auxiliaries permitted Rome to employ the military skills of subject populations and gave provincials, as arrangements developed, a route to citizenship, but they also meant that provincial communities were affected directly by conscription and military presence. Augustus also established permanent fleets, notably at Misenum and Ravenna, to protect the Mediterranean and suppress piracy, and smaller river fleets on the Rhine and Danube.

In Rome and Italy the princeps maintained a small force of guard cohorts, the Praetorian Guard, chosen from Italian recruits and paid at higher rates than the legions. Augustus stationed only some of the cohorts in Rome itself, and the guard was initially modest in visibility. It served as a personal bodyguard and a force to maintain order, but it would acquire a very different political role under his successors. It should not be described simply as a secret police. Its function was primarily military and ceremonial, and its later political importance was a product of subsequent events. The city was also served by urban cohorts and, from 6 CE, by a firefighting and night watch force, the vigiles, discussed further in Section 21.10.

These reforms did not entirely depoliticize the army. Soldiers' loyalty was to the princeps as much as to the state, and the succession would repeatedly raise the question of who commanded the armies. Frontier armies could proclaim their commanders, as the mutinies on the Rhine and Danube in 14 CE demonstrated when Augustus died, and later civil wars showed. But by concentrating command, pay, and reward in one source of authority, Augustus removed the Republican pattern in which ambitious governors used provincial armies to challenge the center. That achievement, more than any single battle, lay behind the relative stability of his reign.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Soldier's Career under Augustus: A Reconstruction

The following is a composite reconstruction based on general patterns in the evidence, not the biography of a particular individual. It should be read as an illustration of a typical legionary career, and details varied according to period, region, and unit.

A young man, likely from Italy or an early colony in the western provinces, enlisted in his late teens, after passing an inspection of his physical fitness, freeborn status, and citizenship. He swore the military oath to the princeps and began training in marching, fortification building, weapons, and formation drill. He was assigned to a century within a cohort of a legion stationed in a frontier province. Pay was set at a standard rate, but deductions were made for food, clothing, equipment, and a savings fund, so that actual cash in hand was much less than the nominal figure. Camp life combined military routine with labor: building roads, forts, and bridges, and working in quarries and workshops. Discipline was harsh, with punishments ranging from fines and demotion to flogging and, in extreme cases, execution.

Serving soldiers were not permitted to marry legally, although many formed unions with local women and had children, whose status was legally uncertain. This regulation appears to have been a feature of the Augustan system. Possible motives included maintaining mobility and discipline and limiting competing family obligations, but it created family complications that the state eventually recognized. Campaigns exposed soldiers to danger, disease, and the risk of defeat, as the destruction of three legions in Germany in 9 CE showed. After sixteen or twenty years, a soldier who survived could expect discharge with a cash bonus or land in a colony. Veterans often became local notables, and their settlement in the provinces expanded Roman citizen communities, landholding networks, and military connections.

Auxiliary soldiers had a different experience. They were recruited from provincial communities, often served in regions distant from their homes, and endured longer terms and lower pay. Later evidence shows that they could hope for citizenship at discharge, a significant change of status for their families, although the precise Augustan arrangements are less clear. The evidence for the period is fragmentary, drawn from inscriptions, tombstones, papyri, and archaeological remains, which are richer for the later first and second centuries CE, and historians must be cautious about projecting later conditions back onto the Augustan army.

  1. How did the terms of service, pay, and discharge tie the soldier's interests to the princeps and the regime?
  2. How might the prohibition on marriage have shaped the social life of military communities?
  3. What kinds of evidence would historians need to reconstruct the experience of an ordinary soldier, and what does each type omit?
Section 21.7

War, Diplomacy, and the Limits of Expansion

Augustan imagery portrayed the princeps as the conqueror of the world, and the Res Gestae lists peoples brought under Roman power. The reality of policy was more mixed. Augustus's reign saw substantial territorial expansion, particularly in Spain, the Alps, and the Danube region, alongside negotiation with Parthia, the maintenance of client kingdoms, and serious reverses in Germania and the Balkans. Historians increasingly emphasize that Augustan foreign policy was experimental and responsive rather than the execution of a plan of "natural frontiers."

In Spain, campaigns between 26 and 19 BCE subdued the Cantabrians and Asturians in the northwest of the peninsula, ending a long resistance. Augustus led part of the campaign in person but fell ill and withdrew to Tarraco, and the final victory was achieved by his lieutenants, notably Agrippa. The region was then reorganized and settled with veteran colonies. In the Alps, campaigns against mountain peoples from 25 to 15 BCE secured routes between Italy, Gaul, and the Danube. The monument later erected at La Turbie, above modern Monaco, recorded the names of Alpine peoples subdued and expressed the significance of the achievement. The principal commanders in the later Alpine campaign were Augustus's stepsons, Drusus and Tiberius, whose careers became closely connected to the northern frontier.

The Balkans and the Danube saw a long series of campaigns that pushed Roman power deep into southeastern and central Europe. Roman armies operated in Illyricum, Pannonia, and along the Danube, and the territory that would become the provinces of Pannonia, Dalmatia, and Moesia was gradually brought under Roman control. This region was consequential for the history of Europe, since it extended Roman military and administrative rule into regions of central and southeastern Europe that would become deeply integrated into the Latin speaking western empire over subsequent generations. The advance provoked resistance, culminating in a great revolt in Pannonia and Dalmatia between 6 and 9 CE, which Suetonius described as the most dangerous foreign war since the Punic Wars. Rome mobilized enormous resources, including freed slaves, to put it down, and Tiberius led the eventual suppression.

In the East, the principal rival was the Parthian Empire. Instead of launching a large war to avenge Crassus's defeat at Carrhae in 53 BCE, Augustus pursued diplomacy backed by military demonstration. In 20 BCE the Parthian king returned the legionary standards and captives taken from Roman armies. The event was presented in Rome through coins, monuments, and literature as a great victory. The cuirass on the statue known as the Augustus of Prima Porta shows the return of a standard, in an image that transformed a negotiated settlement into a triumph. Armenia, a buffer state contested between Rome and Parthia, remained a recurrent source of tension, handled through client rulers and periodic interventions. Rome also relied on client kingdoms in Anatolia, the Levant, and Africa, whose rulers governed territory on Rome's behalf. Some of these were later annexed, such as Galatia in 25 BCE and Judaea, which became a province in 6 CE when Herod's son Archelaus was removed.

In Germania, the story is one of ambitious advance followed by reverses. Beginning about 12 BCE, Drusus led campaigns across the Rhine and reached the Elbe before dying in 9 BCE. Tiberius and others continued the effort, and by the early first century CE, Roman authorities were attempting to impose more regular administration and taxation east of the Rhine under the governor Publius Quinctilius Varus, in a region whose exact provincial status remains debated. In 9 CE, Varus's column, three legions with auxiliaries, was ambushed and destroyed in the forests of northwestern Germany by a coalition of Germanic peoples led by Arminius, a Cheruscan noble who had served in the Roman army and held Roman citizenship. The precise location of the battle remains debated, though excavations at Kalkriese near Osnabrück have revealed a battlefield of the period that many scholars connect with it. The sources describe severe losses, and Suetonius recorded a tradition that Augustus, in distress, called out to Varus to return the legions, an anecdote whose reliability cannot be established.

The defeat was a shock but should not be portrayed as the end of Roman ambitions east of the Rhine. Roman campaigns in Germany continued in 14 to 16 CE under Germanicus, and only later did Tiberius, after several years of indecisive campaigning, settle on a policy centered on the Rhine. It is anachronistic to describe the Rhine as a preordained border. The frontier developed from a series of decisions, reverses, and adjustments, and the concept of a fixed frontier line was a later development, examined in Chapter 22. Augustus's supposed advice to his successor to keep the empire within its existing limits, mentioned in the sources, was reported by Tacitus and Cassius Dio, and its authenticity and meaning remain debated.

Taken together, Augustan foreign policy demonstrates that the new regime needed both military prestige and restraint. The princeps required victories to sustain his authority and reward his supporters, but expansion was costly, and the regime could not risk major defeats. Diplomacy, client relationships, and selective conquest allowed him to project strength while managing risk.

Section 21.8

Augustus, Religion, and Imperial Ideology

Augustan rule was communicated through religion, ritual, art, architecture, coinage, and literature. Modern scholars often call this "propaganda," but the term can mislead. It suggests a top down manipulation of a passive population, while much Augustan imagery was created by senators, poets, sculptors, local elites, and communities across the empire who had their own reasons for participating. It is better understood as political communication, a continuing negotiation about what the new order was and why it deserved support. Some works were commissioned directly by Augustus or his circle. Others were offered or created by cities, groups, and individuals seeking favor or expressing genuine gratitude.

Religion was central. The civil wars had been accompanied by anxiety about the neglect of the gods, and Augustus presented himself as a restorer of Rome's relationship with them. He rebuilt temples, revived priesthoods and ancient rituals, and carried out the secular games in 17 BCE, a festival celebrating a new age, for which Horace composed a hymn. His office as pontifex maximus, held from 12 BCE, placed him at the head of Rome's priestly colleges. The temple of Apollo on the Palatine, adjacent to his house, reflected his personal devotion to the god who, in his narrative, had supported him at Actium. The temple of Mars Ultor, Mars the Avenger, in the Forum of Augustus, dedicated in 2 BCE, celebrated vengeance on the assassins of Caesar and the return of the Parthian standards, tying family, religion, and military success together.

The themes of Augustan imagery were consistent. Pax, peace, was presented as the result of victory and domination, not simply the absence of war. The Altar of Augustan Peace, the Ara Pacis, decreed in 13 BCE and dedicated in 9 BCE, displays a procession of the princeps and his family, scenes from Rome's legendary origins, and images of fertility and abundance. Pietas, duty to gods, family, and community, was portrayed through Aeneas, the mythical ancestor of the Roman people, as depicted in Virgil's Aeneid, composed under Augustan patronage and left unfinished at the poet's death in 19 BCE. Victory was emphasized through coins and monuments celebrating Actium and the Parthian standards. Restoration was the theme of the temples and the laws. The family, presented as an example, was displayed prominently in art, even though hereditary monarchy was officially denied.

The statue known as the Augustus of Prima Porta, discovered in 1863 at a villa associated with Livia, shows the princeps in military dress, with a breastplate depicting the return of the standards, a cupid at his feet that alludes to his descent from Venus, and an idealized youthful face. It is a valuable source, but it should be treated as an object with its own context, not merely as an illustration. Its date and original setting are debated, and it presents an idealized image whose relationship to any lived reality was deliberately distant.

Literature both supported and complicated the regime. Virgil's Aeneid celebrated Rome's destiny but included ambiguities and tragedy that later readers have interpreted variously. Horace wrote poems that praised Augustus while maintaining an independent tone. Livy produced a monumental history of Rome from its foundations, Tacitus later reports that Augustus called Livy "Pompeianus" because of his praise of Pompey, without ending their friendship; because the relevant books are lost, we cannot know exactly how Livy narrated the civil wars. Ovid, whose Ars Amatoria and later works played irreverently with the regime's moral program, was exiled in 8 CE to Tomis on the Black Sea. The reasons remain unclear, though Ovid himself referred to "a poem and a mistake." The episode shows the limits of tolerated expression.

Beyond Rome, the ruler cult took different forms. In the Greek East, where communities had long honored rulers as divine or divinely favored, cities established temples and festivals honoring Roma and Augustus, as at Pergamon and Nicomedia. In the western provinces, altars to Roma and Augustus were established, most famously at Lugdunum (Lyon) in 12 BCE, where representatives of the Gallic communities assembled annually. In Italy, cults of Augustus's genius and the household gods (lares) were set up in neighborhoods of Rome and in towns, and freedmen served as officials called Augustales. No official state cult at Rome straightforwardly worshipped the living Augustus as a god, although cults of his genius and exceptional honors brought his public status close to divine associations. After his death, the Senate declared him divine (Divus Augustus). The imperial cult was therefore not a single uniform institution but a set of varied practices adapted to local traditions and often initiated by provincial communities themselves.

Not all Romans responded in the same way. Enthusiasm, calculation, gratitude, resignation, and private skepticism coexisted, and the surviving evidence, overwhelmingly created by or for elites, rarely records the views of ordinary people in the city or the provinces.

Section 21.9

Family, Gender, Marriage, and Moral Legislation

The Augustan household was also a political institution. The image of the ruling family, disciplined, pious, and fertile, was a central part of the regime's presentation, and the choices made within it, marriage, adoption, and exile, were affairs of state. This section connects those developments to the broader social history of Roman marriage and gender examined in Chapter 18, but concentrates on the political dimension.

In 18 BCE, and in a further law in 9 CE, Augustus introduced legislation on marriage and adultery. The lex Julia de maritandis ordinibus encouraged marriage and childbearing among the upper classes by imposing penalties on the unmarried and childless, including restrictions on inheritance, and rewarding parents of three or more children with privileges, the ius trium liberorum. The lex Julia de adulteriis made adultery a public offense subject to trial in a special court and punishable by relegation and partial confiscation of property. Fathers were permitted in certain circumstances to kill an adulterous daughter and her partner. The later lex Papia Poppaea of 9 CE adjusted and strengthened the marriage provisions after resistance from elite men.

The motives for this legislation have been debated. One argument holds that Augustus sought to counter a perceived decline in the birth rate among the elite and restore a traditional moral order. Another emphasizes that the laws served political purposes, making the state an arbiter of elite family life, reinforcing the regime's claim to reform, and providing a means to punish opponents. The laws also reflect a concern with the reproduction of the senatorial and equestrian orders after the losses of the civil wars. The claim that Augustus merely restored ancestral practice should be treated cautiously. The legislation was in many respects an innovation, extending state regulation into areas that had been matters for family and censorial oversight.

Enforcement is difficult to measure. Evidence for prosecutions and the effect on marriage rates is limited, and the surviving sources suggest widespread resistance. According to Suetonius, a group of equestrians once protested at a public event, and Augustus responded by displaying a family with many children and rebuking those who resisted.

The most famous case of the gap between ideology and conduct involved Augustus's own household. His daughter Julia, married to Marcellus, then Agrippa, then Tiberius, was exiled in 2 BCE to the island of Pandateria after accusations and proceedings concerning adultery, which the sources describe in detail, though the sensational literary traditions warrant caution, and with a political dimension, since some of her alleged partners were prominent men. Modern scholars debate how much of the accusation was moral and how much political, since a scandal touching senatorial families in the imperial household might also have involved a suspected conspiracy. Augustus's granddaughter Julia the Younger was exiled in 8 CE in similar circumstances. These episodes, together with the poet Ovid's exile, reveal how the regime's moral program could be turned on its own family.

Livia has an important place in this history. Married to Augustus in 38 BCE, she was a prominent public figure, the recipient of honors including a statue and legal privileges, and a patron of temples and dedications. Later hostile writers, including Tacitus and Cassius Dio, portrayed her as a scheming stepmother who engineered the succession of her son Tiberius, even alleging she poisoned rivals. There is no reliable evidence for these charges, which reflect the biases of later writers and the tendency to blame powerful women for the politics of the imperial household. The more defensible picture is of an influential woman working within the possibilities open to elite women in the Roman world, whose position was symbolically important but whose precise political influence is difficult to reconstruct.

Women of the elite generally were not permitted to hold office or vote. They could own property, manage households, and exercise informal influence, and in the Augustan period, their public visibility in imagery and honors increased. But their roles were defined in relation to men and to dynastic ends. Women outside the elite appear in the sources chiefly through inscriptions, legal texts, and occasional literary references, and their experiences of the Augustan order are much less documented.

Section 21.10

Rome Rebuilt: City, Spectacle, and the Princeps

Rome in 30 BCE was a large and crowded city, perhaps close to a million inhabitants by common estimates, though ancient population figures are uncertain, with limited public administration, recurrent fires and floods, and dependence on imported grain. Augustus transformed it physically and administratively. The transformation was a political act, because the city was the stage on which the relationship between the princeps and the people was displayed. Broader questions of urban life across the empire belong to Chapter 22, and this section concentrates on the capital.

Administratively, Augustus established or reorganized offices to deal with urban problems. After a grain shortage in 22 BCE and popular pressure, he assumed responsibility for the grain supply, and later established a prefect of the grain supply (praefectus annonae). He revived and developed the urban prefecture (praefectus urbi), appointing senior senators such as Messalla Corvinus and Statilius Taurus to maintain order, though the office became more firmly institutionalized under his successors, and he organized boards to supervise roads, water, and buildings. In 7 BCE the city was divided into fourteen regions with local officials, and in 6 CE a corps of vigiles, drawn largely from freedmen, was established to fight fires and patrol at night. These innovations improved the functioning of the city while extending the reach of the princeps into daily life.

The physical program was extensive. Agrippa built aqueducts, baths, and other works, including the first version of the Pantheon and the Aqua Virgo. Augustus built or restored temples and public buildings, including his forum with the temple of Mars Ultor, the theater of Marcellus, the Palatine temple of Apollo, and the Mausoleum on the Campus Martius, where the Res Gestae was displayed. Suetonius reports that Augustus boasted that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it one of marble. The statement, whatever its exact form, reflects a real program of monumental construction, although much of the city, including the tenement districts where most people lived, remained crowded and built of cheaper materials, and the claim should not be read as a description of the whole city.

Public spectacle was another instrument of rule. The Res Gestae records numerous gladiatorial shows, athletic contests, animal hunts, and naval battles, and distributions of money and grain to the citizens. These benefactions were essential to the relationship between the princeps and the urban population, who could not vote in the same way as in the Republic but retained political weight through their presence and their ability to acclaim or heckle. Games were also occasions for the display of hierarchy, with seating arranged by status under Augustan legislation.

The population of Rome included citizens, freedmen, and enslaved people, and many immigrants from Italy and the provinces. Many lived in crowded apartment blocks, in conditions of considerable insecurity, and the improvements were uneven. For the urban poor, the princeps was a patron who provided food and entertainment, but also a distant figure whose regime imposed order through new officials and the watchful presence of guards. The experience of urban life under Augustus would be developed and diversified in subsequent generations, and Chapter 22 will examine the mature imperial city and the wider urban system.

Section 21.11

Succession without a Monarchy

The central weakness of the Principate was succession. If Augustus was merely the leading citizen and not a king, there was no constitutional mechanism by which his position could pass to another. Yet without continuity, the settlement could collapse into a new round of civil war. Augustus's solution was to prepare heirs by giving them offices, powers, and public prominence, and to bind them to himself through marriage and adoption, without ever declaring that the position was hereditary.

His first candidate was Marcellus, his nephew, son of his sister Octavia, who was married to Julia in 25 BCE and advanced in office but died in 23 BCE. Agrippa, an older and highly capable associate, was then married to Julia in 21 BCE, and the two produced sons, Gaius (born 20 BCE) and Lucius (born 17 BCE). Augustus adopted the two boys in 17 BCE, marking them as his heirs. After Agrippa's death in 12 BCE, Julia was married to Tiberius, who was required to divorce his wife, Vipsania, an event that appears to have caused him great distress. Tiberius's brother Drusus had died in 9 BCE, leaving Tiberius as the leading commander of the family.

In 6 BCE, Tiberius, who had been granted tribunician power, withdrew to the island of Rhodes, for reasons which the sources describe in various ways, including weariness with public life, frustration with the prominence of Gaius and Lucius, and difficulties in his marriage to Julia. His absence lasted until 2 CE. Meanwhile, Gaius and Lucius were promoted, and Gaius was sent to the East, where he was involved in the settlement of Armenia. Then, in a run of deaths, Lucius died in 2 CE at Massilia and Gaius died in 4 CE after a wound in Lycia, leaving Augustus without direct heirs in the male line.

Augustus responded in 4 CE by adopting Tiberius, then in his forties, and requiring Tiberius to adopt his nephew Germanicus, the son of Drusus, so that Germanicus, who was married to Augustus's granddaughter Agrippina, would follow. Augustus also adopted Agrippa Postumus, the last surviving son of Agrippa and Julia, but Postumus was exiled around 7 CE for reasons that are unclear, and he was killed at the time of Augustus's death or shortly afterward, in circumstances that the sources present in conflicting ways.

Tiberius was then given the powers that would enable him to succeed. He received tribunician power for ten years in 4 CE, and it was renewed. In 13 CE his imperium was made equal to Augustus's, according to the sources, so that the two were, in effect, joint holders of the principal powers of the regime. These measures show how the Principate handled succession: by sharing and extending the components of the princeps's position, so that the successor already held much of it when the founder died.

The history of the succession also displays how contingent the outcome was. The deaths of Marcellus, Agrippa, Gaius, and Lucius, and the scandals involving Julia and Postumus, meant that the final result was a compromise that Augustus had not originally planned. The Principate's survival depended not only on institutional design but on the accidents of mortality, the choices of individuals, and the willingness of the elite and army to accept the outcome. It was a dynastic system without a formal principle of dynastic succession, a paradox which shaped the politics of the following century.

Section 21.12

Augustus's Death and the Principate Established, 14 CE

In his last years Augustus faced the aftermath of the revolt in the Balkans and the disaster in Germany, the exile of members of his family, and growing signs of age. He continued to receive renewals of his powers and carried out a final census in 14 CE. He deposited his will, a summary of his achievements, and instructions for his funeral with the Vestal Virgins. The summary became the Res Gestae.

Augustus died at Nola in Campania on 19 August 14 CE, a few weeks short of his seventy sixth birthday. Tiberius, who had been traveling toward Illyricum, returned to be present at or shortly after the death. The sources give a dramatic account, including the reports of Livia's role and the killing of Agrippa Postumus, which are uncertain. The funeral took place in Rome with great ceremony, his body was cremated on the Campus Martius, and the Senate voted him divine honors. A priesthood was established for his cult, and Livia was designated a priestess in his honor, and the deified Augustus (Divus Augustus) became a permanent presence in Roman religion.

Succession was not entirely smooth. Legions in Pannonia and on the Rhine mutinied, complaining about pay and terms of service, and a period of tension followed before order was restored. In the Senate, according to Tacitus, Tiberius went through a display of reluctance to accept power, and the senators pressed him, in a scene that has been read as the ritualized continuation of the Augustan style. Tiberius nonetheless held tribunician power, imperium, and the backing of the armies, and he assumed effective control.

What had changed between 30 BCE and 14 CE? The Senate, magistracies, and republican vocabulary remained. There was no crown and no formal hereditary monarchy. Yet the essential features of political life had been transformed. A permanent army loyal to one source of command and reward, a princeps holding a bundle of legal powers, a network of provincial governors and officials responsible to him, a redirected aristocracy dependent on his favor, and a religious and cultural landscape that celebrated his role had created a durable system. The most persuasive evidence of that durability was that Tiberius succeeded and the system continued.

Augustus's success should not be seen as inevitable. It depended on his longevity, his skill, the exhaustion of the civil war generation, the availability of resources, the cooperation of the elite, and fortunate outcomes in some of the crises. The dynastic setbacks, the frontier reverses, and the financial pressures showed how vulnerable the system remained. Later emperors would face the same problems with different results. Primary Source Window

Augustus, Res Gestae Divi Augusti, chapter 34, on the Transfer of the Republic

Creator: Augustus, with the text composed in his last years and published after his death in 14 CE · Date: inscribed in Rome and copied in the provinces; the best preserved copy is from Ancyra in Galatia · Type: An official inscription combining autobiography, political justification, and memorial, in Latin with a Greek version

Context: The Res Gestae was intended to be displayed on bronze pillars before the Mausoleum in Rome and reproduced in provincial cities, probably in association with the cult of Roma and Augustus. It has a formal structure, listing offices, expenditures, buildings, spectacles, conquests, and diplomatic successes, and it concludes with a summary of the honors he received. It was written for a wide audience, in Rome and in the provinces, and it presents the achievement of one man in the language of service to the state.

Paraphrase of Res Gestae 34 Augustus states that in his sixth and seventh consulships, after ending the civil wars while holding supreme power by universal consent, he transferred the state from his own control to the Senate and Roman people. As a reward for this act he received the name Augustus by senatorial decree, and afterward he surpassed all others in auctoritas, though he possessed no more potestas than those who were his colleagues in each magistracy. This is a paraphrase of the passage, not a continuous translation.

Analysis: The passage is the most explicit statement of the ideology of the Principate. It presents the events of 27 BCE as a voluntary restoration and locates his authority in prestige, not in legal power. Historians treat it as evidence of how Augustus wished his rule to be understood, not as a neutral description. The distinction between auctoritas and potestas is technically defensible but glosses over facts that the inscription does not emphasize: his control of the provinces with most of the legions, his tribunician and other powers, his financial resources, and his influence over appointments. His claim about "colleagues" applies most plausibly to the formal powers of the offices he held in the earlier period, and it does not account for superior imperium. The document also omits failures, such as the loss of Varus's legions, and the family scandals, and it reflects Roman elite values, with little indication of the perspectives of the provincial populations for whom it was translated into Greek. For those readers, Augustus's role was as much that of a patron and supreme ruler as a restored republican magistrate.

Historical Debate

Did Augustus Restore the Republic or Create a Monarchy?

The question: Was the Augustan settlement a genuine, if modified, restoration of the Roman Republic, a disguised monarchy, or something that does not fit either category? The question has shaped interpretations of Augustus since antiquity and is central to understanding the origins of the Roman Empire.

Interpretation A: Restoration. Republican offices, the Senate, courts, laws, and elections continued to function, and Augustus presented his exceptional powers as compatible with republican precedent. Supporters of this view, drawing on the Res Gestae and the language of contemporaries, note that many senators and other Romans viewed the settlement as a restoration of legitimate government after civil war, and that republican practice had always allowed extraordinary commands.
Interpretation B: Monarchy in disguise. Control of armies, provinces, patronage, and succession made genuine political competition impossible. This view, associated with Tacitus in antiquity and with Ronald Syme's influential twentieth century study of the Roman revolution, argues that Augustus created a monarchy behind a republican façade and that the ruling class was reconciled to loss of freedom by security and reward.
Interpretation C: A new and deliberately ambiguous form. Many recent historians argue that the Principate was neither a restored Republic nor an openly declared monarchy, but a new political order, whose ambiguity was one of its strengths. It allowed diverse groups to interpret it in ways they could accept, and it was capable of adaptation.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same sources, the Res Gestae, inscriptions, coins, monuments, and the narratives of Tacitus, Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, but weigh differently the formal survival of institutions and the practical distribution of power. They also differ in how much weight to give to contemporary perceptions, which are difficult to reconstruct, versus later judgments made with hindsight.

Why it matters: How historians frame the question affects how they understand later Roman politics, the role of the Senate, the nature of imperial authority, and the legacy of Roman ideas of government. The debate also warns students against imposing modern categories such as "republic" and "monarchy" on a Roman political culture that used different distinctions.

Multiple Perspectives

The Augustan Order as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and these groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of a senator: The regime offered stability, honors, and offices, and the empire still needed senators as governors and commanders. But independent competition for glory, command, and popular favor was severely limited, and success depended on remaining in the favor of the princeps.
From the perspective of an equestrian administrator: Service to the princeps created new career opportunities in finance, provincial governance, and military command that had been less available in the Republic, and it offered a route to status based on loyalty rather than birth alone.
From the perspective of a legionary soldier or veteran: Regular pay, defined terms of service, and the prospect of a discharge bonus or land provided greater security than the uncertain rewards of civil war, but service was long and demanding, marriage was restricted, and defeat, as in 9 CE, could be catastrophic.
From the perspective of an auxiliary soldier from a provincial community: Military service offered a possible path to citizenship and a livelihood, but it removed young men from their communities for decades, and the presence of Roman armies could also mean requisition, taxation, and loss of local autonomy.
From the perspective of a resident of the city of Rome: The end of civil war, grain distributions, spectacles, and new public works were welcome, though the urban poor lived in crowded and insecure conditions, and the political voice of the people in elections had diminished.
From the perspective of a provincial city elite: Cooperation with Rome, including participation in the imperial cult, offered predictable taxation, local status, and eventually citizenship, and allowed local elites to preserve their own authority within the imperial framework.
From the perspective of a subject of a recently conquered community, such as in northern Spain, the Alps, or Pannonia: Roman peace could mean military occupation, the loss of independence, forced recruitment, and the disruption of traditional ways of life, and the resistance in the Cantabrian wars and the Pannonian revolt shows that Roman order was not universally accepted.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Octavian's Victory to the Accession of Tiberius

What Changed

  • Command of the army, provincial governance, and the distribution of rewards became concentrated in one person and institutionalized
  • Military service became a permanent profession with regularized pay, terms, and a dedicated treasury
  • Competition for office and military glory was constrained, and triumphs were reserved for the princeps and his family
  • Equestrians and freedmen assumed larger roles in administration, and the imperial household became an institution of government
  • The succession of power was expected to remain within a family and be arranged by the ruler

What Stayed the Same

  • The republican offices, Senate, and much of the legal vocabulary continued in use
  • Provincial government still depended on cooperation with local elites and on thin layers of officials
  • Senators continued to govern provinces and command legions, and aristocratic values of honor and lineage remained central
  • The empire's economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian, dependent on slave and free labor, and on the grain supply of Egypt and other provinces

Who experienced change most directly? Senators, whose competitive politics was transformed, and soldiers, whose careers were restructured. Who experienced greater continuity? The great majority of rural and provincial populations, whose daily lives were governed by local communities, agriculture, and taxation in forms that predated Augustus. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that institutional continuity and political transformation could coexist, and that the Principate should be understood as a change in who controlled the institutions and how they worked, not in their names.

Cause and Effect

How Civil War Victory Became a Durable Principate

Long-term causes

The breakdown of republican competition traced in Chapters 19 and 20, the dependence of soldiers on commanders, the strain of governing a Mediterranean empire with city state institutions, and an aristocracy exhausted by generations of violence.

Short-term causes

Octavian's military supremacy after Actium, the wealth of Egypt, the widespread desire for peace, the cooperation of key senators and generals such as Agrippa, and his skillful use of republican language and precedent.

Event or transformation

The settlements of 27 and 23 BCE, and the accumulation of powers, honors, and structures over four decades, produced the Principate.

Immediate effects

An end to large scale civil war, the reduction and professionalization of the army, restoration of order in Rome and Italy, reorganized provincial government, and a new pattern of aristocratic politics.

Long-term consequences

The pattern of one man's dominance within a republican framework shaped Roman politics for centuries, and the imperial system, examined in later chapters, structured the Mediterranean and European world through Late Antiquity.

Historical complexity

No single factor was sufficient. Military power alone could not secure legitimacy, legitimacy alone could not pay the soldiers, and neither could ensure continuity without an answer to succession. The outcome also depended on Augustus's longevity and contingent events such as the deaths of the heirs.

Historical complexity: Because the same conditions had produced Sulla and Caesar without producing a durable regime, historians look for what was distinctive in the Augustan settlement: the combination of military reorganization, fiscal provision, ideological adaptation, and dynastic management.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Octavian, victorious in civil war in 30 BCE, created a political system in which one man held overwhelming and lasting power while the Republic was declared restored. The central argument is that the Principate was constructed gradually. There was no single constitution and no formal beginning of empire in 27 BCE. There was a sequence of settlements, powers, honors, commands, and precedents that together produced a durable order.

The chapter began with the problems Octavian faced after Actium: demobilizing an enormous army, settling veterans without repeating confiscations, restoring confidence, and avoiding the fate of Caesar. He used the wealth of Egypt, temple restoration, a revision of the Senate, and a carefully managed public image to consolidate his position. In 27 BCE he performed a ceremonial transfer of authority, received the name Augustus, and retained a provincial command that contained most of the legions. In 23 BCE, after a crisis of health and politics, he reorganized his position around tribunician power and an imperium whose precise legal formulation is debated but which was superior in practice to ordinary provincial commands, ending his dependence on the annual consulship.

The conceptual center of the chapter was the relationship between republican form and monarchical practice. The offices, Senate, and assemblies continued, but their political ecology changed because the rewards of command, victory, and office were controlled by the princeps. His position rested on military command, legal powers, wealth, patronage, and auctoritas. The chapter emphasized that he was not an absolute monarch in a modern sense and that his rule depended on cooperation with senators, equestrians, soldiers, and provincial elites. The distinctions among imperium, potestas, and auctoritas, and the meanings of princeps, Augustus, and imperator, are essential to understanding the system.

In administration, Augustus developed a division between senatorial and imperial provinces, employed legates, equestrian prefects, procurators, and members of his household, and treated Egypt as an exceptional administrative case. He improved censuses and revenue collection, but Rome continued to govern indirectly through local elites, and the notion of a centralized bureaucracy would be anachronistic.

The chapter devoted substantial attention to the army. Augustus reduced the legions to a permanent standing force, regularized service and pay, created the military treasury, added auxiliary units and fleets, and established the Praetorian Guard. These measures addressed the late Republican problem of soldiers' fortunes being bound to successful commanders by making the princeps the principal source of command and reward, though they did not fully depoliticize the army, as the mutinies of 14 CE and later civil wars would show.

Foreign policy combined conquest, diplomacy, client relationships, and limits. Campaigns in Spain, the Alps, and the Balkans extended Roman rule, and the Danube region became a lasting part of the Roman world. In the East, the return of the Parthian standards in 20 BCE was a diplomatic success represented as a great victory. In Germany, advances across the Rhine ended in the destruction of Varus's legions in 9 CE, a shock that did not immediately end Roman operations east of the Rhine. The frontiers were the product of decisions and reverses, not a fixed plan.

Ideology was communicated through religion, monuments, coinage, and literature, and interpreted differently by different audiences. The restoration of temples, the priesthoods, the Ara Pacis, the Forum of Augustus, and the poetry of the age expressed themes of peace, piety, restoration, and victory. Imperial cults took varied forms in the East, the West, and Italy. Augustus's marriage and adultery legislation intervened in elite family life, and the scandals involving Julia and others exposed the gap between public morality and household reality. Livia's role should be assessed with caution given the hostility of later writers.

Rome itself was transformed administratively and physically, with new officials, a fire and watch service, regional divisions, aqueducts, and monuments, while spectacle and distributions maintained the relationship between the princeps and the urban population. The succession problem was the most serious structural weakness. Augustus tried a series of heirs, and after the deaths of Marcellus, Agrippa, Gaius, and Lucius, he adopted Tiberius and shared with him the principal powers.

By 14 CE, the Principate had survived its founder. The Senate, magistracies, and republican vocabulary remained, while political power had been concentrated in the princeps. That continuity is the strongest evidence that Augustus had created something more durable than personal dictatorship. The outcome, however, was contingent, dependent on longevity, favorable circumstances, and the cooperation of key groups.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a political system that shaped the Mediterranean and much of Europe for centuries came into being. The Principate was not the inevitable result of Rome's expansion, nor the plan of a single far sighted founder. It emerged from the exhaustion of civil war, the dependence of soldiers on commanders, the survival of republican institutions and vocabulary, and the specific choices of a highly skilled and fortunate leader. Recognizing the contingency of that outcome guards against the teleological habit of reading Roman history as a straight line from republic to empire.

The chapter also illustrates a recurring theme in European political history: that institutions can survive while their meaning changes. The Roman Senate, magistracies, and legal language persisted, yet the distribution of real power moved decisively. The same tension between form and substance would recur in later periods, in medieval kingship, early modern composite monarchies, and modern constitutional systems. It also shows how legitimacy is built through ritual, monument, literature, and law as much as through force.

The Augustan period connected regions across Europe and beyond. Roman power extended into Spain, the Alps, the Danube basin, and the edge of Germania, incorporating peoples whose later histories would be shaped by their experience of Roman rule and by their resistance to it. Soldiers, veterans, officials, and settlers moved across the empire, and provincial elites entered Roman networks. At the same time, the eastern frontier with Parthia and the wealth of Egypt show that Europe's Roman history was inseparable from the wider Mediterranean and Near Eastern world discussed in earlier chapters.

For Volume I, the chapter completes the movement from republic to empire and establishes the framework that Chapter 22 will explore, as well as the political setting for the rise of Christianity examined in Chapter 23 and the crises and reorganizations of later centuries. The Principate's strengths, its flexibility, its army, its administration, and its ideology, and its weaknesses, especially succession and the dependence on the loyalty of the army, would shape the empire's history through the third century crisis examined in Chapter 24 and the reforms of Diocletian and Constantine in Chapter 25. Augustus did not create a constitution called the Roman Empire. He constructed a system in which republican institutions continued beneath the permanent predominance of a princeps, and the success of that system was measured not by the length of his life but by the fact that it outlived him.
