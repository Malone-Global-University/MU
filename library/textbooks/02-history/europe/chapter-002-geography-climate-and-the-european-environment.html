Geography, Climate, and the European Environment

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Long before any human community called any part of this landmass "Europe," the land itself already possessed the mountains, rivers, plains, coastlines, and climate zones within which every later society, from Paleolithic hunting bands to modern industrial states, would have to live, farm, travel, trade, and fight. Geography did not write European history, but no chapter of that history can be understood apart from it.

Picture a merchant standing on a quay at the mouth of the Rhône River in the centuries before Rome's rise, watching goods arrive from the Mediterranean world and depart northward along a river corridor that would, centuries later, still be carrying salt, wine, cloth, and information between the Mediterranean and the cold plains of northern Gaul. Picture, at almost the same moment, a shepherd moving flocks along a transhumance route in the Pyrenees, following seasonal pasture from lowland winter grazing to high summer meadows, a rhythm of life that would persist, largely unchanged in its basic logic, for more than two thousand years. Picture a farmer working heavy, water retentive clay soils on the North European Plain, and another farmer, a few hundred kilometers south, cultivating thin, stony, well drained soil suited to olives and vines on a Mediterranean hillside. None of these people would have described themselves as living in "Europe" in anything like the modern sense discussed in Chapter 1. All of them, however, were shaped daily by physical conditions, rivers, mountains, soils, and seasons, that this chapter examines as the enduring physical stage on which European history unfolds.

This chapter steps back from the conceptual and terminological questions of Chapter 1 to ask a more concrete question: what kind of place is the landmass this textbook studies, physically and environmentally, and how did that physical landscape shape, without determining, the historical possibilities open to the people who lived there? The answer is not simple, because few regions on earth combine such a small overall area with such extraordinary internal variety. Within a relatively compact peninsula of the larger Eurasian landmass, one finds glacier carved fjords, wind scoured northern tundra, dense temperate forests, fertile river valleys, semi arid Mediterranean hillsides, vast open plains, and high mountain ranges capable of isolating valleys from one another for months at a time. This diversity has mattered enormously to every subject this textbook will examine: where farming communities could settle and what they could grow, which regions produced surplus wealth and which remained comparatively poor, where armies could march and where they could not, which cities grew rich from trade and which remained isolated, and how epidemic disease, migration, and cultural exchange moved, or failed to move, from one region to another.

At the same time, this chapter insists from the outset on a caution that will recur throughout: geography creates possibilities and constraints, but it does not by itself explain historical outcomes. Two regions with broadly similar physical conditions have, at different times, produced very different political, economic, and cultural histories. Mountains that isolated some communities became, in other circumstances, corridors of contact through well used passes. Rivers that connected some regions to distant trading partners also, at other moments, became contested military frontiers. Fertile plains that supported dense agricultural populations also, in other periods, became open highways for invading armies with no natural obstacle to slow them. Explaining any particular historical development, whether the rise of a trading city, the spread of an agricultural technique, or the outcome of a military campaign, requires attention to human choices, institutions, technology, and historical contingency alongside physical geography, not physical geography alone.

This chapter therefore surveys, region by region and theme by theme, the physical geography of the space this textbook studies: its peninsulas and islands, its connecting seas, its major river systems, its mountain ranges and the passes that crossed them, its great plains, its climate zones, its forests and steppelands, and its natural resources of timber, salt, metal, and, eventually, coal. It also examines how humans have modified this landscape over many thousands of years, through forest clearance, land drainage, agricultural terracing, and urban settlement, so that the "natural" European landscape encountered by any given generation was already, in most periods, a landscape substantially shaped by earlier human activity.

Understanding this physical setting is not a detour from European history; it is a necessary foundation for it. Chapter 3 will introduce the first identifiable human societies to inhabit this landscape, the Paleolithic hunter gatherer bands who adapted to Ice Age and post glacial environments long before agriculture, writing, or anything resembling a political state existed anywhere on the continent. Understanding what kind of physical world those early populations, and every subsequent European society, actually inhabited is the purpose of this chapter.

Finally, this chapter previews a caution that will apply across the entire textbook. Because physical geography changes only slowly compared to human history, it is tempting to treat it as a fixed and unchanging backdrop against which the human drama of politics, religion, and war unfolds. In fact, the European landscape itself has changed considerably since the end of the last Ice Age, through natural climatic shifts such as periods of relative warmth and cold, and increasingly, from the Neolithic period onward, through deliberate and incidental human modification. Sea levels rose after glacial ice melted, forests were cleared on a massive scale over thousands of years, wetlands were drained, rivers were diverted and dammed, and coal, largely undisturbed for hundreds of millions of years, became from the eighteenth century onward one of the most historically consequential resources on the continent. Geography, in other words, is itself a historical subject, not merely a static stage.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established that "Europe" is a historically constructed geographic, cultural, and political idea rather than a timeless or self evident fact of nature, and that the conventional boundary between Europe and Asia is a human convention rather than a natural division of a single Eurasian landmass. This chapter builds directly on that foundation by asking a different, more physical question: whatever conventional boundaries historians and geographers draw, what does the landscape within those boundaries actually look like, and how has it shaped human life across many thousands of years?

The physical geography examined in this chapter long predates any concept of "Europe," and much of it predates human presence on the continent as well, though not all of it. The mountain systems discussed below, the Alps, the Pyrenees, the Carpathians, and others, were formed principally during the Late Cretaceous and Cenozoic eras, with the main phase of Alpine mountain building generally dated to roughly 65 to 2.5 million years ago, through the collision of tectonic plates, a process too slow to perceive unaided over ordinary human timescales but measurable today with modern geodetic instruments, and one that produced the dramatic relief still visible across much of the continent. The landscape encountered by the earliest hominin populations to enter the continent, examined in Chapter 3, had already been shaped by these ancient geological processes. That landscape was, in turn, repeatedly reshaped by the advance and retreat of continental ice sheets during the succession of glacial and interglacial periods that geologists and climate scientists call the Pleistocene epoch, lasting from roughly 2.58 million years ago until the beginning of the Holocene, 11,700 years ago. The Pleistocene therefore does not simply precede human occupation of Europe; it encompasses essentially the entire long span of early hominin presence on the continent, since archaeological evidence places hominins in parts of Europe by at least 1.4 million years ago, and possibly earlier, long before the appearance of anatomically modern humans discussed in Chapter 3.

The most recent glacial period, often called the last glacial period or, in popular usage, the last Ice Age, reached its coldest and most extensive phase, known to specialists as the Last Glacial Maximum, roughly twenty thousand years ago. At that time, vast ice sheets covered Scandinavia, much of the British Isles, and parts of northern Germany and the Baltic region, while sea levels stood far lower than today because so much of the world's water was locked up in glacial ice. As the climate warmed and the ice sheets retreated over subsequent millennia, a process still not fully complete until roughly ten thousand years ago, sea levels rose, land that had been depressed under the weight of ice slowly rebounded, and the physical outline of the continent's coastlines, islands, and river systems gradually took on a form closer to what exists today, though even this "modern" landscape has continued to change, through both natural processes and human activity, into the present.

Historians do not need the specialized technical vocabulary of geology and climatology to use this material effectively, but they do need to appreciate two things established firmly by modern earth science. First, the landscape inherited by any given historical period was never simply "natural" in an unchanging sense; it was the product of an ongoing geological and climatic history that continued to operate, if usually far more slowly than human affairs, throughout the entire span of this textbook. Second, from a comparatively early period, human activity itself became an increasingly significant force reshaping the landscape, so that the distinction between "natural" and "human made" geography becomes less clear the further one moves into the agricultural, urban, and industrial periods examined in later chapters.

This chapter also depends on a body of evidence quite different from the textual and archaeological sources historians typically rely on for political or cultural history. Geologists reconstruct past landscapes from rock strata, glacial deposits, and coastal sediment. Palynologists, scientists who study fossil pollen preserved in lake beds, peat bogs, and other waterlogged sediments, reconstruct past vegetation and, indirectly, past climate and land use, since a sudden decline in tree pollen accompanied by a rise in cereal or grass pollen can indicate a period of deforestation and agricultural expansion. Dendrochronologists use the annual growth rings of long lived trees, and of timber preserved in buildings, shipwrecks, and archaeological sites, to establish precise chronologies and to detect unusually cold, warm, wet, or dry years. Ice cores drilled from Greenland and Antarctic glaciers, together with cave formations called speleothems and marine and lake sediment cores, provide additional independent evidence of past temperature and precipitation. Historians increasingly draw on this scientific evidence alongside traditional documentary sources, particularly for questions about climate fluctuations such as the Medieval Climate Anomaly and the Little Ice Age, examined later in this chapter and in subsequent chapters of this textbook.

Section 2.1

Europe as a Peninsula of Eurasia

As Chapter 1 explained, Europe and Asia together form a single continuous landmass conventionally called Eurasia, and the boundary separating them is a historically constructed convention rather than a natural physical division. Viewed purely as physical geography, "Europe" is best understood as a large western peninsula of this greater Eurasian landmass, a peninsula that itself branches into a remarkable number of smaller peninsulas, from Scandinavia in the north to Iberia, Italy, and the Balkans in the south, with the Balkan Peninsula lying immediately adjacent to, but geographically distinct from, the separate peninsula of Anatolia.

This peninsular character matters historically in a very direct way. Compared to its total land area, Europe possesses an unusually long and irregular coastline, since every additional peninsula and inlet adds further coastal length relative to the enclosed land. This high ratio of coastline to land area meant that a very large proportion of the continent's population, across nearly every period examined in this textbook, lived within a relatively short distance of navigable water, whether sea, river, or both. Few major regions of the landmass are entirely cut off from maritime or riverine contact, a fact with profound consequences for trade, cultural exchange, migration, and the spread of both useful innovations and, at times, epidemic disease.

At the same time, describing Europe as a peninsula of Eurasia is a reminder that the continent was never geographically isolated from the rest of the landmass or from neighboring regions. The Ural Mountains, the conventional eastern boundary discussed in Chapter 1, form a relatively modest mountain range rather than an impassable barrier, and the plains of European Russia merge gradually into the plains of Central Asia without any dramatic physical transition. To the south, the Mediterranean Sea, though a substantial body of water, has functioned throughout history as much as a connective corridor linking Europe to North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean coastlands as a barrier separating them. To the southeast, the Bosporus and Dardanelles straits, which separate the Balkan Peninsula from the neighboring peninsula of Anatolia, are narrow enough to have been crossed by determined travelers, armies, and traders since antiquity. This chapter's treatment of Europe as a strongly connected peninsula, rather than an isolated continent, sets up a theme that will recur throughout later chapters, including the textbook's sustained attention, established in the Master Instructions governing this series, to Europe's ongoing relationships with North Africa, the Middle East, the Eurasian steppe, and, from the fifteenth century onward, the wider Atlantic world.

Section 2.2

Coastlines, Peninsulas, Islands, and Seas

Few regions of comparable size possess as elaborate a coastline as Europe. The Iberian Peninsula, joined to the rest of the continent only by the relatively narrow land connection across the Pyrenees, projects southwestward toward North Africa, separated from it at the Strait of Gibraltar by barely fifteen kilometers of water at its narrowest point. The Italian Peninsula extends southward into the central Mediterranean, flanked by the large islands of Sicily, Sardinia, and Corsica. The Balkan Peninsula, mountainous and deeply indented by the Adriatic, Ionian, and Aegean Seas, extends toward Anatolia and is itself fragmented by numerous smaller peninsulas and by the thousands of islands of the Aegean archipelago, including Crete. The Scandinavian Peninsula, comprising most of Norway and Sweden, extends far to the north, its western coastline sculpted by deep glacially carved fjords, and connects to the rest of the continent only indirectly, across the Baltic and North Seas or via the far northern land border with Finland and Russia. The Jutland Peninsula, which forms most of continental Denmark, is a distinct and separate peninsula extending northward from Germany, connected to the Scandinavian Peninsula only by sea across the narrow Danish straits. The British Isles, separated from the continent by the English Channel and the North Sea, form a substantial island group whose relative isolation, without ever being complete isolation, shaped a distinctive, though never separate, historical trajectory examined at many points in this textbook.

The seas that these peninsulas and islands enclose or border, the Mediterranean, the Adriatic, the Aegean, the Black Sea, the Baltic, and the North Sea, together with the open Atlantic along Europe's western approaches, deserve particular attention because they have functioned historically far more often as highways of contact than as barriers to it. The Mediterranean, examined extensively in Part II and Part III of this textbook, connected Greek city states, Phoenician trading colonies, Roman provinces, and later medieval and early modern powers around a shared, though far from politically unified, maritime space. Its relatively predictable seasonal winds and generally navigable waters, once appropriate shipbuilding and navigational knowledge developed, allowed goods, people, and ideas to move across it more readily, in many periods, than equivalent overland journeys of similar distance.

The Baltic Sea played a broadly comparable role for northern Europe from the medieval period onward, examined further in Part VII in connection with the Hanseatic League, linking Scandinavia, the eastern Baltic lands, Poland, and northern Germany in networks of maritime commerce even as its harsher winter conditions, including seasonal ice in its northern and eastern reaches, imposed constraints unfamiliar to Mediterranean sailors. The North Sea, bridging the British Isles, the Low Countries, France, and Scandinavia, became similarly important to medieval and early modern commerce, and its waters, along with the English Channel, also served at various times as a defensive barrier that shaped the distinctive political and military history of the British Isles.

The Black Sea, connected to the Mediterranean through the narrow Bosporus and Dardanelles straits, linked the Balkan Peninsula and Anatolia to the steppe lands of southern Russia and Ukraine and, beyond them, to Central Asian trade networks, a connection of particular importance to Byzantine, and later Ottoman, history, examined in Part VI and beyond. Taken together, these connecting seas mean that very few of the historically significant regions examined in this textbook, from ancient Greek city states to medieval Hanseatic ports to early modern Atlantic trading centers, developed in genuine maritime isolation. Coastal access, far more often than not, meant connection rather than confinement.

Section 2.3

Rivers and Inland Corridors

If seas connected coastal regions to one another, rivers performed a comparable function for the continent's interior, and few features of European geography recur as persistently throughout this textbook as its major river systems. The Rhine, rising in the Swiss Alps and flowing northward to the North Sea, and the Danube, rising not in the Alps but at the confluence of the Brigach and Breg streams in Germany's Black Forest and flowing eastward across central Europe and the Pannonian Basin to the Black Sea, are separate river systems whose corridors together form one of the most historically consequential zones of transport, settlement, and frontier on the continent, examined repeatedly from Roman frontier policy in Part III through medieval and early modern central European politics in Parts V, VII, and XII, and again in the context of the Habsburg and Ottoman military and diplomatic frontier discussed in later Parts. The two rivers are linked today only by an artificial modern waterway, the Rhine-Main-Danube Canal, and should not be mistaken for a single natural river system.

Western Europe possesses its own significant river network. The Seine, Loire, and Rhône in France; the Thames in England; the Tagus and Ebro in Iberia; and the Po in northern Italy each supported dense agricultural populations, provided transport routes for goods too heavy or bulky for efficient overland carriage, such as grain, timber, and stone, and often determined the location of major cities, since a defensible site at a river crossing, confluence, or navigable head frequently became a natural point for markets, fortifications, bridges, and eventually large urban centers. Paris, London, Rome, and countless smaller but historically significant towns owe their original location substantially to river geography of this kind.

Central, eastern, and northern Europe possess a comparably important, if less uniformly Mediterranean-oriented, river geography. The Elbe drains much of northern Germany toward the North Sea, while the Oder and, further east, the Vistula drain much of Germany and Poland toward the Baltic; and the Dnieper, Don, and Volga, draining much of the East European Plain, historically connected the forested and forest-steppe zones of what is now Ukraine, Belarus, and western Russia to the Black Sea and, via the Volga, toward the Caspian Sea and the trade networks of the wider Eurasian world, a connection of particular importance to the early medieval Rus polities examined in Part VI.

Rivers mattered historically for reasons beyond simple transport. Because heavy or bulky goods could usually be moved far more cheaply by water than overland, especially before the development of extensive paved road networks and, later, railways, river access strongly shaped where agricultural surplus could be marketed profitably and where towns and cities could grow beyond the size their immediate agricultural hinterland alone could support. Rivers also frequently served as political and military frontiers, since a river, unlike an arbitrary line drawn across open country, offered both a defensible barrier and a clearly identifiable boundary; the Rhine and Danube functioned in exactly this way as the northern frontier of the Roman Empire for centuries, examined in Part III, just as various rivers would later mark contested boundaries between medieval and early modern states. At the same time, rivers were rarely absolute barriers. Fords, bridges, and boats meant that a river frontier required active maintenance and garrisoning to remain effective, a point of considerable importance for understanding Roman frontier policy and many later military and political frontiers examined throughout this textbook.

Section 2.4

Mountains, Passes, and Regional Barriers

Europe's mountain systems, the Pyrenees separating Iberia from the rest of the continent, the Alps arcing across south central Europe, the Apennines running the length of the Italian Peninsula, the Carpathians curving through central and eastern Europe, the Dinaric Alps and other ranges of the Balkan Peninsula, the mountains of Scandinavia, and, at the continent's conventional eastern edge, the Ural Mountains and the Caucasus, were formed, as noted earlier, through immensely long geological processes of tectonic collision. For human history, however, what matters is not primarily how these mountains formed but how they functioned: as barriers isolating some communities from their neighbors, as filters permitting only certain kinds of contact under certain conditions, and, through specific passes, as corridors that in some periods carried more traffic than the surrounding lowlands.

The Alps illustrate this dual character particularly clearly. At their most forbidding, high Alpine terrain could isolate valley communities for months at a time during winter, producing, in some regions examined in later chapters, distinctive local dialects, customs, and political arrangements that persisted for centuries with comparatively limited outside interference. Yet the same mountain range was crossed, from antiquity onward, by a limited number of significant passes, including those associated with Hannibal's famous crossing during the Punic Wars examined in Part III, and by medieval and early modern routes such as the Brenner Pass and the Great St. Bernard Pass, which carried substantial trade, pilgrimage, military, and diplomatic traffic between Italy and lands to the north for many centuries. Control of key Alpine passes shaped the fortunes of towns and, in some cases, entire political entities positioned to profit from or defend them.

The Pyrenees performed a broadly similar dual function for the Iberian Peninsula, substantial enough to give Iberian history, examined at many points in this textbook including the Reconquista and the era of Habsburg Spain, a partially distinct trajectory from developments north of the mountains, while never fully preventing contact, since passes and coastal routes at both the Atlantic and Mediterranean ends of the range permitted movement of armies, pilgrims, and traders throughout the medieval and early modern periods. The Carpathians curved protectively around much of the Pannonian Basin and Transylvania, shaping the political geography of medieval and early modern Hungary and neighboring principalities, examined in Part VII and beyond, while the mountainous terrain of the Balkan Peninsula, fragmented by the Dinaric Alps, the Pindus range, and other systems, contributed to the region's long standing pattern of linguistic, religious, and political diversity, a pattern this textbook examines carefully rather than treating as either an inevitable curse or an exotic curiosity.

Mountains also shaped military history throughout this textbook by channeling the movement of armies into predictable corridors defined by passable terrain, a pattern visible from Hannibal's Alpine crossing through medieval and early modern campaigns and into the twentieth century warfare examined in Part XVII and Part XVIII, where control of Alpine and Balkan mountain passes repeatedly proved strategically decisive.

Section 2.5

Plains, Basins, and Agricultural Heartlands

Where mountains fragmented parts of Europe into isolated pockets, the continent's great plains did the opposite, providing extensive stretches of relatively level land, productive in many districts though highly variable in soil quality, capable in its more fertile stretches of supporting dense agricultural populations, and, at the same time, offering few natural obstacles to the movement of migrating peoples, trading caravans, or invading armies. The North European Plain, stretching with only modest interruption from northern France and the Low Countries across northern Germany, Poland, and the Baltic states, is the most extensive of these lowlands and recurs throughout this textbook, from the settlement patterns of Iron Age peoples examined in Chapter 6 through the military campaigns of the twentieth century examined in Part XVII and Part XVIII.

Further east, the East European Plain extends across most of European Russia, Belarus, and Ukraine, a lowland so extensive and so lacking in significant mountain barriers that it has historically permitted relatively unobstructed movement between the Eurasian steppe corridor and the heart of central Europe, a fact of considerable importance for understanding the migrations of the first millennium CE examined in Part IV and Part V, the later expansion of Rus and Russian political power examined at points throughout this textbook, and successive waves of military conflict extending into the twentieth century. In central Europe, the Pannonian Basin, which contains the Great Hungarian Plain along with other surrounding lowlands, forms a comparatively compact and, in many districts, highly fertile lowland encircled by the Carpathians and Alps, historically important both as an agricultural heartland and as a strategic corridor connecting central Europe to the Balkans and, via the steppe, to Asia, a route used by Magyar peoples during their ninth century migration examined in Part V and later contested between Habsburg and Ottoman power examined in Part XII.

Smaller but historically significant lowlands include the Po Valley in northern Italy, one of the most agriculturally productive and densely populated regions of the Italian Peninsula since antiquity; the Paris Basin in northern France, whose fertile soils supported the political and economic core of the medieval and early modern French monarchy examined in Parts V, VII, and XII; and the English lowlands, whose comparatively fertile and accessible terrain contrasted with the more mountainous and pastoral regions of Scotland, Wales, and parts of Ireland, a contrast with real consequences for the political history of the British Isles examined throughout this textbook.

These plains mattered historically for reasons beyond agricultural productivity alone, though that productivity itself varied considerably from district to district depending on soil, drainage, and climate rather than following automatically from level terrain. Level terrain generally permitted cheaper and faster overland transport, before the era of railways, than mountainous terrain, facilitating the growth of regional and, eventually, long distance trade networks. Open terrain could also facilitate the rapid movement of large armies, since it offered few natural obstacles to slow an advance, but whether a given state responded to this exposure by investing heavily in fortification, standing military forces, or diplomatic alliance systems depended on specific political, technological, and strategic circumstances rather than on open terrain alone, a caution with implications for the state formation processes examined in Part XII.

Section 2.6

Climate Zones and Seasonal Patterns

Europe's climate varies considerably across its relatively modest land area, and historians should understand this variation in broad terms without claiming a precision that the underlying science does not fully support for every period and locality. Three broad climate patterns dominate much of the continent's inhabited area, while subarctic and tundra conditions prevail in the far north of Scandinavia and Russia, and distinct alpine or highland climates, marked by short growing seasons and heavy snowfall, occur at high elevations in the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, and other mountain systems regardless of surrounding latitude. A Mediterranean climate zone, characterized by hot, dry summers and mild, wetter winters, prevails across much of Iberia, Italy, Greece, and the Balkan and Anatolian coastlands, historically favoring crops such as olives, grapes, and certain grains that tolerate summer drought, and shaping the distinctive agricultural economy of the ancient Mediterranean world examined in Part II and Part III. An oceanic or Atlantic climate zone, characterized by relatively mild winters, cool summers, and precipitation distributed fairly evenly across the year, prevails across much of western Europe, including France, the Low Countries, the British Isles, and parts of the Iberian Atlantic coast, historically favoring mixed farming, pasture, and cereal crops suited to a longer, cooler growing season. A continental climate zone, characterized by greater seasonal extremes, colder winters, hotter summers, and generally lower and less evenly distributed precipitation, prevails across much of central, eastern, and northern inland Europe, including large portions of Germany, Poland, Hungary, and European Russia, historically shaping agricultural patterns, settlement density, and vulnerability to both drought and severe winter conditions differently from the milder zones further west and south.

The relatively mild climate of much of western and northern Europe compared to other regions at similar latitudes is often popularly attributed to the Gulf Stream and the broader North Atlantic Drift, ocean current systems that transport warm water from the tropical Atlantic toward northwestern Europe. This explanation captures a real and scientifically significant phenomenon, but historians should state it with appropriate care: the full climatic effect involves the interaction of ocean currents, prevailing westerly winds, and broader atmospheric circulation patterns, and the popular claim that the Gulf Stream alone is responsible for keeping, for example, the British Isles or Norway's coast markedly warmer than they would otherwise be at their latitude somewhat overstates the current's role relative to the contribution of prevailing atmospheric circulation. What is well established is that northwestern Europe experiences a noticeably milder climate for its latitude than many comparable regions elsewhere in the world, with real historical consequences for the length of the growing season, the range of viable crops, and the density of population that regional agriculture could support.

Historians must also be cautious about assuming that Europe's climate has remained constant across the roughly twelve thousand years of the Holocene epoch discussed earlier in this chapter. Modern climate science, drawing on the palynological, dendrochronological, and ice core evidence introduced in the Historical Context section above, has identified meaningful fluctuations within this broadly stable interglacial period, including a period of relative warmth in parts of the North Atlantic region during the high medieval centuries, generally termed by climate scientists the Medieval Climate Anomaly and sometimes called, in older or more popular usage, the Medieval Warm Period, a label many specialists now avoid because it can imply a uniform global warming that the regional proxy evidence does not clearly support, and a subsequent period of generally cooler conditions across much of the Northern Hemisphere, sometimes called the Little Ice Age, extending in various regional forms from roughly the fourteenth through the mid nineteenth centuries. Neither of these periods was uniform in timing, intensity, or geographic extent, and historians should avoid treating either term as though it described a single, precisely dated, globally synchronized event. Even so, this evidence provides valuable environmental context for developments examined later in this textbook, including the agrarian expansion of the High Middle Ages discussed in Part VII and the recurring famines and agrarian crises of the fourteenth century discussed in Part VIII.

Section 2.7

Forests, Steppe, Soils, and Ecological Regions

Beyond climate alone, Europe's historical geography is shaped by patterns of vegetation, soil, and ecological zone that interact closely with climate but are not reducible to it. In the millennia following the retreat of the last glacial ice, much of temperate Europe, from the British Isles across France, Germany, and Poland into western Russia, became covered by extensive deciduous and mixed forest, dominated in various regions by oak, beech, hornbeam, and other broadleaf species, along with coniferous forest at higher latitudes and elevations. This postglacial forest cover, reconstructed by palynologists through fossil pollen evidence preserved in lake sediments and peat bogs, provides the essential baseline against which historians measure the scale of subsequent human deforestation associated with the spread of farming from the Neolithic period onward, examined in Chapter 4, and continuing at varying paces through the medieval, early modern, and modern periods examined in later chapters.

To the south, Mediterranean vegetation zones developed distinctive drought resistant scrubland and woodland, often called by the regional terms maquis or garrigue, adapted to hot, dry summers, alongside cultivated zones of olives, vines, and grain that had been managed by human societies for thousands of years by the time this textbook's narrative reaches classical antiquity in Part II. To the east, the forest gradually gives way, moving from the temperate zone into southern Ukraine and southern Russia, to the forest-steppe and then to open steppe grassland, a transitional ecological zone of considerable historical importance discussed further below and examined again in later chapters covering the medieval Rus principalities, the Mongol invasions, and the long history of steppe nomadic contact with settled European societies.

Soil quality varied as significantly as vegetation and shaped agricultural productivity in ways not always visible on a simple political or climate map. Some of the most fertile agricultural soils in Europe developed from loess, a fine, wind-deposited silt laid down during the glacial periods and subsequently covering substantial portions of the North European Plain, parts of Ukraine, and other regions; the soils that formed from this silt, generally easy to work and highly fertile, supported some of the earliest and most productive Neolithic farming communities in Europe, examined in Chapter 4. By contrast, heavier clay soils, thinner mountain and upland soils, and the often less fertile sandy soils found in parts of northern Germany and the Baltic region posed greater agricultural challenges and, in some periods, required different farming techniques, tools, or crop choices than the lighter and more fertile soils found elsewhere.

The steppe corridor deserves particular attention because of its recurring historical significance. Extending from the plains north of the Black Sea eastward across southern Russia and Kazakhstan into Central Asia and eventually toward Mongolia, this belt of open, largely treeless grassland supported, across many centuries, pastoral nomadic societies whose mobility and, at various periods, military organization repeatedly brought them into contact, sometimes peaceful and commercial, sometimes violent, with the settled agricultural societies of Europe examined throughout this textbook, from the Iron Age steppe peoples mentioned in Chapter 6 through the Huns and other migration era groups examined in Part IV, the later Magyar migration into the Pannonian Basin examined in Part V, and the Mongol campaigns into eastern and central Europe examined in Part VII. This textbook treats the steppe corridor not as an external, alien intrusion into European history but as a genuine ecological and historical borderland whose interactions with European societies form an important and recurring theme.

Section 2.8

Resources: Timber, Salt, Metals, and Coal

The historical importance of European geography extended well beyond agriculture, climate, and transport routes to encompass the natural resources embedded in the landscape itself, resources whose location and availability shaped patterns of trade, settlement, craft production, and, eventually, industrial development across many centuries.

Timber was, for most of the period covered by this textbook, one of the most universally essential resources in European economic life, required for construction, shipbuilding, fuel, tools, and countless other purposes. Forested regions, particularly in Scandinavia, the Baltic region, and parts of central Europe, supplied timber not only to their immediate localities but, from the medieval period onward, to distant markets through maritime trade networks such as those of the Hanseatic League, examined in Part VII, which carried Baltic timber, along with grain, furs, and other commodities, to western European markets increasingly short of easily accessible woodland as centuries of agricultural clearance reduced forest cover closer to major population centers.

Salt, essential for food preservation as well as human and animal nutrition in an era before refrigeration, was extracted through several distinct methods across different regions: solar evaporation of seawater in Mediterranean and Atlantic coastal salt pans, extraction from inland salt springs and brine deposits in parts of central Europe, and, in a small number of locations such as the celebrated deposits at Wieliczka in Poland, mining of solid rock salt. Control of major salt sources and the trade routes carrying salt to inland markets generated substantial wealth for the cities, rulers, and merchant networks that controlled them across many centuries examined throughout this textbook.

Metal ores, including copper, tin, iron, silver, and gold, were unevenly distributed across the continent, and their location shaped patterns of trade and, in some cases, political power from the Bronze Age onward, examined in Chapter 5. Copper and tin, the components of bronze, came from different and often widely separated regions, encouraging the long distance exchange networks discussed in that chapter, since tin sources adequate for large scale bronze production were comparatively rare across much of Europe. Iron ore, by contrast, was far more widely distributed, a fact of considerable importance for the Iron Age societies examined in Chapter 6 and for the broader accessibility of iron tools and weapons compared to bronze. Silver mining, notably in parts of Iberia during the Roman period examined in Part III and in central European regions such as the Erzgebirge, the mountain range straddling the border of Saxony and Bohemia, during the medieval and early modern periods examined in Parts VII and XII, provided crucial monetary resources for the states and merchants who controlled it.

Coal, though present in substantial deposits across parts of Britain, Belgium, northern France, the German Ruhr region, Silesia, and elsewhere, remained of comparatively limited historical significance for most of the period covered by the earlier volumes of this textbook, used in some localities for domestic heating or small scale craft production but not yet central to the continent's economic life. This changed decisively from the eighteenth century onward, when expanding coal extraction, particularly in Britain, became fundamental to the industrial transformations examined in Part XV. This chapter introduces coal here only as a geological resource whose distribution helped determine, centuries later, which regions of Europe would become centers of large scale industrial production; the industrial history itself belongs to later chapters and is not developed further here.

Section 2.9

Human Modification of the European Landscape

Although this chapter has so far emphasized the physical landscape as a setting for human history, that landscape has itself been substantially transformed by human activity over many thousands of years, so that by nearly every period examined in this textbook, the "natural" European environment encountered by a given generation was already, to a significant degree, a landscape shaped by earlier human choices.

The most extensive and long running of these transformations has been deforestation. Palynological evidence, discussed earlier in this chapter, shows that the extensive postglacial forest cover of temperate Europe began to decline in various regions from the Neolithic period onward, as farming communities cleared woodland for fields and pasture, a process examined further in Chapter 4. This clearance continued, at varying rates in different regions and periods, through the Bronze and Iron Ages examined in Chapters 5 and 6, accelerated significantly during the medieval agricultural expansion of the eleventh through thirteenth centuries examined in Part VII, and continued, with periodic regional reversals during periods of population decline such as the aftermath of the Black Death examined in Part VIII, into the early modern and modern periods. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, examined in Parts XV through XX, forest cover across much of densely settled western and central Europe had been reduced to a small fraction of its early postglacial extent, though some regions, particularly in Scandinavia and parts of eastern Europe, retained far more extensive woodland.

Wetland drainage and land reclamation represent a second major category of human environmental transformation. The Low Countries offer perhaps the most dramatic example, where centuries of dike building, drainage, and land reclamation, intensifying from the medieval period onward and continuing into the modern era, transformed extensive areas of marsh, peat bog, and shallow coastal water into productive agricultural and urban land, a process closely connected to the distinctive economic and political development of the Netherlands examined in Part XII. Comparable, if generally less extensive, drainage projects reshaped fenland in eastern England, coastal marshes along parts of the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts, and various inland wetlands across the continent, particularly from the eighteenth century onward as agricultural improvement movements, examined in Part XIII, sought to expand cultivable land.

Agricultural terracing in hillside and mountainous regions, particularly across much of the Mediterranean world, represents a third significant, long term human modification of the landscape, converting steep terrain unsuitable for conventional plowing into a series of level cultivable steps, often supporting olive groves, vineyards, or garden crops, and requiring substantial and sustained labor investment across generations to construct and maintain. Urban growth constitutes a fourth category of transformation, as the cities examined throughout this textbook, from ancient Mediterranean cities through medieval market towns to modern industrial metropolises, progressively converted agricultural or wild land into built environments, while also generating demand for construction timber, stone, food, water, and fuel drawn from an increasingly extensive rural hinterland.

Recognizing this long history of human environmental modification matters for historical interpretation in at least two ways. First, it means that historians examining any particular period's landscape, agriculture, or resource use must ask what earlier generations had already done to that landscape, rather than assuming an unchanging natural backdrop. Second, it provides essential context for understanding the accelerating environmental transformations of the industrial and contemporary periods, examined in Parts XV and XX, as a continuation and intensification of processes with much older roots rather than as an entirely unprecedented development.

Section 2.10

Geography without Geographic Determinism

Having surveyed Europe's physical geography at length, this chapter closes with a methodological caution of considerable importance for the rest of this textbook. It is tempting, having established how significantly mountains, rivers, plains, coastlines, and climate have shaped European history, to conclude that geography effectively determined that history, that fragmented mountainous terrain produced political fragmentation and, eventually, competitive innovation, that navigable coastlines and rivers produced commercial dynamism, or that a temperate climate produced particular forms of social or political organization. Explanations of this kind, sometimes called environmental or geographic determinism, have a long history in European thought, associated with various eighteenth and nineteenth century writers, and they retain a certain intuitive appeal because they offer simple, sweeping answers to complex historical questions.

This textbook rejects strong geographic determinism while still taking geography seriously, an approach sometimes described by geographers and historians as environmental possibilism: the view that physical geography establishes a range of possibilities and constraints within which human societies make historically contingent choices, shaped by institutions, technology, culture, religion, and countless particular decisions and events, rather than a fixed destiny that geography alone dictates. Several considerations support this more cautious approach.

First, societies occupying broadly similar physical environments have produced markedly different historical outcomes at different times. Mountainous terrain contributed to political fragmentation in some periods and regions but coexisted with substantial centralized states in others; the Alps did not prevent the formation of large territorial states in the surrounding lowlands, and mountainous Switzerland's particular political trajectory, examined in Part XII, resulted from specific historical choices, alliances, and conflicts, not from mountains alone, since other mountainous regions of Europe developed very differently. Second, the same physical feature has served different, even opposite, functions depending on historical circumstance and available technology: a river that served as a defensible Roman frontier in one century could, once bridging and boat technology or political circumstances changed, become instead a corridor of contact and exchange; a mountain pass that isolated a valley for months during a snowbound winter could, when opened, carry more valuable long distance trade than routes across open, but insecure, lowland. Third, geographic determinist arguments have historically been used to justify claims of civilizational superiority or inferiority, including some of the ethnocentric nineteenth century usages of "Europe" discussed in Chapter 1, a history that should make historians especially cautious about deploying geography as a simple, deterministic explanatory tool.

None of this means geography is historically unimportant; the opposite conclusion would be equally mistaken. Geography shaped which crops could be grown where, which regions could support dense populations and which could not, which cities could plausibly become major trading centers, which military campaigns were logistically feasible, and which regions remained relatively isolated from broader currents of cultural and economic exchange. The correct historical approach, and the one this textbook adopts throughout its one hundred and forty four chapters, treats geography as one significant factor among several, interacting with political institutions, economic structures, religious and cultural traditions, technological capacity, and the specific decisions of historical actors, rather than as a master key that by itself explains European historical development.

Historical Vocabulary
Eurasia and the Continental Convention

Eurasia is the term geographers use for the single, continuous landmass comprising both Europe and Asia. As Chapter 1 explained, the division of this landmass into two separate "continents" is a long standing but historically constructed convention, not a natural physical fact, since no comparable oceanic or otherwise unambiguous physical break separates the two supposed continents along most of their conventional boundary. This chapter treats Europe, physically, as the western peninsula of Eurasia, a description that helps explain why the continent has never been isolated from developments, migrations, or exchange networks originating further east.

Historical Vocabulary
Environmental Possibilism versus Environmental Determinism

Environmental determinism refers to explanatory frameworks holding that physical geography, climate, or environment directly and predictably determines the character, capabilities, or historical trajectory of a given society, often in ways that flatten human agency and historical contingency. Environmental possibilism, the framework this textbook adopts, holds instead that geography establishes a range of possibilities and constraints within which human societies, through institutions, technology, culture, and specific historical choices, produce varied and not fully predictable outcomes. Section 2.10 of this chapter develops this distinction at length and explains why this textbook favors possibilism throughout.

Primary Source Window

Strabo, Geography, Book 2, on the Character of Europe, c. late 1st century BCE–early 1st century CE

Creator: Strabo of Amasia, Greek geographer and historian writing under Roman rule · Date: composed over several decades, substantially completed in the early first century CE · Type: A wide ranging geographical treatise describing the physical features, peoples, and regions of the known world as understood in the early Roman imperial period

Context: Strabo composed his seventeen book Geography drawing on earlier Greek geographic writing, including the work of Eratosthenes and Polybius, along with his own extensive travels and access to Roman administrative and military knowledge gained during the reign of Augustus. His treatment of Europe emphasizes its indented coastline, its river systems, and the variety of peoples inhabiting its different regions.

Paraphrase of Strabo, Geography, Book II Across several passages of Book II, Strabo described Europe as a landmass unusually well suited to human habitation because of its irregular, deeply indented coastline, its numerous peninsulas, and its many navigable rivers, which he contrasted favorably with the more compact and, in his view, less accessible landmasses of Asia and Libya, while also cataloguing the diverse and, to his Greco-Roman perspective, sometimes unfamiliar peoples inhabiting its different regions, from the Mediterranean south to the colder lands of the north. This is a synthesis of Strabo's argument across multiple passages rather than a translation of a single continuous quotation.

Analysis: This passage is valuable to students of this chapter because it shows that ancient geographers had already identified, in broadly accurate terms, the coastal and riverine features this chapter treats as historically significant, more than two thousand years before modern geographic science. At the same time, Strabo wrote from a distinctly Greco-Roman, and often Rome-favorable, vantage point, with only partial and sometimes inaccurate knowledge of regions distant from the Mediterranean world, particularly northern and eastern Europe, and his descriptions of peoples beyond direct Roman contact should be read with appropriate caution regarding their accuracy and the assumptions embedded within them.

Historical Debate

Does Geography Explain European Historical Development?

The question: To what extent, if any, does Europe's distinctive physical geography, its fragmented peninsulas, indented coastlines, navigable rivers, and varied climate zones, help explain broad patterns in its historical development, including its long standing political fragmentation into competing states, compared to other world regions that developed larger, more centralized empires over longer periods?

Interpretation A — Geography significantly shaped European political and economic patterns: Some historians and geographers argue that Europe's mountainous and peninsular geography, combined with its many navigable rivers and coastlines, made durable, continent wide political unification unusually difficult to sustain while simultaneously encouraging competitive, interconnected regional states and vigorous maritime and riverine trade, conditions that, in this view, contributed over centuries to distinctive European patterns of state competition, commercial development, and technological exchange.
Interpretation B — Institutions, contingency, and human choice better explain European development: Other historians argue that this geographic explanation overstates its case, noting that other regions with comparably fragmented or mountainous terrain did not follow a similar historical path, that large empires did in fact form and endure for extended periods within Europe, including the Roman Empire examined in Part III and the Carolingian and Habsburg realms examined in later Parts, and that specific political institutions, religious developments, dynastic accidents, technological changes, and contingent events better explain Europe's long term political fragmentation than geography alone.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying physical geography described throughout this chapter, but differ in how much explanatory weight to assign geography relative to political, religious, economic, and technological factors examined in later chapters and volumes of this textbook.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how much independent causal significance to grant physical geography throughout the rest of this textbook, and cautions against treating any later chapter's political, economic, or military developments as simply predetermined by the terrain described in this chapter.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that could support each interpretation, then explain which combination of geographic and non-geographic factors you find most persuasive, and why relying on geography alone would be historically insufficient.

Multiple Perspectives

Living with the Same Landscape: Perspectives on European Geography

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and geographers have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Mediterranean coastal merchant: The sea is a highway, not a boundary; predictable seasonal winds, familiar ports, and a shared, though politically fragmented, maritime world make distant coastal cities easier to reach profitably than inland towns only a modest distance away over difficult terrain.
From the perspective of an Alpine or Pyrenean valley farmer: Mountains structure daily and seasonal life directly, through transhumance, avalanche risk, short growing seasons at altitude, and months of relative isolation each winter, producing strong local community bonds and distinct customs that outside observers in the lowlands often fail to appreciate.
From the perspective of a farmer on the North European Plain: Level land, where the soil is good, supports a good harvest in most years, but the same openness that eases the cart and plow also offers little natural shelter from an approaching army or a harsh winter storm sweeping in without any mountain range to break its force, and not every stretch of plain is equally blessed with productive soil.
From the perspective of a pastoral nomadic herder of the steppe borderlands: The grasslands north of the Black Sea are not a barren edge of the world but a productive homeland requiring seasonal movement across great distances, a way of life that settled agricultural societies further west, accustomed to fixed fields and permanent villages, have often misunderstood or mischaracterized.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Europe's Changing Landscapes from the Postglacial Era to the Modern Age

What Changed

  • Coastlines, sea levels, and river courses shifted substantially between the Last Glacial Maximum and the establishment of broadly modern configurations by the mid Holocene
  • Forest cover across much of temperate Europe declined dramatically between the Neolithic period and the modern era as farming, grazing, and urban growth expanded
  • Extensive wetlands, particularly in the Low Countries and parts of eastern England, were drained and converted to agricultural and urban land, especially from the medieval period onward
  • The historical importance of specific resources shifted over time, as coal moved from marginal significance to central economic importance from the eighteenth century onward

What Stayed the Same

  • The underlying tectonic and mountain framework of the continent, formed over tens of millions of years, has remained essentially stable throughout the entire span of human habitation
  • Major river corridors, including the Rhine and Danube river corridors, have continued to function as significant transport, agricultural, and political corridors across many different historical periods
  • The connective rather than purely divisive function of Europe's seas has persisted from antiquity through the modern era, even as the technology and political organization of maritime trade changed considerably
  • Regional climatic and ecological differences among the Mediterranean, oceanic, and continental zones have persisted throughout the historical record, even amid shorter term fluctuations such as the Medieval Climate Anomaly and the Little Ice Age

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both long term stability and significant change prevents two opposite errors: treating European geography as a timeless, unchanging stage, or underestimating how much of the "natural" landscape encountered by any given historical generation had already been reshaped by earlier human activity.

Cause and Effect

Coastlines, Rivers, and the Growth of Long-Distance Exchange

Long-term causes

Europe's unusually indented coastline and its network of navigable rivers connecting interior regions to the sea meant that a large proportion of the continent's population lived within reach of relatively cheap water transport.

Short-term causes

The development, in particular periods and regions, of improved shipbuilding, navigation, and river craft technology allowed existing coastlines and rivers to be exploited more fully for trade than in earlier periods.

Effect

Regions with strong maritime or riverine access, from ancient Greek and Phoenician trading cities through medieval Hanseatic and Mediterranean ports to early modern Atlantic harbors, repeatedly developed disproportionate commercial wealth and cultural exchange relative to more landlocked regions.

This chapter's caution

Coastal or riverine access created opportunity, not guaranteed outcomes; specific cities and regions still required favorable political institutions, security, capital, and commercial organization to convert geographic advantage into sustained prosperity, a reminder against simple geographic determinism.

Historical complexity: Some of the most historically dynamic trading cities examined later in this textbook, including Venice and the Hanseatic ports, combined favorable geography with specific institutional, legal, and political innovations; geography alone does not explain why some well situated ports flourished while other, comparably situated coastal or river settlements did not.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living within the Land: Geography and Ordinary Experience

Physical geography shaped not only trade routes and military campaigns but the daily and seasonal rhythms of ordinary life across every period examined in this textbook. Farmers timed plowing, planting, and harvest according to local climate and soil conditions that varied considerably between, for example, the short growing season of an Alpine valley and the longer, milder season of a Mediterranean coastal plain. Pastoral communities in mountainous regions such as the Alps and Pyrenees practiced transhumance, moving flocks seasonally between lowland winter pasture and high summer meadows, a pattern of life requiring specialized knowledge of terrain, weather, and grazing conditions passed down across many generations.

Access to navigable water shaped ordinary economic life as much as it shaped long distance trade: a village situated on a navigable river or coastal inlet generally had far better prospects of selling surplus produce, purchasing goods unavailable locally, and hearing news from distant regions than a comparable village situated well inland without such access. Building materials, too, reflected local geography, as communities near good stone quarries built in stone while communities in heavily forested or clay rich regions built more often in timber or brick, a pattern of local ecological adaptation visible in the architecture examined at many points throughout this textbook. Understanding these ordinary, geographically shaped patterns of daily life helps ensure that this textbook's attention to states, wars, and institutions does not obscure the physical realities within which most people across European history actually lived.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has surveyed the physical geography, climate, and environmental history of the landmass this textbook studies, establishing the material setting within which every subsequent chapter's political, economic, religious, and cultural developments unfolded. Europe, understood physically as the western peninsula of the larger Eurasian landmass, possesses an unusually indented coastline, a dense network of navigable rivers, several major mountain systems, and extensive plains, a combination of features that recurs as a significant factor throughout this textbook's account of settlement, agriculture, trade, migration, and warfare.

The chapter examined Europe's coastlines, peninsulas, islands, and connecting seas, emphasizing that the Mediterranean, Baltic, Black, and North Seas functioned historically far more often as highways of contact than as impassable barriers. It traced the continent's major river systems, particularly the Rhine and Danube, and explained their recurring importance for transport, agriculture, and political and military frontiers across many centuries. It examined Europe's principal mountain systems, the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, and others, as features that functioned simultaneously as barriers, filters, and, through specific passes, corridors of contact, and it surveyed the continent's great plains, including the North European Plain, the East European Plain, and the Pannonian Basin, as regions whose openness supported both agricultural productivity and, at various times, the rapid movement of migrating peoples and invading armies.

The chapter also examined Europe's climate zones, distinguishing Mediterranean, oceanic, and continental patterns while cautioning against oversimplified claims about the Gulf Stream's role and emphasizing that Europe's climate has fluctuated meaningfully over the Holocene epoch, including the Medieval Climate Anomaly and the Little Ice Age. It surveyed the continent's forests, steppe, and soil variation, with particular attention to the historically significant steppe corridor connecting the Black Sea region to Central Asia, and examined the historical importance of natural resources including timber, salt, metals, and, from the eighteenth century onward, coal.

A recurring theme throughout the chapter has been that the European landscape has itself changed considerably over time, both through natural geological and climatic processes operating across immense timescales and, increasingly from the Neolithic period onward, through deliberate and incidental human modification, including large scale deforestation, wetland drainage, agricultural terracing, and urban growth. The "natural" landscape encountered by any given historical generation was, in most periods examined by this textbook, already substantially shaped by earlier human activity.

Finally, the chapter closed with an extended methodological caution against geographic determinism, arguing instead for an approach, sometimes called environmental possibilism, in which geography establishes a range of possibilities and constraints within which human societies, through institutions, technology, culture, and specific historical choices, produced varied and historically contingent outcomes. The Historical Debate feature examined this question directly, weighing arguments that emphasize geography's shaping influence on European political fragmentation and commercial development against arguments emphasizing the primacy of institutions, contingency, and human agency.

These themes, connection through seas and rivers, the dual barrier and corridor function of mountains, regional climatic and ecological diversity, the long history of human environmental modification, and the necessary caution against determinism, will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of prehistoric, ancient, medieval, early modern, and modern European history. Chapter 3 now turns to the first identifiable human societies to inhabit this landscape, the Paleolithic populations whose adaptation to Ice Age and post glacial environments represents the earliest chapter of the human story examined in this textbook.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Eurasia
The single continuous landmass comprising Europe and Asia, of which Europe is conventionally treated as a large western peninsula.
Peninsula
A piece of land nearly surrounded by water, connected to a larger landmass; Europe as a whole, and many of its major regions such as Iberia, Italy, and Scandinavia, can be described this way.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch lasting from roughly 2.58 million years ago until the beginning of the Holocene, 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated glacial and interglacial cycles across the Northern Hemisphere and encompassing the long span of early hominin occupation of Europe.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago, characterized by a relatively, though not perfectly, stable interglacial climate.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period, roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, when continental ice sheets reached their greatest recent extent across parts of northern Europe.
Deglaciation
The process of ice sheet retreat and melting that followed the Last Glacial Maximum, producing rising sea levels and reshaping coastlines over several thousand years.
Isostatic rebound
The gradual rise of land that had been depressed under the weight of glacial ice, occurring over thousands of years following deglaciation, particularly in Scandinavia.
Transhumance
The seasonal movement of livestock and often their herders between lowland and highland pastures, historically practiced in mountainous regions such as the Alps and Pyrenees.
Watershed
The area of land that drains into a particular river or river system; also used to describe the boundary, often along high ground, separating one river's drainage area from another's.
Continental climate
A climate pattern characterized by significant seasonal temperature extremes and generally lower, less evenly distributed precipitation, typical of much of inland central, eastern, and northern Europe.
Oceanic climate
A climate pattern characterized by mild winters, cool summers, and precipitation distributed fairly evenly across the year, typical of much of western Europe.
Mediterranean climate
A climate pattern characterized by hot, dry summers and mild, wetter winters, typical of Iberia, Italy, Greece, and other Mediterranean coastlands.
North Atlantic Drift
An ocean current system, associated with the broader Gulf Stream system, that transports warm water toward northwestern Europe and contributes, along with atmospheric circulation, to the region's comparatively mild climate for its latitude.
Palynology
The scientific study of fossil pollen, used by historians and scientists to reconstruct past vegetation, land use, and climate.
Dendrochronology
The scientific study of tree growth rings, used to establish precise chronologies and detect unusually cold, warm, wet, or dry years in the past.
Medieval Climate Anomaly
A period of relative warmth in parts of the North Atlantic region, approximately the tenth through thirteenth centuries CE, sometimes called in older or popular usage the Medieval Warm Period, a label many specialists now avoid because it can imply a uniform global warming not clearly supported by the regional proxy evidence; its timing and global extent remain subjects of ongoing scientific study.
Little Ice Age
A period of generally cooler climate across parts of the Northern Hemisphere, approximately the fourteenth through mid nineteenth centuries CE, marked by advancing Alpine glaciers and, at times, shortened growing seasons.
Loess
A fine, wind-deposited silt, commonly accumulated during glacial periods, that often weathers into highly fertile soil, covering substantial portions of the North European Plain and parts of Ukraine.
Steppe
A belt of open, largely treeless grassland extending from the Black Sea region eastward into Central Asia, historically home to pastoral nomadic societies.
Environmental possibilism
The view that physical geography establishes a range of possibilities and constraints within which human societies make historically contingent choices, rather than a fixed destiny geography alone determines.
Environmental determinism
The view that physical geography, climate, or environment directly and predictably determines the character or historical trajectory of a society; a framework this textbook treats with considerable caution.
Deforestation
The clearance of forest cover, historically associated with the spread of farming, grazing, and urban settlement across many centuries of European history.
Land reclamation
The process of converting wetland, marsh, or shallow coastal water into usable agricultural or urban land, exemplified by centuries of drainage projects in the Low Countries.
Isthmus
A narrow strip of land connecting two larger landmasses, such as the Isthmus of Corinth connecting the Peloponnese to mainland Greece.
Forest-steppe
A transitional ecological zone between temperate forest and open steppe grassland, found in parts of Ukraine and southern Russia.
Maquis and garrigue
Regional terms for drought resistant Mediterranean scrubland and woodland vegetation adapted to hot, dry summers.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has established the physical stage on which the remaining one hundred and forty two chapters of this textbook unfold. Every subsequent development, the spread of farming, the rise of Greek city states and the Roman Empire, the formation of medieval kingdoms, the growth of trading cities, the movement of migrating peoples, the outbreak and course of wars, and the industrial transformations of the modern era, occurred within, and was shaped by, the mountains, rivers, plains, coastlines, and climate zones surveyed here.

At the same time, this chapter has insisted throughout that geography is not destiny. The same river that served as a Roman military frontier could, centuries later, become a corridor of medieval commerce. The same mountains that isolated some communities for months at a time also, through specific passes, carried pilgrims, traders, and armies across political and cultural boundaries. The same plains that supported dense agricultural populations also, in periods of political weakness, offered invading armies an unobstructed path. Understanding this dual character, geography as a shaper of possibility and constraint rather than a determinant of outcome, is a habit of mind students should carry into every subsequent chapter of this textbook.

This chapter has also emphasized that the European landscape has itself been a subject of historical change, not a fixed and unchanging backdrop. Coastlines shifted as glaciers melted and sea levels rose. Forests receded as farming communities cleared land across many centuries. Wetlands were drained, rivers were diverted, and, eventually, coal extracted from beneath the ground helped power an industrial transformation that would, in turn, reshape the landscape yet again. Recognizing this ongoing environmental history equips students to read later chapters' discussions of agriculture, settlement, resource use, and, eventually, industrialization and environmental change with appropriate historical depth.

Chapter 3 now turns from this physical setting to the first identifiable human societies to inhabit it: the Paleolithic hunter gatherer populations who adapted, over tens of thousands of years, to the shifting glacial and post glacial environments this chapter has described. Understanding the landscape they encountered, and the landscape that would continue to change around every subsequent European society examined in this textbook, is the essential foundation this chapter has sought to provide.
