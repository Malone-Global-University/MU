The Persian Wars and Classical Greece

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 480 BCE, a Persian army drawn from dozens of subject peoples of an empire stretching from the Aegean to the Indus crossed the Hellespont on bridges of lashed boats, while a coalition of Greek city states, themselves deeply divided about whether resistance was even wise, tried to decide where, and whether, to make a stand. Neither side was as unified as later memory would suggest.

Consider first a Greek city on the Anatolian coast, one of the prosperous Ionian towns whose harbors, workshops, and marketplaces had for two generations paid tribute to the Persian crown, provided ships and soldiers when the Great King required them, and lived under local rulers whom Persian satraps confirmed or removed as it suited imperial policy. For most residents of a city such as Miletus or Ephesus, Persian rule was not a distant abstraction, it was the ordinary framework of political life, no more remarkable in daily experience than any other government's tax collectors and appointed officials. Consider next a Spartan citizen soldier, trained from childhood in a system of collective discipline that Chapter 10 examined at length, whose polis had built its entire social order around producing hoplites capable of holding a battle line under extreme pressure. Consider, finally, a rower in the Athenian fleet, very likely a citizen of modest property who could not have afforded the equipment of a hoplite but who found, in the crisis of 480 BCE, that his labor at an oar had become as militarily indispensable as any nobleman's spear.

These three figures, the Ionian subject of the Persian crown, the Spartan hoplite, and the Athenian rower, all lived through what this chapter calls the Persian Wars, yet they experienced entirely different wars, shaped by entirely different political relationships to the conflict. This is the central historical problem this chapter must confront. Popular memory, reaching back to the ancient world itself and renewed by countless later retellings, has flattened a genuinely complicated set of events into a single, satisfying story, small, free, democratic Greece defending itself against a vast, despotic Asian empire, and winning through courage and civic virtue alone. That story is not simply an exaggeration, it actively misrepresents several of the most important facts established by both ancient testimony and modern scholarship.

The Achaemenid Persian Empire, which this chapter must introduce on its own terms rather than merely as an antagonist in someone else's story, was one of the most sophisticated and successful imperial systems the ancient world had yet produced. It governed an extraordinary diversity of peoples, languages, and religious traditions across a territory that dwarfed the entire Greek world many times over, through an administrative structure of provinces, tribute assessments, royal roads, and local elites. Greek city states on the Anatolian coast had been part of this empire for decades before the wars this chapter examines, and many mainland Greek states, when confronted with the prospect of Persian invasion, chose neutrality, submission, or active cooperation rather than resistance.

At the same time, this chapter does not tell a revisionist story in which the traditional narrative is simply inverted, with Persia recast as an innocent civilizing power and Greek resistance dismissed as parochial obstinacy. The invasions of 490 and 480 to 479 BCE were real military campaigns with real consequences, the burning of Athens actually happened, the battles at Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, Plataea, and Mycale actually occurred, and the political outcomes of those battles genuinely reshaped the relationship between Sparta and Athens in ways that made the following century's history possible. The task of this chapter is not to diminish the significance of these events but to explain them with the same historical precision this textbook has applied throughout, distinguishing established fact from later legend, examining Persian imperial logic alongside Greek political fragmentation, and resisting the temptation to reduce a complicated, multi-decade conflict to a morality tale about freedom and tyranny.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about evidence. Nearly everything historians know about the Persian Wars in narrative detail comes down through a single continuous literary source, the Histories of Herodotus of Halicarnassus, composed decades after the events it describes, drawing on oral tradition, local memory, and the author's own extensive travel and inquiry. Herodotus is an indispensable and often remarkably careful historian, but he wrote a literary and interpretive work, not a modern battlefield report, and his numerical claims about the size of Persian forces in particular require the same critical scrutiny historians apply to any ancient source making extraordinary claims. A near contemporary literary source, Aeschylus's tragedy the Persians, performed in Athens in 472 BCE, offers a different kind of evidence entirely, a dramatic imagining of the Persian court's reaction to defeat, written by a citizen who had very likely fought at Salamis himself. Comparing these two very different kinds of evidence will be one of this chapter's central exercises.

Finally, this chapter exists to prepare students for Chapter 12. The alliance of Greek states that defeated Xerxes' invasion did not resolve the underlying tension between Sparta's dominance on land and Athens's growing confidence at sea. If anything, victory intensified that tension by giving each power new resources, new prestige, and new reasons to distrust the other's ambitions. Understanding how the Persian Wars actually unfolded, and understanding precisely what they did and did not settle, is the necessary foundation for understanding why the alliance that saved mainland Greece from Persian conquest would, within half a century, dissolve into the catastrophic internal war examined in the next chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the administrative and territorial structure of the Achaemenid Persian Empire and explain why it should be understood on its own imperial terms rather than solely as an antagonist in Greek history.
  2. Explain how Greek cities in western Anatolia came under Persian rule and what that rule involved in practice.
  3. Trace the causes, course, and suppression of the Ionian Revolt of 499 to 494 BCE.
  4. Analyze the campaign of 490 BCE and the Battle of Marathon, distinguishing probable events from later legend.
  5. Explain why Athens developed significant naval power between the two Persian invasions and why that development mattered.
  6. Describe the scale, organization, and logistics of Xerxes' invasion of 480 BCE while evaluating Herodotus's numerical claims critically.
  7. Identify the genuine divisions within the Greek world during the invasion, including neutrality, submission, and cooperation with Persia.
  8. Analyze the battles of Thermopylae, Artemisium, Salamis, Plataea, and Mycale and distinguish their military significance from their later cultural memory.
  9. Evaluate multiple, interacting explanations for the coalition's eventual success rather than relying on a single cause.
  10. Explain how the wars altered the relative positions of Sparta and Athens without creating lasting Greek political unity.
  11. Assess the reliability, perspective, and limitations of Herodotus and Aeschylus as historical sources for this period.
  12. Connect the outcome of the Persian Wars to the tensions that will structure Chapter 12's account of Athenian empire and the Peloponnesian War.

  2. What kind of empire was the Achaemenid Empire, and how did its administrative structure allow it to govern such a vast and diverse territory?

Historical Context

Chapter 10 left the Greek world around 500 BCE with two increasingly distinct political experiments well underway. Sparta had built a militarized society of citizen hoplites, supported by a large subjugated population and organized around collective discipline, austere equality among citizens, and a dual kingship balanced by a council of elders and an assembly. Athens, following the reforms traditionally associated with Cleisthenes at the end of the sixth century BCE, had reorganized its citizen body into new tribes cutting across older aristocratic and regional loyalties, established a Council of Five Hundred chosen in part by lot, and created the institutional foundations of what would develop, over the following decades, into full participatory democracy. Neither system had yet been tested by a genuine external threat capable of destroying the polis altogether. The conflict this chapter examines provided that test.

To understand why that test arrived, students must first understand the empire that arrived with it. By the final decades of the sixth century BCE, the Achaemenid Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great in the middle of that century and expanded dramatically under his successors Cambyses and, above all, Darius I, controlled an almost unimaginably vast territory. Its lands stretched from the Aegean coast of Anatolia in the west to the Indus valley in the east, from the Caucasus and Central Asian steppe in the north to Egypt and the Persian Gulf in the south. No Greek polis, and indeed no combination of Greek poleis, approached this empire in territory, population, revenue, or military manpower. The empire governed this territory through a system of provinces called satrapies, each administered by an appointed governor, or satrap, responsible for collecting tribute, maintaining order, and supplying troops when the Great King required them. A network of roads, most famously the Royal Road connecting Sardis in western Anatolia to the imperial heartland at Susa, allowed royal messengers, officials, and, when necessary, armies to move across the empire's enormous distances with a speed that impressed even Greek observers such as Herodotus. Local elites, including Greek tyrants and oligarchs in the Anatolian cities, often continued to govern their own communities under Persian oversight, a system of indirect rule that required far fewer imperial resources than direct administration would have demanded.

Greek cities in western Anatolia, the region the Greeks called Ionia, came under this system following the Persian conquest of the kingdom of Lydia around the middle of the sixth century BCE. Before that conquest, many Ionian cities had already been subject to or closely aligned with Lydia under its king Croesus, so Persian rule replaced one regional overlord with another rather than introducing an entirely unfamiliar political relationship. Persian administration typically confirmed or installed local tyrants in the Ionian cities, men who owed their positions to Persian backing and who, in exchange, ensured that tribute was paid and that Ionian ships and soldiers were available for imperial campaigns when required. For several decades, this arrangement, while not always popular locally, proved workable enough that no sustained Ionian resistance emerged.

This chapter's story begins where that arrangement began to break down, in the final years of the sixth century BCE, and it ends with the Greek victories of 479 BCE that ended the immediate Persian attempt to bring mainland Greece under direct imperial control. It does not end Greek-Persian relations altogether. Persia remained the dominant imperial power of the Near East and a major power in the eastern Mediterranean for more than a century after Plataea and Mycale, continuing to intervene in Greek politics, fund and defund rival Greek alliances, and shape the strategic calculations of every major Greek state examined in the chapters that follow. Students should resist any temptation to treat 479 BCE as the end of Persian involvement in Greek affairs. It marks the end of a specific invasion, not the end of an empire, and not the end of a relationship.

Section 11.1

The Achaemenid Empire and the Greek World

No chapter of Greek history can be understood in isolation from the empire that repeatedly intervened in it, and no fair account of that empire can treat it merely as a foil for Greek achievement. By the time this chapter's narrative begins, the Achaemenid Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great around the middle of the sixth century BCE, had become the largest and most administratively sophisticated state the ancient Near East had yet produced. Cyrus's conquests brought Media, Lydia, and Babylon under a single crown within roughly two decades. His successor Cambyses added Egypt. Darius I, who came to the throne in 522 BCE after a period of internal crisis, regularized tribute assessment, expanded imperial communications, and governed through a provincial administrative system whose foundations had developed under Cyrus and Cambyses, producing an imperial framework that would endure for nearly two centuries.

The empire governed its territory through provinces called satrapies, each administered by an appointed official, the satrap, who was typically a member of the Persian aristocracy or, in some cases, a trusted local elite. The foundations of this provincial system had developed under Cyrus and Cambyses, and Darius, whose precise role in reshaping it remains debated among modern historians, is credited above all with regularizing tribute assessment across the provinces and expanding the empire's communications network. Satraps collected tribute, assessed in silver, grain, livestock, and other regional products according to each province's productive capacity, administered justice under royal oversight, maintained local garrisons, and supplied troops and ships when the Great King called for them. A network of roads, most famously the Royal Road linking Sardis in western Anatolia to the imperial capital region around Susa, allowed messengers on relays of fresh horses to cross distances that would have taken ordinary travelers months in a matter of days, a logistical achievement that Herodotus himself, writing as a Greek observer generations later, described with evident admiration.

This system allowed the empire to govern an extraordinary diversity of subject peoples, Babylonians, Egyptians, Lydians, Phoenicians, various Iranian peoples, and, from the middle of the sixth century BCE onward, the Greek cities of western Anatolia, without imposing uniform religious, linguistic, or cultural practices. Persian administration generally tolerated local religious traditions, local law, and, within limits, local political arrangements, provided that tribute was paid and loyalty maintained. This should not be mistaken for a modern conception of pluralism or tolerance as an end in itself, the empire's flexibility served practical administrative purposes, allowing a comparatively small Persian ruling elite to govern an enormous and diverse territory with limited direct coercion. But it does mean that Persian rule over the Ionian Greek cities, examined in the next section, was neither uniquely oppressive by the standards of the ancient world nor experienced uniformly as intolerable by all its subjects.

Understanding this imperial context matters for a specific reason central to this chapter's argument. When later Greek writers, and even later modern popular culture, describe the Persian Wars as a contest between Greek freedom and Persian despotism, they generally do so from a Greek perspective centuries removed from the practical realities of Achaemenid administration, and they generally do so with the benefit of hindsight, knowing that the invasions ultimately failed. Approaching the empire on its own administrative terms, as a functioning, sophisticated, and largely successful system of imperial governance, allows students to understand why many Greek communities, weighing their own interests against the manifest scale and organization of Persian power, chose accommodation rather than resistance, a choice examined directly in Section 11.7.

Section 11.2

Greeks under Persian Rule and the Ionian Revolt

The Greek cities of Ionia, including Miletus, Ephesus, Samos, and numerous smaller communities along the Anatolian coast and its adjacent islands, had passed under Persian authority following Cyrus's conquest of Lydia in the middle of the sixth century BCE. Before that conquest, several of these cities had already been tributary to Lydia under its king Croesus, so Persian overlordship, in practical terms, often meant a change of distant master rather than a wholly new experience of subjection. Persian administration in Ionia typically worked through local tyrants, Greek individuals whose authority within their own cities depended substantially on Persian backing and who, in exchange, ensured the flow of tribute and military support to the imperial system described in Section 11.1.

This arrangement proved workable for several decades, but it rested on the continuing cooperation of local elites whose own political fortunes could change. Around 499 BCE, Aristagoras, the Persian-appointed tyrant of Miletus, found himself in a difficult position after a failed campaign against the island of Naxos that he had promised Persian authorities would succeed easily. Facing possible removal from power and the destruction of his own political standing, Aristagoras chose an extraordinary gamble, he renounced his tyranny, encouraged the other Ionian cities to do the same, and launched what historians call the Ionian Revolt, an uprising against Persian authority across much of the Anatolian Greek world.

Students should approach the Ionian Revolt with considerable interpretive caution. Herodotus is essentially the sole continuous literary source for its origins and course, and his account, composed decades later, presents Aristagoras's motives in a manner shaped by his broader narrative purposes, emphasizing personal ambition and political calculation rather than any straightforward nationalist desire for Greek freedom. Modern historians generally accept the broad outline of Herodotus's account while remaining cautious about attributing clear ideological motives to participants whose actual reasoning is not independently documented.

The revolt drew limited but significant mainland Greek support. Athens sent a modest naval contingent, and the small city of Eretria on the island of Euboea also contributed forces, a decision that Herodotus later connects directly to Darius's decision to invade both cities in 490 BCE. Athenian and allied forces, together with rebel Ionians, marched inland and burned Sardis, the regional Persian administrative center, in 498 BCE, an event of real symbolic and material significance regardless of its limited direct military consequence. The revolt subsequently spread to other regions, including Cyprus and parts of the Hellespontine coast, before Persian forces, methodically reasserting control city by city, brought the rebellion to its decisive end at the naval Battle of Lade in 494 BCE, where a combined Persian fleet decisively defeated the Ionian rebel fleet. Miletus itself, the revolt's origin point, was captured and severely punished shortly afterward.

The revolt's suppression restored Persian authority throughout Ionia within a few years, and by most practical measures it had failed entirely to achieve Ionian independence. Its historical significance, however, extends well beyond its immediate military outcome. It provided Darius with a documented grievance against Athens and Eretria for their support of the rebels, a grievance that Herodotus presents as a central motivation for the 490 BCE expedition examined in the next section. It also demonstrated, to both Persian authorities and mainland Greek observers, that resistance to Persian power, while difficult to sustain, was not automatically hopeless, a lesson whose full significance would become clear only a decade later.

Section 11.3

Darius and the Campaign of 490 BCE

Following the suppression of the Ionian Revolt, Darius I turned his attention westward, toward the mainland Greek states that had supported the rebellion. Herodotus presents Darius's motivation largely in terms of punishment and personal anger, reporting a tradition in which the king instructed a servant to remind him three times at every meal, "Master, remember the Athenians." Whatever the precise accuracy of this particular anecdote, historians generally agree that Darius's campaign served broader imperial purposes beyond simple retribution, extending Persian authority and influence into the Aegean world, securing the empire's western maritime frontier, and punishing states that had demonstrated a capacity and willingness to resist Persian power.

In 492 BCE, a Persian force under the command of Mardonius, a relative of Darius, campaigned along the northern Aegean coast, reasserting Persian authority over Thrace and Macedon, though this expedition suffered significant losses when its supporting fleet was wrecked in a storm off the promontory of Mount Athos. Two years later, in 490 BCE, Darius dispatched a more direct expedition under the joint command of Datis, a Median commander, and Artaphernes, a nephew of the king, sailing directly across the Aegean via the islands rather than risking another passage around the exposed northern coastline.

This expedition proceeded through the Cyclades before reaching the Greek mainland. Herodotus reports that the Persian force attacked Naxos, the island whose earlier successful resistance to Aristagoras's campaign had helped trigger the Ionian Revolt examined in Section 11.2, burning its main town and compelling the submission of other Cycladic islands along the way, before forcing the city of Carystus, on the island of Euboea, into submission as well. Only after this intermediate campaign did the expedition turn to Eretria, the Euboean city that had contributed forces to the Ionian Revolt. After a siege of several days, the city fell, reportedly through the treachery of certain citizens who opened its gates, and Herodotus reports that its population was deported, though the precise scale and permanence of this deportation remains difficult to verify with certainty from other evidence. The Persian force then crossed to the Attic coast and landed at the plain of Marathon, a location Herodotus attributes in part to the advice of Hippias, the exiled former tyrant of Athens who accompanied the Persian expedition and who may have hoped that his own restoration to power in Athens would follow a Persian victory.

Students should resist any temptation to reduce Darius's motivation to a single simple cause. Herodotus's narrative foregrounds personal vengeance for the burning of Sardis, but a fuller historical explanation must also account for the campaign's broader strategic logic within the empire's western expansion, the value of securing Aegean sea lanes and northern Aegean territories already under partial Persian influence, and the political utility, within Persian court politics, of a decisive demonstration of the Great King's power against states that had supported rebellion. The available evidence, dependent overwhelmingly on Herodotus's later reconstruction, does not allow historians to weigh these motivations against one another with great precision, a limitation this chapter's Historical Debate feature will examine further in connection with the broader question of Persian strategic failure.

Section 11.4

Marathon

The plain of Marathon, roughly forty kilometers northeast of the city of Athens, offered the Persian force a landing site with open ground suited to cavalry, a significant tactical advantage that Persian commanders may have specifically sought. Athens, upon learning of the Persian landing, mobilized its citizen hoplite militia and, according to Herodotus, sent a professional runner named Philippides, often rendered Pheidippides, to Sparta before the battle to request assistance. A later and separate legend, not found in Herodotus, holds that a runner then carried news of victory from Marathon back to Athens after the battle, and students should not confuse these two distinct traditions. Spartan forces, according to Herodotus, could not march immediately because of a religious observance connected to the festival of the Carneia, and they arrived at Marathon only after the battle had already been fought and won, a detail that later Greek memory sometimes downplayed in favor of a more unified narrative of Panhellenic resistance.

The Athenians, joined by a contingent from the small neighboring city of Plataea, whose loyalty to Athens would remain a significant feature of Athenian political culture for generations afterward, faced the Persian force largely without Spartan support. The Athenian commander Miltiades, who had personal experience of Persian military methods from earlier service under Persian authority in the northern Aegean, favored engaging the Persian force in open battle. Herodotus describes the Athenian line on the day of battle as unusually thin in the center and stronger on the wings, an arrangement that allowed the flanks to envelop the Persian force once fighting began, but whether this reflected a deliberate double-envelopment plan devised by Miltiades himself, as later tradition has often assumed, or a reconstruction shaped by hindsight and Miltiades' later celebrated reputation, is not something Herodotus's account clearly establishes. Whatever the precise tactical details, which remain difficult to reconstruct with full confidence from a single retrospective literary source, the heavily armored Greek hoplites, fighting at close quarters in the phalanx formation examined in Chapter 9, proved devastatingly effective against a Persian force whose infantry relied more heavily on missile weapons and lighter equipment.

Herodotus reports one hundred and ninety two Athenian dead against an alleged Persian total exceeding six thousand. The Athenian figure became embedded in Athenian civic commemoration, inscribed and remembered for generations, while the Persian total cannot be independently verified and should be treated with the same caution this chapter applies to other Herodotean casualty figures. The defeated Persian force withdrew by sea, reportedly attempting a subsequent approach toward the undefended city of Athens itself before the returning Athenian army, marching rapidly back from Marathon, arrived in time to deter any landing.

The historical significance of Marathon lies less in its immediate strategic consequences, which were real but limited, since the Persian Empire's capacity to mount further campaigns was scarcely affected, than in its psychological and political impact on Athens itself. A citizen militia, fighting without its most celebrated Greek military ally, had defeated a Persian expeditionary force in a pitched battle, a result that later Athenian political culture would remember and celebrate for generations, commemorated in public monuments, in the burial mound raised for the Athenian dead that remains visible on the plain today, and in a civic memory that would shape Athenian self-understanding well into the following century. Students should distinguish this genuine and significant tactical victory from the later, considerably inflated tradition that would eventually credit Marathon with saving Greek civilization from Persian conquest altogether, a claim that overstates both the scale of the 490 BCE campaign and its relationship to the far larger invasion that would follow a decade later.

Section 11.5

Between the Invasions: Athens, Sparta, and Naval Power

The decade separating Marathon from Xerxes' invasion was not a period of Greek complacency, at least not in Athens, though its significance becomes clear only in light of what followed. This section connects directly to the political institutions Chapter 10 examined in detail, since it was precisely Athens's Cleisthenic system of mass citizen participation that enabled the dramatic strategic decision this section describes.

Around 483 BCE, a rich vein of silver was discovered, or more productively exploited, at the state-owned mines of Laurion in southern Attica. Rather than distributing this unexpected revenue directly among Athenian citizens, a proposal that some sources suggest had genuine popular support, the Athenian statesman Themistocles persuaded the citizen assembly to devote the funds to a massive expansion of the Athenian navy, ostensibly justified by an ongoing rivalry with the nearby island state of Aegina but understood by Themistocles, according to later tradition, as essential preparation against a renewed Persian threat that he judged inevitable. Students should note the significance of this decision having been made through Athens's democratic assembly rather than imposed by a single ruler, a point of considerable importance for understanding how Athenian institutions, examined in Chapter 10, translated into concrete strategic capacity.

This program produced a fleet of roughly two hundred triremes, oared warships whose construction, maintenance, and crewing required substantial ongoing public investment and a large body of citizens, many of them from the poorer property classes who could not afford hoplite equipment, trained and available to serve as rowers. This development mattered enormously for Athenian society as well as Athenian strategy, since it created a new and politically significant category of citizen, the naval rower, whose military indispensability in the coming crisis has often been linked by historians to the further development of democratic participation, though the precise causal relationship between naval service and democratic reform remains debated rather than settled, a development examined further in Chapter 12's account of Athenian democracy's continued evolution.

Sparta, meanwhile, remained committed to its established model of land-based military power, examined at length in Chapter 10, continuing to rely on its citizen hoplite army, supported by allied Peloponnesian states within the alliance system historians generally call the Peloponnesian League. Sparta possessed little independent naval tradition and would depend heavily, in the coming invasion, on the naval contributions of other Greek states, particularly Athens, Corinth, and Aegina. This asymmetry, Spartan land dominance paired with Athenian naval expansion, would prove decisive in shaping how the coming invasion could be resisted at all, and it directly foreshadows the divided command structure and occasionally uneasy Spartan-Athenian cooperation examined in the sections that follow.

Section 11.6

Xerxes and the Invasion of 480 BCE

Darius I died in 486 BCE, before he could launch a renewed campaign against mainland Greece, and the Achaemenid throne passed to his son Xerxes I. After several years devoted initially to suppressing rebellions in Egypt and Babylon, Xerxes turned his attention to Greece, assembling over a period of years an invasion force that Herodotus describes in terms meant to convey overwhelming, almost unimaginable scale. Herodotus's specific numerical claims, which place the combined land and naval forces in the millions once support personnel are included, are almost universally rejected by modern historians as substantially inflated, whether through the exaggeration of Herodotus's informants, the rhetorical conventions of ancient historical writing, or simple logistical impossibility given the agricultural and transport capacity of the ancient Mediterranean world. Modern estimates, while necessarily uncertain and still substantially disputed among historians, generally place the actual invasion force far below Herodotus's millions, with reconstructions ranging from many tens of thousands to well over one hundred thousand combatants, supported by a large fleet, a scale that would still have represented an overwhelming numerical advantage over any Greek force that could plausibly be assembled.

What matters more for historical understanding than any precise figure is the invasion's demonstrated logistical sophistication, which reflects the imperial administrative capacity examined in Section 11.1 far more clearly than any specific troop count. Xerxes ordered the construction of a canal through the isthmus at Mount Athos, avoiding the promontory where a Persian fleet had been wrecked by storm in 492 BCE, and commissioned a bridge of lashed boats across the Hellespont, a narrow strait separating Europe and Asia, to allow his land forces to cross without relying on ships alone. Herodotus reports that an initial bridge was destroyed by a storm and that Xerxes, in a fit of anger, ordered the sea itself to be whipped and branded, an anecdote that later tradition treated as an illustration of royal hubris but that modern historians treat with appropriate caution as a literary embellishment rather than a verified event.

The invasion force, drawn from across the empire's many subject peoples, included contingents described by Herodotus in considerable, if not always verifiable, ethnographic detail, Persians, Medes, Bactrians, Indians, Egyptians, Phoenicians providing much of the fleet's naval strength, and Ionian Greeks compelled to serve under Persian command, a detail of particular importance for the theme, developed further in Section 11.7, that the invasion was never simply "Persia against Greece" in any ethnically or politically uniform sense. This multiethnic composition reflected the empire's fundamental character as a vast tributary system rather than a single national army in any modern sense, and it should caution students against using the phrase "the Persians" as though the invading force were homogeneous.

The scale of preparation, spanning several years and drawing on resources from across the empire's provinces, illustrates a strategic logic considerably more calculated than Herodotus's emphasis on personal royal motivation alone might suggest. Securing the Aegean world, punishing continued resistance, and extending direct Persian authority over mainland Greece would have consolidated imperial control over the empire's entire western maritime frontier, a strategic objective consistent with the broader pattern of Achaemenid expansion under both Darius and Xerxes.

Section 11.7

A Divided Greek Response

No aspect of the Persian Wars has been more consistently distorted by later memory than the assumption that "the Greeks" responded to Xerxes' invasion as a single, unified people defending a shared homeland. The historical reality, well documented in Herodotus's own account even as later tradition often smoothed over its implications, was considerably more fractured.

In 481 BCE, representatives of a number of Greek states, meeting at Corinth, formed a defensive alliance historians generally call the Hellenic League, placing Sparta, as the leading military land power, in overall command of both land and naval operations, notwithstanding Athens's far larger naval contribution. This alliance, however, included only a minority of the Greek world's many independent poleis. Many states, weighing the manifest scale of Persian power against their own limited resources and the practical likelihood of Persian victory, chose neutrality or outright submission rather than joining a resistance that many contemporaries, quite reasonably, judged unlikely to succeed.

The region of Thessaly, in northern Greece, initially sent representatives to the Hellenic League and requested that allied forces be positioned to defend the northern approach to Greece, but after the allied position at the Vale of Tempe proved strategically untenable and was withdrawn, Thessalian leaders submitted to Persian authority, a decision Herodotus attributes partly to the practical calculation that continued resistance, without allied support actually present on Thessalian soil, offered little benefit and considerable risk. Thebes and much of Boeotia likewise submitted to Persian authority as Xerxes' forces advanced southward, a decision that would have lasting political consequences for Thebes's later reputation among other Greek states, though modern historians generally treat Boeotian submission as a rational response to exposed geographic position and Theban political rivalry with Athens rather than as straightforward treachery.

Historians commonly refer to Greek cooperation with Persia during this period using the term Medism, derived from "Medes," an Iranian people closely associated with the Persians within the Achaemenid imperial system, whose name Greek writers often used loosely and interchangeably with "Persians" in general. Students should treat this term with considerable care. Later Greek political rhetoric, particularly in Athens and among Athens's allies, frequently deployed accusations of Medism as a moralizing weapon against political rivals, retroactively condemning wartime accommodation as treachery in a manner that obscures the genuine range of rational calculations available to Greek communities facing overwhelming Persian power. Modern historians increasingly emphasize that submission, neutrality, and even active cooperation represented reasonable responses available to communities that, unlike Sparta and Athens, lacked either substantial military resources or confidence in the resistance coalition's eventual success.

It is essential, finally, to remember that Greeks were present within the Persian invasion force itself, not merely as unwilling conscripts but, in some cases, as communities whose leaders had made calculated political decisions to align with Persian power, just as Ionian Greeks had lived under Persian authority for decades before the revolt examined in Section 11.2. The conflict this chapter examines was never a clean confrontation between two ethnically or politically unified sides. It was, more accurately, a crisis that exposed and intensified existing divisions within an already fragmented Greek political world, divisions that the chapters ahead, particularly Chapter 12's account of Athenian empire, will show to have lasting consequences.

Section 11.8

Thermopylae and Artemisium

As Xerxes' forces advanced into central Greece in the late summer of 480 BCE, the Hellenic League attempted to block their progress at two connected points, a narrow coastal pass at Thermopylae, where mountains crowded close to the sea and left only a constricted route for a large army to pass, and the nearby strait of Artemisium, where the allied fleet positioned itself to prevent the Persian navy from outflanking the land position by sea. These two engagements should be understood together, as complementary components of a single strategic effort to use Greece's fragmented geography, examined in Chapter 2, to offset the invaders' overwhelming numerical advantage.

The Spartan king Leonidas commanded the allied force at Thermopylae, and later tradition, reinforced by countless retellings across the centuries, has reduced this force in popular memory to "the three hundred Spartans." This simplification requires direct correction. Herodotus's own account makes clear that Leonidas commanded a considerably larger combined force, including contingents from Thespiae, Thebes, and other Greek states, likely numbering several thousand troops in total during the battle's opening days, before a local resident named Ephialtes revealed a mountain path that allowed a Persian detachment to outflank the defenders. Herodotus reports that, upon learning of this outflanking movement, Leonidas sent most of the allied contingents away, retaining only his three hundred Spartans, a smaller group of Thespians who chose to remain, and, according to Herodotus, a contingent of Thebans whose presence at this final stage remains a matter of some ancient dispute, possibly reflecting later Theban attempts to distance the city from accusations of Medism discussed in the previous section. Herodotus presents this decision as Leonidas's own choice, though the reasoning behind it, whether military calculation, religious obligation, or later narrative shaping, cannot be established with full confidence from a single retrospective source.

The Spartans and Thespians who remained were killed in the final fighting, a military outcome that was, in the most direct sense, a Persian victory and a Greek defeat. The Thebans who remained met a different fate, Herodotus reports that most surrendered and were taken alive, though branded as slaves, rather than being killed alongside the Spartans and Thespians, a distinction students should not collapse into a single undifferentiated account of annihilation. The battle did not prevent the Persian advance into central Greece and, ultimately, into Attica itself. Its historical significance lies not in its tactical outcome but in what it demonstrated and in how it was subsequently remembered, a demonstration that a heavily outnumbered but well-positioned hoplite force could inflict disproportionate casualties on a much larger opponent, and a memory, cultivated deliberately in the following decades, that would come to symbolize willing sacrifice in defense of Greek freedom, commemorated in later inscriptions and literary tradition that transformed a tactical defeat into one of the most powerful narratives in the entire corpus of Greek historical memory.

At Artemisium, the allied fleet fought a series of inconclusive engagements against the Persian navy over the same days, suffering losses but preventing a decisive Persian naval breakthrough, before withdrawing southward upon receiving news of Leonidas's defeat, since the fleet's position at Artemisium depended on the land force at Thermopylae remaining intact. This coordinated, if ultimately unsuccessful, effort to use narrow geography on both land and sea to offset Persian numbers established a strategic pattern that the coalition would apply with greater success in the following weeks, at the far more consequential engagement examined in the next section.

Section 11.9

Athens Abandoned, Athens Destroyed, Salamis Fought

Following the fall of Thermopylae, the road into Attica lay open to Persian forces, and Athens faced an immediate and existential decision. Rather than attempt to defend the city itself, a proposal that would almost certainly have led to Athens's destruction along with its population, the Athenian assembly, guided substantially by Themistocles, chose evacuation. The city's population was transported to safety, primarily to the nearby island of Salamis and the Peloponnesian city of Troezen, while the fleet built with Laurion silver revenue, examined in Section 11.5, was mobilized to confront the Persian navy at sea rather than attempting any defense of the city itself.

Xerxes' forces subsequently occupied and burned Athens, an event of genuine and severe material and psychological consequence for the Athenian population, whatever its ultimately limited strategic significance for the war's overall course. The destruction of the city's temples and public buildings would remain a powerful element of Athenian collective memory for generations, cited in later political rhetoric and, according to some ancient sources, influencing decisions about reconstruction and commemoration for decades afterward.

With Athens destroyed, the strategic question facing the coalition became where the allied fleet should make its stand. Peloponnesian commanders, according to Herodotus, favored withdrawing to the narrow isthmus of Corinth, protecting the Peloponnese directly even at the cost of abandoning any further defense of Attica or the waters around it. Themistocles argued forcefully, and according to tradition somewhat coercively, threatening that the Athenian fleet, without which the coalition possessed no meaningful naval advantage, would sail away to found a new city in the west if the allied commanders refused to fight at Salamis, for engaging the Persian fleet in the narrow strait between the island of Salamis and the Attic coast, where the Persian numerical advantage in ships would be substantially neutralized by the confined space, preventing the larger fleet from maneuvering or bringing its full numbers to bear simultaneously.

The resulting Battle of Salamis, fought in September of 480 BCE, resulted in a decisive coalition victory. The exact tactical course of the engagement remains obscure in its details, and modern scholarship cautions against confidently attributing the outcome to superior Greek seamanship, since some ancient testimony credits Persian crews, particularly Phoenician contingents, with considerable naval skill of their own. What can be said with more confidence is that the confined waters of the strait prevented the numerically larger Persian fleet from deploying and maneuvering coherently, crowding its ships together and allowing Greek triremes to disable and destroy Persian vessels in disproportionate numbers as the Persian formation became disorganized within the constricted space. Xerxes himself reportedly watched the battle from a vantage point on the shore, a detail preserved in both Herodotus's account and Aeschylus's later dramatic treatment, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window.

Students should understand Salamis primarily as a coalition victory dependent heavily, though not exclusively, on Athenian naval strength and Themistocles' strategic judgment, rather than treating it as an achievement belonging to Athens alone, since Corinthian, Aeginetan, and other allied contingents also contributed meaningfully to the engagement. Equally important, students should understand that the defeat at Salamis, while a severe blow to Persian naval prestige and capability, did not by itself end the invasion or collapse the wider Achaemenid imperial system. Xerxes himself withdrew a substantial portion of his forces to Asia following the battle, concerned about the security of his bridge across the Hellespont and the broader stability of his empire, but he left a large land army under the command of Mardonius, examined in the following section, to continue the campaign into 479 BCE.

Section 11.10

Plataea, Mycale, and the End of the Invasion

The winter following Salamis brought renewed negotiation and uncertainty rather than immediate resolution. Mardonius, commanding the Persian forces remaining in Greece, attempted through diplomatic overtures to detach Athens from the Hellenic League, offering favorable terms that the Athenian assembly, according to Herodotus, rejected despite the severe hardship its population continued to endure in exile from their destroyed city. When these overtures failed, Mardonius reoccupied and further damaged Athens in the summer of 479 BCE, intensifying pressure on the coalition to commit its full land forces to a decisive engagement rather than continuing a strategy of limited, defensive resistance.

That engagement came at Plataea, in Boeotia, where a large allied land army, commanded by the Spartan regent Pausanias and drawing hoplite contingents from across the Hellenic League, including a substantial Athenian force, confronted Mardonius's remaining army in a battle that Herodotus presents, at considerable length, as the decisive land engagement of the entire war. After a period of maneuvering and skirmishing, the allied forces achieved a decisive victory, and Mardonius himself was killed in the fighting, effectively ending organized Persian land operations within mainland Greece.

According to a tradition Herodotus reports, though one modern historians treat with appropriate caution given the practical difficulty of coordinating events across such distances in the ancient world, a second major engagement occurred on the same day at Mycale, on the Anatolian coast opposite the island of Samos, where an allied Greek fleet destroyed a Persian naval force and its associated land encampment, an engagement that also encouraged renewed Ionian Greek resistance to Persian authority in the very region where this chapter's narrative began.

These two victories, Plataea in Boeotia and Mycale on the Anatolian coast, brought the immediate Persian attempt to establish direct control over mainland Greece to an end. Students must resist treating 479 BCE as the conclusion of Greek-Persian relations altogether, however. The Achaemenid Empire remained, by any reasonable measure, the dominant imperial power of the Near East and a major power in the eastern Mediterranean for more than a century afterward, continuing to intervene diplomatically and militarily in Greek affairs, funding and defunding rival alliances, and shaping the strategic calculations of every major Greek state examined throughout the remainder of this Part. What ended at Plataea and Mycale was a specific invasion attempt, not the empire's power, and not its involvement in Greek political life.

Section 11.11

Why Did the Coalition Succeed?

Having traced the sequence of events, this chapter now turns to a more analytical question, one that later Greek and, indeed, later Western tradition has too often answered with comfortable but historically unsatisfying explanations. The coalition's success cannot be reduced to any single cause, and this section deliberately identifies several interacting factors rather than privileging one explanation above the rest.

Geography mattered enormously, as Chapter 2 established for European history more broadly. The narrow passes at Thermopylae and the confined waters at Salamis repeatedly allowed smaller coalition forces to neutralize the Persian numerical advantage by preventing the larger force from bringing its full strength to bear at any single point of contact. Greek hoplite equipment and phalanx tactics, examined in Chapter 9, proved highly effective in the specific, constrained circumstances of pitched infantry engagement, though this effectiveness depended heavily on terrain and formation discipline rather than representing any inherent or racial superiority of Greek soldiers, a claim later ancient and modern writers have sometimes advanced but that this textbook explicitly rejects.

Coalition naval preparation, particularly the Athenian fleet built through the Laurion silver program examined in Section 11.5, provided the specific military capability that made Salamis possible, while coalition strategic coordination, however imperfect and marked by genuine disagreement between Peloponnesian and Athenian priorities, allowed the alliance to make and execute difficult decisions under severe pressure. Persian logistical challenges, meanwhile, though often understated in accounts focused narrowly on individual battles, deserve serious attention, since supplying an enormous invasion force, however well organized by the standards of ancient imperial administration, over an extended campaign at considerable distance from the imperial heartland presented genuine difficulties that likely constrained Persian strategic options as the campaign wore on.

Command decisions and contingency also mattered. Themistocles' insistence on fighting at Salamis rather than withdrawing to the isthmus, a decision that might easily have gone otherwise given Peloponnesian reluctance, proved strategically decisive. The storm that wrecked an earlier Persian fleet off Mount Athos in 492 BCE, and the storm that destroyed Xerxes' first bridge across the Hellespont, illustrate the role that simple chance, entirely outside human control, played in shaping the campaign's course.

This chapter explicitly rejects several popular but historically unsupported explanations for the coalition's success. Democracy did not defeat despotism in any straightforward sense, since Sparta's decisive military leadership rested on an oligarchic and militarized social order, not democratic institutions, and since democratic Athens's naval program was itself a strategic decision made possible by the specific windfall of Laurion silver rather than an inevitable product of democratic government generally. Greek soldiers were not inherently superior fighters to Persian soldiers, whose empire had, after all, conquered and governed a territory of unprecedented scale through considerable military competence. Persia was not a decadent or incompetent power in any meaningful sense, a claim contradicted by the empire's demonstrated administrative and logistical sophistication examined throughout this chapter. And "freedom," understood as an abstract ideal, did not automatically produce military effectiveness, since many Greek communities that valued their own independence just as highly as Athens or Sparta chose submission or neutrality rather than resistance, a choice that reflected reasonable calculation rather than any lesser attachment to liberty.

Section 11.12

The Wars and the Beginning of Classical Greece

This chapter's title promises an account not only of the Persian Wars themselves but of their contribution to what historians conventionally call Classical Greece, the period, roughly spanning the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, most closely associated with Athenian democracy at its height, the great tragedians and philosophers examined in Chapter 13, and the intense interstate rivalry that will occupy Chapter 12. Understanding how the wars produced this transition, without producing lasting Greek political unity, is this chapter's final and most important task.

The wars transformed Athens most dramatically. A polis that had fielded a citizen militia at Marathon a decade earlier now possessed, by 479 BCE, the most powerful navy in the Greek world, a body of citizen rowers whose military indispensability has often been linked by historians to broader arguments for democratic political participation, and a level of civic confidence and prestige that would translate, within a very few years, into Athenian leadership of a new alliance system, the Delian League, formed in 478 or 477 BCE to continue the war against Persia under Athenian leadership, with Aegean and eastern Greek allies contributing ships or tribute. Chapter 12 will examine in full how this alliance gradually transformed into something resembling an Athenian empire, but this chapter's task is only to note that its origins lie directly in the naval capability and strategic confidence Athens developed during the invasions examined here.

Sparta, meanwhile, emerged from the wars with its reputation as the preeminent land power of the Greek world substantially confirmed and enhanced, having led the Hellenic League and commanded the decisive victory at Plataea. Yet Spartan leadership of the continuing anti-Persian effort did not survive long past 479 BCE. Ancient testimony emphasizes the conduct of Pausanias, the victorious commander at Plataea, whose increasingly high-handed and, according to later report, autocratic behavior while leading allied operations in the Aegean alienated many of the allies under his command, particularly Ionian Greeks who had only recently thrown off Persian rule and who came to prefer Athenian leadership instead. Sparta's own internal structure, examined in Chapter 10, including its reliance on a large subjugated helot population and its historic reluctance to commit forces to distant or prolonged campaigns, further limited its appetite for continued leadership of an ongoing anti-Persian coalition focused on Aegean and Anatolian operations far from the Peloponnese. Together, allied dissatisfaction with Pausanias and Sparta's own structural constraints created the political space that Athens would fill with the Delian League, a development whose long-term consequences neither Spartan nor Athenian leaders could have fully anticipated in the immediate aftermath of Mycale.

The wars also produced, at least temporarily, a heightened sense of shared Hellenic identity, expressed in commemorative monuments erected at Delphi and elsewhere celebrating the victorious Greek states, and in a body of literature, including Herodotus's own Histories and Aeschylus's Persians, that helped construct a lasting cultural memory of Greek resistance to Persian invasion. This Panhellenic sentiment, however, proved considerably less durable than the political rivalries the wars had simultaneously intensified. The very states that had cooperated, however imperfectly, against a common external threat now possessed new resources, new prestige, and new reasons to view one another with suspicion, since Athenian naval power and Spartan land power, no longer required for common defense against Persia, increasingly appeared, to observers on both sides, as potential threats to be balanced or contained rather than as complementary contributions to a shared Hellenic cause.

The coalition that defeated Xerxes therefore contained within it, from the very moment of its greatest triumph, the two powers that would soon compete for leadership of the Greek world. That competition, and the war it eventually produced, is the subject to which Chapter 12 now turns.

Historical Vocabulary
Satrapy and the Structure of the Achaemenid Empire

A satrapy was a province of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, governed by an appointed official called a satrap, who was responsible for collecting tribute, maintaining local order, administering justice under royal oversight, and supplying troops and ships when the Great King required them. The empire's provincial structure, whose foundations developed under Cyrus and Cambyses and whose tribute assessment and communications Darius I regularized and expanded, encompassed dozens of such provinces stretching from the Aegean to the Indus valley, each assessed for tribute according to its productive capacity and each permitted, within limits, to retain local law, religion, and, in many cases, local rulers who governed under Persian oversight rather than direct imperial administration. This system of indirect, tribute-based rule allowed a comparatively small Persian governing elite to control an extraordinarily large and diverse territory, and it is essential background for understanding how the Greek cities of Ionia, examined in Section 11.2, experienced Persian rule as an ordinary, if not always welcome, feature of political life rather than as an unprecedented catastrophe.

Historical Vocabulary
Medism

Medism, derived from "Medes," an Iranian people closely associated with the Persians and whose name Greek writers often used loosely and interchangeably with "Persians" in general, describes cooperation or submission by a Greek community to Persian authority during the invasions examined in this chapter. Students should treat this term with considerable caution, since later Greek political rhetoric, particularly in Athens, frequently used accusations of Medism as a moralizing weapon against political rivals, retroactively condemning wartime accommodation as treachery. Modern historians generally treat submission, neutrality, and cooperation as reasonable and, in many cases, rational responses available to communities that lacked the military resources or strategic confidence of Sparta and Athens, rather than treating every instance of Medism as straightforward betrayal of a shared Hellenic cause that, in practice, did not yet command universal Greek loyalty.

Primary Source Window

Herodotus's Histories and Aeschylus's Persians on the Battle of Salamis

Creators: Herodotus of Halicarnassus, Greek historian, and Aeschylus, Athenian tragedian and probable veteran of the battle · Dates: Aeschylus's play was performed in Athens in 472 BCE, only eight years after the battle it depicts, while Herodotus's Histories were composed decades later, likely completed in the 430s or 420s BCE · Types: a historical and ethnographic narrative, and a tragic drama performed at a civic religious festival

Context: These two sources offer strikingly different kinds of evidence for the same event. Aeschylus's Persians is unusual among surviving Greek tragedies for depicting recent historical events rather than mythological subject matter, and it imagines the reaction of the Persian royal court, including Xerxes' mother Atossa and the ghost of Darius, to the news of defeat at Salamis. Herodotus, writing later and drawing on oral tradition gathered from many informants, constructed a fuller narrative reconstruction of the battle's strategic background and course.

Paraphrase of themes from Aeschylus's Persians and Herodotus's Histories, Book 8 Aeschylus's drama presents the Persian defeat as a consequence of Xerxes' overreaching ambition, imagining the Persian court's grief and the ghost of Darius warning against further campaigns against Greece, while Herodotus's later narrative emphasizes the tactical circumstances of the battle, the confined strait, Themistocles' strategic insistence on fighting there, and the disorganization of the larger Persian fleet once battle was joined. This is a synthesis of themes across both sources rather than a translation of any continuous passage.

Analysis: Comparing these sources illustrates how genre, audience, and date shape historical evidence. Aeschylus wrote as a participant, or at least a close contemporary, for a civic audience at a religious festival, and his dramatic purposes required an interpretive, moralizing framework centered on hubris and divine retribution rather than a neutral tactical account. Herodotus, writing later for a different purpose, offers more tactical and strategic detail but depends on oral tradition collected long after the event, tradition that had already been shaped by decades of retelling, commemoration, and political use.

Questions for students:

  1. How does Aeschylus's dramatic purpose shape what his play can and cannot tell historians about the actual battle?
  2. Why might a source composed only eight years after an event still require careful historical scrutiny?
  3. What does Herodotus's much later composition date suggest about the reliability of the oral tradition on which he depended?
  4. How does comparing these two very different kinds of evidence help historians distinguish probable historical fact from later cultural memory?
Historical Debate

Why Did Xerxes' Invasion Fail?

The question: Given the Achaemenid Empire's overwhelming advantages in territory, population, and administrative resources, why did its second and much larger invasion of Greece ultimately fail to achieve lasting control over the mainland Greek states?

Interpretation A — Greek strategic and tactical advantages were decisive: Some historians emphasize that Greek hoplite tactics, coalition naval preparation, and, above all, the deliberate use of constrained geography at Thermopylae and Salamis neutralized Persia's numerical superiority at the specific points of engagement that mattered most, and that Themistocles' strategic judgment in particular proved decisive in securing a battle on terms favorable to the coalition.
Interpretation B — Persian logistical limits and contingency mattered as much as Greek strategy: Other historians emphasize that supplying an enormous invasion force over an extended campaign at considerable distance from the imperial heartland, combined with genuine misfortune, the storm that wrecked an earlier fleet off Mount Athos and the storm that destroyed Xerxes' first Hellespont bridge, imposed constraints on Persian strategic options that had little to do with Greek tactical brilliance, and that a somewhat different sequence of chance events could plausibly have produced a different outcome regardless of Greek strategy.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying narrative established by Herodotus, but differ in how much weight to assign Greek agency and skill relative to Persian logistical constraint and simple contingency.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes whether the Persian Wars are understood primarily as a triumph of Greek military and political institutions or as an outcome substantially shaped by circumstances largely outside any commander's control, a distinction with real consequences for how later chapters interpret Greek self-confidence in the decades that followed.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, then explain which combination of factors you find most persuasive, and why relying on a single explanation, whether Greek virtue or Persian misfortune, would be historically insufficient.

Multiple Perspectives

One War, Different Participants

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Persian imperial official: The campaign against Greece represents a logical extension of Achaemenid authority over a region that had already produced rebellion and continued resistance, and the empire's vast administrative resources, roads, tribute, and multiethnic manpower make eventual success appear, before the campaign's outcome is known, entirely reasonable to expect.
From the perspective of an Ionian Greek serving under Persian command: Persian authority has structured daily political life for decades, and continued service, however unwelcome the specific campaign against fellow Greeks may feel, is the ordinary and rational course for a subject community lacking realistic alternatives, whatever mainland Greek writers may later say about loyalty and betrayal.
From the perspective of an Athenian citizen rower: Years of labor at an oar, funded by silver revenue that might otherwise have been distributed directly to citizens, have produced a fleet whose crews now hold the fate of the entire coalition in their hands, a fact that ought to matter, and will increasingly be argued to matter, when questions of political voice within Athens itself are decided.
From the perspective of a Theban or Thessalian elite navigating Persian demands: Submission to an overwhelming imperial force, when no allied army stands ready to defend one's own territory, is a rational judgment about survival and continuity rather than an abandonment of any prior loyalty to a still largely notional Hellenic cause that most Greek states, in practice, do not yet treat as binding.
From the perspective of a displaced Athenian civilian: Evacuation to Salamis or Troezen means watching one's home burned from a distance, trusting a strategic judgment made by the assembly and its leading statesmen, and enduring genuine hardship in the hope that a fleet, not a defended city, will somehow determine whether Athens survives at all.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Archaic Rivalry to Classical Power Politics

What Changed

  • Athens acquired, for the first time, a naval force capable of contesting the eastern Mediterranean's dominant imperial power, and a body of citizen rowers whose military role has often been linked by historians to the further development of democratic participation
  • Interstate coalition warfare, coordinated across many independent poleis under a shared command structure, became a demonstrated possibility, even if an imperfect and temporary one
  • A heightened, though ultimately unstable, sense of shared Hellenic identity emerged, expressed in commemorative monuments and a developing body of historical and dramatic literature
  • Sparta and Athens emerged from the wars with substantially enhanced prestige and resources, setting the stage for the rivalry Chapter 12 examines

What Stayed the Same

  • The Greek world remained politically fragmented into independent, often rival poleis, with no permanent unifying institution
  • Sparta's hoplite-centered, oligarchic social order and Athens's expanding democratic institutions, both examined in Chapter 10, continued largely unchanged in their basic structure
  • The Achaemenid Empire remained the dominant imperial power of the Near East and a major power in the eastern Mediterranean, continuing to shape Greek political calculations for more than a century afterward, even as Athens became the dominant naval power within the Aegean itself
  • Individual Greek poleis continued to weigh cooperation, neutrality, and resistance against outside powers according to their own particular interests rather than any binding shared loyalty

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating the Persian Wars as the moment Greek unity was achieved, or assuming that nothing of lasting significance resulted from a conflict that, in immediate territorial terms, changed comparatively little.

Cause and Effect

From the Ionian Revolt to the Postwar Power Shift

Long-term causes

Persian rule over the Ionian Greek cities since the middle of the sixth century BCE, and the empire's broader strategic interest in securing its Aegean maritime frontier.

Short-term causes

Aristagoras's failed Naxos campaign and subsequent political calculation, Athenian and Eretrian support for the Ionian Revolt, and the burning of Sardis in 498 BCE.

Event or transformation

Two Persian invasions of mainland Greece, in 490 and 480 to 479 BCE, met by an incomplete but ultimately successful Greek coalition resistance.

Immediate and long-term effects

The end of the immediate Persian invasion attempt, the dramatic expansion of Athenian naval power and confidence, enhanced Spartan prestige, and the formation of the Delian League, setting the stage for the Athenian-Spartan rivalry examined in Chapter 12.

Historical complexity: This sequence should not be read as an inevitable chain of events. At several points, including Aristagoras's political gamble, Themistocles' insistence on fighting at Salamis, and the storms that repeatedly disrupted Persian plans, the outcome could plausibly have unfolded very differently.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Experience amid Invasion

Behind the strategic decisions and pitched battles examined in this chapter lay the ordinary, disrupted lives of people who did not command armies or fleets. Athenian civilians evacuated to Salamis and Troezen in 480 BCE endured the anxiety of watching their city burn from a distance, uncertain whether the fleet on which their fate depended would prevail. Farmers throughout Attica, Boeotia, and other regions crossed by the invading army lost crops, livestock, and homes regardless of which side their community ultimately supported, since an army of tens of thousands of soldiers required enormous quantities of food and supplies typically drawn from the countryside it passed through, whether through purchase, requisition, or outright seizure.

Rowing a trireme, the labor that made Athenian naval power possible, was itself demanding and dangerous work, requiring sustained physical exertion in cramped, poorly ventilated conditions, and it drew heavily on citizens from the poorer property classes who could not afford hoplite equipment, a fact whose relationship to subsequent democratic change has often been emphasized by historians but remains debated. For communities that submitted to Persian authority rather than joining the resistance, everyday life likely continued with comparatively less disruption, a reminder that the dramatic narrative of invasion and resistance this chapter has traced coexisted, for many ordinary Greeks, with the more mundane calculation of how best to preserve one's household, land, and community through a dangerous and uncertain period.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the conflict between the Achaemenid Persian Empire and the Greek world between roughly 499 and 479 BCE, tracing its origins in the Ionian Revolt, its course through the invasions of 490 and 480 to 479 BCE, and its consequences for the internal balance of power among the Greek poleis. Throughout, the chapter has insisted on a more historically precise account than the familiar popular narrative of united, democratic Greece defending freedom against Persian tyranny.

The Achaemenid Empire, introduced on its own administrative terms, was a sophisticated and successful imperial system governing an extraordinary diversity of subject peoples through satrapies, tribute, and an efficient road network. Greek cities in Ionia had lived under this system for decades before the revolt of 499 BCE, a rebellion whose causes owed as much to the particular political calculations of the Milesian tyrant Aristagoras as to any broadly shared Ionian nationalism, and whose suppression at Lade in 494 BCE restored Persian authority throughout the region.

Darius's punitive expedition of 490 BCE, defeated at Marathon by an Athenian and Plataean force fighting without Spartan support, produced a significant but limited victory whose psychological and political importance for Athens exceeded its immediate strategic consequences. The following decade saw Athens transform itself into a major naval power through Themistocles' program of fleet construction, funded by silver revenue from Laurion, a development that would prove decisive in the far larger invasion Xerxes launched in 480 BCE.

That invasion, prepared over years and drawing on resources from across the empire's provinces, confronted a Greek coalition that was, from the outset, far less unified than later memory suggests. Many Greek states submitted, remained neutral, or actively cooperated with Persia, choices this chapter has examined as rational responses to overwhelming imperial power rather than simple betrayal of a Hellenic cause that most poleis did not yet treat as binding.

Thermopylae was a land defeat for the coalition, while the accompanying naval engagements at Artemisium were inconclusive and were followed by a strategic withdrawal once news of Leonidas's defeat arrived; both nonetheless demonstrated the strategic value of constrained geography and produced a cultural memory whose power would far outlast their immediate military significance. The subsequent evacuation and destruction of Athens, followed by the decisive coalition naval victory at Salamis, secured largely through Themistocles' strategic insistence and the confined geography of the strait, marked the war's turning point, while the victories at Plataea and Mycale in 479 BCE ended the immediate Persian attempt to conquer mainland Greece.

This chapter's analytical section rejected single-cause explanations for the coalition's success, emphasizing instead the interaction of geography, naval preparation, hoplite tactics, coalition coordination, Persian logistical constraint, and simple contingency. It likewise rejected popular claims that democracy defeated despotism, that Greek soldiers were inherently superior, that Persia was decadent, or that abstract freedom automatically produced military effectiveness.

Finally, the chapter has traced how the wars, rather than producing lasting Greek unity, instead intensified the underlying rivalry between Sparta's land power and Athens's newly acquired naval strength and confidence. The formation of the Delian League in 478 or 477 BCE, introduced here only as a transition, marks the beginning of the developments Chapter 12 will examine in full, the transformation of a defensive alliance into an instrument of Athenian power, and the gradual breakdown of the wartime coalition into the rivalry that would eventually produce the Peloponnesian War.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized the limits and character of the evidence on which this entire narrative depends, above all Herodotus's Histories, composed decades after the events it describes, supplemented by Aeschylus's near-contemporary dramatic treatment of Salamis. Both sources require careful, critical reading rather than uncritical acceptance, a habit of mind students should carry forward into every subsequent chapter of this textbook.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a conflict whose later memory has often overshadowed its actual complexity. The Persian Wars were not a simple contest between a unified Greece and a monolithic Persian enemy, they were a decades-long, multi-sided crisis that exposed the genuine divisions within an already fragmented Greek political world while simultaneously producing, for a brief period, moments of coordinated resistance whose success required naval preparation, favorable geography, coalition cooperation, and no small measure of chance.

Students who understand the Achaemenid Empire as a sophisticated imperial system rather than a mere backdrop, who can explain why many Greek communities chose submission or neutrality rather than resistance, and who can distinguish the genuine military and political significance of Marathon, Thermopylae, and Salamis from their later cultural legend, are well equipped to read the chapters that follow with appropriate historical care.

Above all, this chapter has argued that the coalition's victory did not resolve the tensions within the Greek world, it transformed them. Athens emerged with a navy, a newly confident citizenry, and a growing sense of its own indispensability to Greek security. Sparta emerged with its reputation as the preeminent land power confirmed but its appetite for distant, ongoing campaigns limited by its own social structure. The Delian League, formed to continue the fight against a still-powerful Persian Empire, would soon become something its founders may not have fully anticipated, an instrument of Athenian power that would draw Sparta and Athens into the very conflict Chapter 12 now examines.

The story of Classical Greece, so often told as though it began simply with victory over Persia, in fact began with a victory that solved nothing permanently and unsettled a great deal. Understanding that unsettled inheritance is the necessary foundation for everything that follows.
