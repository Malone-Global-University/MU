The Crisis of the Roman Republic

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 133 and 78 BCE the Roman Republic did not simply collapse. Elections continued, citizens continued to vote, and politicians continued to claim that they defended the republic. What changed was the range of methods that Romans considered available in political competition: colleagues were removed from office, citizens were killed without ordinary trial, armies marched on the city, and a victorious general rewrote the constitution.

In the summer of 133 BCE, on the Capitoline Hill in the center of Rome, a meeting of the popular assembly was interrupted by a crowd of senators and their followers. The tribune Tiberius Gracchus, who had spent the year pressing a program of land distribution against the fierce opposition of much of the Senate, was struck down along with a number of his supporters. Their bodies were thrown into the Tiber. Ancient authors, writing later and with their own purposes, agreed that something new had happened. Romans had quarreled over land, debt, office, and privilege for centuries, and they had rioted before, but a sitting tribune of the plebs, whose person was supposed to be inviolable, had been killed by fellow citizens in the middle of a political dispute. Nobody in 133 BCE could know that this was the first act of a long sequence. It became clear only later that the event had begun to change what Romans regarded as thinkable.

This chapter traces that sequence across roughly half a century. It follows the Gracchi brothers and their land, grain, judicial, and colonial programs; the first use of the emergency decree later known as the senatus consultum ultimum; the war against the Numidian king Jugurtha and the rise of Gaius Marius; the wars against the Cimbri and Teutones and the debate over how the Roman army changed; the violent tribunates of Saturninus and Livius Drusus; the Social War, in which many of Rome's Italian allies fought against the city; and finally the struggle over the command against Mithridates VI of Pontus, which led Lucius Cornelius Sulla to march an army on Rome in 88 BCE. The chapter ends with Sulla's civil war, his proscriptions, his dictatorship, and his attempt to fix the constitution, and it closes with Sulla's abdication and death in 79 and 78 BCE.

Chapter 18 examined the social structures that lay beneath Roman politics: hierarchy, the household, slavery, wealth, rural and urban life, and the uneven expansion of citizenship. Chapter 18 deliberately stopped before the political crisis. Chapter 19 asks how disputes that the Republic had long managed through elections, assemblies, Senate deliberation, law, and aristocratic competition came increasingly to be settled through exceptional measures, street violence, armies, confiscation, and killing. It does so without assuming that the outcome was inevitable. A tempting chain of explanation runs from inequality to the Gracchi, from the Gracchi to a Marian professional army, from that army to soldiers loyal to generals, and from generals to the fall of the Republic. This chapter argues that the chain is too mechanical. Several problems interacted: public land, wealth and labor, citizenship and Italian grievances, recruitment and long campaigns, elite competition, the powers of tribunes and assemblies, senatorial resistance, and the competition for lucrative commands. At every stage Romans made choices, and alternatives existed.

The central theme of the chapter is precedent. Each side in the conflicts examined here justified its actions by pointing to earlier events, and each exceptional action enlarged the range of measures that later politicians regarded as legitimate. Tiberius appealed to the sovereignty of the assembly. His opponents appealed to the safety of the republic. Later consuls cited the killing of Gracchans as a model for emergency action, later tribunes cited Tiberius, and Sulla cited the disorder of his enemies to justify his own remaking of the state. The point is not that violence was inevitable, but that violence itself acquired precedents.

The evidence for this period is uneven, and students should understand why. No contemporary narrative history survives. The main continuous accounts were written long after the events: Sallust's monographs on the Jugurthine War and Catiline, written in the 40s and 30s BCE; Plutarch's biographies of the Gracchi, Marius, and Sulla, written in the early second century CE; and Appian's Civil Wars, written in Greek in the second century CE and drawing on earlier lost authors. These are supplemented by speeches and letters of Cicero, brief summaries of the lost books of Livy, the historian Velleius Paterculus, and the historian Diodorus Siculus. Surviving inscriptions, including fragments of laws from the 120s and 110s BCE, boundary markers set by the Gracchan land commission, coins struck by the rebel Italians, and legal texts, provide a crucial check on the literary narrative. Sulla himself wrote memoirs, but they are lost. Nearly all the surviving narrative sources were produced by senatorial or elite authors, and many reflect the political preoccupations of the later civil wars, when the Gracchi and Sulla were remembered as the origin of later troubles.

Within Part III, this chapter marks the transition from the study of how the Republic worked and expanded (Chapters 15 through 18) to the study of how its settlement broke apart (Chapters 19 and 20). It also carries the Volume I argument forward. Rome was a Mediterranean power, not a "European" one in any narrow sense, and the crisis examined here was shaped as much by Numidia, Asia Minor, Gaul, and the Italian peninsula as by the city itself. Chapter 20 will follow the generation after Sulla, in which Pompey, Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar operated in a political world altered by the precedents examined here. The dating conventions of this chapter follow the book's standard BCE system, but students should remember that Romans dated years by the names of the consuls and that the Roman civil calendar of this period was not aligned exactly with the solar year, a matter discussed in Section 19.1.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians disagree about when and whether a structural "crisis" of the Roman Republic began, and why this chapter avoids treating collapse as inevitable.
  2. Trace the land reform of Tiberius Gracchus and the constitutional questions raised by the deposition of Octavius, the use of Attalus III's bequest, and Tiberius's killing.
  3. Compare the programs of Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus and explain why Gaius built a broader coalition.
  4. Analyze the significance of the first use of the senatus consultum ultimum in 121 BCE and the creation of political precedent.
  5. Evaluate which claims about inequality, smallholders, and slave labor are established by evidence and which remain debated.
  6. Assess the Jugurthine War as a Numidian political conflict and as a source of Roman debates about corruption and command.
  7. Distinguish among the several military changes traditionally grouped as the "Marian reforms" and explain why historians reject a single reform package.
  8. Explain the causes, course, and consequences of the Social War, including the paradox of Rome fighting its allies while conceding many of their demands.
  9. Interpret the dispute over the Mithridatic command and Sulla's march on Rome as a constitutional rupture.
  10. Evaluate whether Sulla restored the Republic or remade it through methods that contradicted the order he claimed to defend.
  11. Describe the experience of military service, veterans, Italians, and enslaved people in a period of repeated warfare.
  12. Connect the developments of 133 to 78 BCE to the political world addressed in Chapter 20 without treating later outcomes as predetermined.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did political competition in the Roman Republic become increasingly violent between the 130s and 70s BCE, and how did repeated attempts to solve problems of land, citizenship, military service, and elite competition change the rules of republican politics?
  2. Could a tribune use the sovereign authority of the popular assembly to overcome senatorial resistance, and what happened when opponents concluded that ordinary constitutional mechanisms were insufficient?
  3. How does the concept of precedent help explain the escalation from the death of Tiberius Gracchus to the proscriptions of Sulla, and what alternatives existed at each stage?
  4. What evidence supports the traditional account of rural dispossession and military recruitment, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  5. Was there a single "Marian reform" of the army, or did military relationships change through a longer and more varied process?
  6. Why did Rome's Italian allies revolt in 91 BCE, and why did different Italian communities make different choices?
  7. How did the profits and prestige of extraordinary military commands change the incentives of Roman politicians?
  8. Was Sulla restoring the Republic or transforming it?
  9. How do we distinguish political labels used by ancient writers, such as populares and optimates, from organized political parties?

Historical Context

By 146 BCE, the year in which Rome destroyed Carthage and Corinth, examined in Chapter 17, the Republic had become the dominant power of the Mediterranean. It governed provinces in Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica, Spain, Africa, Macedonia, and later Asia, and it exercised influence over kingdoms and cities from Numidia to Pergamum. The political order that presided over this expansion, described in Chapter 15, distributed power among annually elected magistrates, the Senate, and popular assemblies. Consuls and praetors held imperium, the authority to command armies and administer justice. Ten tribunes of the plebs each year could propose legislation to the plebeian assembly and veto the acts of other magistrates. The Senate, made up largely of former magistrates, controlled finance, foreign policy, and the assignment of provinces. Political competition was fierce, but it was regulated by shared conventions: the sequence of offices, the expectation of rotation, the acceptance of election results, and reluctance to use force against fellow citizens inside the city.

That order rested on a partnership with Italy. As Chapter 16 explained, Rome had built its power on a peninsula that was a patchwork of Roman citizen communities, Latin colonies enjoying special rights, and formally independent allies, the socii, bound to Rome by treaties requiring military contributions. By the later second century BCE the allies supplied roughly as many soldiers as Roman citizens did in many armies, yet they had no vote in Roman assemblies and only limited legal protections against Roman magistrates. Chapter 18 described an Italian society transformed by conquest: great wealth flowing to a small number of senatorial and equestrian families, extensive enslaved labor on estates in parts of Italy and Sicily, cities swelling with migrants, and rural households whose circumstances varied widely. It also cautioned that older accounts of a wholesale disappearance of citizen smallholders are not securely proven.

Rome's overseas commitments deepened these pressures. Wars in Spain, North Africa, and the Balkans required long campaigns far from home and repeated recruitment. Provincial governance created opportunities for profit, and, as the establishment of a standing court for extortion in 149 BCE shows, also created concern about abuse. Sicily saw a massive rebellion of enslaved people beginning in 135 BCE, which took years to suppress. In 133 BCE the king of Pergamum in western Anatolia, Attalus III, died and left his kingdom to Rome. After the revolt of Aristonicus, Rome organized the province of Asia by about 129 BCE, but the bequest and the royal treasury became immediate political issues at Rome. These circumstances did not by themselves cause a constitutional crisis. They form the setting in which specific political choices, made by individuals with specific goals, produced results that were much larger than their authors expected.

Two features of the Roman system deserve emphasis because they shaped everything that follows. First, the system depended on norms more than on written rules. Romans had no single constitutional document. The powers of tribunes, the authority of Senate decrees, and the limits on consular power rested on custom, precedent, and the shared assumption that senators would not push their advantage to the point of destroying the system. Second, the system depended on the assumption that competition among aristocrats would remain roughly balanced. Individual senators sought glory through office and command, but no one man was supposed to become dominant. When land, citizenship, and military command became sources of unusual political capital, that balance was tested. The rest of this chapter examines how.

Section 19.1

What Was the "Crisis" of the Republic?

The phrase "crisis of the Roman Republic" is convenient, but it carries a risk. It can suggest that the Republic was a single organism that entered a fatal illness in a particular year, so that everything after that date should be read as decline. Roman writers themselves helped create this impression. Sallust, writing in the aftermath of the civil wars that finally ended the Republic, argued that Roman morals had decayed after the destruction of Carthage removed the fear of a great rival. Later authors such as Appian, in the second century CE, opened his account of Roman civil wars with the Gracchi, presenting them as the beginning of a sequence of internal conflict. Modern historians inherited this framing, and some continue to use 133 BCE as a starting point of "the Roman Revolution," a phrase made famous in the twentieth century.

More recent scholarship has been more cautious. It has stressed that many elements of Roman government continued to function through the decades treated in this chapter. Magistrates were elected each year, provinces were governed, wars were fought and won, laws were passed, priesthoods were filled, and the courts met. The Republic suffered repeated shocks and it also showed considerable resilience. Some historians therefore prefer to speak of a sequence of crises with different causes rather than a single structural crisis, and some question how much weight should be placed on the year 133 itself. This chapter adopts a middle position. It treats roughly 133 to 78 BCE as its principal period, because the sequence of events in those decades involved a real and cumulative change in political practice, but it insists that the timing, pace, and outcome were contingent.

Dating requires a brief note. Romans named years after the two consuls, so that the year of Tiberius's tribunate was "the year of the consuls Publius Mucius Scaevola and Lucius Calpurnius Piso." Tribunes took office on 10 December, not on 1 January, and the Roman civil calendar of this period was lunar in origin and adjusted by priests through the insertion of extra months, sometimes for political convenience, so that civil dates could drift from the seasons. Modern scholars express these years in BCE, and this chapter does the same, but many of the dates below are dates of the consular year and may fall slightly earlier or later in the solar year than a reader might expect.

Two terms need care from the outset. Populares and optimates are Latin words used by Roman authors, especially Cicero, to describe political styles: the first for politicians who sought support by appealing to the assemblies and the people, the second for those who defended the authority of the Senate. They were not organized political parties, they had no membership lists or shared platforms, and the same individual could shift between the two styles depending on circumstances. Many so called popular politicians were wealthy aristocrats, and many defenders of the Senate had also used popular methods when it suited them. The terms are discussed further in the vocabulary box below, and they appear in this chapter only with these qualifications.

Section 19.2

Land, Citizens, and Tiberius Gracchus

Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus belonged to one of the most distinguished families of the Republic. His father had been consul twice and censor, and his mother, Cornelia, was the daughter of Scipio Africanus, the conqueror of Hannibal. He served with distinction in Spain and North Africa. In 133 BCE he was elected tribune of the plebs and introduced a bill on the public land of the Roman people, the ager publicus. Understanding what he proposed requires understanding the land itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Ager publicus

Ager publicus, "public land," referred to territory that Rome had acquired by conquest and retained as the property of the Roman people rather than distributing it into private ownership. Much of it lay in Italy, confiscated from communities defeated in the wars described in Chapter 16. In practice, much public land was occupied by individuals, usually wealthy, who worked it and treated it almost as if it were their own, although in law they were possessors rather than owners. Older traditions attributed to a fourth century BCE law a limit on how much public land any one person might occupy, traditionally given as 500 iugera (roughly 300 acres, or 125 hectares). Whether that limit was actually enforced before Tiberius, and how consistently, is uncertain.

The concept matters because Tiberius's proposal was not a plan to confiscate private property. It concerned land that, in law, already belonged to the community. Yet the possessors had held some of it for generations, had inherited, sold, and improved it, and regarded it as effectively theirs, which is why the proposal produced such fierce resistance. A modern reader should not assume that the legal distinction between possession and ownership was as clear to those affected as it seems in retrospect.

The bill of Tiberius, as reconstructed from later sources, reasserted the limit on occupation of public land, with allowances for sons, and provided that land recovered above the limit would be distributed in small plots to poor citizens. A commission of three men, elected by the assembly, was to carry out the task. Appian and Plutarch present the measure as a response to the decline of the Italian citizen peasantry and a shortage of men for the army. This explanation was accepted by many older historians. Section 19.5 discusses why it is debated. What is clearer from the sources is that the proposal had practical support among many Roman citizens, and among some senators. Tiberius was not acting alone. He was connected to senators of great standing, including his father in law Appius Claudius, a former consul, and the jurist Publius Mucius Scaevola, who was consul in 133.

The political controversy, however, was not only about the land. It turned on procedure. Ordinarily, a major matter of this kind would be discussed in the Senate before it went to the people. Tiberius chose to take his bill directly to the plebeian assembly, and he did so in the face of the Senate's evident hostility. Another tribune, Marcus Octavius, vetoed the bill on behalf of the possessors. The veto was a legitimate and long established power, and it had been used to block legislation on many occasions. Tiberius's response was extraordinary. He argued that a tribune who acted against the interest of the people had ceased to be a true tribune, put the question to the assembly, and had Octavius removed from office by vote. He then passed his land law and the commission began work. Ancient writers already debated the propriety of what he had done. Even some who sympathized with his aims regarded the removal of a colleague as a dangerous step, because it treated an office that had been protected by sacred tradition as subject to the assembly's recall.

A second controversy followed. In 133 the kingdom of Pergamum passed to Rome by the will of Attalus III. Tiberius proposed that the royal treasure be used to fund tools and stock for the new settlers. This was an assertion that the people, not the Senate, should control the disposition of a major new source of revenue and, implicitly, the administration of the new territory. The Senate normally handled foreign affairs and finance, and the proposal was seen as an infringement. Later in the year, as opposition intensified and as it became apparent that he might be prosecuted once he left office, Tiberius sought reelection for a second consecutive tribunate. Consecutive tribunates were not clearly forbidden, but they were unusual, and the proposal was contested on the day of the election. Tiberius's opponents claimed that he intended to make himself permanent ruler. This was a familiar charge of aspiring to kingship, and one that some ancient authors presented as a pretext.

What happened next is described by several ancient authors with variations in detail. On the day of the election, a group of senators, led by Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica Serapio, who was pontifex maximus, left the Senate after the consul Scaevola refused to act against Tiberius, and moved against the assembly, armed with clubs and pieces of broken benches. Tiberius and many of his supporters were killed on the Capitoline Hill, and their bodies were thrown into the Tiber. The sources give varying figures for the dead, from a few hundred to more, and modern scholars treat the numbers with caution. There was no trial, and no formal decree of the Senate authorizing the action. Afterward the Senate established a special inquiry which pursued many of Tiberius's followers, and Nasica was sent to Asia, where he died, perhaps as a way to remove him from the political fallout.

The land commission, remarkably, continued to function. Boundary markers set up by the commissioners have been found in various places in Italy, providing physical evidence that distributions took place, although we cannot say how many people benefited. Tiberius's brother Gaius, his father in law Appius Claudius, and later Marcus Fulvius Flaccus and Gaius Papirius Carbo served as commissioners. The work was slowed, however, when disputes arose about the status of land that belonged to Italian allies, and the commission's jurisdiction was transferred to the consuls, an act that effectively halted it. The important political question that the episode raised was not whether Tiberius was right about land. It was this: could a tribune use the sovereign authority of the popular assembly to overcome senatorial resistance, and what would happen if his opponents concluded that ordinary constitutional mechanisms were insufficient to stop him? The answer given in 133 was violent, and the violence was not punished. That precedent mattered.

Section 19.3

Gaius Gracchus and a Broader Reform Program

Gaius Gracchus, nine years younger than his brother, was elected tribune for 123 BCE and reelected for 122. He is often presented as a second Tiberius, but the surviving evidence suggests a different and more complex politician. Gaius had served as quaestor in Sardinia and had experience of provincial administration and the army. Where Tiberius had concentrated on a single problem, Gaius introduced an unusually wide legislative program, and he built a more varied coalition of support. It appealed to the poor citizens of Rome, to wealthy equestrians, to veterans and soldiers, to some provincials, and to Italians.

Gaius reenacted his brother's agrarian law and restored the powers of the land commission. He introduced a grain law under which the state would sell grain to citizens in Rome at a fixed price, below the market price at moments of scarcity. Historians debate whether this was meant to be a charitable measure, a means of stabilizing prices, or a way of securing the support of the urban population. Whatever the intention, it created a long lived and politically significant institution, and later politicians would compete to expand it. Gaius also proposed colonies, both in Italy and overseas. The most ambitious was a colony at the site of Carthage, called Junonia, which was the first Roman citizen colony outside Italy. Its establishment provoked religious and political objections, and it was later largely abandoned.

His judicial and financial measures were equally important. Under the law on extortion by provincial governors, the standing court that heard such cases had been staffed by senators. A law of this period, part of which survives on a bronze inscription and is known as the lex Acilia (the precise dating and authorship of the inscription are debated), reformed procedures, and Gaius is credited by ancient authors with transferring jury service to the equestrian order. The equestrians were wealthy citizens outside the Senate, many of them businessmen who contracted to collect provincial taxes and to supply the army. Gaius also passed a law arranging the collection of taxes in the new province of Asia through auctions in Rome, which favored these contractors, the publicani. The political effect was to give the equestrians a powerful stake in the system, and to create a new source of tension between them and the senators whom they might now judge. Gaius also introduced measures concerning roads and granaries, and military legislation that provided for soldiers' clothing at public expense and restricted the recruitment of very young men, although the details are known only from later authors.

Most significant, and most dangerous, were his proposals concerning Italians. Gaius, along with his ally Marcus Fulvius Flaccus, who had already proposed the extension of citizenship in 125, put forward measures to grant full Roman citizenship to Latin allies and Latin rights to other Italians. Many Roman citizens, including many of Gaius's supporters among the urban poor, did not want to share their privileges, distributions of grain, and access to public land. The Senate exploited this hostility. One of Gaius's colleagues, Marcus Livius Drusus, proposed a series of popular measures designed to outbid him, including twelve colonies for needy citizens and relief from rents charged on public land allotments. The tactic showed the way in which competition for popular favor could be used to divide a reformer's coalition. Gaius's proposal on citizenship failed. He lost the support of much of the Roman crowd, was not elected to a third tribunate, and returned to private life in 121 vulnerable to prosecution and to the enmity of powerful men.

Gaius's program deserves to be understood as more than a continuation of his brother's. He was designing an alternative pattern of power and patronage in which the assembly, the equestrian order, and the poor of Rome were connected to the state through legislation rather than through the traditional patronage of senators. That is the reason that his opponents feared him, and the reason that later Romans regarded him as both a great statesman and a dangerous demagogue. It would be a mistake, however, to describe him as a democrat in the modern sense. His measures were designed to serve particular groups, not to make Rome more democratic in the modern meaning of the word, and the citizens who benefited from his reforms did not include women, enslaved people, or the great majority of Italians.

Section 19.4

From Political Conflict to Political Killing

The events of 121 BCE took a course that resembled 133 but went a decisive step further. In that year the consul Lucius Opimius, an opponent of Gaius, sought to repeal parts of the Gracchan legislation, in particular the colony at Carthage. Both Gaius and his ally Flaccus, who was a former consul, gathered supporters. A minor incident in which a consul's attendant was killed in a scuffle became an occasion for the Senate to pass a decree calling on the consuls to "see to it that the republic suffers no harm." This became known in later centuries as the senatus consultum ultimum, the "final decree of the Senate."

Historical Vocabulary
Senatus consultum ultimum

The senatus consultum ultimum ("final decree of the Senate," a modern name, not a contemporary technical term) was a Senate resolution urging the consuls, and sometimes other magistrates, to take whatever measures necessary to protect the state. It did not create a new legal power. It was an opinion of the Senate, which lacked the formal authority to suspend the right of a citizen to appeal against a capital sentence or to a regular trial, and its legality was disputed. Opponents such as Gaius Gracchus's sympathizers argued that it authorized killing citizens without process, and defenders such as Cicero later argued that it declared enemies of the state to have placed themselves outside the protection of law.

The decree matters because it provided a language and procedure for the use of force against citizens. It was invoked in 121, again in 100 BCE, and repeatedly thereafter, most famously against Catiline in 63 BCE, examined in Chapter 20. Each use produced controversy and enlarged the body of examples that later politicians could cite.

Armed with the decree, Opimius summoned senators and equestrians, some of whom brought armed slaves, and moved against Gaius's supporters on the Aventine Hill. Gaius was killed, perhaps by his own order or that of a slave who assisted him, and Flaccus was killed with his sons. Later ancient authors gave figures of about three thousand supporters executed in the aftermath, although such numbers are unreliable. Whatever the actual numbers, a large group of citizens were put to death, many of them without a trial. Opimius later dedicated a temple of Concord in the Forum, an irony noticed by ancient writers. He was later prosecuted for his conduct and acquitted, which suggests that a substantial part of the Roman political class accepted his justification.

Between 133 and 121 something changed, and the change was not simply an increase in the number of victims. In 133, the killers acted without a formal decree, and the case remained ambiguous: the killing was defended as an act by private citizens against a would be tyrant, and it was denounced by others as murder. In 121 the Senate had, for the first time, authorized a magistrate to act against fellow citizens by force, and the act was subsequently accepted as legitimate by a jury. In other words, what had begun as an aberration became a procedure. The tribunate, in which Tiberius and Gaius had placed so much confidence, was shown to be vulnerable, because the assembly's decision could be answered by armed force under the banner of the state's safety. At the same time, the tribunate survived, and its legislative power remained in principle intact. Neither the Senate nor its opponents had won a clear victory.

Historians disagree about how to describe this. Some see the killings as a turning point that made the fall of the Republic difficult to avoid. Others emphasize that after 121 there followed a decade of relative calm in Rome's internal politics, in which, for example, most Gracchan legislation was allowed to stand, and that the possibility of other outcomes remained open. The most defensible statement is that the events created precedents whose meaning depended on how later actors used them. Between 121 and 100, no politician was killed in a political dispute at Rome, but everyone knew it could be done.

Section 19.5

Inequality, Reform, and the Limits of the Evidence

Ancient authors, and many modern historians, explained the Gracchan movement as a response to social distress: wealthy Romans had seized public land and replaced free tenants and smallholders with enslaved laborers, the free peasantry had declined, cities had filled with the dispossessed, and the army had suffered a shortage of recruits from the class of property owning soldiers on which it depended. Chapter 18 introduced this picture and cautioned that it is not proven. This section returns to the problem and separates the elements.

Some parts of the picture are well supported. Rome's conquests had brought enormous wealth into the hands of a small elite, and some of that wealth was invested in land, particularly in central and southern Italy and in Sicily. Enslaved people were brought into Italy in very large numbers as prisoners of war and through the slave trade, and the great revolts in Sicily in 135 to 132 and 104 to 100 BCE show how many were working in large agricultural enterprises. Cities, especially Rome, grew, and the grain law suggests that supply of food to Rome had become a political issue. The Gracchan land commission actually functioned, which shows that there was land to redistribute and people willing to receive it. Boundary stones and later legislation, including a land law of 111 BCE that survives in part and that confirmed the holdings of many possessors of public land as private property, show that the land problem was real enough to produce sustained legislative attention.

Other parts are far less certain. Census figures, which ancient sources preserve for the second century, record a slight fall in the number of registered citizens between the 130s and the early 120s and then an increase to nearly 395,000 by 125 to 124. Such numbers are difficult to interpret. It is not clear who was counted, what property qualification was applied, whether the totals reflect changes in the population or changes in registration practice, or whether the increase reflects the work of the land commission. The claim that a large class of small farmers had disappeared, and that this explains the army's recruitment difficulties, is contested by modern archaeologists and demographers, who point to rural survey evidence suggesting that many small farms continued to exist and that the pattern of change differed from region to region. Large estates worked by enslaved labor certainly existed, but their spread was uneven, and small and medium farms did not vanish. Some soldiers may have avoided service, and some Roman families may have been unable to meet property qualifications, but we lack the evidence to generalize confidently.

For these reasons, students should treat the connection between economic inequality and political crisis with care. Inequality was real and important, but it does not automatically explain political violence. Many societies have experienced severe inequality without civil war. What the evidence does show is that land, distribution of the profits of empire, and the treatment of poorer citizens and Italians became political issues, that some elite politicians made those issues the basis of political careers, and that other elite politicians perceived that they threatened their power. The Roman political system was thus stressed by social change, but the form that the stress took was determined by political choices. The Gracchi were not simply responding to an economic crisis, and their opponents were not simply defending property. They were participants in a contest over who would control the resources of an empire, and about which institutions had authority to allocate them.

Section 19.6

Jugurtha, Corruption, and the Rise of Marius

The war in Numidia began as a dynastic quarrel in a North African kingdom and became a crisis of Roman politics. Numidia, a kingdom of the Berber speaking peoples west of Carthage, had been an ally of Rome since the Second Punic War, and under Micipsa, who died in 118 BCE, it was a prosperous state with a cavalry famed across the Mediterranean. Micipsa left the kingdom to his two sons, Adherbal and Hiempsal, and to his nephew and adopted son Jugurtha. Jugurtha, who had served with distinction with a Roman army in Spain and had friends among Roman nobles, arranged the murder of Hiempsal, and Adherbal fled to Rome to appeal for support.

A Senate commission, which included Lucius Opimius, the consul of 121, divided the kingdom. Jugurtha received the more valuable western part. The sources, particularly Sallust, allege that he had bribed senators. Jugurtha then attacked Adherbal and besieged him at Cirta in 112, and when the city fell, he killed Adherbal along with many Italian traders who had helped defend the city. The killing of Italian traders and residents, some of them potentially Roman citizens, provoked outrage among the equestrians and merchants who had commercial interests in Africa, and it forced the Senate's hand. War was declared in 111. A Roman commander, the consul Calpurnius Bestia, quickly concluded a settlement with Jugurtha which was widely believed to have been purchased. Jugurtha was summoned to Rome under safe conduct to testify, and there, according to Sallust, he corrupted a tribune to veto his examination and arranged the murder of a rival Numidian prince. He left Rome with the reported remark that the city was for sale and would perish if it found a buyer, a line that we cannot verify and that may express Sallust's own moral outlook more than Jugurtha's.

The war continued badly. In 110 a Roman army under Aulus Postumius Albinus was defeated and forced to pass under the yoke, a humiliating symbol of submission. Popular anger led to the establishment in 109 of a special court, proposed by the tribune Gaius Mamilius, to investigate senators accused of collaboration with Jugurtha, and several eminent men were convicted. This episode is important for two reasons. It shows that the popular assemblies and courts could hold members of the elite accountable, and it shows that the Senate's control of the war was seen as a failure. The command then passed to Quintus Caecilius Metellus, a capable commander who restored discipline and won victories but was unable to end the war, because Jugurtha adopted a strategy of harassment, using his cavalry and mobile bases, and drew on the help of his father in law Bocchus, king of Mauretania.

One of Metellus's officers was Gaius Marius, a man from the town of Arpinum, about a hundred kilometers southeast of Rome. Marius was a novus homo, a "new man," that is, a man none of whose ancestors had reached the consulship. He was not a poor outsider. He belonged to a wealthy local family of equestrian status with connections to great Roman houses, and he had served with distinction in Spain and been elected praetor. But the consulship was normally held by members of a limited group of families, and Marius's election was a genuine exception. Marius asked Metellus for leave to stand for the consulship of 107, was refused, and used the frustrations of the army and the Roman people, and the support of equestrian businessmen who wanted the war ended, to win the election. He then succeeded to the command through a vote of the popular assembly, which passed over the Senate's preference.

Marius's campaign ended the war less through decisive battle than through diplomacy. His quaestor, Lucius Cornelius Sulla, negotiated the surrender of Jugurtha by Bocchus in 105. Jugurtha was displayed in Marius's triumph in 104 and executed in Rome. In later years Sulla and Marius would quarrel over who deserved credit, and Sulla commemorated the event on a signet ring, an example of how a small episode could turn into a personal rivalry. Numidia itself, meanwhile, was not annexed but remained a client kingdom, divided between Bocchus and Jugurtha's half brother. The Numidian political setting deserves independent emphasis: Jugurtha's actions were those of an African ruler operating in a political world that increasingly included Rome, and his methods, alliances, and ambitions cannot be understood merely as a prelude to Marius's rise. Sallust, our main source, wrote for a Roman audience interested in corruption, and he presented the war as a story about Roman vice. The Numidian perspective is barely represented, except through the surviving evidence of coins, inscriptions in Punic and Libyan scripts, and Roman reports.

Section 19.7

Marius and Military Transformation: Reform or Long Process?

The Jugurthine War coincided with a far greater emergency. In the later 110s and 100s BCE, migrating groups called the Cimbri and Teutones, along with associated peoples, moved through Gaul and the Alps region and defeated Roman armies several times. Ancient authors located their origin far to the north, perhaps in Jutland, although modern scholars are uncertain about their language and identity, and about the accuracy of applying later ethnic labels like "Germanic" to them. In 105 BCE, at Arausio (modern Orange, in southern France), two Roman armies were destroyed in a disaster that ancient sources described as the worst since Cannae. The losses were very great, and the quarrel between the two commanders, one a senator of aristocratic family and the other a novus homo, was blamed by some ancient authors.

In these circumstances Marius was elected consul for 104 in his absence, and reelected in each of the next four years, to 100. This was in flagrant contradiction of the custom that a decade should separate consulships, and it was justified by the emergency. He trained his army, defeated the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae in 102 in southern Gaul, and, with his colleague Quintus Lutatius Catulus, the Cimbri at Vercellae in northern Italy in 101. Marius was celebrated as a savior of Rome, and some sources say as a "third founder of the city."

The Marius of many textbooks did something further. According to the familiar account, in 107 BCE he abolished the property requirement for military service, created a professional army of landless volunteers, reorganized the legion into cohorts, introduced the silver eagle as the standard, standardized equipment, and made soldiers loyal to generals instead of Rome. This package, often called the "Marian reforms," is largely a modern construction. The ancient evidence supports only parts of it, and it supports them at different dates.

What can be said with reasonable confidence? Sallust states that in 107 Marius enrolled soldiers from the capite censi, the "counted by head," those below the lowest property class, who had not normally been recruited. But this must be read in context. The property qualification for service had been reduced over generations, and the pool of eligible citizens had shifted as the value of money changed. Recruitment of poorer men had probably occurred in earlier emergencies, and Marius's recruitment was in significant part a request for volunteers in a specific campaign, not a general abolition of the rule. Marius did not create a standing professional army: soldiers continued to serve for a campaign or a series of campaigns and were then discharged, and the state did not maintain a permanent salaried force. The Roman army of the later first century BCE was different, but the change was gradual, and much of it came later, in the 80s to 40s BCE.

The cohort, formed by grouping three maniples, was already used tactically in Spanish campaigns in the second century BCE and increased in importance over time. The evidence does not show a single reorganization by Marius. The silver eagle as standard is associated by the Elder Pliny, writing in the first century CE, with Marius's second consulship in 104, but that is a statement about a symbol rather than a reform of the entire army. Ancient authors also refer to "Marius's mules," meaning soldiers who carried their own equipment, a phrase that may reflect a change in logistics or in discipline. Equipment was also changing across the period. What the evidence does show is a pattern of interrelated developments. Long campaigns kept men away from farms. Poorer citizens, for whom military service and its rewards, including booty, pay, and a share of land, were attractive, were increasingly willing to serve. Commanders who won victories could plausibly ask the state for land for veterans. And commanders who held commands for longer periods had time to develop personal ties with their soldiers.

It is this last development, the growth of bonds between soldiers and commanders, that mattered most for the political story. But it should not be overstated. In 107 soldiers did not transfer loyalty from the Republic to an individual general. Marius's soldiers remained citizens, expected to return to civilian life, and most Roman armies of the following decades, including those of the Social War, behaved in accordance with the traditions of the Republic. The first time a Roman army marched on Rome was in 88 BCE, nearly twenty years after the Jugurthine command, and in that case the army followed a consul who claimed to be defending his lawful command against an illegal transfer. Marius mattered greatly, above all through his repeated consulships and his command practices, but a single "Marian revolution" in the army did not occur. Historical Debate

Did Marius Transform the Roman Army?

The question: Did Gaius Marius, in or around 107 BCE, create a new kind of Roman army, one of landless professional soldiers personally loyal to their commanders, and was that transformation a principal cause of the fall of the Republic?

Interpretation A — A decisive Marian military revolution: For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many historians, drawing especially on Sallust, Plutarch, and Aulus Gellius, argued that Marius abolished the property qualification and thereby created a professional army drawn from the landless poor. They further argued that these men, dependent on a commander for pay and land, transferred their loyalty from the state to their generals. On this reading, the army became the instrument of the men who overthrew the Republic.
Interpretation B — A gradual and poorly attested process: Many scholars since the later twentieth century have argued that the supposed reforms are gradual, earlier, later, or poorly attested. The property qualification had been declining for generations, the cohort predates Marius, the equipment changes are attested over a long period, and Sallust's statement concerns a specific recruitment for a specific campaign. Soldiers of the 100s and 80s BCE often behaved as citizens in a traditional way, and the real emergence of armies loyal to individual commanders came in the 80s to 40s BCE.

Evidence: The debate depends on a small number of ancient passages, all written long after the events and with concerns of their own, on comparison of the Roman army's organization at different dates, on archaeological evidence for equipment, and on inferences from soldiers' political behavior in the 100s to 80s BCE.

A synthesis: Marius mattered a great deal, especially through his recruitment of volunteers from poorer citizens, his repeated consulships and commands, his use of army veterans in politics, and the political circumstances of his time. But there was no single documented "Marian reform package." The change was a long process with many contributors, and the relationship between soldiers and commanders depended on circumstances more than on a fixed institutional shift.

Why it matters: If the army was transformed by one man's reform, the fall of the Republic can look mechanical: a structural change makes generals dominant, and generals destroy the Republic. If the change was gradual and depended on political choices, then the sequence of civil wars looks more contingent, with more room for alternatives and for decisions by political leaders in the Senate, the assemblies, and the camps.

Student task: Identify two specific claims in the traditional "Marian reforms" and explain what kind of evidence would be needed to date each of them. Then explain why a historian might accept that the army changed while rejecting the idea of a single reform.

Section 19.8

Veterans, Popular Politics, and Saturninus

Marius returned from his victories as the most celebrated man in Rome, and he discovered the limits of that celebrity. His soldiers needed rewards. Soldiers who had fought in Africa and against the Cimbri and Teutones expected to be settled on land, and the Senate, which had control over the distribution of public land and the founding of colonies, was not disposed to provide it. Marius turned to political allies. Lucius Appuleius Saturninus, who had been tribune in 103 and again in 100, and Gaius Servilius Glaucia, a praetor, sponsored legislation that established colonies for veterans, particularly in Africa and in the Gallic provinces, and subsidized grain. Saturninus's first tribunate had featured a law on treason, the lex Appuleia de maiestate, aimed at punishing commanders who had brought loss on the state, perhaps reflecting the anger at the disaster of Arausio.

The events of 100 illustrate how far political violence had become part of the toolkit. Saturninus's land bill contained a clause requiring senators to swear an oath to uphold the law, on pain of removal. Marius, after appearing to hesitate, swore. Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus, Marius's former commander and enemy, refused and went into exile. During the elections for 99, a rival candidate for the consulship, Gaius Memmius, was beaten to death in the presence of Glaucia's supporters. The Senate passed the emergency decree again, and Marius, who was consul that year, was asked to act. He armed men, including veterans, and confronted his former allies. Saturninus and Glaucia took refuge on the Capitol, and after their water supply was cut off, they surrendered on assurances of safety. They were locked in the Senate house, where a crowd broke the roof, and killed them with tiles.

The Saturninus affair matters for reasons that go beyond the death toll. It shows that the emergency decree could be used by a consul who had himself been a partner of the men whose destruction he directed. It shows that a victorious general, however great his prestige, could not simply command Roman politics, because he depended on the goodwill of assemblies, the Senate, and alliances that could dissolve. And it damaged Marius. His attempt to have both the veterans' support and the Senate's approval left him distrusted by both, and the following decade saw his prestige decline. Metellus Numidicus was recalled, and Marius went abroad, to Asia. The episode also suggests a persistent feature of the crisis: violence was carried out by crowds, clients, gladiators, veterans, and slaves recruited in the interest of political figures, and it is often impossible to say who was responsible for what.

Section 19.9

Italians, Citizenship, and the Social War

The Social War (from socii, "allies"), fought from 91 to 88 BCE with mopping up operations afterward, was one of the most serious wars in Roman history, and it belongs at the center of this chapter. It brought to a head a problem that Chapters 16 and 18 followed across generations: the ambiguous status of the Italian allies. Rome had built an unusually flexible system of incorporation, granting citizenship of various degrees to some communities and treaty status to others, and it had drawn on Italian manpower for its wars. In the later second century, the allies supplied soldiers in numbers equal to or greater than Roman citizens, yet their communities had no vote in Rome and could be subject to the arbitrary treatment of Roman magistrates, and the distribution of the spoils of empire, including land, was not shared equally with them. The Gracchan land commission had also affected allied holdings, and Gaius's and Flaccus's proposals to extend citizenship had failed.

In 91 BCE the tribune Marcus Livius Drusus, son of the man who had opposed Gaius Gracchus, proposed a program of laws that included a judicial reform balancing senators and equestrians, land and grain measures, and, apparently, the extension of citizenship to the Italians. It was opposed by both senators and many citizens, and the Senate subsequently invalidated his enacted laws on procedural grounds, with opponents invoking rules regulating legislative procedure. Drusus was assassinated in his own house by an unknown assailant. Italian leaders who had counted on him concluded that their hopes for reform within the Roman system had ended, and that they faced a prosecution of those accused of conspiracy against Rome under the terms of the lex Varia, a special law aimed at those who had encouraged the allies.

The revolt began at Asculum in Picenum, where the population killed a Roman praetor and other Romans present. It spread through central and southern Italy, involving the Marsi, Paeligni, Marrucini, Vestini, Picentes, Samnites, Lucanians, and others. The rebels formed a confederation with its capital at Corfinium in the Apennines, which they renamed Italia. They established a senate, two consuls, and twelve praetors, and they minted coins with Latin and Oscan legends, some showing a bull, the symbol of Italy, goring the Roman wolf. This detail is important. The rebels did not simply seek to join the Roman state. They created a rival state that imitated Roman institutions, which suggests that many of them were willing, at least at first, to leave Rome behind.

The war was fought with great intensity on both sides, in several theaters, by Roman armies commanded by consuls and legates, among them Marius, then in his late sixties, and Sulla, as well as Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo, father of Pompey the Great. The Romans suffered serious early defeats, but the rebels were divided, and lacked the resources of Rome, which still drew on the loyal Latin and Greek communities and on provincial revenues. Casualty figures in the sources are unreliable, and archaeological evidence for destruction in some Italian towns suggests severe local damage, but we cannot count the dead.

What ultimately turned the war was a political decision. In 90 BCE the consul Lucius Julius Caesar carried a law, the lex Julia, offering Roman citizenship to Italian communities that had not revolted or that laid down their arms. In 89 BCE the lex Plautia Papiria allowed individuals to claim citizenship, and the lex Pompeia extended Latin rights to communities north of the Po. These measures separated potential rebels from the hard core of the revolt, and they show the paradox of the Social War: Rome fought its allies on the battlefield while conceding much of what many of them had demanded. This was not simply a matter of "the Italians wanted citizenship and Rome refused." Different Italian communities had different histories and motives. Some, including those in the Latin and Etruscan zones, had good reason to fear the effects of rebellion. Some wanted equal membership, while others, especially among the Samnites, may have wanted independence. The Roman concessions helped fragment the rebellion. Fighting continued in the south, and Samnite resistance persisted until 87 or later. The city of Nola held out until 80 BCE.

After the war, Roman citizenship theoretically encompassed most free Italians south of the Po, and Latin rights covered the region between the Po and the Alps. The transformation was extraordinary, but the practical integration was contentious. The new citizens needed to be registered in the census and enrolled in voting tribes. Old citizens feared that the new ones would dilute their influence, and the Senate initially assigned them to a small number of tribes, which limited their influence in the assemblies. Attempts to redistribute them among all thirty five tribes were an important element of the political conflicts of 88 BCE and after, and the census of 86 to 85, which is reported to have registered about 463,000 citizens (the transmission and interpretation of the figure are debated), was followed by a much higher total, about 910,000, in 70 to 69 BCE, showing that registration lagged behind the new legal reality. The Italians, in short, had won a great deal, and in their pursuit of full participation they had also become participants in the struggles that follow. The cities of Italy, which had been secondary partners in the Roman system, became the source of manpower and political support that Roman leaders would compete to command. The war also left many Italian communities weakened and resentful, and the fighting between Sulla and his enemies in the following decade would again involve them.

Section 19.10

Mithridates and the Struggle for Command

While Rome was preoccupied by the Social War, a crisis in the east opened a new arena of conflict. Mithridates VI Eupator, king of Pontus on the southern coast of the Black Sea, had built a substantial kingdom, expanding around the Black Sea and into Anatolia. He was of Iranian and Greek heritage, and cultivated an image as protector of Greeks against Rome. In 89 to 88 BCE, taking advantage of Rome's distraction and of resentment against Roman rule and tax collection in the province of Asia, he invaded, overran the province, and, according to ancient sources, ordered the killing of Romans and Italians in the cities of Asia in a single coordinated action in 88. The numbers reported by ancient authors, up to tens of thousands, are not reliable, but the event was clearly grave and widely remembered. Many Greek cities, including Athens, subsequently sided with Mithridates.

Roman commanders had held the province in trust, and the war offered the prospect of great prestige and great wealth, since provinces in Asia were among the richest in the empire. It also created a problem in the distribution of commands. For 88 BCE the Senate assigned the war against Mithridates to the consul Sulla, who had distinguished himself in the Social War. Marius, then over sixty and still eager for glory, resented the appointment, and it was said that he trained publicly in the Campus Martius to show he was fit for command. A tribune of 88, Publius Sulpicius Rufus, initially an ally of Sulla's colleagues and a supporter of the interests of the new Italian citizens, introduced legislation that included distributing them among all the tribes and, in a further measure, transferring the command against Mithridates from Sulla to Marius by popular vote.

Sulpicius's supporters, including armed young men, drove consuls out of the Forum during the debate, and Sulla left the city. The dispute was thus both a struggle over a command, and a struggle over whether the assembly could transfer a command that the Senate had assigned. In legal terms, Sulpicius's law was passed by the assembly, and the assembly had the legal power to do so. Sulla, however, regarded the law as invalid because it had been passed under violence and because it deprived him of a command he had been duly assigned. Sulla went to Nola, in Campania, where his army was stationed, and made his case to his soldiers, who, according to the sources, supported him in part out of fear of losing the opportunity for the wealth of the east.

Sulla then marched on Rome with six legions. Plutarch reports that his senior officers except one refused to take part, and left him. That the officers, aristocrats with their own conceptions of legitimacy, refused, while the soldiers followed, shows something important about the state of the army and about the limits of the earlier debate over "professional" armies. The soldiers were not simply a tool of a general. They followed a consul with a specific claim to legitimacy and a specific promise, while many of their commanders, who shared the aristocratic conventions of the Republic, regarded the act as unthinkable. In 88, for the first time, a Roman army under the command of a Roman magistrate entered the city as an enemy. Sulpicius was killed, Marius and other opponents fled, and Sulla had the Senate declare Marius and his allies public enemies, hostes. Sulla passed legislation designed to limit the powers of tribunes and of the assemblies, arranged the election of consuls for the following year, and left for the east in 87.

This was the constitutional rupture. The earlier episodes had involved crowds, clients, and armed followers, but the army, the instrument with which Rome had conquered the Mediterranean, had always been kept outside the city, and crossing the boundary of the sacred city limit, the pomerium, was taboo. Sulla did not argue that he was overthrowing the Republic. He argued that he was restoring it, freeing it from the tyranny of a tribune and an old general. But the precedent was now available to everyone. It is a remarkable indication of the fragility of norms in this period that, once broken, they could not be restored merely by the wish of those who had broken them.

Section 19.11

Sulla, Civil War, Proscriptions, and Dictatorship

Sulla's departure did not end the crisis in Rome. In 87, the consul Lucius Cornelius Cinna, who had been elected under Sulla's arrangement, proposed to distribute the newly enfranchised Italians among all the voting tribes and to recall Marius. His colleague Gnaeus Octavius resisted, and there was fighting in the city, after which Cinna was driven out and deprived of his consulship. Cinna raised an army from Italian communities, and Marius returned from exile with a force of his own. Together they besieged Rome, which surrendered. Marius and his supporters entered the city, and the following days saw a series of killings of political opponents, including the consul Octavius, whose head was displayed in the Forum, and several eminent senators. The extent of the killings is debated, but the sources agree that they were a serious blow to the aristocracy. Marius and Cinna were elected consuls for 86, and Marius, for the seventh time, died at the start of the year, in January, an old man consumed by anxiety, according to the tradition.

For the next three years Cinna dominated Rome with colleagues, holding repeated consulships. Modern historians increasingly stress that the regime of Cinna was not merely a tyranny of terror. It governed, held elections, dealt with the debts that had accumulated during the wars, and organized a census that enrolled the new citizens. It also had to face the question of how to deal with Sulla. Rome sent an army under Lucius Valerius Flaccus to the east to replace him, but the army, after being led by an unpopular commander who was killed by his own officer, joined the campaign against Mithridates under his successor. Cinna was killed in 84 during a mutiny of troops who did not wish to sail against Sulla. The regime that Sulla returned to fight was thus divided, and it had control of Rome and much of Italy but no clear leader.

Meanwhile Sulla, in Greece and Asia Minor, campaigned against the armies of Mithridates. He besieged Athens, which he took in 86 after a difficult siege and sacked, and he defeated Mithridates's forces at Chaeronea and Orchomenus. Short of money, he requisitioned sacred treasures from Greek sanctuaries, including Olympia and Delphi; according to Plutarch, he represented the Delphic funds as assets he would restore if they were spent. In 85 he concluded a settlement with Mithridates at Dardanus that returned the king to his own realm in exchange for payment and ships, and did not destroy him. The terms were criticized by some Romans as too lenient, but they let Sulla turn his attention to Italy. Mithridates's later wars with Rome, taken up by Pompey, belong to Chapter 20. Sulla also punished the cities of Asia, imposing large indemnities that produced long lasting resentment and debt in the province, another example of how the crisis in Rome affected the provinces.

Sulla landed at Brundisium in the spring of 83 BCE with about forty thousand men, and wrote to the Senate that he was coming to defend the Republic. He was joined by important allies, among them Marcus Licinius Crassus, Quintus Caecilius Metellus Pius, and Gnaeus Pompeius, then in his early twenties, who raised legions on his own initiative from the region of Picenum. The war that followed was truly a civil war, and it was not simply Sulla against the survivors of Marius's party. The anti Sullan side included many Italians, especially Samnites, who did not trust him, and many Roman aristocrats who wished to defend the legally elected government. Several commanders of Sulla's enemies defected. Fighting occurred in Campania, Etruria, and Cisalpine Gaul, and Marius's son, Marius the Younger, was besieged at Praeneste. The decisive battle was fought on 1 November 82 BCE at the Colline Gate of Rome, where Sulla's army confronted a large Samnite and Lucanian force led by Pontius Telesinus. The battle was hard fought and nearly lost by Sulla's left wing, but the victory was Sulla's. The prisoners taken, reported as several thousand, were killed, and Praeneste fell soon afterward.

What followed was more far reaching than the battle itself. Sulla had lists of names published of men declared enemies, who could be killed by anyone, and whose property would be confiscated and sold at auction. These lists, the proscriptions, began in late 82. Plutarch reports 520 names across the first three lists, while Appian gives a substantially different figure of about forty senators and 1,600 equestrians; the incompatible ancient totals make precise reconstruction impossible. Rewards were offered to killers, and penalties to those who sheltered the proscribed, and the sons and grandsons of the proscribed were barred from holding office. Many Romans used the process to eliminate personal enemies and to acquire property cheaply. Sulla's freedmen and supporters, such as Chrysogonus, became notoriously wealthy through the auctions, an issue that Cicero raised in his first major speech in 80 BCE, in defense of Sextus Roscius. Proscription differed from the earlier killings in that it was formalized, public, and financially motivated. It made the elimination of political opponents into an administrative procedure and turned confiscation into a reward.

Sulla was appointed dictator. The dictatorship was an old republican office, held for at most six months in emergencies, and it had not been used since the Second Punic War, more than a century earlier. Sulla's office was extraordinary because of its title, "dictator for writing laws and for restoring the state," and because it had no time limit. It was created by a law proposed by an interrex, because the consuls were dead or absent, and the law also declared valid all Sulla's acts, past and future. His task was constitutional legislation, and he carried it out between 81 and 79.

His program aimed to strengthen the Senate and to limit the tools that had been used against it. He enlarged the Senate from about 300 to about 600 members, drawing on equestrians, and he increased the number of quaestors to twenty, so that the Senate would be filled automatically from former magistrates. He restricted the tribunate, stripping tribunes of the power to propose legislation without the Senate's prior approval, limiting the veto, and barring former tribunes from higher office. He established or reorganized standing courts, transferring jury service to senators, and he added courts for treason, forgery, and other matters. He tried to regularize the career ladder, the cursus honorum, setting minimum ages for magistracies and an interval before a man could hold the same office again. He restricted the power of provincial governors, and defined more clearly the boundary of the city as the limit of military authority, at least in theory. He settled his veterans, in numbers that ancient authors put in the tens of thousands, on land confiscated from Italian communities that had opposed him, which created many colonies of soldiers with an interest in defending his settlement. He also freed thousands of slaves of the proscribed, who took his family name of Cornelius and became a loyal base.

In 79 BCE Sulla resigned the dictatorship, an act that astonished observers and that remains one of the most debated of his decisions. He retired to a villa in Campania and died in 78. He was given a public funeral of extraordinary splendor. Ancient authors, and modern ones, ask what motivated him: the completion of his program, disillusionment, poor health, or confidence in the durability of his creation. We cannot know, because his memoirs are lost.

The question the historian must face is whether Sulla was restoring the Republic or remaking it through methods that contradicted the republican order he claimed to defend. The answer is not a slogan. Sulla's stated goal was to strengthen the traditional rule of the Senate and to prevent a repetition of the disorders of the previous decades, and many of his measures, such as the age limits for office, the enlargement of the Senate, and the reform of the courts, were intended to return to earlier norms or improve the working of existing institutions. But he did so by seizing the city by force, killing thousands of citizens, expropriating others, assuming powers no republican magistrate had held, and dismantling the tribunate as an instrument of popular politics. A settlement built on such foundations was fragile. The precedent that a general with an army could impose a constitution was now established, and many of his own supporters, Pompey and Crassus among them, later showed that they did not consider his methods unique to him. Several provisions of the Sullan order were dismantled within a decade, as Chapter 20 will explain.

Section 19.12

What Had Changed by 78 BCE?

By 78 BCE, most of the institutions of the Republic still existed. There were consuls and praetors, a Senate, popular assemblies, tribunes, standing courts, and priestly colleges. Elections continued to be held, and the year was still named for the consuls. Roman law continued to develop. Rome had also, through the Social War, acquired a much broader Roman citizen body extending across most of Italy south of the Po. In this sense, the Republic had survived, and it may be an error to describe these decades as the "collapse" of a system that continued to function for another half century.

Yet the range of acceptable political methods had changed. Tribunes had removed colleagues. Senators had killed a tribune and his followers, and the Senate had sanctioned lethal force against citizens on the basis of an emergency decree. Consuls had been elected repeatedly for consecutive years. A tribune had carried a law transferring an army command, and a consul had answered by marching on Rome. Rival governments had killed opponents in the city. A victor had proscribed his enemies, confiscated their property, and rewarded his followers with it. And a dictator without a time limit had remade the political order. None of these precedents made Caesar inevitable. In 78, when the consul Marcus Aemilius Lepidus tried to reverse parts of the Sullan settlement and led a rebellion, the Sullan order held. Some senators concluded that the Sullan constitution was the necessary answer to the crisis, others that it was an unlawful imposition, and still others wished simply for a return to normal politics. But they operated in a political world in which the precedents were known.

The chapter's period also reshaped the relationship of Rome to Italy and to the provinces. Italy became, in legal terms, a single citizen space, though one still divided by local loyalties and by the effects of confiscation and settlement. The provinces bore the costs of Rome's civil conflicts, through the extraction of resources to pay for armies, and through Roman commanders' use of provincial funds. Asia, punished by Sulla, would remain in debt for decades. Numidia and Mauretania, drawn into Rome's political struggle, would reappear in the wars of the next generation. Chapter 20 turns to the men who inherited this world, Pompey and Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar, and to the failure of the post Sullan generation to contain their competition.

Primary Source Window

Plutarch, Life of Tiberius Gracchus, on a speech of Tiberius, written c. early second century CE

Creator: Plutarch of Chaeronea, a Greek biographer and philosopher living under Roman rule · Date: written roughly two centuries and a half after the events, in the late first or early second century CE · Type: A parallel biography, written to illustrate character and moral qualities, drawing on earlier Greek and Latin sources that are now mostly lost

Context: Plutarch paired the lives of Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus with those of two Spartan kings, Agis and Cleomenes, who had also attempted social reforms. He used earlier writers, some sympathetic to the Gracchi and some hostile, and he inserted passages of speech that he presents as the words of his subjects.

Paraphrase of Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus, chapter 9 (not a direct quotation) In a passage describing Tiberius's public appeals, Plutarch has him contrast the wild beasts of Italy, each with a den or lair, with the men who fight and die for Italy, who have only air and light and wander with their families without homes. Tiberius is made to say that the citizens are called masters of the world, though they do not possess a clod of earth of their own. This is a summary, not a translation, and students wishing to read the exact words should consult a standard published translation.

Analysis: The passage is a vivid statement of the moral case for land distribution, and it has often been quoted by modern historians to support the picture of a dispossessed peasant soldiery. Its value as evidence, however, is limited. Plutarch wrote long after the event, in Greek, for a Greek speaking audience, and he was interested in moral character. It is possible that he preserved a tradition of Tiberius's speech, but the wording may be his own or that of an earlier author, and rhetorical reconstruction of speeches was normal in ancient historical writing. The passage creates an image of the Roman soldier as a landless wanderer that fits Plutarch's moral aims and does not necessarily describe the actual condition of Roman soldiers. As Section 19.5 shows, the extent of rural dispossession remains debated. The passage tells us more about how later writers understood the Gracchan movement than about the state of Italian agriculture in 133. It also gives no voice to those who opposed the reforms, to Italian allies, or to enslaved people.

Historical Vocabulary
Populares and Optimates

Populares ("those who favor the people") and optimates ("the best men") are labels that Roman authors, especially Cicero in the 50s BCE, used to describe political styles or alignments. A politician who acted as a popularis pursued his goals by appealing to the popular assemblies, often through legislation on land, grain, or debt, in opposition to the wishes of the Senate majority. An optimas, in Cicero's usage, was a defender of the authority of the Senate and of the established order.

The labels are helpful for describing rhetoric and methods, but they do not describe parties. Romans had no organized parties with permanent membership, platforms, or discipline. Politicians and families formed shifting alliances, individuals changed their approach as circumstances required, and many so called populares, including the Gracchi, were members of the highest aristocracy. Modern historians therefore use the terms cautiously and often prefer to speak of individual policies and alliances.

Multiple Perspectives

The Republic in Crisis: Perspectives on Sulla's Settlement

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and these groups did not possess equal power, resources, or responsibility for events.

From the perspective of a supporter of Tiberius Gracchus: The land law recovered land that belonged in law to the Roman people, and its opponents used the veto and later violence to protect private gain. The killing of Tiberius showed that the aristocracy would not accept even a lawful decision of the assembly.
From the perspective of a senatorial opponent of the Gracchi and later of Saturninus: Tribunes who removed colleagues or sought continuous office threatened the balance that protected every citizen from tyranny. Emergency measures were painful but necessary to preserve the constitution, and the Senate's authority over land and finance was a safeguard, not an obstacle.
From the perspective of an Italian ally, for example in a Marsic town: Italians fought Rome's wars and shared its risks, yet were denied a vote and vulnerable to the decisions of Roman magistrates. Some looked for full citizenship, some considered independence, and the concessions of 90 and 89 BCE divided communities according to their circumstances.
From the perspective of a Roman citizen soldier or veteran of Marius: Service brought pay and booty, but also long absence, danger, and dependence on a commander to obtain land. Veterans expected the state to honor their service, and the failure of the Senate to do so encouraged them to look to political patrons.
From the perspective of a Numidian or Mauretanian observer: Rome's intervention in a succession dispute turned local rivalries into a war fought partly for the benefit of Roman politicians. For Numidian elites, the question was less Roman corruption than how to survive between a dominant power and rival claimants.
From the perspective of a victim or beneficiary of the Sullan proscriptions: A proscribed senator or equestrian lost his life or property, and his sons lost their political future. A settled veteran or an enriched buyer at the auctions, by contrast, gained land and wealth, and Sulla's freed slaves gained citizenship, which shows how the same measures created both victims and clients.
Continuity and Change Over Time

What Remained Republican after Sulla?

What Changed

  • Magistrates had used emergency decrees to justify lethal force against citizens, while Sulla's proscriptions formally exposed listed opponents to killing and confiscation without ordinary trial
  • Armies had marched on Rome, and commanders with loyal soldiers could dictate terms to the Senate
  • Repeated consulships and exceptional commands had become thinkable, and the dictatorship was revived with no fixed time limit and exceptional constituent powers
  • Roman citizenship extended across Italy south of the Po, transforming the political community
  • The powers of the tribunate were restricted by Sulla, and the composition of the Senate and the courts was changed

What Remained Continuous

  • Annual elections of magistrates, the Senate, popular assemblies, priestly colleges, and standing courts continued to operate
  • Aristocratic competition for office and glory, and the ideal of service to the republic, remained the language of politics
  • Rome's rule over provinces and client kingdoms continued, and the provincial system remained largely intact
  • The tribunate survived, and its full powers would be restored within a decade

Who experienced change most directly: Senators and equestrians on the losing sides, Italian communities that lost land to veteran colonies, and provincial communities that paid indemnities felt the change most sharply. Who experienced greater continuity: Many rural households and city dwellers outside the reach of the proscriptions continued their daily lives with fewer immediate changes, though they might be affected by taxation, recruitment, or confiscation. Why the pattern matters: It prevents both errors: treating the Republic as already dead in 133, and treating it as unchanged in 78.

Cause and Effect

How Political Violence Became Reproducible

Long term causes

Wealth and power drawn from empire, disputes over public land and the profits of conquest, the ambiguous position of Italian allies, dependence on norms rather than written rules, and an aristocracy competing intensely for a limited number of high offices.

Short term causes

Senatorial resistance to Tiberius's land law and to tribunician initiative, the bequest of Pergamum, failures of Roman generals in Numidia and against the Cimbri, the frustration of veterans, the murder of Drusus, and the dispute over the Mithridatic command.

Event or transformation

The escalation from the killing of Tiberius in 133 to the emergency decree of 121, the deaths of Saturninus in 100, the Social War, the march on Rome in 88, the civil wars of the 80s, and Sulla's proscriptions and dictatorship.

Immediate effects

Loss of political leaders and their supporters, confiscation and resettlement, the suspension or restriction of tribunician powers, the extension of citizenship to Italians, and the enlargement of the Senate.

Long term consequences

A political world in which leaders and their opponents could cite precedents for violence, extraordinary commands, and constitutional engineering, and in which soldiers and veterans became major political constituencies. This is developed in Chapter 20.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Individual choices, such as Tiberius's decision to depose Octavius or Sulla's decision to march, mattered, and so did the responses of senators, soldiers, Italians, and assemblies. At many points a different decision might have led elsewhere.

Historical complexity: Violence was not simply the result of social pressures. It was a strategy chosen by participants who believed that it would protect their interests, and each success or failure changed what other participants believed was possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Military Service, the Household, and the Veteran

For a Roman or Italian household of moderate means, the decades of this chapter meant repeated demands for military service. A man conscripted or recruited for a campaign in Spain, Africa, Gaul, or Asia might be gone for years, leaving a wife, children, and elderly relatives to manage the farm or workshop, sometimes with the help of enslaved workers or relatives, and sometimes without. Soldiers received pay, food rations, and a share of booty, but the rewards were uneven, and the risks of disease and death were high. Camp life imposed discipline enforced by beatings and executions, and long marches and fortification labor. Wives and mothers of soldiers, whose voices seldom survive in our sources, bore the practical burdens of running households and of securing what soldiers had promised.

The expectation of land at discharge, which had been an ad hoc reward in earlier periods, became a central concern of soldiers and their commanders as campaigns lengthened. Veterans settled in colonies were expected to farm, defend the settlement, and remember the general who had settled them. Sulla's colonies, planted on land taken from communities that had opposed him, created neighborhoods in which veterans lived beside dispossessed neighbors. Elsewhere, Italian communities faced the aftermath of the Social War with damaged towns, disrupted harvests, and altered status. Enslaved people, meanwhile, were the labor force of the estates that supplied Rome and armies, and they were also the victims and sometimes participants of the period's violence, as the Sicilian revolts show. Written sources largely reflect elite interests, and the daily life of ordinary soldiers, farmers, enslaved people, and women must be reconstructed carefully from inscriptions, archaeological survey, tombstones, and incidental references.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the escalation of political conflict in the Roman Republic between 133 and 78 BCE, from the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus to the dictatorship of Sulla. It began by defining the "crisis" with caution. The Republic did not collapse in a single moment, and its institutions continued to operate throughout the period. What changed was the range of methods that political actors considered available, and the central theme was precedent: each exceptional action enlarged the repertoire of later actors.

Tiberius Gracchus proposed a distribution of public land in 133 BCE, and to overcome a tribunician veto he had a colleague removed by the assembly. The question was whether a tribune could use the sovereignty of the assembly to defeat senatorial resistance. His opponents answered with violence, and Tiberius and many followers were killed without trial. The land commission continued, and boundary markers attest to its work, but the political questions it raised were not resolved.

Gaius Gracchus built a broader program of grain distribution, colonies, judicial reform, provincial taxation, and proposals on Italian citizenship, and constructed a coalition of urban poor, equestrians, soldiers, and provincials and Italians. His opponents divided his coalition and, in 121 BCE, obtained the first emergency decree of the Senate, followed by the killing of Gaius, Flaccus, and many supporters. The event produced a procedure for lethal force against citizens, and it was accepted in the courts and by much of the political class.

The evidence for social and economic causes must be handled with care. Concentration of wealth, enslaved labor, and urban growth were real, but the claim that a free peasantry had disappeared and that this explains the army's recruitment problems is contested. The crisis was not simply a response to economic distress. It was a contest over who would control the resources of empire and which institutions would allocate them.

The Jugurthine War brought a Numidian succession dispute into Roman politics, and exposed anger at senatorial corruption and failure. It brought Gaius Marius, a novus homo, to the consulship and to command. Marius's recruitment of poorer volunteers, his repeated consulships, and his victories over the Cimbri and Teutones changed the relationship between commanders and soldiers, but the traditional "Marian reforms" are largely a modern construction. Recruitment, the cohort, equipment, and veteran expectations evolved over a long period.

Marius's alliance with Saturninus and Glaucia showed the limits of military prestige in politics. Saturninus's tribunate ended in violence and a second emergency decree, and Marius's position was damaged. The episode demonstrated that veterans had become a political constituency and that political violence was carried out by mixed groups of crowds, clients, and soldiers.

The Social War of 91 to 88 BCE was the culmination of a long Italian grievance. After the murder of Drusus, much of central and southern Italy rebelled and formed a rival state. Rome won militarily, but it conceded citizenship through the laws of 90 and 89, dividing the rebels. The result was a transformed citizen body, but its integration into tribes, censuses, and elections remained contentious and shaped the politics of the 80s.

The dispute over the command against Mithridates VI brought the crisis to a climax. Sulpicius Rufus's legislation transferred the command from Sulla to Marius, and Sulla responded by marching on Rome with an army, a decisive rupture in the norms of republican politics. His act was answered in 87 by Cinna and Marius, whose seizure of Rome was followed by killings. Sulla returned from the east in 83, won the civil war at the Colline Gate in 82, proscribed his enemies, and held a dictatorship with no fixed time limit and exceptional constituent powers, in which he rewrote the constitution to strengthen the Senate and restrict the tribunate.

Sulla's settlement raises the question of whether he restored or remade the Republic. His measures aimed at restoration, but his methods, including military seizure of the city, formalized killing and confiscation, and unprecedented use of the dictatorship, contradicted the order he claimed to defend. His abdication and death in 78 did not resolve the problem, because the precedents he had used were now available to others.

By 78 the Republic had not fallen, but it had changed. Elections, magistracies, assemblies, and courts continued, and political leaders continued to claim republican legitimacy. Yet the world of Pompey, Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar would be shaped by the precedents examined here. None of them made Caesar inevitable. They did alter the field on which political competition took place, as Chapter 20 will show.

Key Terms

Ager publicus
Land acquired by Rome through conquest and held as the property of the Roman people, often occupied by wealthy possessors; the subject of Tiberius Gracchus's land law.
Tribune of the plebs
An annually elected magistrate with the power to propose legislation to the plebeian assembly and to veto other magistrates, whose person was traditionally protected as sacrosanct.
Veto (intercessio)
The power of a tribune or other magistrate to block an action or bill; Octavius's veto of Tiberius's land bill led to his removal.
Imperium
The authority to command armies and to exercise justice, held by consuls, praetors, and provincial governors.
Senatus consultum ultimum
A Senate decree, so named by modern scholars, urging magistrates to protect the state by any necessary means; first used in 121 BCE and of disputed legality.
Novus homo
A "new man," the first in his family to reach the consulship, such as Marius.
Equites
Wealthy citizens of the equestrian order, many of them businessmen, who did not sit in the Senate and who gained influence through jury service and tax contracts.
Publicani
Private contractors who collected provincial taxes and supplied public services, especially prominent after Gaius Gracchus's Asian tax law.
Socii
Italian allies bound to Rome by treaties requiring military contributions, without Roman citizenship or a vote in Roman assemblies.
Latin rights
A legal status conferring certain privileges, such as commercial and marriage rights with Romans, less than full Roman citizenship.
Social War
The war of 91 to 88 BCE between Rome and many of its Italian allies, resulting in a widespread extension of citizenship.
Capite censi
Citizens whose property was too small for registration in the property classes, traditionally not recruited; Sallust reports that Marius enrolled them in 107 BCE.
Legion and cohort
The legion was the main Roman army unit; the cohort was a subdivision, which grew in tactical importance over the second and first centuries BCE.
Marian reforms
A modern label for a bundle of supposed military changes attributed to Marius; largely a construction, since the changes occurred at different times and through varied processes.
Proscription
A published list of individuals declared outlaws, who could be killed by anyone and whose property was confiscated; used by Sulla from late 82 BCE.
Dictator
An old republican magistrate with supreme authority for a limited period; Sulla's dictatorship had no time limit and constituent powers.
Hostis
A public enemy; a status that placed a person outside legal protection, used against Marius and allies in 88 BCE.
Populares and optimates
Labels for political styles, not parties; the former appealed to the assemblies, the latter defended the Senate's authority.
Cursus honorum
The customary sequence of offices, from quaestor to consul; Sulla regularized it with age limits and intervals.
Quaestio
A standing court dealing with a specific type of offense, such as provincial extortion or treason.
Pomerium
The sacred boundary of the city of Rome, within which armed force was traditionally excluded; crossed by Sulla's army in 88 BCE.
Lex Acilia
A law on extortion known from a bronze inscription, associated with the reforms of the Gracchan period; its date and authorship are debated.
Grain law (lex frumentaria)
Gaius Gracchus's law providing for the sale of grain to citizens at Rome at a subsidized price.
Census
The periodic registration of citizens and their property; its figures are important but difficult to interpret for this period.
Triumph
A ceremonial procession granted to a victorious general, a major source of prestige.
Precedent
A prior act cited to justify later action; central to the Roman political system, which depended on custom more than written law.
Sullan constitution
Sulla's measures of 81 to 79 BCE on the Senate, tribunate, courts, and magistracies, intended to restore senatorial authority.
Colline Gate
The gate of Rome near which Sulla defeated the Samnite and other forces on 1 November 82 BCE.
Clientela
The system of reciprocal obligation between patrons and clients, including those of political leaders and their followers.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political system can change without formally ending. The Roman Republic of 78 BCE looked in many ways like the Republic of 150 BCE, with the same offices, the same assemblies, and the same claim to represent the Roman people. What had shifted was the set of things that leaders considered possible and legitimate. That change is difficult to see if one looks only at institutions, and it becomes visible when one follows the sequence of precedents, disputes, and decisions.

The chapter also shows how connected the Republic's internal politics were with its empire and its Italian foundations. The Jugurthine War, the Cimbric threat, the Social War, and the Mithridatic command were not merely background to a Roman constitutional story. They were the arenas in which political conflict took shape, and they involved Numidians, Gauls and other peoples, Italians, Greeks, and Anatolians. The crisis was Mediterranean as much as Roman, and it illustrates the argument of this Volume that the ancient world cannot be understood as a purely European story.

Finally, the chapter warns against two opposite mistakes. One is to treat the fall of the Republic as fated, so that every step from Tiberius Gracchus to Sulla appears as a necessary stage. The other is to treat each event as isolated, without noticing how precedents accumulated. The historian's task is to hold both in view: contingency and cumulative change. Chapter 20 will turn to the generation that inherited the Sullan settlement, and Chapter 21 to the political order that eventually replaced the Republic. Both should be read with the recognition that the outcomes were the product of choices, not of destiny.
