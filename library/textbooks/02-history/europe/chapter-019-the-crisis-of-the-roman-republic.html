The Crisis of the Roman Republic

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between the 130s and the 70s BCE the Roman Republic did not simply collapse. Its magistrates, assemblies, and courts kept working, but disputes once settled by elections, debate, and aristocratic bargaining were increasingly settled by exceptional decrees, mobs, armies, confiscation, and killing, and each such act became a precedent for the next.

In 133 BCE, on the Capitoline Hill in Rome, a group of senators and their followers, led by a private citizen who held the office of chief priest, seized wooden benches and clubs and attacked the supporters of a sitting tribune of the plebs. The tribune, Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, was killed along with a number of his followers, and their bodies were thrown into the Tiber. Ancient tradition remembers this as the first bloodshed in a Roman civic dispute in a very long time, and although modern historians qualify that memory, the event clearly shocked contemporaries. Fifty years later, in 82 BCE, another man, Lucius Cornelius Sulla, entered Rome as the victor of a civil war between Roman armies and began posting lists of citizens whose lives and property were forfeit. The distance between these two moments is the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 18 ended with a society that had changed under the pressures of conquest: richer and more unequal, more dependent on enslaved labor, more mobile, and increasingly reliant on Italian allies who fought beside Roman citizens without sharing full political membership. That chapter deliberately stopped before the political crisis, and it warned against treating social change as a mechanical cause of collapse. Chapter 19 now asks how such pressures became political conflict, how conflict became institutional breakdown, and how breakdown eventually became armed violence among Romans. The central question is why political competition in the Republic became increasingly violent between the 130s and the 70s BCE, and how repeated attempts to solve problems of land, citizenship, military service, and elite competition changed the rules of republican politics.

The chapter resists a single explanation. A familiar chain is often taught: inequality produced the Gracchi, Marius created a professional army, soldiers became loyal to generals, Sulla marched on Rome, and the Republic was doomed. Every link in that chain has been questioned by modern scholarship, and the chain as a whole conceals the role of contingency. The chapter instead traces several interacting problems: the use of public land, wealth and economic change, Italian grievances, recruitment and prolonged warfare, elite rivalry, the powers of tribunes and assemblies, senatorial resistance, competition for extraordinary commands, and the expectations of soldiers. Its organizing theme is precedent. Each side justified its actions by appealing to earlier events, and each exceptional act enlarged the range of measures that later politicians thought available.

The chapter covers roughly 133 to 78 BCE, the years from the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus to the death of Sulla. Its main actors include the Gracchi brothers, senatorial leaders, Gaius Marius, Lucius Cornelius Sulla, Italian leaders such as those of the Marsi and Samnites, and the eastern king Mithridates VI of Pontus. It also includes many people who rarely appear as named individuals: citizens who voted for or against land bills, soldiers who served in Africa, Gaul, Italy, and Asia, Italian townspeople who sought recognition, and the victims of proscription and their families. Evidence for their experience is uneven and often hostile or later, and the chapter treats source criticism as part of its historical argument.

The chapter also connects several longer threads in the textbook. Chapter 15 described how republican institutions worked when they worked well. Chapter 16 showed how Rome built a differentiated system of Italian citizenship and alliance, and Chapter 17 how it acquired provincial empire. Chapter 18 showed how that empire changed Roman society. Here those threads meet: the Italian question that began in Chapter 16 finally produces war, the provinces supply both wealth and command opportunities, and the household and land structures described in Chapter 18 shape debates over settlement and service. The chapter ends with Sulla's dictatorship and death, which is the endpoint of this chapter rather than the end of the Republic. Chapter 20 examines the generation of Pompey, Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar, who inherited the world Sulla had made.

Finally, the chapter asks students to think about the erosion of norms. Political systems rest not only on written rules but on shared understandings about what may and may not be done. The Roman Republic had few written constitutional limits on tribunes, senators, or commanders, and it relied heavily on custom (mos maiorum), the authority of the Senate, and elite self-restraint. The story of this chapter is in large part the story of how that self-restraint wore down, not because Romans stopped claiming to defend the Republic, but because they disagreed sharply about what defending it required.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians disagree about when a "crisis" of the Republic began, and why collapse should not be treated as inevitable.
  2. Trace the land legislation of Tiberius Gracchus and the constitutional questions raised by the deposition of Octavius, the use of Attalus III's bequest, and the attempt at reelection.
  3. Compare the programs of Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus and explain why Gaius's reforms cannot be reduced to a repetition of his brother's.
  4. Analyze how the senatus consultum ultimum and the killing of citizens without ordinary trial created dangerous precedents.
  5. Assess which causal links between inequality and political crisis are supported by evidence and which remain debated.
  6. Explain the Jugurthine War, its African political setting, and its role in accusations of elite corruption and the rise of Marius.
  7. Evaluate the claim of a single "Marian reform," distinguishing recruitment practice, tactical organization, equipment, and soldier-commander relations.
  8. Analyze the Social War as the culmination of the Italian question and explain the paradox that Rome fought its allies while conceding much of what many demanded.
  9. Explain how the Mithridatic command dispute produced Sulla's first march on Rome.
  10. Interpret Sulla's civil war, proscriptions, and dictatorship, and assess whether he restored or remade the Republic.
  11. Evaluate the strengths and limits of Sallust, Plutarch, Appian, Cicero, inscriptions, and coins as evidence for this period.
  12. Connect the developments of 133 to 78 BCE to the political world of Pompey and Caesar without treating later events as predetermined.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did political competition in the Roman Republic become more violent between the 130s and 70s BCE, and why is a single cause unsatisfactory?
  2. Could a tribune use the authority of the popular assembly to overcome senatorial resistance, and what happened when opponents concluded that ordinary mechanisms were insufficient?
  3. How did precedent function in Roman politics, and how did each exceptional act change what later actors considered legitimate?
  4. What evidence supports and what evidence complicates the traditional story that conquest ruined the small farmer and thereby caused the Gracchan crisis?
  5. Was there a single "Marian reform," or did military recruitment, organization, and loyalty change gradually?
  6. Why did the unresolved Italian question become a war in 91 BCE, and why did Rome concede so much during and after it?
  7. Was Sulla restoring the Republic or remaking it through methods that contradicted the order he claimed to defend?
  8. How do the biases and dates of Sallust, Plutarch, Appian, and other sources shape our understanding of this period?

Historical Context

By the 130s BCE Rome had destroyed Carthage and Corinth (146 BCE), organized provinces in Africa and Macedonia, and made itself the dominant power of the Mediterranean. Its political system, examined in Chapter 15, rested on annually elected magistrates, a Senate composed mainly of former magistrates, and popular assemblies. There was no written constitution and no professional civil service. Authority rested on office, law, custom, and the prestige of families that had held high office. The tribunate of the plebs, an office with the power to propose legislation before the plebeian assembly and to veto the actions of other magistrates, was a powerful and flexible instrument. For generations it had been used mostly in cooperation with the Senate's leaders, though it had also been a channel for conflict.

The 130s were not a peaceful decade. In Spain, Roman commanders faced prolonged and humiliating warfare, culminating in the siege of Numantia, which Scipio Aemilianus captured in 133 BCE after an earlier commander, Mancinus, had negotiated a treaty in 137 that the Senate repudiated. In Sicily, a large revolt of enslaved people, mentioned in Chapter 18, began in 135 BCE and required consular armies and several years to suppress. These conflicts reminded Romans of the costs and risks of empire, and they shaped debates about military service and the security of slave-worked regions. In 133 BCE, too, King Attalus III of Pergamum died and, according to Rome's tradition, bequeathed his kingdom to the Roman people, raising the question of who would control the wealth and administration of a rich new province in western Anatolia.

Behind these events lay the social changes traced in Chapter 18. Wealth from conquest had increased the resources of an elite already dominant in politics. Italian allies contributed large forces, and many resented their lack of political voice. The use of public land (ager publicus), acquired through conquest and held by Rome, had long been contested. Wealthy citizens occupied large tracts of it, and the census and levy depended on maintaining a body of citizens with enough property to serve. Chapter 18 showed that the extent to which small farmers disappeared is debated, so the point here is not that a peasantry had been destroyed but that Romans, including reformers and their opponents, believed that land, manpower, and inequality were pressing problems, and that the state possessed land it might distribute.

The chapter's period is defined by these tensions but not determined by them. Historians who write about a "crisis of the Republic" stress how many Romans, at different times, sought different remedies, and how outcomes depended on decisions taken in particular moments, on personalities, and on chance. Students should keep in mind two errors: treating the Republic as flawless before 133 BCE, and treating its later collapse as the necessary consequence of earlier problems.

Evidence and Its Limits

The evidence for this period is both richer and more problematic than that for earlier chapters. Contemporary writing survives, but much of it is oratory and letters from later in the first century BCE, and the principal narratives were composed decades or centuries afterward by authors with their own purposes. Students should ask of every account who wrote it, when, and for what audience.

Narrative sources. Sallust, a senator and supporter of Caesar who wrote around the 40s and 30s BCE, composed a monograph on the Jugurthine War that is the main account of that conflict. It is vivid, moralizing, and hostile to the senatorial nobility, and it shapes the tradition of corruption. Plutarch, a Greek biographer writing around 100 CE, wrote lives of Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, Marius, and Sulla, drawing on earlier sources, some of which are lost. Appian, a Greek writing in the second century CE, gives a narrative of the Roman civil wars beginning with the Gracchi and includes the fullest surviving account of the Social War and Sulla's proscriptions. Velleius Paterculus, Livy's epitomes, and other later summaries also matter. All wrote long after the events, in the light of the fall of the Republic, and their narratives tend to read the Gracchi as the start of a downward path. Sulla himself wrote memoirs, now lost, and his opponents' accounts are also largely lost.

Contemporary and near contemporary voices. Cicero, who was born in 106 BCE, wrote speeches, treatises, and letters that discuss the Gracchi, Saturninus, Marius, and Sulla, often for rhetorical purposes and from an aristocratic point of view. Fragments of Gaius Gracchus's speeches survive in quotation. They are valuable but brief, and they were preserved by later authors for their style or their controversy.

Documents, inscriptions, and coins. A number of inscribed texts survive, including a large bronze fragment of a land law of 111 BCE, which regulates public land and shows how the Gracchan settlement was later reshaped, and fragments of the law on extortion (lex repetundarum) associated with the late second century. Boundary markers set up by the Gracchan land commission, bearing the names of its commissioners, survive in parts of Italy and are rare physical evidence of the program in operation. Coins are also important. During the Social War, rebel communities minted silver coins with Oscan legends naming Italia, and one design shows a bull goring the Roman wolf. Roman coin types advertised the achievements of families and commanders, and they provide evidence for competitive political display.

Archaeology and demography. Field surveys and excavations bear on the debates over land and settlement discussed in Chapter 18 and in Section 19.5. The census figures transmitted by later authors are crucial and contested, since it is unclear who was counted and how completely.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, the hostile and later tradition shaped the story of "reform" and "reaction," and words such as "conservative" or "radical" are modern impositions on a world without modern parties. Second, many events occurred in tense, confused circumstances, so that even basic details, including numbers killed, are uncertain. The chapter states these uncertainties where they matter.

Section 19.1

What Was the "Crisis" of the Republic?

The phrase "crisis of the Republic" invites two assumptions: that there was a coherent process leading to collapse, and that Romans experienced it as a crisis. Both need care. Historians usually take the period 133 to 78 BCE as the chapter's core because it begins with the first killing of a tribune in the political struggles of the era and ends with Sulla's death. But this is a convenience. Some historians would begin earlier, pointing to tensions in the 150s or to the wars in Spain, and others would question whether "crisis" is a helpful word at all, since the institutions of the Republic continued to function throughout, and since many of the actors thought they were defending or repairing it.

A historiographical problem: was there a structural crisis?

Older accounts, influenced by the ancient tradition, saw the Gracchi as the beginning of a revolutionary period in which a healthy Republic began to disintegrate. Some modern scholars emphasize structural causes: an empire too large for the institutions of a city-state, inequality, and the problem of citizens and allies. Others emphasize the resilience of the system, pointing out that Roman government continued to raise armies, administer provinces, and hold elections for decades after 133 BCE, and that the collapse came only after another century of political change. A third group emphasizes contingency, stressing particular decisions and accidents. The most defensible position for a textbook is a combined one. There were real structural pressures, but they became crisis through specific political choices, and no ancient or modern historian can show that the Republic was doomed at any given moment before its final civil wars.

Historical Vocabulary
Populares and Optimates

Roman writers, especially Cicero in the 50s BCE, used populares for politicians who appealed to the people and pursued measures through popular assemblies, often against senatorial resistance, and optimates for those who claimed to defend the authority of the Senate and the "best" citizens. The terms are useful for describing political style, rhetoric, and tactics.

They are misleading if taken as names of parties. Roman politics had no disciplined parties with platforms and membership. Both groups were drawn from the same aristocratic class, individuals moved between styles as circumstances required, and self-interest, family alliance, and personal rivalry were as important as principle. In this chapter the words appear only with these limits in mind.

The chapter does not use the Gracchi as the mere beginning of an inevitable story. It follows the sequence of events but asks at each stage what alternatives existed and what the actors could have expected. It also treats "precedent" as the key concept: not that violence was destined to grow, but that each exceptional act created a reference point that later actors could claim, extend, or condemn. Section 19.4 examines this most directly, and the Cause and Effect box in Section 19.12 returns to it.

Section 19.2

Land, Citizens, and Tiberius Gracchus

Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus belonged to one of Rome's most distinguished families. His father had been consul twice and censor, and his mother, Cornelia, was the daughter of Scipio Africanus. Tiberius had served in Africa under Scipio Aemilianus and in Spain, where he had helped rescue the army of Mancinus by negotiating the treaty that the Senate later repudiated. He was elected tribune for 133 BCE and proposed a land law.

The land question

Rome held large amounts of public land (ager publicus) acquired through conquest. Individuals had long occupied portions of it, paying little or nothing, and wealthy occupants held very large tracts. Tradition held that a law of the fourth century BCE had limited occupation to five hundred iugera per person, but that limit was not enforced, and the historicity of the early law is doubted. Tiberius's bill, as the sources describe it, proposed enforcing the limit, with some allowance for sons, and distributing the recovered land in small allotments to poorer citizens. Occupants who gave up land might in some versions receive secure title to what they kept. A commission of three men would carry out the survey and allotment, and the original commissioners were Tiberius himself, his younger brother Gaius, and his father in law, Appius Claudius Pulcher.

Several points need emphasis. The proposal concerned public land, not the confiscation of private property, though holders who had treated occupied land as their own regarded it as a threat, and the boundary between occupied public land and private holdings was often unclear. The bill also served more than one purpose. Distribution would provide livelihood for citizens who might otherwise be poor, and it would in principle expand the pool of citizens who met the property qualification for military service. As Chapter 18 explained, historians dispute how severe the shortage of smallholders was, so the bill should not be read as proving a demographic collapse. It shows instead that a significant group of Romans, including some senatorial leaders, considered land distribution a legitimate and urgent remedy for perceived problems, and that many landholders opposed it.

Primary Source Window

Plutarch, Life of Tiberius Gracchus, on Tiberius's Appeal to the Roman Poor, written c. 100 CE

Creator: Plutarch of Chaeronea, Greek biographer and philosopher writing under Roman rule · Date: around 100 CE, roughly two centuries after the events · Type: Biography paired with a Greek counterpart, drawing on earlier, mostly lost, Greek and Latin sources

Context: Plutarch composed paired Lives of Greek and Roman figures, and his account of the Gracchi is paired with lives of the Spartan kings Agis and Cleomenes, reformers whose fates he compares with those of the Gracchi. He used earlier sources, including works now lost, and he says that some details came from writings associated with Gaius Gracchus. He wrote for an educated Greek readership under the empire and had interests in moral character and in the fate of reformers.

Paraphrase, not quotation In a passage describing Tiberius's public speaking, Plutarch has him contrast wild animals in Italy, which each have a den or lair to retreat to, with the men who fight and die for Italy and who, he says, possess only air and light and wander with their wives and children without homes. Plutarch adds that those called masters of the world did not own a single clod of earth. This is a summary of the sense of the passage, and readers should consult a reliable translation for the exact wording.

Analysis: The passage makes a powerful image of the citizen soldier as a dispossessed defender of the state, and it has shaped much modern thinking about the Gracchan crisis. Students should treat it critically. It is unlikely to be a verbatim record of a speech from two centuries earlier, though Plutarch may have preserved echoes of an earlier tradition. It is a rhetorical picture that serves a moral and political argument, and it does not tell us how many citizens were in the situation it describes. Plutarch also notes elsewhere the traveling impression, attributed to Gaius, of the emptiness of parts of Etruria and the presence of foreign enslaved shepherds and farmers, an observation that fits the debate in Chapter 18 but cannot settle it. The source shows how a later tradition understood Tiberius's appeal and what image of the peasant it constructed. It cannot show the actual demographic and economic condition of Italian farmers, the arguments Tiberius's opponents made, or the views of the Italian allies who fought for Rome without citizenship.

Octavius, Attalus, and the constitutional question

The bill met resistance from landholders and their allies in the Senate, who persuaded another tribune, Marcus Octavius, to veto it. A tribune's veto was a recognized power, and the veto might normally have ended the matter, since tribunes were expected to negotiate and avoid deadlock. Tiberius responded by putting to the tribal assembly the question of whether Octavius should be removed from office, arguing that a tribune who opposed the people's will had forfeited his office. The assembly voted to depose him, and the law passed. There was no clear precedent for removing a sitting tribune. Tiberius's supporters could argue that tribunes served the people and could be recalled by them, while opponents argued that the action violated the sacrosanct status of the office and destroyed the collegiality that gave the veto meaning.

The controversy grew when King Attalus III of Pergamum died in 133 BCE and the terms of his will reached Rome. Tiberius proposed to use the bequeathed royal treasure to finance settlers' equipment and, it seems, to place the decision about the kingdom before the people. Traditionally the Senate controlled foreign policy and finance, and this proposal challenged that. The status of Attalus's bequest and what Tiberius actually proposed are reported in different ways by later authors, so students should note the uncertainty. What is clear is that Tiberius was widening the scope of popular and tribunician action in areas the Senate considered its own.

Reelection and killing

As the year ended, Tiberius appears to have feared prosecution once he left office and sought reelection as tribune, an unusual step. There was no clear written prohibition on consecutive tribunates, but custom was against it, and opponents portrayed the attempt as a bid for permanent power. On the day of the election, disorder erupted. Scipio Nasica, the chief priest (pontifex maximus) and a senator, called on those who wished to save the Republic to follow him, and he led a group of senators and supporters who attacked Tiberius and his followers with clubs and pieces of furniture. Tiberius and several hundred others, by tradition, were killed. The consul Publius Mucius Scaevola, according to the tradition, had refused to use force against Tiberius, and the Senate later supported Nasica's action, though he soon left for Asia, perhaps to avoid the anger of the people.

The major constitutional question is not simply whether Tiberius was right about land. It is whether a tribune could use the sovereign authority of the popular assembly to overcome senatorial resistance, and what would happen when opponents concluded that ordinary constitutional mechanisms were insufficient. Tiberius's supporters could argue that the Republic was a community of citizens whose assembly held ultimate authority. His opponents could argue that he was undermining the balance among magistrates, Senate, and people by bypassing a veto, threatening senatorial control of policy, and seeking personal prolongation. Both arguments appealed to legitimate strands in Roman political thought. The killing was not ordered by a magistrate through a legal procedure, and it was carried out by senators acting as private citizens against a magistrate whose person was sacrosanct. It was, however, defended, and it went largely unpunished.

The land commission nevertheless continued its work. Boundary markers survive, and the commission's activity is reflected in later legislation. Around 129 BCE, according to later accounts, the commission's judicial powers were transferred to the consuls, which reduced its effectiveness, and distributions seem to have slowed thereafter. The subsequent history of the land legislation, including a law of 111 BCE that appears to have converted many holdings to private property, shows how contested and changing the settlement was.

Section 19.3

Gaius Gracchus and a Broader Reform Program

Gaius Sempronius Gracchus, Tiberius's younger brother, had served on the land commission and in Sardinia as quaestor. He was elected tribune for 123 BCE, reelected for 122 BCE in what seems to have been a more accepted way, and passed a range of laws, many of which were aimed at building a coalition. He should not be presented as "Tiberius II." His program was broader, better organized, and more politically complicated than his brother's, and it addressed different groups.

Grain, roads, and the urban population

Gaius introduced a law providing for the sale of grain to citizens at a fixed price below the usual market price, with public granaries to store it. Ancient authors present this as a demagogic measure, and later chapters trace the long history of grain distribution in Rome. Its intent may have been to stabilize prices and provide security for urban citizens, and it offered a way of drawing the support of Rome's residents. Gaius also promoted road building and public works, which offered employment and connected Italy.

Colonies, provinces, and the equestrians

He revived the land program and proposed colonies, including one at the site of Carthage, called Junonia, an overseas colony for Roman citizens that was controversial and perhaps partly a pretext for later attacks. He also reshaped the provincial administration in ways that benefited men of wealth. A law concerning the province of Asia, the former territory of Attalus, provided for the auctioning of the right to collect its taxes in Rome, which increased opportunities for the contracting companies (publicani) that Chapter 18 described. He is also credited with a judicial measure transferring service on the extortion court (quaestio de repetundis), which tried provincial governors accused of extortion, from senators to men of equestrian standing. The details are debated, but the change gave wealthy non senators a stake in judging senatorial governors, and it made the courts a field of political conflict for decades. His measures thus created ties with businessmen, urban citizens, and rural settlers, and they affected the relations of the Senate to the provinces.

Soldiers and citizenship

Gaius is credited with a law that soldiers should be supplied with clothing at state expense without deductions from pay and that conscripts should not be enrolled below the age of seventeen. These measures, if accurately reported, suggest attention to the burdens of military service. He also supported extending citizenship to Latins and Latin rights to other Italians, working with his associate Marcus Fulvius Flaccus, a former consul. The proposal met strong resistance, and much of the citizen body, including the urban population, was reluctant to share privileges. The Senate countered by encouraging another tribune, Marcus Livius Drusus the elder, to propose even more generous measures to the people, and Gaius's popularity waned. He failed to win a third tribunate.

The Gaian program is important because it shows a wider set of solutions. It also shows that Italian claims were already a major political issue, a fact that becomes crucial in Section 19.9. Its combination of urban benefits, provincial administration, and judicial change created new constituencies, but it also created new enemies, including senators who resented his intrusion into areas they controlled and citizens who resisted sharing citizenship. It thereby exposed the weakness of relying on a single tribune's coalition to change long standing arrangements.

Section 19.4

From Political Conflict to Political Killing

Gaius's final months turned on the disputed colony at Carthage and the actions of Lucius Opimius, elected consul for 121 BCE and an opponent of the Gracchan program. When Opimius moved to repeal some of Gaius's laws, crowds gathered, and in a scuffle a follower of the consul was killed. The Senate then passed a decree, later called the senatus consultum ultimum, calling on the consuls to ensure that the state suffered no harm. Opimius treated it as authority to use force. He gathered armed supporters, including some Cretan archers, and Gaius and Fulvius Flaccus, who had taken up a position on the Aventine Hill, were pursued. Flaccus and his sons were killed, and Gaius died, according to the tradition, by having a slave kill him to avoid capture. Many of their supporters were killed or executed afterward, in numbers that are uncertain and vary widely in the sources.

Historical Vocabulary
Senatus Consultum Ultimum

The senatus consultum ultimum ("final decree of the Senate") is a modern name for a resolution in which the Senate urged magistrates to take whatever measures were needed to protect the state, using words to the effect that the consuls should see that the Republic suffered no harm. It first appears in 121 BCE and was used again on later occasions.

It was not a law and had no clear constitutional basis. Its supporters argued that it recognized a right of the community to defend itself against subversion. Its critics, including later Roman popular leaders, argued that it allowed the Senate to declare fellow citizens enemies and have them killed without trial, contrary to the citizen's right of appeal (provocatio). The debate over its legitimacy continued into Cicero's time and beyond.

What changed in 121 BCE? The killing of Tiberius had been the act of a mob led by a senator. In 121 BCE, a consul acted under a formal senatorial resolution against citizens who were denounced as threats to the state, and the killings were followed by executions carried out without normal trial. Opimius was later prosecuted, and he was acquitted, and the acquittal reinforced the argument that emergency measures could be justified. He built a temple of Concord in the Forum, which was said to have been defaced with a critical inscription, and the episode shows that contemporaries contested what had occurred.

The essential question is what happened when political opponents became enemies of the state. To call an opponent a threat to the Republic, and to authorize force, moved the boundary between political conflict and war. That could be defended as necessary in extreme circumstances, but once it was accepted it could be used again, and each use involved a claim about who was the real defender of the Republic. In Roman political culture, both sides invoked the safety of the Republic and the will of the people. The Gracchi episodes did not by themselves cause later civil wars, but they supplied vocabulary, precedents, and grievances that later actors used.

Section 19.5

Inequality, Reform, and the Limits of the Evidence

It is tempting to connect the Gracchan conflicts directly to the social changes of Chapter 18, and many ancient authors did so. This section asks which links are established and which remain debated.

What is well supported. Elite wealth increased, and estates using enslaved labor grew in some regions. The use of enslaved labor in Sicily led to a large revolt beginning in 135 BCE, and later another revolt in the 100s BCE, which showed the risks of concentrated slave labor. Urban Rome grew, and questions of food supply were prominent. Italian allies bore heavy military obligations without political voice. Public land was contested, and Romans, including some prominent senators, thought redistribution a serious option. The Gracchan commission's boundary stones show that land was in fact surveyed and assigned.

What remains debated. The debate discussed in Chapter 18 about the extent of peasant displacement remains unsettled, and the Gracchan evidence does not settle it. The ancient claim that Italy was being emptied of free farmers and filled with foreign enslaved workers appears in Plutarch and Appian, writing much later and interpreting earlier tradition. Census figures show a citizen population that did not decline steadily. The fact that Tiberius could gather a large following does not prove that it consisted of displaced farmers rather than urban residents, veterans, or others, and the concern of the elite about recruitment did not necessarily reflect a real shortage. Whether the land law was intended primarily to help the poor, to secure military manpower, or to win political advantage for Tiberius and his allies is also disputed, and probably all elements mattered.

Why inequality alone is not enough. Inequality is a real background condition, but economic inequality does not by itself produce political violence. Many societies with great inequality do not experience civil war. What mattered in Rome was how inequality interacted with institutional features: a governing class competing for a limited number of offices, a Senate resisting change, assemblies that could pass laws, tribunes who could initiate legislation, Italians excluded from politics, and the absence of any body to mediate between senatorial and popular claims. It also mattered that both reformers and opponents treated the moment as critical and used the tools available. The Gracchi were not modern democrats or socialists, and their opponents did not form a single conservative party. They were members of an aristocracy who disagreed about how to solve problems and whose disagreement turned violent.

Section 19.6

Jugurtha, Corruption, and the Rise of Marius

The war against Jugurtha, king of Numidia, is often treated as an interlude before Marius. It deserves its own place, because it was an African political conflict as well as a Roman one, and because it shaped Roman attitudes toward aristocratic leadership.

Numidia and the Roman intervention

Numidia was a kingdom of North Africa west of the Roman province of Africa, ruled since Rome's victory over Carthage by Masinissa's dynasty, which had been an ally of Rome. Masinissa's grandson Jugurtha had served with Roman forces at Numantia and enjoyed connections with Roman senators. After King Micipsa died in 118 BCE, his sons Adherbal and Hiempsal and Jugurtha, the adopted successor, contested the kingdom. Jugurtha had Hiempsal killed and drove Adherbal to Rome, and a Senate commission divided the kingdom, with accusations that Jugurtha had bribed Romans. In 112 BCE Jugurtha captured Cirta, where he killed Adherbal and a number of Italian traders who had helped defend it, and the killing of Italians in Africa made the matter politically urgent in Rome. War was declared in 111 BCE.

Command failures and accusations of corruption

The war went badly. Roman commanders made settlements that appeared too favorable to Jugurtha, and Jugurtha himself came to Rome under a safe conduct, where he allegedly bribed officials, and where a rival, Massiva, was murdered. A Roman army was humiliated in 110 BCE. Sallust, our main source, tells this as a story of noble greed, in which Jugurtha claims that Rome was a city for sale. Students should treat that as a literary and political theme as much as a record of facts, since Sallust's presentation is shaped by his own political outlook. Yet the sequence of commands, settlements, and inquiries, including special courts established to investigate senators, shows that many Romans were persuaded that aristocratic leaders had failed and were possibly corrupt.

Marius

Quintus Caecilius Metellus took command in 109 BCE and campaigned with more success, and he brought as a legate Gaius Marius. Marius came from Arpinum, a town in Latium that had received Roman citizenship, and from a family of local standing rather than senatorial ancestry. He was a novus homo, as Cato had been, and he had built a career through military service, a tribunate, and a praetorship. He sought the consulship for 107 BCE, criticizing the nobility's conduct of the war, and was elected. The people, through a vote in the assembly, gave him the African command, in place of Metellus, though the Senate had assigned it otherwise. This was an early example of the assembly assigning a military command against senatorial preference.

Marius ended the war in 105 BCE, though not by a decisive battle. His quaestor, Lucius Cornelius Sulla, negotiated with Bocchus, king of Mauretania and Jugurtha's father in law, who handed Jugurtha over. Marius and Sulla both claimed credit, a rivalry that later became important. Numidia's future was reorganized, and Jugurtha was paraded in Marius's triumph and died in prison. For Numidians, the war involved destruction, shifting alliances, and Roman influence over the succession, and Numidian rulers continued to be enmeshed in Roman politics. The African setting therefore provided not just a backdrop but a political system of its own, in which local rulers pursued their interests and manipulated Roman politics.

Section 19.7

Marius and Military Transformation: Reform or Long Process?

At the same time as the Jugurthine War, Rome faced a more terrifying threat in the north. Migrating groups called the Cimbri, Teutones, and their allies moved through Gaul and inflicted a series of defeats on Roman armies, including a disaster at Arausio (Orange) in 105 BCE, in which two commanders, one a proconsul and the other a consul, failed to cooperate and their armies were destroyed. Romans feared an invasion of Italy. The people elected Marius consul in his absence for 104 BCE, and he held the consulship in each year to 100 BCE, an unprecedented run of five in succession. He defeated the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae in 102 BCE and, with his colleague Quintus Lutatius Catulus, the Cimbri at Vercellae in 101 BCE.

The myth of the "Marian reforms"

Many textbooks state that Marius, in 107 BCE, abolished the property requirement, created a professional army of landless volunteers, reorganized the legion into cohorts, introduced the eagle standard, standardized equipment, and made soldiers loyal to their generals rather than to the state. That package, often called the "Marian reforms," is largely a modern construction, and it should be dismantled into its parts, which happened at different times.

  • Recruitment. Sallust reports that Marius enrolled men from the poorest census class (capite censi) in his African army. That was notable, but the property qualification for service had been declining across the second century, and poorer men had already served in some contexts. Marius's action was a striking use of a trend, and it is not clear that it created a permanent change in law. Recruitment of volunteers, including poorer men, continued in practice.
  • Tactical organization. The cohort, a unit larger than the old maniple, became increasingly important, and cohorts appear in earlier campaigns, notably in Spain. Its adoption as the main tactical unit was gradual, and it cannot be assigned to a single Marian decree.
  • Equipment and training. Equipment and drill changed over time. Ancient tradition credits Marius with improvements in baggage handling and training, and other commanders, such as Publius Rutilius Rufus, are associated with training reforms. These belong to a broader process.
  • The eagle. Pliny the Elder, writing later, says Marius made the eagle the principal standard of the legion in 104 BCE. That is a claim about one standard, not a wholesale army reform.
  • Loyalty. Soldiers did not suddenly transfer political allegiance from the Republic to individual commanders in 107 BCE. What changed over decades was that long service abroad, dependence on commanders for rewards, and the failure of the state to provide veterans with land systematically strengthened ties between soldiers and commanders.

Marius nevertheless mattered greatly. His repeated commands and consulships showed that the Senate and people could give extraordinary powers in an emergency. His recruitment practice broadened the pool of soldiers, and his victories made him enormously prestigious. His veterans expected settlement, and that expectation, together with the state's lack of a regular mechanism to provide it, drove political conflict. But these changes came about through political circumstances and long processes, not through a single reform.

Historical Debate

Did Marius Transform the Roman Army?

The question: Did Gaius Marius create a new kind of army, professional, landless, and loyal to commanders, that made later civil war possible? Or is the "Marian reform" a simplification of longer processes?

Interpretation A: A decisive Marian revolution: Influential accounts, especially in the twentieth century, held that Marius's recruitment of the poor in 107 BCE ended the old citizen militia, created a professional army whose soldiers depended on generals for pay, plunder, and land, and thus produced armies loyal to individuals who could attack the state. They relied on Sallust's remark, later summaries, and the later behavior of armies under Sulla, Pompey, and Caesar.
Interpretation B: Gradual, earlier, later, or poorly attested changes: Revisionist scholars point out that the property qualification had been reduced before Marius, that many proposed reforms cannot be dated to him, that some are attested only in later sources, that armies under Marius's contemporaries were still composed of mixed elements, and that Roman soldiers continued to see themselves as citizens and to obey the state on many occasions. They emphasize evolution over several generations and the crucial role of political circumstances.

Synthesis: Many historians now hold that Marius mattered greatly, especially through recruitment practice, repeated commands, veteran expectations, and political circumstances, but that there was no single documented "Marian reform package." The later dependence of soldiers on commanders arose from a combination of long campaigns, the absence of state provision for veterans, and the prizes of civil conflict.

Evidence: The debate depends on Sallust's statement about enrolling the poorest citizens, Pliny's remark about the eagle, scattered references to cohorts in earlier campaigns, changes in equipment visible archaeologically, and the later behavior of armies. Each is fragmentary, later, or ambiguous.

Why it matters: If a single reform created the army of civil war, the fall of the Republic can be read as a mechanical consequence of a military decision. If change was gradual and politically mediated, then political choices, including the failure to settle veterans and the pursuit of commands, bear more weight.

Student task: Identify which claims about Marius are supported by strong evidence and which rest on later tradition, and explain what other evidence would be needed to decide whether a discrete reform occurred.

Everyday Life and Social History

Military Service during Decades of Campaigning

For a soldier of the later second and early first centuries BCE, service meant months or years away from home, in Spain, Africa, Gaul, or the East. Pay was modest, and it was supplemented by a share of booty when campaigns succeeded. Soldiers marched with heavy equipment and, in Marius's time, according to the tradition, were sometimes nicknamed "Marius's mules" for the loads they carried. They ate mainly grain, prepared in camp, with sour wine, and they built fortified camps each night. Discipline was harsh and could include flogging and, for serious offenses, execution.

Absence affected families. Wives and relatives managed farms or shops, and a soldier's death or disability could threaten a household. Some soldiers returned and resumed civilian life, and others hoped for land or a settlement. Because the state did not systematically provide land to discharged soldiers, they looked to commanders and to politicians who could propose colonies. Allied Italian soldiers experienced service differently, as noncitizens sharing the danger and often receiving less reward. Enslaved people and camp followers also accompanied armies, and evidence about their experience is scarce. Historians reconstruct these experiences from literary accounts, inscriptions, and archaeological remains, and they should remember that most soldiers left no written record.

Section 19.8

Veterans, Popular Politics, and Saturninus

After his victories, Marius stood at the height of prestige, but it soon became clear that military success did not translate easily into political control. The problem of veterans was one reason. His African veterans needed land, and his Italian allied soldiers who had served under him were not necessarily citizens who could receive it through ordinary channels. He allied with Lucius Appuleius Saturninus, a tribune in 103 BCE, who proposed land for Marius's veterans in Africa, and later with Saturninus and Gaius Servilius Glaucia, a praetor.

In 100 BCE, when Marius was consul for the sixth time, Saturninus was again tribune and proposed further legislation on settlement in Gaul, Sicily, Achaea, and Macedonia, a grain measure, and other matters. The law included a requirement that senators swear an oath to uphold it, and Marius's rival Metellus Numidicus refused and went into exile. The legislation was passed amid intimidation and violence in the assemblies. The final blow came when Saturninus and Glaucia had a rival candidate for the consulship, Gaius Memmius, murdered, and the Senate issued the emergency decree. Marius, as consul, took the side of the Senate, armed citizens, and besieged Saturninus's supporters on the Capitol. They surrendered on assurances of a trial, but a crowd killed them, according to the tradition by throwing tiles from the roof of the Senate house where they were held.

The episode shows several things. First, a successful general could not simply command politics through military prestige. Marius had to balance loyalty to allies, his position as consul, and the demands of the Senate, and he lost credibility with both sides. Second, emergency decree and popular violence were now recurrent parts of Roman politics, and second use of the decree, this time with a famous general's help, strengthened its status. Third, the land problem for veterans remained unsettled, a point that later commanders and legislators would confront. Marius's political career declined, and he traveled to the East, and returned to prominence only when the wars of the 80s gave him another opportunity.

A second Sicilian slave revolt, which lasted from around 104 to 100 BCE, was suppressed at about the same time, and it reminds us that the Republic's military and political leaders faced a number of pressures at once. It should not be treated as a mere footnote, since it shows the persistence of the vulnerability of slave-worked regions, and the continuing demands on Roman military resources.

Section 19.9

Italians, Citizenship, and the Social War

The Social War (from socii, allies), also called the Italian or Marsic War, was the culmination of a story that began in Chapter 16 with the creation of differentiated Italian status, and continued in Chapter 18, which showed that Italians fought for Rome without sharing full political membership. Its causes were long term. The allies contributed large numbers of troops and bore heavy burdens, and they were often treated harshly by Roman magistrates. Roman citizenship offered legal, political, and social advantages, and by the late second century, it was increasingly attractive. Attempts to extend it, including those associated with Gaius Gracchus and Flaccus, had failed, and in 125 BCE a proposal by Flaccus to extend citizenship or the right of appeal was opposed, and the Latin colony of Fregellae, which revolted, was destroyed. The Italian question therefore remained open.

Drusus and the outbreak of war

In 91 BCE the tribune Marcus Livius Drusus, son of the tribune of 122 BCE, put forward a broad program that combined judicial reform, land and grain measures, and, it seems, citizenship for the Italians. The program aimed to reconcile senators, equestrians, and the people, and it fell apart. The Senate annulled his laws, and Drusus was murdered before the end of the year. Italian leaders, who had counted on his proposals, decided on revolt. The war began in Asculum, where Roman officials were killed, and spread through central and southern Italy.

The rebellion

The Italian rebels, notably the Marsi, Paeligni, Marrucini, Vestini, Picentes, Samnites, and others, formed a confederation with its capital at Corfinium, which they renamed Italica. The confederation issued coins, appointed magistrates, and organized armies with structures that resembled Rome's. Its leaders included Quintus Poppaedius Silo of the Marsi and Gaius Papius Mutilus of the Samnites. It is important to see the rebellion as an alliance of communities with different histories and motives, not a single movement. Some wanted citizenship, some may have wanted independence or a reorganization of relations with Rome, and others remained loyal. Latin colonies, most Etruscans and Umbrians, and many Greek cities of the south did not join, at least at first.

The war was hard and costly. Roman commanders included Marius, Sulla, and Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo, father of Pompey, and Rome suffered serious defeats early. Ancient sources give large casualty figures, and the war involved great destruction in Italian communities, but exact numbers are uncertain.

The paradox of concession

The most striking feature of the war is that Rome fought its allies while conceding much of what many of them demanded. In 90 BCE a law, the lex Julia, offered citizenship to communities that had remained loyal or laid down their arms, and this fragmented the rebellion by separating those who wanted citizenship from those who fought on. In 89 BCE further laws, associated with Plautius and Papirius and with Pompeius Strabo, extended citizenship to individuals and granted Latin rights to communities north of the Po. By about 88 BCE, most of the war was over, though the Samnites and Lucanians continued fighting into the later 80s. The outcome was that Roman citizenship theoretically embraced most free Italians south of the Po, and Latin rights were extended to the communities of the north.

The result transformed the Republic. Citizenship was no longer the possession of a limited central Italian group, and the citizen body enlarged. The census of 70 to 69 BCE recorded about 900,000 adult male citizens, in contrast with around 395,000 in 125 BCE. That increase reflects the enfranchisement of many Italians, though the counting method may also have changed, and the figures should be treated with caution. But the integration of the new citizens was not simple. How they would be enrolled in the tribes, whether in a limited number or across all of them, mattered because it determined their voting influence, and the issue remained contentious. They also had to be included in the census, the levy, and elections. Roman politicians disagreed about how to do this, and that dispute shaped the events of 88 BCE.

Section 19.10

Mithridates and the Struggle for Command

While Rome was absorbed in the Social War, a crisis developed in the East. Mithridates VI Eupator, king of Pontus on the southern coast of the Black Sea, had built a large kingdom and expanded into Anatolia and the Black Sea region, and by the late 90s and 80s BCE he came into conflict with Rome's allies and its province of Asia. In 88 BCE he overran much of Asia and, according to ancient tradition, ordered the killing of Romans and Italians in the province. Ancient sources give a figure of eighty thousand victims, but that figure is uncertain, and the extent of the killing and the participation of local communities are debated. Many Greek cities, including Athens, joined or supported Mithridates, in some cases because they resented Roman exactions, and in some cases under pressure.

The eastern command promised glory, plunder, and control of a wealthy region. The Senate assigned it to Sulla, one of the consuls of 88 BCE, who had distinguished himself in the Social War and who was to lead an army against Mithridates. Sulla was a patrician from a family that had declined in wealth and prominence, and he had risen through the Jugurthine war and the Social War, in which he became a rival of Marius, who wanted the command for himself.

The dispute was entangled with the citizenship question. Publius Sulpicius Rufus, a tribune of 88 BCE, proposed to distribute the new citizens and freedpeople across all the tribes, which would increase their voting weight, and allied himself with Marius. He also proposed the recall of exiles and, as Sulpicius's ally, the transfer of the eastern command from Sulla to Marius. Violence occurred in the assemblies. Sulla, who was consul, fled the city, and Sulpicius's legislation passed, including the transfer of the command to Marius, who sent officers to take over Sulla's army at Nola.

The command was thus a prize in a struggle among ambitious men and interest groups, and its transfer by a popular vote against the Senate's decision showed how political and military power had become intertwined. Sulla's response, examined in the next section, turned this dispute into a constitutional rupture.

Section 19.11

Sulla, Civil War, Proscriptions, and Dictatorship

The first march on Rome

Sulla went to his army at Nola, in Campania, where the soldiers, told that they would be transferred to Marius's command and might lose the prospect of eastern plunder, supported him. He marched on Rome with six legions, and, according to the tradition, all his senior officers except one refused to follow him. The soldiers were Roman citizens, but their commander led them into Rome in arms, which had never before been done under a Roman magistrate. Sulla took the city, and Marius and Sulpicius fled. Sulpicius was killed, Marius escaped to Africa after adventures, and Sulla had Marius and others declared public enemies (hostes) by senatorial vote. He passed laws to strengthen the Senate's control over legislation, and then departed in 87 BCE for the East, having left the city under the consuls Gnaeus Octavius and Lucius Cornelius Cinna.

Sulla in the East and Cinna in Rome

Sulla waged a hard campaign in Greece against Mithridates's forces and their allies, besieging Athens and its port Piraeus, and taking the city in 86 BCE with much destruction, and defeating Mithridates's armies at Chaeronea and Orchomenus. He funded the war partly with the treasures of Greek temples. In 85 BCE he made a settlement with Mithridates at Dardanus, which left the king in his kingdom and imposed an indemnity, because Sulla wished to return to Italy.

Meanwhile, in Rome, Cinna, who as consul tried to reintroduce the distribution of the new citizens through the tribes, was driven from the city by his colleague Octavius, and the Senate declared him deposed. Cinna gathered troops among Italian communities and was joined by Marius, who returned from Africa with a force. In 87 BCE they took Rome, and for several days their followers carried out killings of their opponents, including Octavius, and Marius's unrestrained supporters, including a band of slaves who had joined him, took part in the violence, according to ancient authors. Marius and Cinna proclaimed themselves consuls for 86 BCE, and Marius died in January 86 BCE in his seventh consulship. Cinna held the consulship for several years, and the government sought to rally Italians and to regulate the new citizens' enrollment, while also preparing to meet Sulla. Cinna was killed by mutinous troops in 84 BCE as he prepared to sail against Sulla. The regime of these years is usually described by hostile later sources, and Sulla's victory shaped the memory of them.

Civil war

Sulla landed at Brundisium in 83 BCE with his army and offered assurances that he would respect the new citizens, and he attracted allies, including Quintus Caecilius Metellus Pius, and the young Gnaeus Pompeius (Pompey), who raised a private force in Picenum, and Marcus Licinius Crassus. Several opposing armies dissolved or defected. In 82 BCE Gaius Marius the Younger held the consulship, and after his defeat at Sacriportus he was besieged at Praeneste. Sulla's forces fought Samnites and Lucanians, who supported the Marian government or fought their own war against Rome. The decisive battle was at the Colline Gate, at Rome, on 1 November 82 BCE, against an army under the Samnite leader Pontius Telesinus, and it was a hard fight in which Sulla's army came close to defeat. Sulla was victorious, and the Samnite prisoners were killed, according to the tradition.

Historical Vocabulary
Proscription

Proscription (proscriptio) was the public posting of lists of names of persons declared outlaws, whom anyone could kill with reward, and whose property was confiscated and sold. Sulla's lists, beginning in late 82 BCE, were the first of their kind in Roman history, and they were later imitated in the triumvirate of 43 BCE, discussed in Chapter 20.

The measure turned political victory into legal killing and property transfer. It affected senators, equestrians, and others, and it also affected their descendants, who were barred from office. It also created a group of beneficiaries, including those who bought confiscated property. Ancient figures for the number of victims vary widely, and they should be treated as uncertain.

Proscription and confiscation

After the victory, Sulla published lists of the proscribed, and killings continued in Rome and across Italy. The number of victims is uncertain, and ancient sources vary, including references to many senators and equestrians and many hundreds or more names in the lists, with additional killings by personal enemies. Property was auctioned, and it enriched Sulla's supporters. Communities that had opposed Sulla suffered confiscations and loss of status, and Sulla settled many of his veterans, whom ancient authors number in the tens of thousands, in colonies, especially on confiscated land in Italy. Modern historians note that these settlements produced tension and unsettled local property, and some appear to have been poorly established. The proscription was also a social and economic event, in which political enemies, local rivals, and the ambitious used the lists.

Dictatorship and constitutional program

In 82 BCE a law proposed by the interrex Lucius Valerius Flaccus made Sulla dictator for writing laws and establishing the state (dictator legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae causa), with no fixed term. The dictatorship was an old republican magistracy, used in emergencies for limited periods, and it had not been held for more than a century. What was unprecedented was the revival of the office with no time limit and with constituent powers, and the use of it to remake the state.

Sulla's legislation was extensive. He enlarged the Senate, apparently to about six hundred, by adding members from the equestrian order, and he increased the number of quaestors to twenty, who would enter the Senate automatically on election. He increased the number of praetors and reorganized the courts, giving juries again to senators, and creating or reorganizing standing courts for different crimes. He sought to regularize the sequence of offices (the cursus honorum) by setting minimum ages and an interval before reelection to the same office. Many details of the age limits and their date are reconstructed and debated. He sharply curtailed the tribunate, requiring that legislation be approved beforehand by the Senate, limiting the power of the veto, and barring former tribunes from other offices. He also regulated provincial commands, distinguishing the year in office from the year as governor of a province, with the aim of reducing the risk of armed commanders. Some of his measures have been recognized as enduring, and others were soon challenged.

In about 81 BCE, Sulla resigned the dictatorship, held the consulship again for 80 BCE, and then retired to his estates. He died in 78 BCE and was given a large public funeral.

The central question is whether Sulla was restoring the Republic or remaking it through methods that contradicted the republican order he claimed to defend. The contradiction is evident. He presented his measures as reestablishing the authority of the Senate and the rule of law after the disorder of the Marian regime and the abuses of tribunes. His reforms did strengthen senatorial control and attempt to regularize careers. But he achieved this through a civil war, through killing and property confiscation, through a dictatorship that suspended normal limits, and through the use of a personal army. The order he restored depended on the power he had used to create it, and it provided a model of what a victorious commander could do. It would be a mistake to answer the question with a slogan. Sulla was both a reformer who thought the Republic needed strengthening and a warlord whose methods shattered its norms.

Section 19.12

What Had Changed by 78 BCE?

Between 133 and 78 BCE the Roman Republic did not simply collapse. Its institutions continued to function, elections continued, citizens continued to vote, and politicians continued to claim republican legitimacy. What changed was the range of methods considered available in political competition. Tribunes had removed colleagues, senators had sanctioned lethal force against citizens, armies had marched on Rome, competing governments had killed opponents, victors had confiscated property, and Sulla had used military victory to rewrite the constitutional order.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What Remained Republican after Sulla?

What Changed

  • Killing of political opponents, first without trial and then by proscription, became a known tool
  • An army under a Roman commander had marched on Rome and fought for control of the state
  • Extraordinary commands and repeated consulships had been given, and the assembly had assigned commands against senatorial wishes
  • Emergency decrees authorizing force against citizens had been used and defended
  • The citizen body was much larger, including most free Italians south of the Po, with unresolved questions of integration
  • The tribunate, the courts, the Senate, and the sequence of offices were reshaped by Sulla's legislation

What Remained Continuous

  • The Senate, annual magistracies, and popular assemblies continued to operate, and elections were held
  • Politicians continued to appeal to republican language, law, and the safety of the state
  • Aristocratic competition, family prestige, and patronage still shaped politics
  • The household, slavery, land, and provincial empire described in Chapters 17 and 18 continued to structure society
  • The office of tribune and popular politics survived, though restricted, and were soon challenged

Who experienced change most directly? Victims of political violence and proscription, Italian communities, soldiers and veterans, and provincial inhabitants dragged into civil and eastern wars experienced the greatest change. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural households and much of the ordinary routine of Roman law and government continued. Why the pattern matters: It shows a system whose forms endured while its norms were altered, which is how many political orders erode.

Cause and Effect

How Political Violence Became Reproducible

Long-term causes

A system without written constitutional limits, dependent on custom and elite restraint, with intense aristocratic competition, an empire that placed enormous resources in commanders' hands, and unresolved questions of land, Italian status, and veteran settlement.

Short-term causes

Specific contests: the land law and the removal of Octavius, the clash over Gaius's laws and Carthage, the threats of the Cimbri, the failure of Drusus's program, and the dispute over the Mithridatic command.

Event or transformation

Killing of tribunes, emergency decrees, armies marching on Rome, rival governments, proscription, and dictatorship, each justified by earlier acts.

Immediate effects

The loss of life, property confiscations, redistributions of land to veterans, the Senate's changed structure, and constraints on the tribunate.

Long-term consequences

A political world in which force and armies were part of competition, and in which later figures could point to precedent for their own claims, as examined in Chapter 20.

Historical complexity

At every stage, actors had alternatives, and many Romans opposed escalation. Contingency, individual choices, resources, and institutional weaknesses interacted. The sequence is not a staircase to inevitable collapse.

Historical complexity: The claim that precedent mattered does not mean that violence was destined. It means that once certain acts had happened and been defended, they were available to later actors as models, and it became harder to argue that they were unthinkable.

None of these precedents made Caesar inevitable, but they profoundly altered the political world in which Pompey, Crassus, Cicero, and Caesar would operate. Sulla's arrangements were challenged almost at once. The tribunate, the courts, and the question of Italian integration remained contested, and commanders with armies and veterans still needed rewards. Chapter 20 examines how the post Sullan generation failed to contain competition among Pompey, Caesar, and their rivals, and how that failure ended the republican settlement.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how political competition in the Roman Republic became increasingly violent between the 130s and 70s BCE, and how repeated attempts to solve problems of land, citizenship, military service, and elite competition changed the rules of republican politics. Its central argument is that the Republic did not simply collapse. It continued to function, but the range of methods that politicians considered legitimate widened as each exceptional act became a precedent.

The chapter began by defining the problem. The "crisis" is a convenient label for 133 to 78 BCE, and historians debate whether it reflects structural weaknesses, the resilience of the system, or a series of contingent choices. The chapter adopted a combined view: real pressures existed, but they became crisis through specific decisions. It also cautioned against treating populares and optimates as parties.

Tiberius Gracchus's tribunate in 133 BCE raised the central constitutional question of whether a tribune could use the assembly's authority to overcome senatorial resistance. His land law concerned public land, not private confiscation, and it was accompanied by the deposition of Octavius, the use of Attalus III's bequest, and an attempt at reelection. His killing by senators and supporters was a major precedent. Plutarch's later account of his appeal was examined as source criticism, not as proof of peasant displacement.

Gaius Gracchus's broader program included grain sales, colonies, provincial tax arrangements, judicial changes affecting the equestrians, soldier welfare, and proposals on citizenship. His fall in 121 BCE involved the first recorded senatus consultum ultimum and the killing and execution of many supporters, which created a dangerous precedent for emergency measures against citizens. The chapter then reconsidered the relationship between inequality and crisis, distinguishing what is supported from what remains debated and emphasizing that inequality alone did not produce political violence.

The Jugurthine War, understood in its African setting, produced accusations of elite corruption and gave Marius, a novus homo, a command. His repeated consulships against the Cimbri and Teutones concentrated prestige and military power, but the chapter dismantled the myth of a single "Marian reform," separating recruitment, cohorts, equipment, the eagle, and soldier loyalty, and showing that change was gradual and political. The Historical Debate presented older and revisionist views and a synthesis.

Marius's difficulties after his victories, and the violence around Saturninus and Glaucia in 100 BCE, showed that military prestige did not automatically command politics and that veterans needed land the state did not systematically provide. The Social War then brought the long Italian question to a head. Rome fought its allies while conceding much of what many demanded, fragmenting the revolt through citizenship laws and enlarging the citizen body, though integration remained contentious.

The struggle over the command against Mithridates, which involved the citizenship question, the transfer of the command to Marius, and Sulla's first march on Rome, marked a rupture in which a Roman army entered Rome under a consul. The years 87 to 82 BCE saw Cinna and Marius seize the city, Sulla's return, and a civil war culminating at the Colline Gate. Proscriptions and confiscations formalized killing and property transfer, and Sulla's dictatorship remade the Senate, courts, magistracies, and tribunate.

The chapter asked whether Sulla restored or remade the Republic and answered that he did both, in ways that contradicted his own claims. By 78 BCE the Republic's forms persisted, but tribunes had removed colleagues, senators had authorized lethal force, armies had marched on Rome, governments had killed opponents, and Sulla had used victory to rewrite the constitution. These changes did not make later civil wars inevitable, but they altered the world in which Pompey, Caesar, and their contemporaries acted, as Chapter 20 will show.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can be transformed while its forms remain. The Roman Republic of 78 BCE still had consuls, a Senate, assemblies, and courts, and its leaders still spoke of law and the safety of the state. Yet the ways in which power was contested had changed profoundly. The chapter's lesson is not that decline was inevitable, but that norms wear away through a series of decisions, each defended by reference to the last, and that once wearing has occurred, the space of the possible is different.

The chapter also gathers the threads of Part III. The Italian question that began with differentiated citizenship in Chapter 16 and the social pressures of Chapter 18 reached war and reform in the Social War. The empire that Chapter 17 described supplied both wealth and commands that made competition among aristocrats more dangerous. The arguments over land, service, and citizenship that Chapter 18 traced became the substance of political conflict. For the argument of Volume I, the chapter shows that Rome's success in the Mediterranean generated internal problems that its institutions found difficult to handle, and it prepares the ground for the transformation of the Republic into the empire.

Later developments were shaped by this period in several ways. Proscription, emergency decrees, the march on Rome, and the use of veterans as a political force would recur in the generation of Pompey and Caesar, examined in Chapter 20, and the Augustan settlement of Chapter 21 would be built in part in response to the disorder Sulla's world had produced. The enlarged citizen body, which included most free Italians south of the Po, would become the basis for later Roman citizenship and for the integration of Italy that Chapter 22 examines. Yet none of this was determined in 78 BCE.

Finally, the chapter models a method. It asks students to treat later narratives with suspicion, to ask what evidence supports a familiar story such as the Marian reforms, to distinguish structural background from contingent choices, and to notice how actors used the past to justify the present. Those habits are valuable for the study of any political crisis, and they will be needed again as the textbook turns from the collapse of the republican settlement to the creation of the Principate.
