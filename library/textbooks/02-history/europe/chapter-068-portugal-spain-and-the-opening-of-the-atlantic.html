Portugal, Spain, and the Opening of the Atlantic

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

The Atlantic was never an empty ocean waiting to be found. It was a coast of fishing communities, a margin of the trans-Saharan gold trade, a home to island peoples, and, for those who learned its winds, a road. Between the 1410s and the first years of the sixteenth century, Portuguese and Castilian mariners, backed by crowns, financiers, and local rulers, turned that road into a durable connection between Europe, Africa, the Americas, and the Indian Ocean, and they did so through coercion and collaboration in uneven measure.

On 8 August 1444, in a field outside the Algarve port of Lagos, a crowd gathered to watch the division and sale of 235 people brought from the West African coast by a small fleet of caravels. The fullest account was written roughly a decade later by the royal chronicler Gomes Eanes de Zurara, who described parents separated from children and captives in visible distress, and who noted that Prince Henry, whose licenses had authorized the expedition, rode out to receive his customary fifth. Zurara's narrative is a source of the first importance, and also a deliberately shaped one. It presents the voyages as a Christian and chivalric enterprise, yet the human cost shows through his own prose. The scene at Lagos belongs at the beginning of this chapter because it displays, in a single morning, several of the forces that made Atlantic expansion possible: royal and noble patronage, maritime skill, religious justification, commercial calculation, and violence against people who had not chosen to take part.

Forty-eight years later, on 3 August 1492, three vessels left Palos de la Frontera on the Andalusian coast under the command of the Genoese captain Christopher Columbus, whose crews were drawn largely from the seafaring communities of Palos, Moguer, and Huelva. Their voyage, sponsored by the Castilian crown only months after the conquest of Granada, ended with landfall in the Bahamas on 12 October (Julian calendar) and began a sustained European connection with the Americas. Within six more years a Portuguese fleet under Vasco da Gama would reach Calicut on the Malabar coast of India by way of the South Atlantic and the Cape of Good Hope. Between Lagos and Calicut lay a transformation in what Europeans could reach, what they knew, and what they claimed.

This chapter argues that the opening of the Atlantic was a cumulative and contested historical process rather than a sudden technological breakthrough or a sequence of heroic discoveries. It drew on Mediterranean chart making, Islamic and Jewish astronomical learning, Iberian fishing and coasting traditions, and the knowledge of African pilots, merchants, and rulers. It depended on kings who needed revenue and prestige, noble households seeking lordships, merchant bankers from Genoa, Florence, and Flanders seeking returns, and ordinary sailors who accepted the dangers of scurvy, shipwreck, and starvation for wages and the chance of profit. It also depended on the choices of peoples who were encountered rather than discovered: Canarian communities who resisted conquest, West African states that controlled the terms of coastal trade, Kongo and Benin rulers who received Portuguese envoys as diplomatic partners, Taíno communities who offered hospitality in the Caribbean, and Indian Ocean merchants who had no need of what the Portuguese first brought.

The chapter's chronology requires a note. Chapter 67 ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, the settlement of the great confessional conflicts examined in Part X. Part XI turns back in time, because the maritime expansion it examines began long before the Thirty Years' War and was already producing global empires by the time that war ended. Chapter 68 therefore centers on approximately 1380 to 1500, with selective developments into the first years of the sixteenth century. It establishes the mechanisms and initial encounters of overseas expansion. The Spanish conquest of the Americas and the Columbian Exchange belong to Chapter 69, the mature Atlantic slave trade and plantation economies to Chapter 70, and the Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires to Chapter 71. The initial violence and coercion of expansion, however, belong here.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusual mixture of evidence. Royal charters, papal bulls, treaties, customs accounts, and notarial records survive in Portuguese and Castilian archives. Chronicles by Zurara, João de Barros, and Bartolomé de las Casas, the journals and letters of Columbus, the account of Gama's voyage known as the Roteiro, and the reports of the Venetian Alvise Cadamosto give narrative detail but reflect the interests of their authors. Charts and maps, shipwrecks and fortifications such as Elmina Castle, the excavated town of Cidade Velha in Cape Verde, Canarian burial sites, and Taíno settlements add independent evidence. African and Indigenous voices are recorded mainly through European writers, which means that historians must read carefully for what those writers could not or would not report.

Finally, the chapter has a place in the argument of Volume III. The Renaissance, the Reformation, and the formation of competing states, treated in Parts IX, X, and XII, are usually narrated as developments within Europe. Maritime expansion shows that European states and societies were being reshaped by their connections to Africa, the Americas, and Asia, even as those connections were forming. The Iberian crowns examined here were also still fighting the last phase of the Granada war, expelling Jewish communities, and building the administrative capacities that Chapters 74 and 75 will examine.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Portugal and Castile, rather than wealthier Mediterranean powers, lead European Atlantic expansion in the fifteenth century?
  2. How did existing Mediterranean, African, and Islamic knowledge systems contribute to Iberian navigation, and why do "discovery" narratives obscure that contribution?
  3. What was the relationship between island colonization in the Atlantic and later voyages to Africa, India, and the Americas?

  8. How should historians evaluate sources written by Europeans about African and Indigenous peoples whose own accounts do not survive in comparable form?

Historical Context

By the late fourteenth century the Iberian Peninsula was divided among several kingdoms with different resources and ambitions. Portugal, whose mainland frontiers had been settled since the thirteenth century, was a small, Atlantic facing kingdom with a few strong ports. Castile, far larger and the dominant power of the peninsula, held the Guadalquivir valley and the Andalusian ports after the conquests of the thirteenth century, as examined in Chapter 47 in connection with Iberian crusading. The Crown of Aragon, a composite of Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia with extensive Mediterranean possessions, looked eastward. The Nasrid emirate of Granada survived as a tributary Muslim state in the south until 1492. Jewish and Muslim communities lived within the Christian kingdoms in large numbers, and their position was eroding after the anti-Jewish violence of 1391 in Castile and Aragon.

The wider economic setting was shaped by the commercial revival examined in Chapter 43 and by the demographic shock of the Black Death, discussed in Chapter 50. Italian merchants, especially Genoese and Venetians, controlled much of Mediterranean trade and had established regular galley voyages to Flanders and England, which made the Portuguese and Andalusian coasts waystations of long distance Atlantic trade. Labor shortages after the plague encouraged landlords and merchants to seek new sources of income and coerced labor. Sugar, grain, and wine were increasingly profitable commodities, and Mediterranean sugar plantations on Cyprus, Sicily, and in Valencia had already used enslaved labor on a limited scale.

Gold was also at issue. West African gold, mined chiefly in the Bambuk, Bure, and later Akan regions, moved north across the Sahara by caravan to terminals in the Maghrib, from which it reached Mediterranean mints. The Mali Empire's control of much of this trade had been famously displayed by the pilgrimage of Mansa Musa in 1324 and was recorded on the Catalan Atlas of 1375. European coinage depended heavily on this gold, and European interest in reaching its sources more directly was long standing.

Religious and political ideas also mattered. Crusading ideology, examined in Chapter 47, had not disappeared with the loss of the Latin states in the eastern Mediterranean, and the Iberian frontier continued to supply a model of Christian conquest. Medieval legends of Prester John, a Christian king supposed to rule somewhere beyond the Muslim world, encouraged hopes of allies against Islam. After the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453, discussed in Chapter 41, some writers argued that Europeans were forced to find alternative routes to Asia, but this older explanation is now widely regarded as insufficient, since Portuguese exploration of the African coast was already well advanced by 1453 and the Mediterranean routes continued to function.

The Atlantic itself was not a blank. Basque, Breton, Galician, and Andalusian fishermen worked its coasts; Moroccan, Canarian, and Wolof communities sailed or fished along the African shore; Norse settlers had crossed the North Atlantic centuries earlier, though their Greenland communities were fading and left no lasting connection to Iberian expansion. The chapter begins with this Atlantic, then follows the political crisis in Portugal that placed a new dynasty on the throne in 1385.

Iberia and the Atlantic before Overseas Expansion

This division establishes the economic, geographical, and political conditions that made the Atlantic an increasingly important arena of Iberian activity, beginning with the recognition that the Atlantic was already a human environment.
Section 68.1

The Atlantic Before European Maritime Expansion

The phrase "opening of the Atlantic" is convenient but can mislead. Along the European and African shores the Atlantic had been used for centuries. Basque, Breton, Galician, and Andalusian fishing crews worked the continental shelf and sold salted fish across Europe. Genoese and later Venetian galleys had carried Mediterranean goods to Bruges, Southampton, and London since the late thirteenth century, calling at Lisbon and Andalusian ports. Moroccan Atlantic harbors such as Safi, Asilah, and Salé served merchants who linked the interior to coastal markets, and Castile and Portugal traded with them in grain, textiles, and horses despite periodic warfare.

South of Morocco the Sahara met the sea, and trade moved inland rather than along the coast. Caravans carried salt from desert mines such as Taghaza southward and returned with gold, ivory, and enslaved people, passing through towns such as Sijilmasa, Walata, and Timbuktu under the political umbrella of Mali. Muslim merchants, scholars, and rulers shaped this commercial world, and it connected West Africa to the Mediterranean and the wider Islamic world long before Portuguese ships appeared. Farther south, canoe borne trade moved along lagoons and rivers from Senegambia to the Gulf of Guinea, and coastal societies such as the Wolof, Sereer, Akan, and Edo had their own political and commercial institutions.

The Atlantic islands were inhabited unevenly. The Canary Islands, within sight of the African coast, were home to Indigenous communities whose languages and material culture show links to Amazigh (Berber) populations of North Africa. Madeira, the Azores, and Cape Verde appear to have been uninhabited when Europeans reached them, though knowledge of them may have circulated earlier in some form. The Norse had crossed the northern Atlantic by around 1000 CE and left archaeological traces in Newfoundland, but their Greenland settlements dwindled by the fifteenth century and had no continuous connection with Iberian voyages. For the Iberian sailors of the 1400s, the real novelty was not entering an unknown ocean but learning to move systematically through it, southward and outward, and to return.

This matters for the chapter's method. A history that begins with European "discovery" treats Africans, Canarians, and Indigenous Americans as scenery. A history that begins with an inhabited Atlantic allows readers to ask what each party wanted, what it knew, and what it could resist or exploit. The sections that follow therefore treat each coastal and island encounter as a negotiation among unequal but active participants.

Section 68.2

The Iberian Peninsula and Its Maritime Frontiers

Iberia in 1400 was a patchwork of kingdoms, not an early version of modern Spain and Portugal. Portugal had reached its mainland frontiers in the thirteenth century, controlled the long Atlantic coast from Porto to the Algarve, and depended on Lisbon, Porto, and the Algarve ports for trade. Castile, with a much larger population, controlled the interior meseta, the Cantabrian coast, and, since the conquest of Seville in 1248, the Guadalquivir valley and Andalusian ports such as Cádiz, Sanlúcar de Barrameda, Palos, and Huelva. The Crown of Aragon was a composite of kingdoms with separate laws and assemblies, and its strengths lay in Barcelona, Valencia, and its Mediterranean holdings in Sicily, Sardinia, and, from 1442, Naples. The emirate of Granada, ruled by the Nasrid dynasty, held the southern mountains and ports such as Málaga and Almería and paid tribute to Castile in many periods.

The Strait of Gibraltar shaped the peninsula's maritime frontier. Less than fifteen kilometers wide at its narrowest, it was a corridor for commerce, raiding, and diplomacy between Iberian Christian kingdoms, Granada, and the Marinid sultanate of Morocco. Genoese merchants had a long presence in Seville and Lisbon, and Genoese ships, crews, and capital would remain integral to Atlantic ventures. Jewish and Muslim communities, together with a growing population of converts from Judaism known as conversos, formed part of port life and commercial networks, and Jewish scholars at Iberian courts transmitted astronomical and geographical knowledge.

Relations with North Africa combined hostility and exchange. Castile and Portugal, like Aragon, undertook raids on North African coasts and fortified positions, while merchants bought grain, leather, and other goods from Moroccan towns. Marinid Morocco, weakened after its defeat at the Battle of Salado in 1340, remained a significant regional power and would resist Portuguese advances at Ceuta and Tangier. The Iberian Atlantic was thus both a frontier of religious war and a zone of long standing commerce, a duality that persisted as ships moved farther south.

Section 68.3

The Portuguese Monarchy and the Conquest of Ceuta

The death of King Ferdinand I of Portugal in 1383 without a male heir created a succession crisis. His daughter Beatrice was married to John I of Castile, who claimed the Portuguese throne. Many Portuguese nobles supported him, but Lisbon's townspeople, merchants, and artisans rose in support of John, Master of the Order of Avis, an illegitimate son of King Peter I. The Cortes at Coimbra acclaimed him king in 1385, and the Portuguese army, commanded by Nuno Álvares Pereira and supported by English archers, defeated the Castilian army at Aljubarrota on 14 August 1385. The Treaty of Windsor in 1386 confirmed an Anglo-Portuguese alliance, and John's marriage in 1387 to Philippa of Lancaster brought links to the English royal house and a generation of well educated princes.

The new dynasty was politically distinctive. It owed its throne to urban support, to lesser nobles, and to military leaders who needed rewards, and it had to prove itself against a hostile Castile. Peace with Castile was not fully settled until 1411. With internal war ended and many fighting men without employment, the crown, the princes, and the nobility looked to external conquest as a way to provide revenue, honor, and lordship.

The result was the expedition against Ceuta. On 21 August 1415, a Portuguese fleet that chronicles describe as very large took the Moroccan port, a trading town on the Strait of Gibraltar and a terminus of caravan routes. John's sons Duarte, Pedro, and Henry took part, and the assault was presented as a crusade and a chivalric trial. Motives were mixed: dynastic prestige, noble ambition, the desire to control the Strait, religious war, and expectations of commercial gain. The reality was disappointing. Trans-Saharan merchants shifted their routes away from Ceuta, the garrison was costly, and a Marinid counterattack in 1418 had to be repelled. A further attack on Tangier in 1437 failed disastrously, and the king's youngest son, Fernando, was left as a hostage and died in captivity in 1443. These setbacks did not end Portuguese interest in Morocco, which continued under Afonso V, but they encouraged the search for other sources of gold, captives, and honor along the Atlantic coast.

Section 68.4

Commerce, Religion, Political Ambition, and Maritime Patronage

Why did Portuguese princes, nobles, and merchants invest in Atlantic voyages that, for decades, produced little profit? The chronicler Zurara, writing in the 1450s, gave several motives for Prince Henry's patronage: curiosity about the coast beyond Cape Bojador, the hope of commerce, the wish to know the strength of Muslim powers, the search for Christian allies, and the desire to spread the faith. Historians treat this list as an expression of what contemporaries found respectable, not a complete account. The motives should be seen as overlapping, and different participants held different priorities.

Gold was a central objective. West African gold reached Europe indirectly through North African intermediaries, and a direct route could bypass them. Religious aims were also real. The Order of Christ, which Henry governed from 1420, held lands and revenues that financed expeditions and received spiritual jurisdiction over newly claimed territories. The legend of Prester John, and the real but poorly understood Christian kingdom of Ethiopia, nourished hopes of alliance. Noble households, whose members sought lordships, offices, and plunder, supplied captains, and the crown wanted revenue, prestige, and a field for the younger sons of the royal house.

Merchants and financiers were indispensable. Genoese, Florentine, and Flemish firms, and increasingly Portuguese merchants including converso families, supplied credit, ships, and markets. Lisbon's merchant community was politically significant after 1385, and its interest in new trades was consistent with the dynasty's origins. Sailors, pilots, and soldiers participated for wages, shares, and sometimes pardon: criminals sentenced to exile (degredados) were used as interpreters and settlers. Captives, enslaved people, and the inhabitants of coasts and islands were affected by decisions in which they had no voice.

The older argument that the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 forced Europeans to seek new routes is not supported by the chronology. Portuguese ships had been working down the African coast since the 1430s, and Mediterranean spice routes remained active. Expansion arose instead from long standing Iberian interests, the crusading tradition, the commercial needs of Atlantic ports, and the opportunities offered by particular winds and coasts. No single motive explains it, and the relative weight of each shifted over time.

Section 68.5

Atlantic Winds, Currents, and the Geography of Oceanic Navigation

The North Atlantic is governed by a large circulation of winds and currents. South of roughly thirty degrees north, the northeast trade winds blow steadily toward the southwest, and the Canary Current runs southward along the Iberian and Moroccan coasts. Farther north, in the latitudes of the Azores and northern Iberia, the prevailing winds are westerlies. A ship leaving Lisbon for West Africa could therefore run south with the wind and current astern, but returning against those same conditions was slow, difficult, and dangerous. This asymmetry, as much as any fear of monsters, explains why Cape Bojador and the coasts beyond it were a practical barrier: ships might go, but could they come home?

The solution was the volta do mar, the "turn of the sea." Instead of beating north along the coast, pilots learned to sail northwest, far into the Atlantic, until they reached the westerlies near the Azores, and then to run east to Portugal. The technique appears to have developed gradually in the 1440s and 1450s, through trial, accumulated pilot knowledge, and the use of the Azores as a waystation. On the South Atlantic, a similar principle applied, and Portuguese captains in the 1480s and 1490s learned to swing wide to the southwest, away from the coast and the unfavorable winds near the equator, to pick up the westerlies of the South Atlantic that would carry them to the southern tip of Africa. Gama's voyage of 1497 followed a very wide arc of this kind and spent about three months out of sight of land on the outward leg.

The transatlantic voyages of Columbus used the same logic. Starting from the Canaries, he could ride the northeast trades to the west, and on the return, by turning north to the latitudes of the westerlies, he reached the Azores. The maritime knowledge of Atlantic winds that made his voyages possible was developed through Portuguese and Castilian experience, not discovered by him alone. In the Indian Ocean, by contrast, the seasonal monsoon winds were long known to Arab, Persian, Gujarati, and Swahili mariners and had structured trade for centuries, a point that becomes important in Section 68.27.

Section 68.6

Ships, Sailors, and the Development of Ocean-Going Vessels

The vessel most associated with early Portuguese exploration is the caravel, a small, light ship of perhaps fifty to sixty tons, often rigged with lateen (triangular) sails that allowed it to sail closer to the wind than square rigged ships. It drew little water, which made it suitable for exploring unfamiliar coasts and river mouths, and its handiness helped in the contrary winds and currents of the African coast. The type developed from Iberian fishing and coasting craft, and some scholars suggest influences from Mediterranean and Arab shipbuilding, though the origin of the term and the type is debated. By the later fifteenth century, a larger variant with square sails on some masts, the caravela redonda, was better suited to long runs with following winds, and Columbus's Niña was converted in this way during his first voyage.

Larger vessels, the naus or carracks, carried cargo, supplies, and troops in greater volume. They were slower and less maneuverable in coastal exploration but could carry the provisions required for long voyages. Shipbuilding was concentrated in Lisbon's royal shipyards, in Porto, and in Basque and Andalusian yards, using timber and naval stores drawn from Iberia and, increasingly, from the Baltic and Atlantic coasts. A Portuguese caravel might carry a crew of twenty to thirty, a nau perhaps over a hundred, comprising a master, a pilot, sailors, ship's boys (grumetes), gunners, carpenters, soldiers, and occasionally interpreters, some of them African or Moroccan.

Provisioning limited range. Crews lived on hard biscuit, salted meat and fish, beans, olive oil, wine, and stored water, and the lack of fresh food led to scurvy on long voyages, a major killer on the Gama expedition. Water and fresh food had to be obtained at landfalls, which is one reason islands and coastal trading posts were so valuable. Logistical constraints, not only winds, determined what routes were feasible.

Historical Vocabulary
Caravel and Volta do Mar

A caravel (Portuguese caravela) was a small, maneuverable sailing ship, usually lateen rigged, used for coastal and Atlantic exploration. It was not the only or largest type of ship used in expansion, and the popular image of all explorers sailing caravels oversimplifies the mix of vessels. The volta do mar (turn of the sea) was the technique of sailing wide into the Atlantic to find favorable winds for the return voyage. Together, vessel and method made repeated journeys to West Africa feasible. Modern readers should not equate either with a sudden invention: both were the outcome of decades of experience.

Section 68.7

Charts, Instruments, and the Transmission of Navigational Knowledge

Mediterranean sailors had long used portolan charts, detailed coastal maps marked with networks of compass bearings (rhumb lines), made in Genoa, Venice, and Majorca from the thirteenth century. Majorca's workshops, in which Jewish cartographers such as Cresques Abraham were prominent, produced the Catalan Atlas of 1375. These charts were designed for relatively short sea crossings with known coastlines and did not include latitude. The compass itself, whose earlier history involves Chinese, Islamic, and Mediterranean developments that are not fully traced, was in common use on Iberian ships.

As voyages lengthened and lost sight of known coasts, Portuguese pilots adopted methods of determining latitude, the distance north or south of the equator, by measuring the height of the Pole Star above the horizon with a quadrant or mariner's astrolabe. South of the equator the Pole Star disappeared, so pilots measured the noon Sun and used tables of solar declination. The astronomical knowledge underlying these tables owed much to Islamic and Jewish scholarship. The Salamanca and Lisbon astronomer Abraham Zacuto compiled tables printed in 1496, and royal advisers, including the mathematician Joseph Vizinho, tested and adapted methods under John II. Longitude, by contrast, could not be measured at sea with the instruments of the time, and navigators relied on dead reckoning, estimating position from compass heading, time measured by sandglass, and judged speed.

Navigational knowledge was practical, collective, and transmitted through apprenticeship, rutters (written sailing directions), charts, and pilots' experience. The Portuguese crown increasingly kept charts and information secret, though Genoese, Venetian, Florentine, and German agents often obtained them. The transmission of navigational knowledge reflects the multicultural character of the Mediterranean and Iberian world, a point that argues against any narrative of purely Portuguese or purely European invention.

Section 68.8

Madeira and Porto Santo: Settlement and Atlantic Agriculture

Porto Santo was reached by expeditions led by João Gonçalves Zarco and Tristão Vaz Teixeira in about 1418, and Madeira, whose name means "wood" in Portuguese, was visited in 1419 or 1420. Both islands appear to have been uninhabited. Settlement began in the 1420s, organized through captaincies (capitanias): hereditary grants to captains such as Zarco at Funchal, Teixeira at Machico, and Bartolomeu Perestrelo at Porto Santo, under the overall lordship of Prince Henry. Settlers came from continental Portugal, and their initial work was clearing forest and building irrigation channels, the levadas, that carried water from the wet northern highlands to dry southern terraces.

The first crops were cereals, exported to Portugal, and timber, which also served as fuel. From the 1450s, sugar cane was introduced and expanded, financed in significant part by Genoese and other foreign merchants. Madeira's sugar economy grew rapidly toward the end of the century and drew on a labor force that included free settlers, tenant cultivators, and, increasingly, enslaved Africans and Canarians. The scale of slave labor on Madeira in the fifteenth century is debated, and it was smaller than on later Atlantic plantations, but the combination of sugar, foreign capital, intensive irrigation, and coerced labor foreshadowed what would later become the plantation complex examined in Chapter 70.

The environmental consequences were substantial. Forest clearance for fields, fuel, and boiling houses, and the use of land for sugar, changed the island's landscape and ecology rapidly. The legend that early settlers set a fire that burned for years may be exaggerated, but the combination of fire, clearance, and introduced animals transformed the island. Madeira thus offers an early example of Atlantic colonization as an ecological as well as political process.

Section 68.9

The Azores and the Expansion of Atlantic Settlement

The Azores, about 1,400 kilometers west of Lisbon, were reached by Portuguese captains in the 1420s and 1430s, with Santa Maria usually dated to around 1427 to 1432. The islands were uninhabited, volcanic, and prone to earthquakes. Systematic settlement was authorized from about 1439 and developed in the following decades under captaincies, with Prince Henry and later his heirs holding lordship. The settlers raised wheat, which was exported to Madeira and Lisbon, cattle, and, notably, woad, a blue dye plant in demand among Flemish and other cloth producers. Flemish settlers, linked to the dynastic connection between the Portuguese royal house and the Duchy of Burgundy, settled on Faial and neighboring islands, and for a time the Azores were referred to in some sources as the Flemish islands.

Population remained small compared to Madeira, and the economy was agricultural and dependent on the mainland. The islands' strategic importance was greater than their direct wealth. Their position near the westerlies made them essential on the return leg of the volta do mar, and later Terceira's harbor became a major stopping point for ships from Africa, India, and the Americas. The Azores demonstrate how island settlement created infrastructure for sustained Atlantic traffic.

Everyday Life and Social History

Shipboard and Island Life

For most participants, Atlantic expansion meant labor and risk rather than glory. On a caravel, a few dozen men shared cramped space, slept on deck or in simple bunks, ate rationed biscuit and salted food, and drank wine and stored water. Ship's boys performed some of the hardest and lowest paid tasks, and officers, pilots, and masters held clear hierarchical status. Mortality from storms, disease, and conflict with coastal populations was high.

Island settlers, including small farmers, craftsmen, convicts, and enslaved workers, faced isolation, unfamiliar soil, and dependence on infrequent shipping. Settlement involved a heavy investment of labor: clearing forest, digging irrigation channels, building mills, and establishing churches and local administration. Women appear in the surviving record far less often than men, but their labor, as wives, servants, and enslaved domestic workers, was central to household production. The evidence for these lives is fragmentary, drawn from land grants, tax records, and notarial documents, and rarely records what ordinary participants thought.

Section 68.10

The Canary Islands: Conquest, Indigenous Resistance, and Iberian Rivalry

The seven main Canary Islands, Lanzarote, Fuerteventura, Gran Canaria, Tenerife, La Gomera, La Palma, and El Hierro, were inhabited when Europeans arrived. The Indigenous population, whose ancestors had probably arrived from North Africa in the first millennium BCE or the early centuries CE, spoke languages related to Amazigh and had distinct societies on each island. Economies combined herding of goats and sheep, cereal cultivation, and gathering, and people lived in caves and stone houses. Political organization varied: Tenerife was divided among several chiefdoms ruled by menceyes, while Gran Canaria had a more centralized structure. The term "Guanche" originally designated the people of Tenerife but is often applied to all the islands' inhabitants, which obscures their diversity. Evidence comes from archaeology, including burials and mummification practices on some islands, from European accounts, and from later oral and linguistic traces.

European contact increased in the fourteenth century, including Genoese, Majorcan, and Portuguese expeditions that raided for captives and, in some cases, sought to establish missions. In 1402 the Norman knight Jean de Béthencourt, with Castilian backing, began the conquest of Lanzarote, Fuerteventura, and El Hierro, and these islands came under lordships recognizing Castilian authority. Portugal contested Castilian claims through the fifteenth century, and popes issued conflicting concessions. The densely populated and more strongly defended islands of Gran Canaria, La Palma, and Tenerife resisted for decades.

The Castilian crown under Isabella and Ferdinand directed the final conquests: Gran Canaria between 1478 and 1483, La Palma in 1492–1493, and Tenerife in 1494–1496, led by Alonso Fernández de Lugo. The Tenerife campaign shows the character of this conquest. The Guanche chiefs achieved a notable victory at Acentejo in 1494, but later defeats, epidemic disease, and the support of some Indigenous allies for the invaders turned the balance. Many survivors were enslaved and sold, others were converted and absorbed, and the Indigenous population declined sharply. The conquest was completed in 1496. Contemporary legal debates about whether baptized Canarians could be enslaved were inconsistently applied. The Canaries became a Castilian base for sugar, provisioning, and, in 1492, the last stopping point for Columbus's fleet. They also served as a model of conquest and settlement that Castilian officials later applied to the Caribbean, as Chapter 69 explains.

Section 68.11

Beyond Cape Bojador: The First Sustained Coastal Voyages

Cape Bojador, on the Atlantic coast of the western Sahara at about twenty-six degrees north, was the first major goal of the Henrician expeditions. Several attempts in the 1420s and early 1430s turned back. The cape itself is a low headland fringed by shoals and strong currents, and ships that passed it faced the problem of returning against wind and current, as Section 68.5 explained. Zurara later portrayed the passage as the overcoming of a psychological barrier of ancient dread. That account has literary force, but a practical explanation is at least as persuasive: pilots hesitated to risk ships on a coast with no known harbor and a difficult return.

In 1434 Gil Eanes, a squire in Henry's household, passed the cape on a second attempt, and the voyage was followed in 1435 by Eanes and Afonso Baldaia, who sailed beyond to the Río de Oro. Further expeditions in the 1440s reached Cape Blanc (1441), the island of Arguin, and the green headland of Cape Verde on the Senegambian coast (1444–1445), marking the end of the desert shore and the beginning of a more densely populated, wetter region. The Saharan coast itself was inhabited by Sanhaja Berber speaking fishing and herding communities, referred to by the Portuguese as Azenegues, who sometimes traded and sometimes resisted. Muslim geographers, including al-Idrisi in the twelfth century, had written about parts of this coast, and local communities and caravan traders knew it well. What changed was the Portuguese capacity to sail it regularly.

The voyages thus extended European knowledge rather than establishing the existence of the coast. Each expedition brought back information on harbors, currents, peoples, and trade goods, which was incorporated into charts and sailing directions that remained closely held. By the late 1440s, Portugal had established a fortified trading station on the island of Arguin, an early example of the feitoria system discussed in Section 68.15.

Section 68.12

Coastal Raiding, Captivity, and Early Portuguese-African Encounters

The earliest Portuguese expeditions south of Bojador were as much raids as voyages of exploration. In 1441, Antão Gonçalves and Nuno Tristão captured a small number of people on the Saharan coast and brought them to Portugal, where they were presented to Prince Henry. This was followed by larger expeditions. The landing at Lagos in 1444, described in the chapter's opening and in the primary source below, involved some 235 captives taken in a series of attacks and exchanges along the coast. Later narratives describe a gradual shift from raids to bargaining, but the transition was uneven and violence continued.

Slavery was not new to Iberia. The peninsula had long included enslaved people of Muslim, Slavic, Canarian, and sub-Saharan origin, and Mediterranean markets such as Genoa and Valencia traded in captives. What the Portuguese voyages added was a new, expanding source of enslaved Africans, brought directly to Portuguese ports and to Madeira and, later, other islands. Many captives were baptized, and chroniclers such as Zurara presented their conversion as a justification. Church authorities supported this reasoning: papal bulls of 1452 and 1455 (Dum Diversas and Romanus Pontifex) granted the Portuguese crown rights to conquer and enslave non-Christian peoples in Africa and affirmed its exclusive right to navigation in those areas. These documents reflected the Portuguese crown's lobbying, and they did not represent the consent or perspective of any African society.

African resistance was effective in many places. Expeditions were attacked, ships were lost, and Nuno Tristão himself was killed around 1446 on the coast south of Cape Verde during an attack by local people. Coastal communities could use poisoned arrows, shallow channels, and local knowledge. Over the following decades the Portuguese found that raids carried high costs and uncertain rewards, and they increasingly sought to acquire captives, gold, and other goods through exchange with African merchants and rulers. The number of Africans taken to Portugal in the fifteenth century is uncertain, and modern estimates, which vary, place it in the tens of thousands over the century, with accelerating numbers toward its end. Enslaved Africans in Lisbon, the Algarve, and Andalusia worked as domestics, laborers, and artisans, and by the sixteenth century Lisbon contained a visible Black population, both enslaved and free.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Zurara on the Landing at Lagos, 1444

Creator: Gomes Eanes de Zurara, royal chronicler and keeper of the royal archive · Date: completed c. 1453–1454, describing events of 8 August 1444 · Type: Official chronicle, Crónica dos feitos notáveis que se passaram na conquista da Guiné

Context: Zurara wrote under royal and princely patronage, and his purpose was to celebrate Prince Henry's achievements. His chronicle is the principal contemporary narrative of the Guinea voyages through the 1440s. He drew on documents and the recollections of participants but was not himself an eyewitness to the events described here.

Paraphrase of Zurara, chapters on the landing at Lagos Zurara describes how the captives were brought ashore and gathered in a field near Lagos, where they were divided into shares among the expedition's backers and the prince. He portrays them as frightened and grieving, notes that families were separated in the division, and says that he himself was moved by the scene. He nevertheless frames the event as beneficial, because the captives would be converted and brought into Christian society, and records that Henry rode out to receive his fifth share, which he distributed among others. This is a summary of the account, not a translation of a continuous passage.

Analysis: The source is valuable because it documents a public sale in a Portuguese town and shows how contemporaries justified it. It also records the distress of the captives, though always through the writer's frame. Zurara's religious and chivalric justification coexists with an acknowledgement of suffering that he could not entirely conceal. The source does not tell us the names, languages, communities, or later lives of the captives, how they were captured on the African side, or what African authorities or traders were involved. Those perspectives are missing.

Section 68.13

Senegambia, Upper Guinea, and African Commercial Networks

South of the Sahara, the Portuguese encountered political systems that were not small or isolated. In Senegambia the Wolof states, loosely under the authority of the Jolof (Jolof Empire) and, as the fifteenth century proceeded, increasingly autonomous coastal kingdoms such as Kajoor, ruled over agricultural and trading populations. The Mandinka, heirs to the Mali tradition, controlled river valleys of the Gambia and its hinterland, and Muslim merchants, known in the region as Dyula or Wangara, operated networks that carried gold, kola nuts, cloth, and captives across long distances. Fulani herders, Sereer farmers, and fishing communities completed a complex mosaic. To these societies, Portuguese ships were new arrivals in an existing trade system.

The Venetian Alvise Cadamosto, who sailed in Portuguese service in 1455 and 1456, described Senegambia in detail. He found rulers who demanded gifts and control of commerce, merchants who understood the value of horses (which were in demand for warfare and prestige and did not breed well in the tropical climate), and coastal peoples whose political structures were evidently sophisticated. Portuguese traders exchanged horses, cloth, metalwork, and other goods for gold dust and for captives, who in many cases were already enslaved within African societies through war, debt, or judicial punishment. Cadamosto's reports of rates of exchange vary and should be treated with care. The important point is that Portuguese merchants were operating through African intermediaries and under local customs, such as paying tolls and giving gifts, rather than commanding terms.

Where Portuguese agents attempted violence, results were often poor. Poisoned arrows and organized defense proved dangerous to the crews of small ships, and by the late 1450s the crown's emphasis was on peaceful trade. Further south, along the Upper Guinea coast, riverine societies including the Bijagó islanders and Mande speaking communities maintained their own commerce. Diogo Gomes, who sailed on the Gambia in 1456, reported negotiating with representatives of the Mali ruler, though his account was written decades later and its details should be treated cautiously. The Portuguese were learning that access to gold depended on partnership with African merchants and rulers, and that violence was a poor substitute for diplomacy. They did not always act on this knowledge, and coercion remained a persistent feature of the trade.

Section 68.14

Cape Verde and the Development of Atlantic Island Networks

The Cape Verde islands, a volcanic archipelago about 500 kilometers off Senegal, were uninhabited and reached by Portuguese and Italian captains in 1456 and in the following years, including Cadamosto, Diogo Gomes, and the Genoese António de Noli. Settlement began in the 1460s on Santiago, where the town of Ribeira Grande, today Cidade Velha, became the first lasting European colonial town in the tropics and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The islands were dry and drought prone, and agriculture was limited, but their position made them ideal as a depot for trade with the mainland coast.

In 1466 and again in 1472 the crown granted settlers the privilege of trading on the Upper Guinea coast, the "Rivers of Guinea," which allowed Cape Verdean residents to operate outside the crown monopoly that applied elsewhere. This produced a distinctive class of intermediaries. Some Portuguese and Cape Verdean traders, known as lançados, settled among African communities on the mainland, married local women, learned languages, and acted as brokers. Their descendants formed Luso African communities with mixed cultural identities. African cloth, woven in Upper Guinea and in the islands by enslaved weavers, was a major commodity.

Cape Verde thus linked Atlantic islands, African coastal markets, and the emerging slave trade. Enslaved Africans purchased on the coast were held and sometimes put to work on the islands before being sent to Portugal, Madeira, and eventually the Americas. Famine and drought struck repeatedly, with serious consequences for the enslaved and poor. The development of Cape Verde shows how islands functioned as nodes in a network of coerced labor, trade, and cultural mixture, a pattern that Chapter 70 will examine in its mature form.

Section 68.15

The Gold Coast and São Jorge da Mina

The coastal region that the Portuguese came to call Mina, "the mine," lay on the Gulf of Guinea in what is now Ghana. It was the outlet for gold produced inland in the Akan forest zone, which also fed overland routes north through Begho and Dyula networks toward the Niger. Portuguese traders reached the coast around 1471 and found that gold could be obtained in large quantities in exchange for cloth, copper and brass goods, and other items. The region's states and merchants actively bargained, and the Portuguese had to adapt to local demands, including the particular forms of brass and textiles that were valued.

The new king, John II, decided to make this trade secure and exclusive. In January 1482, a fleet commanded by Diogo de Azambuja arrived with building materials and several hundred men and negotiated with the local ruler, whom Portuguese sources call Caramansa, for permission to build a fortified post at Edina, which became Elmina. Later Portuguese chroniclers described the negotiations as ceremonial and tense, and the ruler's consent seems to have been given with reluctance and an eye toward the benefits of trade. The fort, São Jorge da Mina, was built of stone within months and became the Portuguese crown's central establishment on the coast. Its stone walls still stand and are a principal material record of the period.

Gold exports from Mina were significant, though estimates are uncertain. Scholars commonly estimate that deliveries in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries reached the order of hundreds of kilograms a year, representing a substantial share of the crown's income and of the gold entering western Europe. At the same time, Portuguese authority was strictly limited. Elmina controlled a small peninsula; inland and coastal rulers set prices, dictated terms, and could shift trade to rival ports or to other European ships. In the early sixteenth century the Portuguese also began selling enslaved people at Mina, purchased in the Bight of Benin and elsewhere, to Akan buyers who exchanged gold for them. The fort therefore anticipated the entanglement of gold, trade goods, and coerced labor that characterized later phases.

Historical Vocabulary
Feitoria and Royal Monopoly

A feitoria (often translated "factory" in English, from the Latin factor, an agent) was a trading post staffed by crown or company agents who bought, stored, and sold goods. It might be a fortified castle such as Elmina or merely a house, and it did not imply territorial sovereignty. A royal monopoly was an exclusive right claimed by the crown over a particular trade, which it might exercise directly or lease to contractors, as in the Guinea contract of 1469. In practice, monopolies were hard to enforce, because African merchants, private traders, smugglers, and rival Europeans all found ways around them. The terms help students avoid assuming that early European presence in Africa was a system of colonial rule.

Section 68.16

Benin, Kongo, and the Expansion of African Diplomatic Contacts

Farther east and south, the Portuguese met larger states with well developed courts. The kingdom of Benin, ruled by an oba from its capital Edo (Benin City), was a centralized power that controlled trade along the Bight of Benin. João Afonso de Aveiro visited in 1485–1486, and the encounter led to exchanges of envoys, including an ambassador from Benin who traveled to Lisbon. Benin supplied pepper, ivory, and cloth, and bought Portuguese textiles and brass. Benin's rulers kept firm control of foreign traders, and later sources suggest that they limited the sale of enslaved men, so that the Portuguese obtained captives elsewhere. The oba's reign at this time is usually dated to roughly 1481 to 1504, though the dates are approximate.

Diogo Cão's voyages in 1482–1484 and 1485–1486 reached the mouth of the Congo River and the coast of present day Angola and Namibia, where he placed stone pillars (padrões) as markers of Portuguese presence. At the Congo he made contact with officials of the kingdom of Kongo, a large, centralized state with a capital at Mbanza Kongo and a ruler known as the manikongo. Cão took Kongo emissaries to Lisbon, and whether they went willingly or under pressure is not entirely clear in the sources. They returned with Portuguese gifts and with priests, craftsmen, and later soldiers. In 1491 the manikongo, Nzinga a Nkuwu, accepted baptism as João and received a Portuguese mission. His son Afonso, who ruled after him, would pursue a close and eventually troubled relationship with Portugal, a story that belongs to Chapter 70.

The significance of these contacts lies in the form they took. Kongo and Benin were not subordinate to Portugal. They treated Portuguese envoys as foreign visitors and sought benefits including technology, trade goods, literacy, and religious prestige, while the Portuguese crown regarded them as potential allies, trading partners, and objects of conversion. The relationships were asymmetrical in the longer term, but in the 1480s and 1490s they were diplomatic encounters between recognized rulers.

Section 68.17

São Tomé, Príncipe, and the Emerging Plantation Frontier

The islands of São Tomé and Príncipe in the Gulf of Guinea, almost on the equator, were reached around 1470 to 1472 and were uninhabited. An early attempt at settlement in 1486 had limited results, and a more determined effort came in 1493, when the crown granted the captaincy to Álvaro de Caminha. His settlers were a mixture of convicts, adventurers, and, in a particularly harsh episode, children of Jewish families who had been expelled from Spain in 1492 and taken from their parents in Portugal. Estimates of the number of children vary and many died in the first years from disease. Enslaved Africans, brought from the mainland, including Benin and Kongo, formed the core of the labor force.

The islands' climate was suited to sugar. By the early sixteenth century São Tomé had become one of the leading sugar producers in the Atlantic, and its estates used large numbers of enslaved workers, with planters, many of them of mixed European and African descent, holding substantial political power. The island became a place where methods of plantation organization, labor discipline, and shipment developed that were later applied to Brazil and the Caribbean. Rebellions by enslaved people occurred, and mortality was high. The full development of this plantation system belongs to Chapter 70, but its foundations were laid in the experiments with Madeira, Cape Verde, and São Tomé in the late fifteenth century.

Taken together, the island and coastal experiments of the fifteenth century formed a pattern. Islands provided uninhabited land, crown and noble patronage, foreign capital, and enslaved labor from the African coast. The mainland provided trade and captives, and negotiations with African rulers determined what could be obtained. The next division turns from voyages to the institutions and agreements by which the Iberian crowns tried to organize these developments.

Section 68.18

Prince Henry, Royal Patronage, and Portuguese Maritime Administration

Prince Henry (1394–1460), the third surviving son of John I and Philippa of Lancaster, was Duke of Viseu, lord of Madeira and the Azores, and from 1420 governor of the Order of Christ. He held rights over certain Algarve fisheries and other monopolies, and he used these revenues, together with the Order's resources, to fund expeditions from his household. His role was real and important: he sponsored the passage of Cape Bojador, the settlement of Madeira and the Azores, and the early Guinea voyages, and he lobbied successfully for papal support. He was also a leader of the failed attack on Tangier in 1437, and his reputation as a crusader and a lord of conquests was at least as central to his contemporaries as his connection with the sea.

The image of Henry as "the Navigator," presiding over a school of navigation and cartography at Sagres, is largely a creation of later writers, especially in the nineteenth century. Contemporary sources do not describe such a school, and the evidence for an organized institute is absent. Henry did gather pilots and advisers in his household, and Portuguese expansion benefited from accumulated experience, but navigational knowledge developed within wider Mediterranean and Atlantic networks.

After Henry's death in 1460, Portuguese expansion continued under the crown. Afonso V (1438–1481) devoted much energy to North African campaigns, taking Alcácer Ceguer in 1458 and Arzila and Tangier in 1471, and in 1469 leased the Guinea trade to the Lisbon merchant Fernão Gomes for five years in exchange for a payment and an obligation to explore a stretch of coast each year. Under this contract Portuguese captains reached the Gold Coast. John II (1481–1495) reversed the policy of delegation. He took the Guinea trade back into royal hands, built Elmina, sponsored Cão and Dias, consulted mathematicians and astronomers, and sought tighter secrecy. A royal administration centered in Lisbon, with institutions such as the Casa da Guiné and the royal shipyards, collected duties, licensed voyages, and managed gold and captives. The Church supplied ideological support through the Order of Christ and papal bulls.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Prince Henry the Architect of Portuguese Exploration?

The question: To what extent did one prince plan, direct, and inspire Portuguese expansion, and does the language of "discovery" accurately describe what happened?

Interpretation A: Henry as visionary organizer. Earlier writers, including Zurara and especially nineteenth and early twentieth century biographers, portrayed Henry as a far sighted leader who established a navigational school at Sagres, systematically directed voyages, and gave Portugal a unique scientific edge. This image suited national celebration and the imperial self image of later periods.
Interpretation B: A collective and royal enterprise. Modern historians, including the English historian Peter Russell in his biography of Henry, have emphasized that no school at Sagres is documented, that Henry's motives were crusading and seigneurial as much as scientific, that captains, pilots, merchants, and later kings played large roles, and that expansion accelerated markedly after his death under royal direction.

Evidence: The debate rests on Zurara's chronicle, other contemporary writers, royal charters, papal bulls, and the absence of surviving evidence for a school. It also depends on how one reads the chronicle's purposes.

A further problem: The word "discovery" itself reflects a European viewpoint. The Atlantic coasts were known to local peoples, and the Portuguese relied on African and Muslim knowledge and intermediaries. Many historians therefore prefer terms such as voyages, contact, or exploration, while recognizing that the term "discovery" is deeply embedded in older scholarship.

Why it matters: The question affects whether expansion is understood as the product of one exceptional individual, of institutions and economic interests, or of interaction with the societies encountered.

Student task: Identify evidence from this chapter supporting each interpretation, then explain why older narratives of a single heroic originator might have been attractive to later national historians.

Section 68.19

Castile, Aragon, and the Struggle for Atlantic Position

Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon married in October 1469, against the wishes of Isabella's half brother, King Henry IV of Castile. Isabella became queen of Castile in 1474 after Henry's death, and Ferdinand became king of Aragon in 1479. A war of succession (1475–1479) followed in Castile, because Afonso V of Portugal supported the claims of Isabella's rival, Juana. The two monarchs, later styled the Catholic Monarchs, ruled their realms jointly yet separately. Castile and Aragon kept distinct laws, representative assemblies (Cortes), currencies, and administrations. This was a composite monarchy, a dynastic union, not a unified Spain in the modern sense. Isabella and Ferdinand strengthened royal authority through the Santa Hermandad, a police and military force, and through the Inquisition, established in Castile in 1478, which targeted conversos suspected of secretly practicing Judaism.

Castilian interests in the Atlantic were distinctive. Seville and the Andalusian ports, with their Genoese merchant colonies and experienced seafarers, were natural centers of Atlantic activity. Andalusian and Basque ships traded and raided on the Guinea coast in the 1470s, challenging the Portuguese claim to exclusivity. The Canaries were Castile's foothold in the Atlantic, and their conquest was a royal priority. Aragon, by contrast, had Mediterranean concerns, including rivalry with France over Italy, and played a far smaller role in Atlantic enterprise, a point often obscured by the use of the word "Spain."

The final phase of the Granada war (1482–1492) absorbed the monarchs' resources and attention. Granada surrendered on 2 January 1492, ending Muslim political rule in Iberia, and on 31 March the Alhambra Decree ordered the expulsion of Jews who would not convert. Estimates of how many left vary widely, and many others converted. The same year as Granada's fall and the expulsion, Columbus's voyage was authorized. These developments were linked by the monarchs' emphasis on Christian unity and royal prestige, by their need to employ the energies of nobles and soldiers released from war, and by competition with Portugal.

Section 68.20

The Treaty of Alcáçovas and the Division of Atlantic Interests

The war of the Castilian succession ended with the Treaty of Alcáçovas, signed on 4 September 1479, and a related settlement ratified at Toledo in 1480. Afonso V recognized Isabella and Ferdinand as rulers of Castile, and the treaty settled a range of dynastic matters. It also divided Atlantic interests. Castile retained possession of the Canary Islands, while Portugal received recognition of its rights to Madeira, the Azores, the Cape Verde islands, the Guinea coast, and the conquest of the kingdom of Fez in Morocco. Castile renounced claims to navigate and trade in the zones assigned to Portugal. In 1481 Pope Sixtus IV confirmed the arrangement by the bull Aeterni regis.

The treaty is significant for several reasons. It was one of the first European agreements to divide Atlantic space between two states, and it linked dynastic politics to maritime competition. Its language defined Portuguese exclusivity south of the latitude of the Canaries, and this principle shaped Columbus's decisions. In 1492 he sailed from the Canaries and held to a westward course along their latitude, remaining in Castilian waters according to the treaty, rather than sailing south toward the Portuguese sphere. When he returned in 1493 with news of lands in the west, John II argued that they lay within the Portuguese zone as defined by the treaty and began to prepare a fleet, which led directly to the bulls and the Treaty of Tordesillas. The agreements at Alcáçovas demonstrate the close relationship between maritime competition and diplomacy, and the practical difficulty of defining a vast ocean through language written in courts.

Section 68.21

Papal Bulls, the Treaty of Tordesillas, and the Politics of Maritime Claims

When Columbus returned in March 1493, Ferdinand and Isabella moved quickly to secure their claim. They appealed to Pope Alexander VI, a Valencian born Borgia with close ties to the Aragonese crown. In May 1493 he issued several bulls, including Inter caetera (issued in two versions, dated 3 and 4 May), which granted Castile rights over lands discovered or to be discovered west of a line drawn 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde, provided they were not already held by a Christian ruler. A later bull, Dudum siquidem of September 1493, extended Castilian rights further. These documents rested on a medieval theory that the papacy had authority to grant dominion over non-Christian lands, and they asserted that the purpose of the grant was the conversion of inhabitants. Neither the peoples of the Caribbean nor the other European states had been consulted.

John II protested that the bulls violated Portuguese rights and negotiated directly with the Castilian monarchs. At Tordesillas on 7 June 1494 the two crowns agreed to move the line to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Lands and seas to the east of the line would be open to Portugal, those to the west to Castile. The agreement also included provisions for Castilian ships to pass through Portuguese waters on the way west and for a joint expedition to mark the line. Castile ratified in July and Portugal in September 1494, and Pope Julius II confirmed the treaty in 1506. The move westward gave Portugal control of the South Atlantic routes to the Cape and later brought the eastern bulge of Brazil within its half. Whether John II knew of lands there is debated, and the evidence is insufficient to settle it.

The treaty is often described as the division of the world between Spain and Portugal. That description overstates what it did. The agreement bound only two parties. France, England, and later the Dutch did not accept the papal grants, and Indigenous and African societies were not bound by them. The line could not be located with precision, because longitude could not be measured and the length of a league was uncertain, and it generated later disputes over the Moluccas. The treaty is better understood as a legal and diplomatic assertion of claims, valuable to the two crowns in their relations with each other and later with the Church, but distinct from effective control, which remained limited to coasts, forts, and islands.

Historical Vocabulary
Papal Bull and Line of Demarcation

A papal bull is a formal document issued by a pope, named for the lead seal (bulla) attached to it. The bulls of 1452, 1455, 1481, and 1493 gave religious and legal sanction to Iberian claims and conquests, reflecting the papacy's medieval claim to authority over Christendom and, in the argument of the time, over non-Christian lands. A line of demarcation is a boundary dividing zones of claimed jurisdiction. The Tordesillas line was an imaginary north-south meridian at sea, and it differed from a frontier that was surveyed, defended, and administered. Students should not imagine that either bulls or demarcation lines were recognized by every European or non-European power.

Division V · Sections 68.22–68.29

Oceanic Voyages and the Expansion of European Geographical Knowledge

This is the chapter's principal narrative climax. The Portuguese route around Africa and the Castilian voyages across the Atlantic were historically related but distinct developments, resting on different assumptions, knowledge, and encounters.
Section 68.22

Bartolomeu Dias and the Passage around Southern Africa

By the 1480s Portuguese captains had followed the African coast to the Congo and beyond, but whether the continent could be rounded was unknown. John II pursued two lines of inquiry at once. In 1487 he sent two men, Pêro da Covilhã and Afonso de Paiva, overland through Cairo to gather information about the Indian Ocean trade and the Christian kingdom of Ethiopia. Covilhã traveled to Aden and then on a ship to the Malabar coast, visited Calicut and Goa, and sent word back to Cairo in about 1490 or 1491 that the Indian Ocean could be reached from the south by ship. He later went to Ethiopia, where the ruler kept him in the country. His reports, together with Dias's voyage, gave the Portuguese crown the information needed to plan a voyage to India.

Dias left Lisbon in August 1487 with two caravels and a supply ship. Sailing south along the coast of what is now Namibia, he met a strong storm that blew the ships far to the south for about two weeks. When he turned east and then north, he found land on the east side of the continent in early 1488, at a bay named Aguada de São Brás, now Mossel Bay. The coast ran northeast, showing that Africa had been rounded. Local Khoekhoe herders, whom the Portuguese met at the watering place, were alarmed by the visitors, and a confrontation ended with violence and the death of at least one African. Dias went on as far as the Great Fish River region, but his crews pressed for return, and he agreed. On the way back he saw the Cape itself, which he is said to have called the Cape of Storms, and John II renamed it the Cape of Good Hope. Dias reached Lisbon in December 1488.

The voyage proved that the Atlantic and Indian Oceans were connected by sea, but a follow up expedition was delayed. The king was preoccupied with dynastic troubles, notably the death of his only legitimate son in 1491, and with Morocco, and he also had to consider Columbus's competing proposal. Gama's voyage did not occur until 1497, under John's successor Manuel I.

Section 68.23

Christopher Columbus, Geographical Assumptions, and Castilian Sponsorship

Christopher Columbus was born in Genoa around 1451, worked in Genoese commerce, and after a shipwreck off Portugal around 1476 settled in Lisbon. He sailed to Madeira, probably to the Guinea coast, and in the North Atlantic, and he married Filipa Moniz Perestrelo, the daughter of the first captain of Porto Santo, which gave him connections to Portuguese Atlantic circles. By the early 1480s he had developed a project to reach the East by sailing west. His plan rested on a geographical argument. He believed the earth was smaller than most scholars thought, relied on ancient authorities and medieval writers who estimated the eastward extent of Asia generously, and probably misapplied units of measurement, so that the distance from the Canaries to Japan (Cipangu) seemed to him about 3,000 nautical miles when it is far greater.

Portuguese experts, who had long experience of Atlantic navigation and more accurate estimates of the earth's size, advised John II against the proposal when Columbus presented it in about 1484, and the king declined. Columbus then went to Castile. A commission of learned men, convened by Isabella, examined the proposal over several years and was unconvinced, again on the grounds that the distance to Asia was greater than he claimed. They were right, and the voyage succeeded only because an unknown continent lay in the way. Columbus continued his campaign through the late 1480s and early 1490s, helped by Franciscans at La Rábida, by the Andalusian seafaring families of Palos, and by court officials such as the Aragonese treasurer Luis de Santángel, a man of converso family.

Political circumstances changed in 1492. The conquest of Granada freed the crown's attention and finances, Portugal's progress under Dias made the rivalry more pressing, and the monarchs agreed to sponsor the voyage on terms set out in the Capitulations of Santa Fe (17 April 1492). Columbus would receive the titles of Admiral of the Ocean Sea and viceroy and governor of any lands he found, together with a share of the revenue, which he sought to make hereditary. The expedition was financed by the crown with contributions from Andalusian investors and a loan arranged through Santángel. The agreement reflects the monarchs' view that this was a relatively modest gamble with large potential reward.

Section 68.24

The First Columbian Voyage, 1492–1493

Columbus left Palos on 3 August 1492 with three ships, the Santa María, the Pinta, and the Niña, and about ninety men. Martín Alonso Pinzón and Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, prominent mariners of the region, captained the Pinta and the Niña, and their local standing helped recruit crews. The fleet stopped in the Canaries for repairs and left La Gomera on 6 September. Taking the northeast trades, it sailed west for about five weeks. On 12 October (Julian calendar), land was sighted in the Bahamas. The island was called Guanahani by its inhabitants, and Columbus renamed it San Salvador. Its identification with a modern island is debated.

The fleet visited several Bahamian islands and then reached the northeast coast of Cuba on 28 October. Columbus believed he was near Japan or the Chinese mainland and looked for evidence of the Great Khan's realm. In early December he reached Hispaniola (Haiti), which he named Española. The Pinta left the fleet to search for gold on its own, and on the night of 24 to 25 December the Santa María ran aground off the north coast of Hispaniola. With help from the local chief Guacanagarí, Columbus used its timbers to build a small fort, La Navidad, and left about thirty-nine men there. He sailed for Spain in the Niña in January 1493, rejoined by the Pinta, and survived severe storms. He reached the Azores and then Lisbon on 4 March, where he was received by John II, and arrived at Palos on 15 March. He took a few captive Taíno, gold ornaments, plants, and parrots to Barcelona to meet the monarchs.

Columbus's skill in dead reckoning and his use of the wind system were remarkable. His original log survives only in an abstract made by Bartolomé de las Casas, and its text mixes Columbus's words with the editor's, a limitation historians must keep in mind. Columbus never accepted that he had found a new continent, and he died convinced that he had reached the outskirts of Asia. The voyage's historical significance came not from his intentions but from the sustained connection it began.

Section 68.25

The Caribbean's Indigenous Peoples and the First Encounters

The people Columbus met in the Bahamas and the Greater Antilles are known as the Taíno, a name applied to the Arawakan speaking communities of Hispaniola, Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, and the Bahamas (where they are also called Lucayans). They were not a single polity. Hispaniola was divided among several chiefdoms (cacicazgos) led by caciques, such as Guacanagarí in the north, Caonabo in the interior, and Anacaona and her brother Behecchio in the west. Communities lived in villages of round houses around plazas, farmed cassava in raised mounds (conucos), grew maize, sweet potatoes, and other crops, fished, and traveled by large dugout canoes. Religious life centered on spirit beings and ancestral figures called zemís, and ritual specialists mediated with them. The Spanish friar Ramón Pané, who lived on Hispaniola in the 1490s and wrote an account of Taíno beliefs at Columbus's request, is the earliest European source on this subject, and it remains valuable though filtered through his translators and purposes. Archaeology, including excavations at the sites of early Spanish settlement and Taíno villages, supplements the documents.

Population estimates for Hispaniola in 1492 vary enormously, from the low hundreds of thousands to over a million, and the question remains disputed. All agree that numbers fell catastrophically in the following decades, a subject for Chapter 69. Taíno words that entered European languages, including canoe, hammock, hurricane, and barbecue, record the contact.

The first encounters were varied. Columbus's own writings and those of Spanish chroniclers describe generous hospitality, exchange of gifts, and curiosity, though what these gestures meant to Taíno participants must be inferred from their wider culture, in which gift exchange and display of hospitality had political and religious significance. The Spaniards, however, sought gold, food, and labor, and they treated the islands as possessions of the Castilian crown. When Columbus returned in 1493, La Navidad had been destroyed and its garrison killed, evidently as a result of conflicts caused by the Spaniards' demands and abuses. From 1494 and 1495 the colonists imposed tribute in gold and cotton, enslaved captives, and fought wars of conquest, including a battle in March 1495 at the Vega Real in which a Spanish force with horses and dogs defeated a larger Taíno army. Taíno leaders adopted varied responses of alliance, negotiation, flight, and resistance. This pattern of exchange turning into coercion is the beginning of the transition examined in the next section.

Section 68.26

The Later Voyages of Columbus and the Beginnings of Colonial Settlement

The second voyage (1493–1496) was a colonizing expedition: seventeen ships carried well over a thousand men, together with animals, seeds, tools, and priests. The fleet passed through the Lesser Antilles, where it met Kalinago communities, and reached Hispaniola to find La Navidad destroyed. Columbus founded a new settlement, La Isabela, in early 1494. The colonists suffered from disease, shortage of food, and conflict, and many were disillusioned that gold was less plentiful than promised. Columbus explored Cuba and Jamaica, and in 1495 he shipped around five hundred Taíno captives to Spain for sale, a significant number of whom died on the voyage. The monarchs hesitated to approve this enslavement of people they regarded as new subjects, and the issue of the legal and moral status of Indigenous people began a debate that continued for decades.

On the third voyage (1498–1500), Columbus sailed farther south and reached Trinidad and the coast of what is now Venezuela, where the huge outflow of the Orinoco River suggested to him an enormous landmass. Meanwhile, the settlers on Hispaniola, now centered on Santo Domingo, founded by Columbus's brother Bartholomew, were divided by factional rebellion. Complaints reached the court, and the crown sent Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrested Columbus and his brothers and sent them to Spain in chains in 1500. Columbus was released but lost the governorship. In 1502 Nicolás de Ovando arrived as governor with some 2,500 colonists, and he organized the colony under royal authority, distributing Indigenous people to Spanish settlers in a system of forced labor that became the encomienda.

On his fourth voyage (1502–1504), Columbus explored the Caribbean coast of Central America from Honduras to Panama in search of a passage to Asia. His ships were damaged by shipworm, and he was stranded in Jamaica for about a year before being rescued. He died in Valladolid in 1506. During these years other captains, including Alonso de Ojeda, Rodrigo de Bastidas, Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, and the Florentine Amerigo Vespucci, explored the northern coast of South America, and the English crown sponsored John Cabot's voyage from Bristol in 1497 to the North Atlantic coast. By about 1504, the Castilian presence on Hispaniola and nearby islands had shifted from exploration to colonial domination, with settlement, forced labor, and a developing administration. The conquest of the mainland Americas, led by figures such as Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro, belongs to Chapter 69.

Section 68.27

Vasco da Gama and the Ocean Route to India

Manuel I, who became king of Portugal in 1495, decided to continue the program interrupted by John II's death and sent Vasco da Gama, a nobleman and soldier from Sines, to reach India. The fleet of four vessels, with roughly 170 men, left Lisbon on 8 July 1497. It used the great loop through the South Atlantic described in Section 68.5, spent more than three months at sea out of sight of land, rounded the Cape in November, and reached the coast of Natal at Christmas. The principal narrative, the Roteiro attributed to Álvaro Velho, is a participant's account, and it is valuable though terse.

Beyond the Cape lay the Swahili coast, a chain of cosmopolitan trading cities, including Mozambique Island, Kilwa, Mombasa, and Malindi, linked to Arabia, Persia, India, and the interior of Africa. The Portuguese were unfamiliar to them, and relations were mixed. At Mozambique and Mombasa the Portuguese encountered suspicion and, after a conflict, used force, including cannon. At Malindi, whose ruler was a rival of Mombasa, they were received more favorably and obtained a pilot who guided them across the Indian Ocean with the monsoon, a voyage of about three weeks. Later tradition identified the pilot as the Arab navigator Ahmad ibn Majid, but that identification is disputed, and the pilot was probably a Gujarati or other local expert. The significance is that the crossing depended on Indian Ocean knowledge.

The fleet arrived at Calicut on 20 May 1498. The city, ruled by the Zamorin, was a major entrepôt, frequented by Arab, Persian, Gujarati, and local Malabari Muslim merchants, and by merchants from Southeast Asia and China. The Portuguese initially thought that its Hindu temples were churches, and they assumed that its people were Christians of a kind, a mistake recorded in the Roteiro. Gama had little to offer: the gifts he brought were deemed poor, and he was unable to establish a trading agreement on the terms he wished. The Zamorin received him courteously but was under pressure from Muslim merchants who saw the newcomers as competitors. Gama left in August with a cargo of spices and gems bought or acquired in a hostile atmosphere.

The return voyage was disastrous: contrary winds, scurvy, and deaths reduced the crews drastically. Gama reached Lisbon in September 1499 with two ships and a small fraction of the men. Later chroniclers claimed that the cargo yielded enormous profit, but the figures are uncertain. What mattered was the demonstrated viability of the ocean route. The consequences, including the Portuguese attempt to impose force and control on Indian Ocean trade, belong to Chapter 71.

Section 68.28

Pedro Álvares Cabral, Brazil, and the Expansion of Portuguese Routes

Manuel I followed Gama's voyage with a much larger fleet. Pedro Álvares Cabral left Lisbon on 9 March 1500 with thirteen ships and over a thousand men, among them experienced navigators and Franciscan priests. The ships swung far to the southwest in the South Atlantic, and on 22 April they sighted a hill on the coast of what is now Brazil, which Cabral named Monte Pascoal. The fleet anchored in the area of Porto Seguro and stayed about ten days. The Portuguese met Tupiniquim people, who were part of the Tupi speaking societies of the coast, and exchanged gifts. The scribe Pêro Vaz de Caminha wrote a detailed letter to the king describing the land and its inhabitants, which remained in the archives and was not published until the nineteenth century. Cabral claimed the land for Portugal as the Island of Vera Cruz and sent a ship home with the news.

Whether Cabral's landfall was accidental or intended is debated. Portuguese pilots knew that the wide arc of the South Atlantic brought ships close to the coast of South America, and some historians suspect earlier Portuguese knowledge of the area, while others consider the landfall an unplanned result of the route. Spanish voyages of 1499 and early 1500, including that of Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, had already reached the northern Brazilian coast. The evidence does not settle the question. The coast lay on the Portuguese side of the Tordesillas line as measured by Portuguese interpretation, and in the following years Portugal sent expeditions to survey it. Its principal early product was brazilwood, a source of red dye, and settlement did not begin in earnest until the 1530s, a subject for Chapters 71 and 72.

Cabral's fleet continued to India but suffered severe losses in storms off the Cape of Good Hope in May 1500, in which four ships were lost, including that of Bartolomeu Dias. The remaining vessels reached Mozambique, Kilwa, and Malindi, and then Calicut in September. After initial negotiations, relations broke down: Portuguese seizure of a merchant ship and Muslim merchants' hostility led to an attack in December on the Portuguese trading post, in which dozens of Portuguese were killed, and Cabral responded by bombarding the city. He moved on to Cochin and Cannanore, whose rulers were willing to trade, and returned to Lisbon in 1501 with a cargo of spices. The episode set a pattern in which Portuguese force and local politics collided, and it introduced the first Portuguese alliances with Indian rulers hostile to Calicut.

Section 68.29

New Coastlines, New Maps, and the Reconfiguration of European Geography

European geographical knowledge around 1490 was based on a combination of medieval world maps, portolan charts, and the recovered text of Ptolemy's Geography, translated into Latin in the early fifteenth century and printed in the 1470s. These sources did not predict the Americas. The Nuremberg globe made by Martin Behaim in 1492, before Columbus's return, shows an Atlantic with Japan and Asia across it and no intervening continent. Between 1492 and 1507, voyages produced new information that mapmakers struggled to integrate.

The chart by Juan de la Cosa, a Castilian pilot and shipowner who sailed with Columbus, dated about 1500, is the earliest surviving map to show the Caribbean and parts of the American coasts together with Africa and Europe. It depicts Cuba as an island and includes a figure of Saint Christopher, which some interpret as a reference to Columbus. The Cantino planisphere, produced in Lisbon in 1502 and obtained for the Duke of Ferrara by an Italian agent, shows the Tordesillas line, the African coast in detail, the coast of Brazil, and India. The map by Martin Waldseemüller, published in 1507 at Saint-Dié in Lorraine, together with his companion text, argued that the lands of the west were a new continent and proposed the name "America" after Amerigo Vespucci. The attribution to Vespucci of the discovery of a continent rests partly on writings whose authenticity is contested, and Columbus himself never accepted the idea.

Recognition of the Americas as a separate landmass was therefore gradual. In the years around 1500, many European writers still referred to the western lands as Asia or as islands off Asia, and the question was not settled in the minds of all until the Pacific crossings of the following decades, a topic reserved for later chapters. The maps also depended on diverse sources: pilots' logs, African and Indian Ocean guides, Indigenous informants, and information collected in ports. The spread of printed maps and letters, discussed in Chapter 55, accelerated the circulation of this information through Europe and allowed rival powers, including France and England, to enter the field. The transformation of European cosmography around 1500 was a result of experience, and it forced scholars to modify authorities they had considered definitive.

Maritime Expansion and the Foundations of an Atlantic System

The final division synthesizes the chapter's developments without becoming a premature history of colonial empires, which are the subject of Chapters 69 through 73.
Section 68.30

Ports, Merchants, Monarchies, and the Organization of Maritime Expansion

Sustained expansion required institutions. In Portugal, Lisbon became the hub. The royal shipyards (Ribeira das Naus), the customs house, and offices such as the Casa da Guiné and, after 1500, the Casa da Índia, organized the outfitting of ships, the storage and sale of goods, the collection of royal duties, and the management of gold, spices, and enslaved people. The crown acted as shipowner, licensor, creditor, and merchant. Manuel I's revenues from Guinea and India helped finance monuments such as the Jerónimos Monastery in Belém, an example of how maritime wealth entered Portuguese public culture.

In Castile, Seville on the Guadalquivir, with its Genoese and other foreign merchants, was the principal base. After the first Columbian voyages, the crown created the Casa de Contratación in Seville in 1503 to license ships, register cargoes and passengers, collect royal revenue, and later supervise pilots and charts. This institution reflected the monarchs' wish to control access to the Indies, and it gave Seville a legal monopoly that made it the single most important Castilian port for the trade.

Finance was international. Genoese families such as the Centurione and Spinola, Florentines such as Bartolomeo Marchionni and Giannotto Berardi, Flemish merchants, and from the early sixteenth century German houses such as the Welsers and Fuggers invested in sugar, trade goods, and ships. Converso merchants played significant roles in both kingdoms, despite persecution. Risk was high: shipwreck, disease, and piracy meant that many ventures failed, and the crown's role as a guarantor and organizer helped investors. Provisioning, particularly of biscuit, wine, and water, depended on agricultural regions of Portugal, Madeira, and the Azores. Ports and merchants, not just monarchs and captains, therefore made expansion practical, and the interests of royal officials, merchant firms, and settlers did not always coincide.

Section 68.31

The Opening of the Atlantic: Connections, Inequalities, and Historical Consequences

By 1500, durable oceanic connections linked Europe with West and West Central Africa, the Atlantic islands, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean. Regular fleets sailed between Lisbon and Elmina, Lisbon and India, and Seville and Hispaniola. The connections were unequal in different ways. Portuguese and Castilian crowns claimed authority far beyond their power, and in most places on the African coast, Portugal held only forts and depended on African rulers and merchants. In the Caribbean, by contrast, the Spanish quickly imposed forced labor and tribute and began settling. In the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese entered a world of established powers and, by using armed ships, would soon try to alter its terms.

African and Indigenous agency was therefore central, not marginal. Akan, Wolof, Mandinka, Edo, and Kongo rulers and merchants shaped prices, access, and diplomacy. Canarian communities resisted for nearly a century. Taíno leaders negotiated, fought, and allied with or fled the newcomers. These responses were not enough to prevent conquest in the Canaries and the Caribbean, but they limited Portuguese power in much of Africa, and they influenced the forms that European settlement took.

The chapter leaves several transitions for later treatment. In the Americas, exploration was becoming conquest, a process that Chapter 69 examines along with the demographic catastrophe and ecological exchange that accompanied it. Experiments with island sugar and coerced labor were evolving toward the transatlantic slave trade and plantations treated in Chapter 70. The Portuguese attempt to dominate the Indian Ocean trade is the subject of Chapter 71. Back in Europe, the new connections began to alter commerce, finance, and knowledge, themes that Chapter 73 develops.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Atlantic Voyages as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on patterns in the surviving evidence. They are not quotations from any named person, and the groups did not have equal power or share responsibility for the violence of the period equally.

Portuguese and Castilian participants (crowns, nobles, merchants, sailors): For monarchs, expansion promised revenue, prestige, and religious legitimacy. For nobles it offered lordships and plunder. For merchants and financiers it meant new markets and risk. For ordinary sailors and settlers it meant wages, shares, or pardon in exchange for danger, disease, and hardship. Their views were never unified, and they competed with one another.
African rulers and merchants: For rulers in Senegambia, the Gold Coast, Benin, and Kongo, Portuguese ships were a new source of trade goods, weapons, and allies, but also a potential threat. They set terms, demanded gifts and tolls, restricted what could be sold, and sometimes used violence against intruders. Merchants whose networks ran inland saw the coast as one outlet among several. The Portuguese were a new party within an established system.
Indigenous Canarian communities: Canarians faced raids from the fourteenth century and decades of conquest in the fifteenth. Chiefs fought, negotiated, or allied with the invaders depending on circumstance. For many people the result was enslavement, epidemic disease, displacement, and cultural loss, while others were absorbed into the settler society. Their own accounts survive only indirectly.
Taíno and other Caribbean peoples: Taíno communities encountered visitors who initially received hospitality and then demanded gold, food, labor, and obedience. Chiefs differed in response, some seeking accommodation and others armed resistance. Within a few years the newcomers had imposed tribute and enslavement. The evidence for Taíno views is largely mediated by Spanish writers and archaeology.
Enslaved and captive people: Captives taken from Africa and the islands had no say in the voyages that moved them, and their perspective is nearly absent from the record. What survives is their presence in Portuguese households, on Madeiran and island estates, and in the accounts of those who sold them.
Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Sustained Atlantic Expansion Became Possible

Long-term causes

The Iberian crusading frontier, Mediterranean commercial and navigational traditions, demand for West African gold, accumulated knowledge of Atlantic coasting, and the growth of Atlantic ports and shipbuilding.

Short-term causes

The Portuguese dynastic crisis and the Avis monarchy's need for conquest and legitimacy, the capture of Ceuta, the competition between Portuguese and Castilian crowns, the availability of Genoese and other capital, and the discovery of workable return routes.

Event or transformation

Between the 1410s and the 1500s, Iberian ships established regular connections with Atlantic islands, African coasts, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean.

Immediate effects

Island plantation economies, a growing trade in enslaved Africans, fortified trading posts, Canarian conquest, Spanish settlement in the Caribbean, and treaties dividing claims.

Long-term consequences

The conquest of the Americas, the transatlantic slave trade, Portuguese and later Dutch and other European commercial empires, new flows of precious metals and commodities, and the reorientation of European economies and diplomacy toward the Atlantic.

Historical complexity

No single factor suffices. Political sponsorship without navigational knowledge, or knowledge without capital, or capital without African and Indigenous partners or victims, would not have produced the outcome. Expansion reinforced itself as each success increased investment, information, and rivalry.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Established Networks to Transoceanic Routes

What Changed

  • Europeans gained direct maritime access to West African gold and to the Indian Ocean, bypassing the Sahara and the eastern Mediterranean
  • Island colonies tested sugar plantations, coerced labor, and chartered captaincies
  • Sustained European contact began with the Caribbean and the Brazilian coast
  • Crowns developed institutions such as the Casa da Guiné and the Casa de Contratación to regulate ocean trade

What Stayed the Same

  • Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade continued, and African and Asian merchants kept control of most of their commerce
  • Slavery had existed in Iberia, Africa, and the Mediterranean before the Atlantic voyages
  • Crusading ideals, noble ambition, and dynastic rivalry continued to motivate rulers
  • Navigation still relied on winds, currents, and the accumulated knowledge of pilots

Who experienced change most directly: Canarian and Taíno communities, enslaved Africans, and coastal African societies adjacent to Portuguese posts. Who experienced greater continuity: inland African and Asian merchants, and most Iberian peasants.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Atlantic opening was an extension of existing systems that created new, unequal connections, not a break with all earlier history.

Section 68.32

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The chapter's chronology shows a sequence of overlapping phases. From the 1410s to the 1440s, Portugal moved from North African conquest to island settlement and coastal voyages. From the 1440s to the 1470s, expeditions along the African coast mixed raiding with trade and established links to Senegambia and Upper Guinea. From the 1470s to the 1490s, the crowns organized and fortified the trade, signed treaties, and reached the Gold Coast, Kongo, and the Cape. From 1492 to 1500, Castilian and Portuguese voyages opened the Caribbean and India, and a treaty attempted to apportion the results. Throughout, navigation, finance, coercion, and diplomacy reinforced one another.

Three themes tie the sections together. The first is that expansion depended on knowledge drawn from many traditions, and on partners and intermediaries from several continents. The second is that coercion, including raiding, enslavement, conquest, and forced labor, was present from the beginning, though it took different forms in different places and was often limited by African power. The third is that legal and diplomatic claims advanced faster than effective control, a distinction the treaties and bulls make clear.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the opening of the Atlantic was a cumulative and contested transformation, produced by the interaction of political ambition, existing commercial systems, technical knowledge, African and Indigenous societies, and changing maritime capabilities. It began from the recognition that the Atlantic was already an inhabited and partly connected space, bordered by fishing communities, linked to the trans-Saharan gold trade, and home to Canarian and other island peoples.

The political starting point was Iberia. Portugal's succession crisis of 1383–1385 brought the Avis dynasty to power with urban and mercantile support, and the capture of Ceuta in 1415 expressed crusading, chivalric, and commercial ambitions. Ceuta's disappointing returns and the failure at Tangier in 1437 encouraged attention to the Atlantic. Castile, a larger composite monarchy united dynastically with Aragon in 1469, brought Andalusian ports, Genoese capital, and the Canary conquest to the contest, while the final phase of the Granada war absorbed its energies until 1492.

Navigation and shipbuilding made expansion practicable. Portuguese pilots learned the wind system, developed the volta do mar, and used caravels and larger ships, while portolan charts, the compass, the mariner's astrolabe, and astronomical tables, drawing on Mediterranean, Islamic, and Jewish learning, permitted voyages beyond sight of land. Logistics, notably provisioning and disease, constrained range, and islands functioned as waystations and as experimental colonies.

Island settlement produced both economic models and human costs. Madeira and São Tomé developed sugar economies using foreign capital and, increasingly, enslaved labor. The Azores supplied grain, woad, and a mid-Atlantic base. The Canaries were conquered by Castile after prolonged Indigenous resistance, with enslavement and epidemic disease devastating the Guanche population, and Cape Verde became a hub linking the islands to the African mainland.

On the African coast, Portuguese activity moved from raids and captive taking to trade, fortification, and diplomacy. African states and merchants in Senegambia, the Gold Coast, Benin, and Kongo set the terms of exchange, limited Portuguese power, and drew the newcomers into existing networks. Elmina, built in 1482 with the consent of a local ruler, symbolized both Portuguese ambition and its dependence on African partners. The sale of captives at Lagos in 1444 and the later trade in enslaved people show that coercion accompanied exploration from the start.

The crowns sought to organize and defend expansion by monopolies, contracts, institutions, and treaties. Prince Henry was an important patron but not the founder of a school of navigation, and later kings, especially John II, centralized control. The Treaty of Alcáçovas in 1479, the papal bulls of 1493, and the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494 expressed claims to maritime jurisdiction. They bound only the two Iberian crowns and did not translate into effective control over distant coasts and peoples.

The voyages of 1487 to 1500 made the largest geographical difference. Dias rounded southern Africa, Columbus's four voyages established lasting contact with the Caribbean and the mainland coasts of the Americas, Gama reached Calicut through an Indian Ocean already thick with commerce, and Cabral landed in Brazil before proceeding to India. In the Caribbean, hospitality and exchange quickly gave way to tribute, enslavement, and war, and the transition from exploration to colonial domination was underway by 1500. Maps such as those of La Cosa, Cantino, and Waldseemüller registered a growing, though gradual, recognition that the Americas were a distinct landmass.

The chapter's larger significance is that it identifies the mechanisms and first encounters of overseas expansion without exhausting its consequences. Chapter 69 will examine conquest and ecological exchange in the Americas, Chapter 70 Atlantic slavery, Chapter 71 the Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires, Chapter 72 British and French expansion, and Chapter 73 the transformation of Europe itself.

Avis dynasty

The technique of sailing far out into the Atlantic to find westerly winds for the return to Portugal.

Latitude
Position north or south of the equator, which navigators estimated by measuring the Pole Star or the noon Sun.

Estimating a ship's position from heading, time, and judged speed, used because longitude could not be measured.

A simplified astrolabe used at sea to measure the altitude of the Sun or stars.

A hereditary grant of governing and economic rights over a territory, used in Madeira, the Azores, and Cape Verde.

An irrigation channel carrying water across Madeira's terrain.
Guanche
Name originally for the people of Tenerife and often applied to the Indigenous Canarians as a whole.
Mencey
A chief or ruler on Tenerife.

A trading post or factory run by agents of the crown or a company, not necessarily a colony.

An exclusive right to a trade claimed by the crown and exercised directly or through contractors.

A Portuguese or Cape Verdean trader who settled among African communities on the Upper Guinea coast.

Title of the ruler of the Kongo kingdom.

Title of the ruler of the kingdom of Benin.

Portuguese military and religious order, governed by Prince Henry, which helped finance and legitimate expansion.

A Jew or descendant of Jews who had converted to Christianity, often under pressure.

The agreement of 17 April 1492 setting Columbus's titles and shares for his first voyage.

Arawakan speaking peoples of the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas, organized in chiefdoms led by caciques.

A Taíno chief, from a Taíno word adopted by Spanish.

The Castilian agency created in Seville in 1503 to regulate trade with the Indies.
Roteiro
A navigator's account or sailing directions, notably the narrative of Gama's first voyage.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it identifies how a set of connections that now seem permanent began, and how contingent and unequal they were. The voyages of the fifteenth century joined regions that had previously been linked only indirectly, and they did so through institutions, technologies, and violence that would shape the next several centuries. The chapter's central point is that these outcomes were produced by many hands: kings and financiers, pilots and sailors, African rulers and merchants, and Indigenous peoples who resisted, negotiated, or were overwhelmed.

The chapter also reveals continuities. Iberian crusading, Mediterranean commerce, existing slavery, and Islamic and Jewish learning all fed into the Atlantic enterprise. Island experiments with sugar and coerced labor were carried forward to Brazil and the Caribbean, and the practice of claiming jurisdiction by treaty and papal grant shaped later European diplomacy. The Atlantic route to India and the beginnings of Spanish colonization provided the basis for Chapters 69 through 71.

For Volume III, the chapter shows that the Renaissance and Reformation were taking place within a Europe whose horizons and economies were being altered by overseas connections. States such as Portugal, Castile, and soon the Dutch Republic, England, and France would find in maritime commerce and empire new sources of revenue, rivalry, and ideology, themes that connect to the state formation examined in Part XII.

Finally, the chapter invites careful judgment. It would be a mistake to treat the Atlantic opening as a story of pure triumph or of inevitable domination. The African coast showed the limits of European power, the Caribbean the speed with which coercion could take hold, and the treaties the gap between claim and control. Chapter 69 now turns to the Spanish conquest of the Americas and the ecological and demographic transformations that followed.
