Diocletian, Constantine, and the Christian Empire

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 284 and 337 CE the Roman Empire was not restored to its second century form. It was rebuilt. Two emperors, Diocletian and Constantine, preserved Roman rule by changing how it worked: more officials, smaller provinces, several imperial courts, a more systematic tax system, a stable gold coin, a new eastern residence, and, under Constantine, an unprecedented relationship of imperial patronage and involvement with the Christian church.

In the winter of 302 to 303, according to the Christian writer Lactantius, the emperor Diocletian was at Nicomedia, a city in northwestern Anatolia that had become one of his principal residences. There, on an occasion when religious rites were being examined for signs of divine favor, some of the court's Christians were said to have made the sign of the cross, and the ritual was thought to have failed. Lactantius is a hostile and partisan witness, and historians treat the story with caution. But the setting it describes is real and revealing. The emperor who governed the Mediterranean world was not living in Rome. He lived in a provincial city on the road to the Persian frontier, surrounded by soldiers, officials, and priests, and he understood the welfare of the empire as dependent on the correct worship of the gods. Within a few weeks, imperial edicts would begin the most severe persecution of Christians the Roman state ever attempted. Within a decade, another emperor would interpret a civil war victory as won with the favor of the Christian God. Within a generation, that emperor would be founding a new city on the Bosporus and summoning bishops to sit at his table.

This chapter examines that extraordinary half century. It begins in 284, where Chapter 24 ended, with the acclamation of Diocletian by the army, and it ends in 337 with the death of Constantine. Chapter 24 showed that the Roman Empire could survive the political, military, and fiscal disasters of the third century but that its old arrangements repeatedly generated succession wars, frontier overextension, financial strain, and armies capable of making and unmaking emperors. Diocletian did not inherit a collapsed empire. He inherited a reunited empire whose weaknesses had been exposed, and his problem was not how to rebuild Rome from nothing but how to redesign government so that a single man did not have to command every frontier, control every army, supervise every tax collector, and secure every succession at once.

The answers of Diocletian and Constantine were partly administrative and partly political. Diocletian divided imperial power among colleagues, multiplied provinces and officials, reorganized taxation, reformed coinage, strengthened frontiers, and elaborated the ceremonial of the court. Constantine emerged from the civil wars that destroyed Diocletian's succession plan, reunified the empire under his own rule, introduced a durable gold coin, reshaped the army and the administrative hierarchy, and founded Constantinople. Above all, he gave Christianity legal protection and lavish patronage. The consequences of that decision would shape European history for many centuries, but they unfolded far more slowly and unevenly than later tradition suggested.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, the phrase "Christian Empire" in the chapter title does not mean an empire whose population had suddenly become Christian. It means an empire whose ruler increasingly supported Christianity and incorporated Christian institutions into imperial politics, while most of the empire's inhabitants, and many of its officials, remained attached to inherited forms of worship. The Christianization of society was a slower process, and its social, doctrinal, and institutional dimensions are the subject of Chapter 26. Second, the reforms of these two reigns should not be read as a single blueprint executed overnight. Late Roman government developed cumulatively across several decades, and much of what modern textbooks call "Diocletian's" or "Constantine's" system is known from evidence dating to later in the fourth century.

The evidence is rich but tendentious. Imperial laws survive in later codes, in inscriptions, and in papyri from Egypt that preserve the paperwork of provincial administration. Coins document imperial ideology and monetary policy. The Edict on Maximum Prices survives in fragments carved in stone across the eastern provinces. The narrative sources, however, are deeply shaped by religion. Lactantius, Eusebius of Caesarea, and other Christian authors wrote to explain persecution, victory, and divine providence, while pagan panegyrists spoke for emperors and the surviving traditional historians are often brief or later. Reading the period requires asking constantly whose interests a source serves.

Within Part IV, this chapter marks the point at which the late antique empire acquires its characteristic institutional form. Chapter 23 traced the rise of Christian communities within the Roman world, Chapter 24 the severe crisis and temporary fragmentation of the imperial system, and Chapter 26 will examine the church as a doctrinal, institutional, and social force. Chapter 25 links them by showing how emperors rebuilt the state and, in Constantine's case, changed the terms on which Christianity and imperial power met. Within Volume I it also prepares the ground for the migrations and political transformations of Chapters 27 and 28.

Learning Objectives

  2. Analyze the Tetrarchy as an experiment in both administration and succession, and explain why it failed politically after 305 while leaving lasting institutional legacies.
  3. Describe the reorganization of provinces, dioceses, taxation, and the imperial bureaucracy, while distinguishing Diocletianic and Constantinian measures from later developments.
  4. Evaluate the evidence for military reorganization under Diocletian and Constantine and explain why the later distinction between frontier and field armies should not be projected backward.

  8. Compare accounts of Constantine's rise, the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, and his religious development, distinguishing what historians can establish from what depends on Christian interpretation.

Historical Context

The empire Diocletian came to govern had been shaped by the developments described in Chapters 22 and 24. In the first and second centuries CE, the Principate had combined monarchical authority with the outward forms of a republican tradition, a mixed system governed by an emperor, a Senate, provincial governors drawn largely from the senatorial and equestrian orders, and cities whose local elites collected taxes and administered justice with limited direct supervision from imperial officials. The system worked well while the frontiers were stable, the coinage sound, and the succession settled by adoption or dynastic inheritance. From the 230s, it worked much less well. The Sasanian dynasty that replaced the Arsacids in Persia in the 220s proved a newly assertive and formidable eastern rival. Its early rulers strengthened royal authority while retaining powerful aristocratic families and important continuities with the Arsacid political order. Gothic, Alemannic, Frankish, and other groups pressed on the Danube and Rhine, sometimes as raiders, sometimes as parts of changing political groupings and coalitions whose internal cohesion varied. Emperors were proclaimed by armies and killed by armies with dizzying frequency. Two major regional imperial regimes, the Gallic regime in the west and Palmyrene power in the east, carried on effective government for a time before being suppressed in the 270s. Debasement of silver coinage, discussed in Chapter 24, damaged confidence in money, and monetary instability contributed to greater reliance on taxation and requisition in kind.

By 284 the empire had been reunified, but reunification revealed structural problems rather than solving them. An emperor could not be in the Rhine valley, on the lower Danube, and in Mesopotamia at once. Governors of large provinces commanded troops and controlled tax revenues, which made them dangerous when ambition and opportunity coincided. The army's role in acclaiming emperors meant that any successful general was a potential rival. The fiscal system depended on a patchwork of traditional taxes, ad hoc exactions, and local practice that provided uncertain support for a much larger army and court than the early Principate had known. The empire's greatest asset, its unified Mediterranean political space linking Europe, North Africa, and Anatolia and Syria under one legal order, remained intact, but holding it together required new methods.

Religious life formed a second essential context. Traditional Roman religion, in its many local variants, was not a creed but a system of cult, sacrifice, festival, and reciprocal obligation between communities and gods, and Roman authorities had long assumed that neglect of proper rites endangered public welfare. Judaism was tolerated as an ancestral tradition despite periods of conflict. Christianity, examined in Chapter 23, had grown from small urban communities into an empire wide network with bishops, shared texts, charitable institutions, and a distinctive refusal to participate in some public sacrifices. Persecution before the 250s had been sporadic and local, and the more systematic attacks under Decius and Valerian had been brief. A generation of comparative peace before 303 had allowed Christians to build churches openly in many cities, including Nicomedia, and to acquire property and visible social standing. Estimates of the Christian share of the population around 300 vary widely and rest on inference, not census data, and the proportion certainly varied by region, being higher in parts of Anatolia, Egypt, and North Africa than in much of the Latin west.

Finally, the geography of power had already changed. Emperors of the later third century campaigned incessantly and governed from wherever the frontier demanded, so that Rome, though unrivaled in prestige, was frequently unvisited. The senatorial aristocracy had lost much of its military and provincial command role, and equestrian and military officers of Balkan and Danubian origin filled the highest posts. Diocletian himself, like several of his predecessors, came from the Dalmatian coast region. The Rome of the Senate and the empire of the soldiers were increasingly different worlds, and the reforms of the following decades would largely be made in the second.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusually varied body of evidence. Egyptian papyri preserve census returns, tax receipts, petitions, and letters that let historians follow administration at village level, though Egypt was distinctive and cannot be assumed to represent every province. Inscriptions record imperial edicts, milestones, and dedications. Coinage reveals imperial messages and denominations. Archaeology documents fortifications, palaces at Split, Thessaloniki, Trier, and elsewhere, and the growth of Christian building after 312. The narrative accounts, from Lactantius, Eusebius, panegyrists, and later historians such as Aurelius Victor and Zosimus, are hostile or favorable according to the religious and political commitments of their authors.

Section 25.1

Diocletian and the Problem of Governing the Empire

The emperor Numerian died in 284 during the return of the army from a Persian campaign, and the officers gathered at Nicomedia acclaimed Diocletian, then commander of the imperial bodyguard. Ancient sources describe his rise as part of a chain of soldier emperors from the Balkan provinces, and his origins, on the Dalmatian coast in a family of modest standing, were typical of the men who had risen through the army. He was not, however, the sole claimant. Numerian's brother Carinus ruled the western provinces and was marching east. Diocletian's victory over him in 285, in a battle in the Balkans that Carinus was reported to have been winning before his own officers killed him, left Diocletian the only emperor. From that first year, his career shows a pattern that would recur: he was willing to share power with trusted colleagues, and he preferred to make use of loyal officers rather than depend on the traditional senatorial hierarchy.

His problem, and it is worth stating precisely, was structural. The empire stretched from Britain to Egypt and from Spain to the Euphrates, and the frontiers that mattered most, the Rhine, the upper and middle Danube, the lower Danube and Black Sea approaches, and Mesopotamia and Armenia, were separated by weeks of travel. Emperors who spent a year on the Rhine risked losing control of the east, and generals delegated to command elsewhere could be acclaimed by their own soldiers. Every third century solution had exposed a dilemma: concentrating authority in one man risked strategic failure, while delegating power risked usurpation.

Government at the center was also thin in relation to the scale of the task. In the second century, an empire of tens of millions of people had been administered by a small imperial staff, provincial governors with modest offices, and the cities themselves. That arrangement depended on cooperative local elites, a comparatively lean central civilian administration, and a military establishment smaller than the enlarged forces required by the later third century empire. By the 280s the army was larger, the frontier zones more heavily garrisoned, and the state's demands for supplies, transport, and manpower far more insistent. The fiscal system, meanwhile, had been battered by debased coinage and the wartime improvisation of requisitions, leaving the state unsure how much revenue it could count on and its subjects unsure how much they would be asked to give.

What Diocletian did in response should not be read as a complete plan announced in 284. His reign lasted twenty one years, and the reforms associated with him appear in a sequence of measures, some reactive, some experimental, and some possibly not his own. The Tetrarchy of 293 followed the two emperor system of 286, and taxation, coinage, and provincial arrangements evolved through the 290s and early 300s. Later writers, especially the hostile Lactantius, described him as a restless innovator who had multiplied officials and soldiers to a ruinous degree. Modern historians take the criticism seriously as evidence of contemporary costs, while recognizing that many elements of the system continued to develop under his successors.

Section 25.2

The Tetrarchy: Rule by Four

In 286, Diocletian raised his colleague Maximian, a soldier of similar background whom he had made Caesar the previous year, to the rank of Augustus. Two Augusti, one responsible primarily for the east and one for the west, now shared the imperial title. The arrangement solved an immediate problem of coordination and campaign, and it also created an expectation that imperial authority could be divided without the empire itself being divided. Laws continued to be issued in the names of all the emperors, coins bore their portraits and shared iconography, and official language spoke of the emperors as a single, fraternal college.

In 293 the system expanded. Each Augustus adopted a junior colleague with the title Caesar: Galerius for Diocletian and Constantius, usually called Constantius Chlorus, for Maximian. Each Caesar married into his Augustus's family, and each was expected to command armies and administer a region. The resulting four member college, known by the modern Greek term Tetrarchy, distributed imperial responsibility. Diocletian remained in the east, principally at Nicomedia and Antioch. Maximian operated from Milan, which sat closer than Rome to the Alpine passes and the Rhine and Danube frontiers. Galerius was posted to the Danubian region and, for a time, to the east, with Sirmium and later Thessalonica among his bases. Constantius was assigned to Gaul and Britain, with Trier as his principal residence. The Tetrarchs recovered Britain from the usurper Carausius and his successor Allectus in 296, defeated Alemannic and Frankish forces on the Rhine, and repaired the Danube frontier.

Historical Vocabulary
Tetrarchy, Augustus, and Caesar

Tetrarchy is a modern term, from the Greek for "rule of four," for the imperial college of two senior emperors (Augusti) and two junior emperors (Caesares) that Diocletian created between 286 and 293. Contemporaries did not use the term as a title. They spoke of the emperors as colleagues, sometimes brothers, bound by shared divine patronage and by ties of adoption and marriage. Under the system, the Caesar was not merely a deputy. He commanded troops, issued coinage, and was recognized as an emperor in his own right, while ranking below the Augustus.

Students should avoid two common misreadings. The Tetrarchy was not four independent states. The empire remained a legal unity, and imperial edicts were issued collectively. Nor was it a permanent division into east and west. The regional spheres were operational arrangements that could be, and were, adjusted. The system matters because it treated the problem of scale and the problem of succession together.

The Tetrarchy was as much a political experiment in succession as an administrative device. The evident intention was that when an Augustus retired or died, his Caesar would become Augustus and a new Caesar would be appointed. This would replace the murderous improvisation of the third century with a managed transfer of power. The system also aimed to reduce the appeal of dynastic inheritance by emphasizing merit, adoption, and divine selection, with Diocletian associated with Jupiter (Jovius) and Maximian with Hercules (Herculius), so that authority derived from the gods rather than from blood. The arrangement bound the colleagues into a shared ideology expressed in public monuments, such as the group of porphyry figures of embracing emperors now at the church of San Marco in Venice, and in ceremonial that emphasized concord.

At the same time, the plan carried its own contradictions, which became obvious only later. The two Augusti had biological sons and expectations of loyalty. The Caesars were commanders with armies. Provincial elites and soldiers could not easily be persuaded that lineage no longer mattered. The scheme worked while its architect remained in command, and Diocletian's prestige within the college was considerable. Whether it could survive his departure was a different question. Section 25.3

Provinces, Administration, Taxation, and Imperial Government

The most durable changes of the period concerned administration. One central development was the multiplication of provinces. Late third century governors had sometimes controlled vast territories, with large tax bases and substantial military forces, and such concentrations of resources had encouraged rebellion. Beginning under Diocletian, existing provinces were divided into smaller units, so that the number of provinces grew from around fifty to something over one hundred by the early fourth century, though modern estimates vary and the process continued after his reign. Smaller jurisdictions improved supervision, multiplied the number of officials who owed their appointments to the emperor, and reduced the capacity of any single governor to raise a regional revolt. They also increased the number of salaried offices and required a larger staff to serve them.

As the number of provinces increased, an intermediate layer of regional administration also emerged. By Constantine's reign, groups of provinces called dioceses, supervised by vicars or equivalent officials, are securely attested, and the term would later be adopted by the church for its own units. A list from about 314, known as the Verona List, records around a dozen dioceses, though its date has long been disputed. Historians continue to debate exactly when this diocesan structure was created and how much of it should be attributed to Diocletian rather than to his successors. The vicars answered to the praetorian prefects, who had originally been commanders of the imperial guard and whose role changed substantially under Constantine and his successors, when they became senior civil administrators with authority over large regional territories. The separation of civil and military command, in which provincial governors handled justice and taxation while military commanders directed troops, increased steadily across this period, though it was gradual, incomplete, and uneven, and should not be read as a single reform enacted on one date. It is also important not to project the fully developed administrative map of the later fourth century back onto 284.

Government at the center grew more elaborate. Imperial staffs, the palatine departments that served the emperors' households and correspondence, and the corps of officials who collected revenues and supplied the army expanded. Many of the new posts were held by equestrians and military men, and the senatorial order, though it retained wealth, prestige, and its place in the city of Rome, lost much of its former role in provincial and military command. The result was a hierarchy of imperial servants stretching from the palace to the provincial capital, with career paths, ranks, and privileges that would later be codified. Compared with the early empire, government was more visible and more demanding, and the person of the emperor was represented in more places.

Taxation was the largest single challenge. The state needed predictable revenue for the army, court, bureaucracy, transport network, and frontier defenses. Under Diocletian, and continuing under Constantine, the state moved toward more systematic assessment of taxable resources. Assessment employed fiscal units commonly described in the sources as iuga and capita. These related taxable land, productive capacity, persons, and labor in ways that varied between regions, and historians continue to debate precisely how the categories operated and interacted, and whether a single empire wide formula existed. Modern scholars use the labels iugatio and capitatio for the resulting system. Egyptian papyri show regular census cycles, and evidence for assessment appears in Italy, Asia Minor, and elsewhere, but the details varied. Payments were made in money and in kind, especially grain, fodder, clothing, and other supplies, through the system of the annona, which had originally referred to the grain supply of Rome and was extended to the supply of the army and officials. The wider point is that the state increasingly attempted to calculate resources in advance rather than exact them piecemeal.

Italy, which had traditionally been exempt from most direct taxation, was brought into the system, an unpopular measure that reduced the special standing of the peninsula. The reforms also increased the paperwork that fell on local government. City councils, whose members were the curiales, were responsible for collecting taxes and often for making up shortfalls from their own property. Some cities gained from the greater predictability, and some councillors gained positions within the imperial service that exempted them from local duty. But for many the burden grew, and later fourth century legislation would try repeatedly to prevent councillors from escaping their obligations. A stronger state was stronger for particular purposes and at a cost to particular groups, and the experience of it varied by wealth, region, and status.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Tax Assessment in an Egyptian Village, c. 300 CE

This is a composite reconstruction based on patterns visible in the surviving papyri, not an account of a specific documented event. Imagine a village in the Nile valley in the years after the new assessment cycle was introduced. Officials arrive to compile or update the registers. A landholder, whose family has farmed the same plots for generations, must declare the extent and quality of his fields and whatever else the assessors require, which might include persons or livestock, though practice varied and was not necessarily uniform. Village officials, who are themselves local residents with obligations to answer for the community's returns, confirm or dispute the entries. Neighbors watch each other's declarations, because an undervalued field elsewhere may mean a higher burden on the village as a whole.

When the register is complete, the household learns what it owes: a quantity of grain delivered to a state storehouse, perhaps some money, and possibly labor or the loan of a draft animal for transport. The grain must be carried to the river, weighed, and receipted, and the receipts, which survive in large numbers, are the household's only protection against a second demand. Petitions to officials, also preserved, show that mistakes, corruption, and disputes were common. The reform gave the state a clearer view of the village, and gave the village a clearer, and often heavier, view of the state.

Section 25.4

Army, Frontiers, and Imperial Defense

The army was the institution on which imperial survival most directly depended, and here caution is essential. A neat older formula holds that Diocletian created a frontier militia, the limitanei, and Constantine a mobile field army, the comitatenses. Modern scholarship rejects this as too simple. During the late third and early fourth centuries the imperial army increasingly developed two kinds of forces: regional garrisons tied to specific frontier sectors, and more mobile units that accompanied emperors or could be sent to crises. Constantine's reign clearly contributed to the growth of larger mobile forces, partly because his campaigns against fellow emperors required them. But the mature distinction found in later fourth century sources should not be projected backward as a single reform enacted on a single date.

Diocletian's reign did see a substantial increase in military manpower, though the numbers are uncertain. Lactantius claimed that the army had been multiplied several times over, an exaggeration made by a critic. Modern estimates of the size of the late third and early fourth century army vary considerably and rest on inference from unit lists, fortification building, and later sources. It is safe to say that frontier defenses were reinforced. Forts along the Rhine, the Danube, and the eastern desert routes were built or rebuilt, roads were repaired, and new supply depots were established. Recruitment relied on volunteers, sons of soldiers, conscription from rural communities, and the enrollment of groups from beyond the frontier, some of them settled within the empire as farmers obliged to provide troops. Logistics, in the form of the annona, was now inseparable from the tax system, so that military and fiscal reform were parts of one project.

The multiplication of emperors placed them near the frontiers they had to defend. A Caesar at Trier could respond to a Rhine crossing within days, an Augustus at Nicomedia could reach Armenia within weeks, and a colleague at Sirmium was close to the Danube. This is one reason the Tetrarchy worked militarily even as it failed dynastically. In the east, the crucial confrontation was with Persia. In 296 or 297 the Sasanian king Narseh, who had taken the throne after a period of dynastic struggle, invaded Roman Mesopotamia and Armenia. Galerius suffered a serious defeat, was reportedly humiliated by Diocletian, and then returned with reinforcements from the Danube to win a decisive victory in 298, capturing Narseh's camp and members of his family. The subsequent settlement, usually dated to 299, moved the frontier eastward, gave Rome control of several territories beyond the Tigris, and confirmed Roman influence over Armenia and neighboring Caucasian kingdoms, arrangements that lasted for decades. The episode shows that the reforms were designed in part to answer the multi frontier problem revealed in the third century, and that success in one theater depended on the ability to transfer forces from another.

Frontier policy also involved diplomacy and settlement. Rome dealt with groups beyond the Rhine and Danube through treaties, subsidies, the recruitment of fighters, and periodic punitive campaigns, not simply through walls and garrisons. Groups such as the Franks, Alemanni, Goths, and Sarmatians were not unified peoples but shifting networks of communities and war bands, and Roman policy influenced their internal politics as much as it responded to them. The consequences of these relationships for the later western empire are examined in Chapters 27 and 28.

Section 25.5

Money, Prices, Labor, and the Imperial Economy

Chapter 24 described how repeated debasement of the silver coinage undermined confidence in money and pushed the state toward payments in kind. Diocletian addressed the problem in stages. Around 294 the mints were reorganized, with more uniform production and new denominations, including a good quality silver coin, the argenteus, and a larger bronze piece with a thin silver wash. The reform brought more order to the coinage, but it did not stop inflation, and prices continued to rise because the state's spending needs remained large and because market participants, uncertain about the value of small denominations, set prices accordingly. Confidence in the new coins was not fully restored.

In 301 the emperors issued the Edict on Maximum Prices, an attempt to impose legal ceilings on the prices of thousands of goods and services and on the wages of various kinds of labor throughout the empire. The preamble justified the measure by blaming greedy merchants and profiteers, especially those who exploited soldiers, and the penalties were severe. Its schedule covered grain, wine, oil, meat, textiles, leather, timber, transport, and skilled and unskilled labor. It is one of the most remarkable documents to survive from the ancient world, and it reflects both the government's ambitions and the moral language with which it framed the problem. Evidence for the edict's enforcement is uneven, and it does not appear to have remained an effective long term instrument of price control. Hostile literary sources portray it as unsuccessful, but the surviving evidence does not allow historians to reconstruct enforcement uniformly across the empire. Modern explanations look beyond the edict's moral language about greed to monetary instability, state demand, transport costs, supply conditions, and regional variation.

Primary Source Window

The Edict on Maximum Prices, 301 CE

Context: The edict was issued at a time when the state was reforming its coinage and taxation and supplying a large army. It was posted publicly in cities, so that officials and subjects could consult it. The surviving copies come mostly from the eastern provinces, and the prices are expressed in a unit of account, the denarius, that was not a physical coin in circulation at the time.

Paraphrase of the preamble and schedules The preamble describes the emperors as guardians of the common welfare and complains that speculators, driven by insatiable greed, had raised prices to extreme levels, especially in the camps and markets where soldiers spent their pay. It announces fixed maximum prices and wages, threatens harsh penalties for violations, and appeals to the goodwill of the empire's subjects. The schedules that follow list maximum charges for a wide variety of goods and services, from staple foods and drink to clothing, transport, and the labor of scribes, teachers, and farm workers. This is a paraphrase, not a translation of the original text.

Analysis: The edict is valuable because it shows what the government wanted prices to be, how it understood inflation, and the extraordinary geographic reach and standardizing ambitions of imperial government. The use of a common schedule does not prove that actual markets or prices were uniform across the empire. It reveals the moral language of imperial rule, in which the emperors act as protectors against the greedy. It also shows the state's confidence, perhaps excessive, in its capacity to regulate. But the edict cannot be treated as a record of what people actually paid. A legal maximum is not a market price. The list tells us little about regional variation, actual wages, or how the schedule was received. The wording of the preamble, moreover, is a piece of rhetoric addressed to the empire's subjects and does not offer an unbiased analysis of the causes of inflation.

Constantine took a different course. Around 309 to 310, he introduced a gold coin of high and consistent purity, the solidus, struck at 72 to the Roman pound, which became one of the most important monetary institutions of late antiquity and remained the standard high value coin of the Byzantine state for centuries. The contrast with the third century debased silver coinage is striking. Constantine first introduced the solidus in his western territories around 309 to 310, and after his conquest of Licinius in 324 the denomination spread throughout the reunited empire. Constantine's later access to eastern revenues, defeated rivals' treasuries, and confiscated precious metal increased the resources available for large scale gold coinage, but these developments followed rather than financed the solidus's original introduction. The coin gave the government a reliable means of paying officers and collecting some taxes in gold. It did not solve every monetary problem. Small bronze coins remained unstable, ordinary people rarely handled gold, and taxes continued to be collected in a mixture of money and kind. But it gave the state and wealthier subjects a stable reference point, and it strengthened the capacity of those who held gold, including officials, landowners, and, later, churches.

The imperial economy depended on labor as well as coinage. The state needed farmers to remain on productive land, artisans to produce weapons and cloth, and transport workers to move grain. Some late Roman legislation attempted to tie people to occupations, though the great expansion of such compulsory obligations belongs more to the later fourth century than to Diocletian's reign. Agriculture remained the base of the economy. Trade continued across the Mediterranean and along the rivers, although the state's demands on transport and supply also shaped it. The relationship between fiscal pressure and economic activity is contested, and historians disagree over whether the state's demands stimulated production, as some have argued for the eastern provinces, or drained resources from productive uses.

Section 25.6

Emperor, Court, and the Language of Power

Diocletian's reign also transformed the way imperial power was displayed. Emperors were now surrounded by more formal ceremonial. Audiences with the emperor followed a sequence of ranks and rituals, officials wore distinctive dress, and the emperor was addressed with terms emphasizing majesty and divine favor. Kneeling before the emperor and kissing the hem of his robe, a gesture known as adoration, became part of court ritual, at least at formal occasions. Palaces were built or expanded at Nicomedia, Antioch, Split on the Dalmatian coast, Thessaloniki, Sirmium, Trier, Milan, and other residences, and their architecture, with audience halls, processional routes, and adjacent circuses, framed the emperor as a distant and elevated figure.

Historical Vocabulary
The "Dominate"

Dominate is a modern historiographical label, derived from the Latin dominus, "lord," for the form of imperial government that supposedly replaced the Principate under Diocletian. Nineteenth century historians, above all Theodor Mommsen, contrasted the Principate, in which the emperor was "first citizen," with a Dominate in which he ruled openly as lord and master. The contrast has been heavily criticized. Emperors had been addressed as dominus in some contexts from the first century, and monarchical power had been the reality of Roman government since Augustus.

Historians now generally treat the change under Diocletian and Constantine as an evolution in representation, ceremonial, and bureaucracy rather than a constitutional revolution. Older textbooks sometimes described it as the adoption of "oriental despotism," an explanation that rests on stereotypes about the east and ignores the Roman origins of much court ceremonial. The term can still be used, with care, to describe the more formal court culture of the late empire.

The changes were not simply matters of style. They reflected a shift in the emperor's relationship to his subjects and to the elites. The old fiction that the emperor was a magistrate who shared power with the Senate had become implausible after decades of military emperors, and Diocletian and his colleagues chose to ground their authority openly in divine favor and their own accomplishments. They also reduced the emperor's accessibility in some contexts and increased it in others, since the multiplication of courts meant that provincial elites could approach an emperor in person more often than under the earlier system, when the emperor might be far away or a single figure at Rome.

Rome itself remained central to the empire's symbolism. Diocletian celebrated a triumph there in 303 and built the enormous Baths that bear his name, and the Senate, though shorn of much practical power, remained the highest aristocratic body. But the emperors no longer treated Rome as their principal residence, and the city's political role was shifting. This is a crucial point for understanding Constantinople. Emperors were already governing from multiple residences, and Rome's prestige coexisted with its diminished role as a working center of government. When Constantine later established Constantinople as a permanent imperial residence, he did so within a system where capital functions were already distributed.

The ideology of the Tetrarchy also had a religious dimension that helps explain the persecution. The emperors presented themselves as protected by Jupiter and Hercules and as guardians of the traditional cults, whose correct performance ensured the empire's survival. This was not new, but it took on greater urgency in a period when the empire had recently been on the verge of dissolution. For rulers who believed that disaster in the third century had followed neglect of the gods, restoring traditional worship was a political as well as religious duty. It is against this background that the events of 303 must be understood.

Section 25.7

The Great Persecution

Chapter 23 followed the growth of Christian communities and earlier persecution up to the eve of Diocletian's reign. The persecution that began in 303 is the most extensive in Roman history, and because it fell within the reign examined in this chapter, it belongs here. Its background included earlier measures against Manichaeans, whose religion was viewed as a Persian import, and against Christians serving in the army in the years before 303. The immediate circumstances remain debated. Lactantius attributed the persecution largely to Galerius, who is said to have pressed Diocletian to act, and to the failure of a divination ritual, while Christian writers generally portrayed the tetrarchs as motivated by hostility to the faith. Modern historians tend to emphasize several overlapping factors: the belief that the empire's welfare depended on traditional ritual, the ideological unity of the college, the visible growth and social prominence of Christian communities, and court politics in which Galerius and others may have pressed for action.

The first edict, issued in February 303, ordered the destruction of churches and the confiscation and burning of scriptures, stripped Christians of certain legal privileges, and prohibited assemblies. The church at Nicomedia was demolished within days, according to Lactantius. Further edicts in 303 ordered the arrest of clergy. A later measure, conventionally dated to about 304, appears to have extended the requirement to sacrifice much more broadly across the population, although its exact date and wording are not securely preserved. Enforcement followed local circumstances. In the eastern provinces, and especially under Galerius and later Maximinus Daia, the persecution was severe, with torture, imprisonment, forced labor, and execution. In Italy and Africa, enforcement was uneven and at times took the form of demands for the surrender of scriptures, an issue that would later divide African Christians. In the territories governed by Constantius, in Gaul and Britain, the emperor was reported to have limited enforcement largely to the destruction of church buildings, a claim made by Christian authors favorable to his son and therefore to be treated with care.

Sources for the persecution include Lactantius, whose On the Deaths of the Persecutors was written soon afterward with a polemical purpose; Eusebius, who lived through the persecution in Palestine and Egypt and described the martyrdoms he witnessed; martyr acts of varying reliability; and some imperial and archaeological evidence. Martyr narratives were composed to strengthen and inspire Christian communities and often reworked their subjects into models of faith. They cannot be read as neutral accounts, though many reflect real events. The total number executed cannot be established. Hundreds of deaths are securely attested, concentrated especially in the eastern provinces, while many more Christians experienced imprisonment, forced labor, confiscation, or pressure to conform. The picture that emerges is of a serious attack, but one that varied greatly across provinces and over time, and that proved impossible to enforce comprehensively.

The persecution's effects on Christian communities were profound. Some Christians, including clergy, handed over scriptures or sacrificed under pressure, and their treatment became a matter of bitter dispute afterward. Others were celebrated as confessors and martyrs. Christian communities that had grown accustomed to public buildings and legal tolerance faced their first empire wide crisis in two generations. The persecution failed to eradicate Christian communities. Their geographic spread and institutional durability mattered, enforcement differed sharply among rulers and provinces, many Christians adapted or temporarily conformed, and the emperors themselves eventually abandoned the policy between 311 and 313.

By 311 Galerius, portrayed by Lactantius as one of the persecution's principal advocates, had reached that conclusion. Gravely ill, he issued an edict in April 311, probably at Serdica and certainly published at Nicomedia on 30 April, jointly in the names of himself, Constantine, and Licinius, that acknowledged the failure of the policy, permitted Christians to exist and to hold their meetings again, and asked them to pray for the welfare of the emperors and the state. The edict was a concession of pragmatism, not an endorsement of Christianity, but its significance is enormous. Legal existence for Christians was restored before Constantine's victory at the Milvian Bridge. The story that runs directly from persecution to Constantine's triumph omits this crucial step. Galerius died soon afterward, and Maximinus Daia in the east, though he briefly relaxed the persecution, resumed pressure on Christians until his own defeat in 313.

Section 25.8

The Collapse of the Tetrarchic Succession

On 1 May 305, Diocletian, at Nicomedia, and Maximian, at Milan, abdicated. This was an extraordinary act, without precedent in Roman imperial history, and its purpose was to demonstrate that the system worked. Galerius and Constantius became the new Augusti. The new Caesars were Severus, an officer close to Galerius, and Maximinus Daia, Galerius's nephew. The surprising element was who had been passed over. Constantine, the adult son of Constantius, who was serving at Galerius's court, and Maxentius, the son of Maximian and the son in law of Galerius, were both excluded. The message was that succession was no longer a matter of blood. Diocletian retired to his palace at Split, where he would grow cabbages, according to later tradition, and where he died, probably in 311 or 312, having watched the collapse of his design.

The system unraveled quickly. In July 306 Constantius died at York while campaigning in Britain, and his army proclaimed Constantine Augustus. Galerius grudgingly recognized him as Caesar, not Augustus, a compromise that showed how quickly the plan was being bent. In October 306, at Rome, the Praetorian Guard and disaffected citizens, angry at the imposition of taxation on Italy and at the loss of the city's privileged status, proclaimed Maxentius. Severus marched against him and was defeated and killed. Maximian emerged from retirement to support his son, then quarreled with him, and after a series of intrigues, including an attempt to seize power from Constantine, died in 310. Galerius called a conference at Carnuntum in 308 that installed Licinius as Augustus in the west, but by 310 there were five or six men claiming imperial rank, and the empire was divided among competing regimes.

Why did the system fail? Dynastic expectations were one major source of instability. The Tetrarchic scheme attempted to subordinate hereditary claims in a political culture in which imperial sons, family connections, army acclamation, and established dynastic names could all provide powerful claims to legitimacy. By marrying Caesars into imperial families while later bypassing biological sons, the system created tensions that became especially dangerous once Diocletian's personal arbitration disappeared. The second reason was the role of the army, which retained the power to acclaim, as the proclamation of Constantine at York demonstrated. The third was the personal authority of Diocletian, whose retirement removed the system's arbiter. The fourth was the resentment produced by the fiscal measures, which fed the revolt of Maxentius. The Tetrarchy was a workable device for distributing power among colleagues who accepted a senior authority. Without that authority, it became a framework for competition.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Tetrarchy Fail Politically but Succeed Institutionally?

Long term causes

The succession instability, multiple frontier demands, scale of the empire, and military competition described in Chapter 24. Emperors could not be everywhere, and rivals could always be proclaimed by armies.

Short term causes

Diocletian's creation of Augusti and Caesares, regional imperial courts, and a planned sequence of succession by promotion, intended to replace improvised killing with orderly transfer.

Event or transformation

Several emperors governing simultaneously from different residences, bound by shared ideology, common laws, and a common fiscal and military structure between about 286 and 305.

Immediate effects

Faster regional response, recovery of Britain, victory over Persia, clearer distribution of responsibilities, and greater coordination among the recognized emperors, even though regional usurpations still occurred and had to be suppressed. After 305, dynastic claims and army acclamation produced civil wars.

Long term consequences

The normalization of multiple imperial courts, regional capitals, and shared or divided rule, and of administrative structures, above all the multiplication of provinces and the regional administrative hierarchies that developed further under Constantine and his successors.

Historical complexity

The Tetrarchy was neither a simple failure nor a complete success. It failed to end dynastic politics but succeeded in demonstrating that the empire could be governed by several rulers, an idea that became normal in the fourth century and beyond.

Historical complexity: Students should distinguish the failure of a particular succession scheme from the survival of the institutional habits it created. Constantine himself would later rule with colleagues and Caesars, including his own sons.

Section 25.9

Constantine's Rise and the Battle of the Milvian Bridge

Constantine was born around 272 in the Balkans, the son of Constantius and Helena, a woman of comparatively humble origins. Ancient sources disagree about the legal status of their relationship, variously describing Helena as wife or concubine, and historians cannot resolve the question securely. He grew up in the service of the Tetrarchs, spent years at the courts of Diocletian and Galerius, and joined his father in Britain in 305 and 306. Proclaimed at York, he spent the next six years securing Gaul, Britain, and Spain, campaigning against Franks and other groups on the Rhine, and securing support among his army and provincial elites through military success, patronage, rewards, and claims to legitimacy. Panegyrics delivered before him in Gaul, in 307, 310, and 313, are valuable contemporary evidence for how his supporters presented him. The panegyric of 310 speaks of a vision of the god Apollo at a temple in Gaul and of Constantine's descent from an earlier emperor, Claudius Gothicus, a genealogical claim probably invented to strengthen his legitimacy. His coinage of these years frequently featured Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, as his divine companion. Constantine's religious loyalties were, in these years, publicly expressed in traditional and solar terms.

By 312 Constantine had decided to challenge Maxentius, who controlled Italy and Africa and had survived campaigns by Severus and Galerius. Constantine crossed the Alps with a comparatively small field army, took Turin and Verona, and marched on Rome. On 28 October 312, the two armies met near the Milvian Bridge across the Tiber, north of the city. Maxentius had prepared to defend Rome behind its walls but instead led his forces out, with the river at their back. His army was defeated, and Maxentius drowned in the Tiber as the bridge, or a temporary bridge of boats, collapsed. Constantine entered Rome the following day, disbanded the Praetorian Guard, and became master of the west.

It is here that later tradition places Constantine's conversion, and the sources call for careful reading. Lactantius, writing within a few years, reports that Constantine was instructed in a dream to mark the shields of his soldiers with a heavenly sign, evidently a Christian symbol, before the battle. Eusebius, in an earlier account in his Ecclesiastical History, says little about the vision, and in the Life of Constantine, written after Constantine's death, describes an apparition of a cross of light in the sky at midday, accompanied by words promising victory, which Constantine, he says, later reported to Eusebius under oath. The two accounts differ in date, setting, and form: one a dream on the eve of battle, the other a vision earlier in the campaign. The triumphal arch dedicated to Constantine at Rome in 315 credits his victory to the prompting of an unnamed divinity, a phrase open to Christian and non Christian readings.

What can historians say? Ancient Christian authors associated Constantine's victory with a divine sign and with his growing commitment to the Christian God, but their accounts differ in date, details, and form, and Constantine's religious development appears to have unfolded over time. What can be established is that from about 312 he increasingly identified his rule with the Christian God, that Christian symbolism appeared on his soldiers' equipment and, later, on his coinage, that he wrote letters to Christian bishops, and that his policies materially favored Christianity. Historians cannot independently verify a miraculous vision, and the sources do not permit a confident reconstruction of his inner belief. His solar imagery continued on coins into the 320s, which suggests that his religious language was gradual and, to some audiences, deliberately ambiguous.

The Milvian Bridge matters politically as well as religiously. It was a Roman civil war between imperial claimants, fought for control of the west. Maxentius was popular in Rome for a time, had financed monumental building, and was not simply a pagan tyrant, though his hostile portrayal by Constantine's supporters obscured this. The religious significance of the victory derives substantially from how Constantine and Christian authors interpreted it afterward. To describe it as the victory of Christianity over paganism is therefore misleading. It made Constantine dominant in the west and gave him the opportunity to shape the empire's religious policy, and that opportunity, not the battle itself, proved historic.

Section 25.10

Constantine, Licinius, and Imperial Christianity

In early 313, Constantine and Licinius met at Milan, where Licinius married Constantine's half sister Constantia and the two emperors reached an agreement concerning religion. The agreement is traditionally called the Edict of Milan, but the label needs qualification. The surviving texts, which Lactantius and Eusebius preserve, derive from a letter issued by Licinius at Nicomedia in June 313 after his victory over Maximinus Daia, setting out the terms of a joint policy. It was not necessarily a single formal empire wide edict issued at Milan. The safest formulation is "the agreement commonly called the Edict of Milan." Its substance was broad religious toleration, the freedom of Christians and everyone else to follow the religion they chose, and the restoration to Christian communities of property confiscated during the persecution. It reversed the persecution and extended Galerius's grant into full legal protection. It did not make Christianity the official religion of Rome. That step belongs to later legislation, and even then the legal position needs careful wording.

Historical Vocabulary
"Christian Empire"

In this chapter, "Christian Empire" means an empire whose ruler increasingly supported Christianity and incorporated Christian institutions into imperial politics. It does not mean an empire whose population had suddenly become uniformly Christian, or whose law and institutions had become wholly Christian in character. By 337 Christianity enjoyed unprecedented imperial patronage, legal protection, property, and political prestige, but Christians were not a majority everywhere, traditional cults remained widespread, many temples and priesthoods continued to function although Constantine withdrew support from some cults and restricted some practices, and much of the imperial machinery remained Roman in inherited forms.

The phrase is therefore a description of a changed relationship between imperial power and one religious community. The transformation of society, which unfolded across the fourth century and beyond, is examined in Chapter 26.

Constantine's support for Christianity took concrete forms. He returned confiscated property to churches and made grants of money and land. He financed the construction of major basilicas, including the Lateran basilica at Rome, the first church of St. Peter on the Vatican hill, and churches at the sites in Palestine associated with the life of Christ, including Jerusalem and Bethlehem, where his mother Helena traveled. He extended legal privileges to clergy, including exemptions from certain civic obligations, which raised protests from city councils because such exemptions reduced the pool of councillors, and he gave legal force to bishops' judgments in some kinds of disputes and to the freeing of slaves in church. He legislated a weekly day of rest in 321, using the phrase "the venerable day of the sun," a formulation that could be read in Christian or solar terms. He also placed Christian symbols on military standards, and Christian clergy were welcomed at court. Bishops, who had been persecuted officials of local communities, were becoming figures of imperial notice and civic power.

These policies had major political consequences as well as religious ones. Constantine repeatedly associated religious concord with imperial welfare, and his patronage gave bishops and Christian communities unprecedented access to the state. Historians continue to debate the relative importance of personal conviction, ideas of divine favor, political calculation, and concern for imperial unity in shaping his policy. Patronage also drew him into disputes among Christians. Almost immediately, he had to respond to the Donatist controversy in North Africa, where rival Christian factions disputed the legitimacy of a bishop consecrated by a man accused of having surrendered scriptures during the persecution. Constantine summoned bishops to a council at Arles in 314 and later intervened again. In 325 he summoned a much larger assembly of bishops to Nicaea, in Bithynia, to address a dispute in the eastern church. His decision to do so demonstrated how far imperial-church relations had changed: an emperor now regarded Christian doctrinal conflict as important enough to imperial order to warrant direct intervention. The theological dispute itself and the council's consequences are examined in Chapter 26.

The treatment of traditional religion under Constantine was complex. He did not close the temples or abolish traditional priesthoods. Temples continued to function, the offices of traditional priesthood continued to be held, including by senators, and he retained inherited imperial titles, including that of Pontifex Maximus, the head of Roman state cult. Yet he also transferred temple treasures to the state, apparently acted against certain cults regarded as scandalous, such as some in the eastern provinces, and, according to Eusebius, forbade some sacrifices. How far these measures extended, and whether a general prohibition of sacrifice was issued, probably late in his reign, as some recent scholarship argues, is disputed by scholars, and the sources are colored by Christian triumphalism. What is clear is that imperial patronage was shifting, and that participation in traditional worship no longer carried the same prestige.

Relations between Constantine and Licinius soured after 313. Both emperors were now Augusti, with sons named Caesars in 317, and each ruled his half of the empire with his own court. A first war in 316 to 317 gave Constantine part of the Balkan territory, and tensions continued. Licinius has been portrayed by Christian sources as a persecutor, and there is evidence that he increasingly restricted Christian clergy and assemblies in the east, but the conflict cannot be reduced to a morality play of Christian against pagan. Territorial control, dynastic rivalry, and competing imperial ambition all mattered, and Licinius himself had been a party to the agreement of 313. In 324 the two emperors fought decisive campaigns, at Adrianople in July and at Chrysopolis, across the Bosporus from Byzantium, in September, and Constantine won. Licinius was spared briefly and executed in 325. Constantine, sole emperor from 324, now governed a reunited empire.

Historical Debate

Did Constantine "Convert" the Roman Empire?

The question: Historians of Constantine ask both about the emperor's personal beliefs and about the effects of his policies on the empire. To what extent did his patronage of Christianity change the empire, and how should its scale and speed be understood?

Interpretation A: Constantine was transformative. Constantine ended the persecution, restored and expanded church property, funded basilicas, favored bishops, granted legal privileges to clergy, involved himself in church disputes, and increasingly incorporated Christian language into imperial ideology. Because emperors dominated Roman patronage, this changed the incentives of elites and the status of Christian institutions in a single generation.
Interpretation B: The transformation was gradual and incomplete. Traditional cults continued and many temples kept functioning, most imperial institutions retained their Roman forms, conversion of the population took generations, and Constantine's own religious language, with its solar imagery and ambiguous terminology, developed over time. On this view, later emperors and the social developments of the fourth century did more to Christianize the empire than Constantine did himself.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on laws, coins, inscriptions, buildings, and the accounts of Eusebius and Lactantius, but they weigh differently the evidence of imperial patronage against the evidence for continued traditional practice, and they read the religious language of the sources differently. Debates about the sincerity or timing of his conversion rely largely on interpretation of texts written by authors with clear religious loyalties.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question affects their view of late antiquity as a whole, whether they emphasize sudden rupture or gradual transformation, and how they understand the roots of later relations between church and state in European history.

Student task: Historians generally agree that Constantine fundamentally changed Christianity's relationship to imperial power without instantly creating a uniformly Christian empire or society. Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence would help determine how quickly society changed.

Section 25.11

Constantinople and a Reoriented Empire

Constantine's victory at Chrysopolis brought him to the shore of the Bosporus, opposite the old Greek city of Byzantium, which he had besieged during the campaign. Within a short time he decided to build a new imperial city on the site. The refoundation was inaugurated in 324 and the city dedicated on 11 May 330, and it was called Constantinople, the city of Constantine, though it was also referred to at times as a "New Rome." The choice of location was strategic. The city sat on a peninsula at the southern end of the Bosporus, commanding the strait that links the Black Sea to the Sea of Marmara and, through the Dardanelles, to the Aegean and Mediterranean. It lay at the meeting point of Europe and Asia and on the main road system connecting the Balkans and Anatolia. It was close enough to the Danube and to the eastern theaters to serve as a base for campaigns, but not directly on a frontier, and its defensible site, protected by water on three sides, offered security. Constantine had known the region well from years at Nicomedia and other eastern courts.

The building program was extensive. Constantine expanded the city's walls, laid out a great avenue lined with colonnades, built a palace beside a new hippodrome for chariot races and public ceremonies, and erected monuments, including a column crowned with a statue of the emperor. He also founded churches, including a major one dedicated to the Holy Apostles, which was to serve as his own burial place. By the early 330s, and clearly by 332, arrangements were being made to supply Constantinople with Egyptian grain, previously destined in part for Rome, and with public distributions. A Senate was established, initially with a lower rank than that of Rome, and members of the elite, both officials and landowners, were encouraged to settle and build houses there. The city's population grew rapidly, though ancient figures are uncertain.

The new city's character has been the subject of exaggeration. It was not a purely Christian city with no traditional elements. Constantine included churches and displayed Christian symbols, but he also adorned the city with statues and works of art taken from temples across the empire, and some traditional rites and dedicatory ceremonies seem to have accompanied the foundation, as the sources are ambiguous. Nor did Constantinople instantly replace Rome. Rome remained the seat of the Senate, a city of immense symbolic importance, and an imperial residence when emperors visited. Constantinople's supremacy developed over the course of the fourth and fifth centuries, as emperors resided there more regularly and as its institutions matured. It is therefore misleading to write that Constantine "moved the capital from Rome to Constantinople." Constantinople became a major permanent imperial residence and eastern political center within an empire whose rulers had already governed from multiple regional courts. Its later history as the center of the empire that modern historians call Byzantine belongs to Part VI, beginning with Chapter 36.

Section 25.12

The Empire Constantine Left Behind, 337 CE

In his last years Constantine attended to church affairs, the eastern frontier, and dynastic arrangements. In 326, for reasons that remain obscure in the sources, his eldest son Crispus, a successful Caesar, was executed, and his wife Fausta died soon after, an episode that shows the dangers of court politics even within the imperial family. He raised his other sons, Constantine II, Constantius II, and Constans, and a nephew, to the rank of Caesar and prepared a campaign against Persia. In spring 337, ill, he was baptized shortly before his death near Nicomedia on 22 May by Eusebius of Nicomedia, a prominent eastern bishop who had supported Arius during the earlier controversy and whose theological position and later reputation are examined more fully in Chapter 26. Delayed baptism until near death was not unusual in this period, and it should not be read as evidence against Constantine's Christian commitment, though it has been read that way by some.

His death immediately confirmed the unresolved problem of succession. In the summer of 337 several of Constantine's half brothers and nephews were killed. Julian later blamed Constantius II, and many modern historians likewise suspect that Constantius was complicit, but the precise chain of responsibility remains disputed. In September the three surviving sons were proclaimed Augusti and divided the empire among them. The empire remained a legal unity, and the sharing of imperial power among colleagues had become normal, but the ideal of orderly succession by promotion had given way to dynastic inheritance combined with violence. Diocletian and Constantine had changed the machinery without solving the succession problem permanently.

What Constantine left behind was nevertheless a very different empire from the one Diocletian had seized. The government was larger and more bureaucratic, with a hierarchy of prefects, vicars, and governors over smaller provinces. Taxation was more systematically assessed. The court was more formal, and imperial government operated from several major residences. A stable gold currency existed. The army included both garrison forces and mobile field armies. Constantinople was rising as a major imperial center. And Christianity, which had experienced its most severe persecution only a generation before, was now the favored religion of the emperor, legally protected and richly supported, with bishops who had become significant public figures.

Many questions were left open. What happens when emperors become involved in Christian doctrine? Who decides what orthodox Christianity is? What power do bishops acquire, and how do they use it? How does Christianity spread through actual society, in cities and in the countryside, among the wealthy and the poor? What happens to traditional religious practice, and to the many people who continued to observe it? These questions belong to Chapter 26, which examines councils, theological controversies, bishops, monasticism, Christianization, religious conflict, and the changing social role of the church. They were, in a sense, the questions Constantine's policies had created.

Multiple Perspectives

One Empire, Seven Experiences: Responses to Diocletianic and Constantinian Change

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of a Tetrarchic army officer: Emperors residing near the frontier meant faster reinforcement, more regular pay and supply through the annona, and a clearer chain of command, but also the continuing risk that loyalty would be tested in civil war.
From the perspective of a provincial taxpayer: Rationalized administration arrived as assessment, registers, and obligations. Greater predictability could help, but so could the increase in burdens and the reach of officials into village life.
From the perspective of a municipal councillor: Caught between the community and the imperial state, the councillor faced growing responsibility for tax collection, and sought exemptions through imperial service or, later, the clergy.
From the perspective of a Christian under Diocletian, c. 304: Church buildings were confiscated or destroyed, scriptures burned, clergy arrested, and each person had to decide whether to sacrifice, hide, surrender texts, or accept punishment. Experiences depended heavily on province and local officials.
From the perspective of a Christian bishop under Constantine, c. 320: Property restored, buildings funded, privileges granted, and access to imperial power opened, but also new expectations, new disputes, and the risk of imperial involvement in church quarrels.
From the perspective of a priest of a traditional cult: Worship remained widespread and important, and many temples continued to function, but imperial favor, prestige, and some financial support were shifting toward a different religion, and certain temples lost treasures or faced intervention, while the scope of restrictions on sacrifice remained uncertain and contested.
From the perspective of a resident of Byzantium or Constantinople: A modest, though strategic, Greek city was transformed by walls, avenues, palaces, and new residents, with employment for builders and opportunity for suppliers, but also disruption, displacement, and the arrival of a demanding court.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Roman Empire in 284 and in 337

What Changed

  • Imperial government became more bureaucratically elaborate, with smaller and more numerous provinces and developing regional hierarchies
  • Multiple emperor rule and multiple imperial residences became normal tools of government, although Constantine himself ruled as sole Augustus from 324 to 337
  • Fiscal assessment became more systematic, and the state more heavily dependent on documentation and requisition
  • Court ceremonial became more formal, and imperial ideology more openly divine
  • A stable gold coin, the solidus, was introduced
  • Constantinople was founded as a major eastern residence
  • Christianity moved from persecution to legal protection and imperial patronage, and bishops became figures of political weight

What Stayed the Same

  • The emperor remained the center of the political system, and the army remained decisive in making and unmaking emperors
  • Taxation funded the state, and provinces and cities remained the basic units of administration
  • Roman law remained foundational, and the Senate still existed
  • Traditional religious practices remained widespread, and many temples and priesthoods continued to function, although restrictions on some practices developed and their scope is debated
  • Succession remained politically dangerous, as the events of 305 to 312 and 337 showed

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, soldiers, tax paying landowners, councillors, and Christian clergy and communities experienced the changes most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural households, agricultural workers, and communities distant from the courts and armies saw continuity in the rhythms of farming and local life even as the demands of the state changed.

Why the pattern matters: Diocletian and Constantine changed the machinery of the empire without ending its dependence on emperors and armies, which explains both the durability of the late Roman state and the recurrence of dynastic crises.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the reconstruction of Roman imperial government between 284 and 337. Its central argument is that Diocletian and Constantine responded to the vulnerabilities revealed by the Third Century Crisis by transforming the empire rather than abandoning it. The empire that emerged was not a restoration of the Principate. It was a more elaborate state, in which shared imperial rule and multiple courts had become normal possibilities, administered through more officials, in smaller provinces, with more systematic taxation, a reformed coinage, and a more formal court.

Diocletian, acclaimed in 284 and sole emperor after 285, inherited a reunited empire whose problems were structural. One man could not command every frontier, control every army, supervise every tax system, and secure every succession. His solution was shared rule, first with Maximian as Augustus in 286 and then with two Caesars in 293. The Tetrarchy distributed responsibility, placed emperors near threatened frontiers, and sought to replace improvised succession with promotion. It also anchored imperial legitimacy in divine patronage rather than blood.

The administrative reforms of the period multiplied provinces, developed regional tiers of supervision that took fuller shape under Constantine, and expanded the imperial bureaucracy, while gradually separating civil and military functions. Taxation moved toward more systematic assessment of land and labor, payable in money and kind, and was extended to Italy. These changes gave the state a clearer view of its resources and its subjects, and they placed heavier and more visible demands on provincial households and municipal councillors. The military reforms were incremental. The army grew, frontier fortifications were strengthened, and mobile forces developed, but the later distinction between frontier and field armies should not be projected back onto a single act. Victory over Persia in 298 illustrated the strategic value of a system with multiple emperors.

Diocletian's monetary reform and Edict on Maximum Prices showed the state's ambition and its limits. The edict reveals how the emperors understood inflation and how far imperial ambition reached, but it records intentions, not actual prices. Constantine's introduction of the solidus provided a more durable answer to monetary instability, though not a complete one. Court ceremony and imperial ideology evolved into a more formal and openly sacral kingship, an evolution that modern scholars increasingly describe without reference to a sudden Dominate or oriental despotism.

The Great Persecution, beginning in 303, was the most severe attack on Christian communities in Roman history. It was rooted in the belief that the empire's welfare depended on traditional cult, in the Tetrarchic ideology of divine protection, and in court politics. Its enforcement varied widely across provinces and rulers, and it failed to suppress the church. Galerius's edict of 311 restored legal existence for Christians before Constantine's victory, a point often omitted from simplified narratives.

The Tetrarchic succession collapsed after the abdications of 305. Dynastic expectations, army acclamation, the loss of Diocletian's arbitration, and resentment at fiscal changes produced a series of usurpations and civil wars. Constantine, proclaimed at York in 306, secured the west, defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge in 312, and defeated Licinius in 324. The Milvian Bridge was a Roman civil war between imperial claimants. Its religious significance derives from later interpretation, and the sources, especially Lactantius and Eusebius, differ on the nature of Constantine's vision or dream. Historians can establish Constantine's increasing identification with the Christian God and his material support for Christianity, but not verify a miracle.

The agreement of 313, commonly called the Edict of Milan, granted broad toleration and restored Christian property. It did not make Christianity the official religion. Constantine then extended patronage, building basilicas, granting privileges to clergy, and intervening in disputes at Arles and Nicaea. Traditional religious practices remained widespread and many temples continued to function, although Constantine restricted some practices and historians debate the scope, date, and enforcement of legislation against sacrifice. The transformation of society was slower than the transformation of imperial patronage. In this sense, the "Christian Empire" of the chapter title describes a changed relationship between emperor and church, not a uniformly Christian society.

The founding of Constantinople, dedicated in 330, established a major permanent imperial residence and eastern political center on a strategic site. It emerged within an empire already governed from several courts, and Rome retained enormous prestige and its Senate. Constantinople's supremacy developed gradually over succeeding generations.

When Constantine died in 337, the empire was more bureaucratic, more fiscally organized, more geographically distributed, and more openly linked to Christianity than in 284. Yet succession remained violent, as the killings of that summer showed. Regional and social variation remained large, and traditional religion remained widespread. The unresolved questions, about doctrine, episcopal power, and the Christianization of society, lead directly to Chapter 26.

Key Terms

Tetrarchy
The system of rule by two senior emperors and two junior emperors devised under Diocletian, intended to distribute administrative and military responsibility and to regularize succession.
Augustus
The title of a senior emperor, in the Tetrarchy one of two who shared supreme authority.
Caesar
The title of a junior emperor and designated successor, who commanded armies and issued laws though ranking below an Augustus.
Diocese
A late Roman administrative unit grouping several provinces under a vicar; the term was later adopted by the church.
Vicar
An official governing a diocese, subordinate to a praetorian prefect and superior to provincial governors.
Praetorian prefect
Originally commander of the imperial guard, later a senior civil official with authority over large regions, with the transformation largely occurring under Constantine and his successors.
Curiales
Members of city councils responsible for local government and, often, for collecting taxes and making up deficits from their own property.
Iugatio and capitatio
Modern labels for the late Roman assessment system, whose fiscal units (iuga and capita) related land, productive capacity, persons, and labor in ways that varied regionally and remain debated.
Annona
The system of supplies, especially grain, for Rome, the army, and officials, extended in late antiquity into a major form of taxation in kind.
Edict on Maximum Prices
The imperial decree of 301 fixing legal maximum prices and wages, revealing state ambition but not actual market conditions.
Solidus
The gold coin introduced under Constantine, struck at 72 to the pound, which became the standard high value coin of the later empire.
Dominate
A modern term for the supposedly more autocratic form of government after Diocletian, now used cautiously because monarchy had long been the reality of imperial rule.
Adoration
A court ritual of prostration before the emperor, associated with more formal ceremonial in the Tetrarchic and Constantinian periods.
Great Persecution
The empire wide attack on Christians, beginning with edicts of 303, that varied greatly by region and ended in the east only after 311 to 313.
Martyr
A Christian who died for the faith, celebrated in later narratives that are important but not neutral sources.
Edict of 311
Galerius's edict of April 311 permitting Christians to exist and hold meetings, and asking for their prayers, probably issued at Serdica and published at Nicomedia on 30 April.
Edict of Milan
The traditional label for the 313 agreement of Constantine and Licinius granting religious toleration and restoring Christian property, not a single formal empire wide statute.
Sol Invictus
The Unconquered Sun, a deity prominent in imperial iconography that appeared on Constantine's coinage into the 320s.
Chi Rho
A Christian symbol formed from the first two Greek letters of Christ's name, associated in later sources with Constantine's victory.
Pontifex Maximus
An ancient Roman priestly title traditionally held by emperors; Constantine retained it despite his increasing Christian patronage, illustrating the complexity of religious transition.
Panegyric
A formal speech in praise of an emperor, valuable as evidence of how rulers wished to be seen but not neutral history.
Basilica
A large hall form used for law courts and later adopted for churches, as in Constantine's foundations.
Comitatenses and limitanei
Later fourth century terms for mobile field troops and frontier garrison troops, whose distinction developed gradually and should not be attributed to a single reform.
Donatist controversy
A North African Christian dispute over the legitimacy of clergy accused of having surrendered scriptures during the persecution, which drew Constantine into church affairs.
Council of Nicaea
The assembly of bishops summoned by Constantine in 325, examined in Chapter 26 as a doctrinal event.
Constantinople
The city on the Bosporus refounded on the site of Byzantium and dedicated in 330 as a major imperial residence.
Imperial residence
A city in which an emperor and court lived for extended periods, such as Trier, Milan, Sirmium, Nicomedia, Antioch, and later Constantinople.
Sasanian Empire
The Persian dynasty that ruled from the 220s to the seventh century and was Rome's principal rival in the east.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a large political system can survive crisis by changing its own structure. Diocletian and Constantine did not restore the Principate. They created a state with more officials, more courts, more paperwork, and more elaborate ceremony, and in doing so they made the Roman Empire capable of surviving several more centuries in the east and of leaving institutional traces in the west. The chapter also shows the costs of that durability, in heavier obligations for many subjects and in a political order that still had no reliable way to transfer power.

The chapter also marks a hinge in the history of religion in Europe and the Mediterranean. In the space of a single generation, Christian communities moved from the most severe persecution they had experienced to imperial protection and patronage. The reasons lay in political calculation, personal religious development, and the character of the Christian church itself, and historians debate the balance. What is not debatable is that the relationship between emperors and bishops, once established, would shape law, politics, and society for many centuries. That relationship is the foundation for the later histories of Latin Christendom and Orthodox Europe treated in Parts V and VI.

Within the argument of Volume I, the chapter reveals the transformation of the Roman world in progress. The empire did not fall in a single blow, and it did not remain unchanged. Its institutions were reshaped in ways that later western and eastern successors would inherit differently. The geography of the empire moved toward frontier residences and, eventually, toward Constantinople, while Rome remained a city of immense memory. The chapter thus prepares the discussion of migration, successor kingdoms, and the end of western imperial rule in Chapters 27 and 28, and of the eastern empire's continuity in Chapter 36.

Finally, the chapter invites careful historical habits. Sources on this period were written by persecuted and triumphant Christians, by panegyrists, and by officials, and each viewpoint shapes what is said and what is silent. Students who learn to distinguish what an edict proposed from what happened, what a bishop believed from what an emperor intended, and what a later tradition remembered from what contemporaries experienced will read the rest of this textbook more critically. Chapter 26 now turns from the emperors to the church itself, asking what Christianity became once it had the emperors' attention.
