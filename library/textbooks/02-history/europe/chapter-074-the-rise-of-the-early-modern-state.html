The Rise of the Early Modern State

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A ruler could issue a command in a single afternoon. Turning that command into taxes collected, soldiers fed, judgments enforced, and treaties honored took years, and it depended on thousands of people who were never entirely the ruler's servants. The history of the early modern state is the history of that gap.

Imagine a royal ordinance, sealed at court, setting out a new tax on salt or a new rule for keeping parish registers. The document itself is the easy part. It must be copied, carried by messengers over bad roads, read aloud or posted in towns, interpreted by local magistrates, and then accepted, evaded, negotiated, or resisted by communities that had their own customs, courts, and privileges. A tax assessor might be a neighbor of the people he assessed. A military recruiter might be a private contractor whose profit depended on enlisting men quickly. A judge might hold his office by purchase and regard it as family property. The monarch whose name stood on the ordinance depended on all of them. This chapter studies that dependence, and the institutions that rulers built, borrowed, bought, and bargained for in order to govern across distance.

Between roughly 1450 and 1750, European governments developed greater capacities than their medieval predecessors to raise revenue, borrow money, maintain armies and navies, record and regulate their subjects, and conduct organized diplomacy. These changes were real, and they mattered enormously for the war, commerce, religion, and politics examined throughout Volume III. Yet the chapter's central argument is that they did not add up to a uniform advance toward the centralized national government familiar from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Most early modern states grew out of inherited dynastic territories with their own laws and privileges. Rulers expanded their power largely by negotiating with nobles, towns, churches, assemblies, creditors, and local officeholders, and the results varied sharply from one part of Europe to another.

The chapter belongs to Part XII, which examines the dynasties, constitutions, and international rivalries of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. It is deliberately an institutional foundation. Chapter 75 turns to the Habsburgs and Spain, Chapter 76 to Bourbon France and Louis XIV, Chapter 77 to the Dutch Republic and the British constitutional revolutions, Chapter 78 to Austria and Prussia, Chapter 79 to Scandinavia, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and the Ottoman European order, and Chapter 80 to war and the balance of power. Those chapters narrate particular political systems. This chapter explains the shared problems they faced and the range of solutions available, so that those later accounts need not introduce every governing concept from the beginning.

It also builds on earlier volumes. The medieval inheritances discussed in Chapters 34, 45, and 46, the fiscal and military pressures of the fifteenth century treated in Chapters 51 and 55, the confessional conflicts of Chapters 62 through 67, and the overseas empires of Chapters 68 through 73 all shaped the environment in which early modern governments operated. Overseas silver, colonial customs duties, and chartered companies mattered to some treasuries far more than to others, and religious division gave rulers both new reasons and new instruments for supervising their subjects. This chapter draws on that background selectively, and does not retell it.

Historians reconstruct early modern government from the paper that governments produced and the complaints that subjects returned. Fiscal registers, treasury accounts, royal ordinances, council minutes, court records, petitions, parliamentary and diet proceedings, diplomatic correspondence, military muster rolls, and treaties all survive in large quantities, and the sheer volume of such records is itself a historical fact, since earlier rulers produced far less. But these sources have a characteristic bias. They record what officials wished to do and what they believed they had done. They are less informative about what happened when a village refused to pay, a magistrate quietly ignored an instruction, or a regiment was billeted on a town for a winter. This chapter therefore reads official sources alongside petitions, litigation, and evidence of resistance.

Finally, the chapter carries a caution that applies to the whole Part. Older narratives treated early modern European history as a movement from medieval fragmentation toward the strong, centralized, sovereign nation-state, with absolute monarchy as the typical intermediate stage. Current scholarship rejects that teleology. Some governments became more effective without becoming more absolute. Others grew larger and more costly while remaining administratively fragile. Republics, composite monarchies, elective monarchies, and loose imperial confederations all survived, and some of them were notably successful. The rise of the early modern state, in short, was a set of uneven, contested, and reversible processes, and understanding why they took the forms they did is the purpose of the sections that follow.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the early modern state and distinguish it from dynasty, kingdom, nation, and government, explaining why the modern nation-state is an inappropriate model for 1500.
  2. Explain the institutional inheritances of kingship, estates, towns, and corporate privilege on which early modern rulers built.
  3. Analyze how war, religious division, commerce, and fiscal pressure encouraged governments to expand their capacities, while distinguishing pressure from success.
  4. Compare direct taxation, indirect taxation, tax farming, and public borrowing, and explain why raising a tax was not the same as collecting it.
  5. Trace changes in recruitment, fortification, logistics, and naval administration, and assess the military revolution debate.
  6. Distinguish among issuing, communicating, enforcing, and obtaining compliance with a law, using examples from royal courts, local officeholders, and churches.
  7. Interpret court society, patronage, and venal office as political mechanisms rather than as mere luxury or corruption.
  8. Describe the development of resident diplomacy and evaluate the Peace of Westphalia as a historical settlement distinct from later sovereignty theory.
  9. Compare monarchies, republics, and composite arrangements in different regions of Europe, using selective examples from Poland-Lithuania, the Holy Roman Empire, Scandinavia, Russia, and the Ottoman European order.
  10. Evaluate the evidence for and against the interpretation of state formation as centralization, and distinguish revenue, military effectiveness, legal reach, consent, and legitimacy as separate measures of state capacity.

Historical Context

By the middle of the fifteenth century, the political map of Europe was a mosaic of overlapping authorities. Kings, emperors, dukes, bishops, city councils, and noble lords each exercised some combination of judicial, fiscal, and military rights over particular people and places, and those rights were frequently layered one upon another. Chapters 33 and 34 described how the western kingdoms emerged from the Carolingian and post-Carolingian world, and Chapter 45 traced how some monarchies developed royal justice and taxation in the High Middle Ages. The Hundred Years' War, examined in Chapter 51, accelerated these changes in England and France by creating permanent demands for revenue and troops, but it also showed how fragile royal authority remained when nobles, regions, and towns withheld cooperation. Meanwhile the Holy Roman Empire, treated in Chapter 46, remained a decentralized political structure of princes, bishops, cities, and imperial knights under an elected emperor, and the kingdoms of eastern and northern Europe followed their own paths with their own balances between crown and nobility.

Around 1500 several pressures intensified. Gunpowder weapons and the expensive new fortifications designed to resist them, examined in Chapter 55, raised the cost of war. The Italian Wars from 1494 drew the French, Spanish, imperial, papal, and Italian powers into prolonged campaigns that required unprecedented sums. Printing, treated in the same chapter, increased the volume of written administration and made it easier to circulate laws and political arguments. Commercial expansion, from the Baltic and the Mediterranean to the Atlantic, enlarged the pool of taxable wealth in ports and trading towns, and the arrival of American silver, discussed in Chapter 69, transformed the finances of some governments, most obviously the crown of Castile, while leaving others untouched. Religious fracture after 1517 added a new dimension, because rulers could now claim authority over the faith of their subjects, and churches of all confessions became partners or rivals of governments in the supervision of society.

The chronological emphasis of this chapter falls on the years 1500 to 1720, with background from the later fifteenth century and selected illustrations from the first half of the eighteenth century. The range is deliberate. By the time the great wars of the eighteenth century, treated in Chapter 80, began in earnest, many governments possessed fiscal and administrative tools that their predecessors of 1450 would hardly have recognized, but the persistence of venal office, corporate privilege, regional law, and military contracting is a reminder that these tools were layered on top of older structures rather than replacing them.

The evidence for this subject differs from that for the periods covered in earlier volumes. Governments generated treasury ledgers, tax assessments, audit records, council minutes, military muster rolls, and diplomatic dispatches in unprecedented quantity, and national and local archives preserve them unevenly. Legal records, including court registers and the correspondence between central councils and local officials, show how orders were received. Petitions, grievance lists, and the records of representative assemblies reveal the language in which subjects bargained with rulers. Treaties and printed political treatises, including Jean Bodin's work on sovereignty, show how rulers and thinkers justified authority. Modern historians have also developed quantitative approaches to fiscal and demographic data, which must be used with care because early modern accounting categories, currencies, and fiscal years do not map neatly onto modern statistics.

Finally, this chapter engages a long-running scholarly debate. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries many historians wrote the history of early modern government as the preparatory stage of the national state, with Prussia, France, England, or Spain each offered as the model by different national traditions. From the 1970s, comparative historians, among them Charles Tilly, Geoffrey Parker, and John Brewer, placed warfare and finance at the center of explanation, while others, including J. H. Elliott on composite monarchies and many historians of local government, emphasized negotiation, privilege, and the persistence of regional law. This chapter draws on both bodies of work and treats centralization as an aspiration and a contested process rather than as a uniform historical outcome.

Division I: Foundations and the Nature of the Early Modern State

This division defines what historians mean by an early modern state and explains why its development cannot be treated as the simple replacement of medieval disorder by modern centralized government.
Section 74.1 · Division I

What Was the Early Modern State?

The word "state" has several meanings, and early modern people used none of them exactly as modern students do. In everyday speech, the "state" might mean a ruler's condition or rank, the estates of a realm, or the general welfare of a commonwealth. Only gradually, during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, did writers begin to use the term for an enduring political organization distinct from the person of the ruler and capable of surviving his or her death. For the purposes of this chapter, an early modern state may be defined, with caution, as a territorially organized political authority that claimed supreme jurisdiction, maintained some capacity to tax, to make war, and to administer justice, and operated through a combination of formal offices and informal relationships. This definition is deliberately modest. It does not assume that the authority was unified, that its territory had precise borders, or that its subjects shared a common language or identity.

It is important to distinguish the state from several related things. A dynasty was a ruling family whose members held, inherited, and exchanged claims to territories, often across what we now call state boundaries. A kingdom was a particular polity with its own laws and traditions, which could be one of several territories held by a single ruler. A nation, in early modern usage, could denote a community of birth, a legal order, or the political class of a realm, and it did not yet carry the nineteenth century expectation of a culturally homogeneous people governing itself. A government was the apparatus of officials and councils through which a ruler acted. These categories overlapped but were not identical. The Spanish Habsburg monarch ruled Castile, Aragon, Naples, Sicily, Milan, the Netherlands, and overseas territories as a dynast and a king in each, and, in the case of Charles V, as Holy Roman emperor, yet no single "Spanish state" with uniform institutions governed all of them.

The modern nation-state is accordingly a poor universal model for 1500. In a modern nation-state, in theory, a single sovereign government exercises uniform authority over a defined territory and population, through impersonal institutions, in the name of a nation conceived as sharing political identity. Early modern governments were much less uniform. Different regions had different taxes, courts, and privileges. Churches maintained their own jurisdictions. Towns administered themselves under charters. Nobles exercised seigneurial justice over dependents. Rulers frequently claimed absolute authority in principle while recognizing in practice that custom, contract, and privilege limited what they could do. Many historians now speak of "state formation" rather than "the state" to emphasize the process rather than a finished form.

Contemporary thinkers developed influential theories of political authority. The French jurist Jean Bodin, in his Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576), argued that every well-ordered commonwealth required a supreme and perpetual authority, which he called sovereignty, able to make law for all subjects without needing their consent, though bound by divine and natural law and by certain fundamental customs. Bodin's work, examined in the Primary Source Window below, is central to the history of political thought. But it was a normative argument composed during the French Wars of Religion, and it should not be mistaken for a description of the capacities of any actual government. In practice, kings who claimed sovereignty still needed the cooperation of assemblies, courts, and local elites in order to collect a tax, enforce an edict, or raise a regiment.

For these reasons, this chapter treats statehood as a matter of degree and as a set of capacities, including the abilities to extract resources, to organize force, to administer territory, to make and enforce law, to conduct diplomacy, and to secure the acceptance of subjects. Each capacity grew unevenly, and each depended on negotiated relationships. Asking how far a given government had developed each of them, rather than whether it was "a real state," is more illuminating and avoids the teleological assumption that all roads led to the centralized national government.

Section 74.2 · Division I

Medieval Foundations: Kingship, Estates, Cities, and Corporate Privileges

Early modern rulers did not build on empty ground. They inherited a dense institutional landscape created over centuries, which they could use, reshape, or circumvent but rarely abolish. The most basic of these inheritances was kingship itself, understood in much of Europe as a sacred and hereditary office with responsibilities to protect the church, defend the realm, and give justice. Coronation rites, royal touching for scrofula in France and England, and the elective procedures of the Holy Roman Empire and Poland-Lithuania all expressed different understandings of how royal authority was conferred. A king was expected to consult his great men, and royal councils of nobles, bishops, and officials were present in nearly every realm.

Alongside kingship stood the estates, the corporate orders of society, typically the clergy, the nobility, and the towns, with the peasantry represented in a few regions such as parts of Sweden, Tyrol, and Friesland. Medieval rulers had learned to summon representatives of these orders to grant extraordinary taxes and to hear petitions. Assemblies of this kind developed under various names, including the English Parliament, the French Estates General and provincial estates, the Cortes of Castile and Aragon, the diets of the German principalities, the Sejm of Poland, and the Swedish Riksdag, treated in Section 74.26. They did not constitute democratic institutions in a modern sense, since they represented privileged groups, but they did establish the principle that significant taxation required some form of consent.

Nobles retained wide judicial, military, and economic powers. They held lordships with rights to try disputes, to collect dues, and to summon dependents to arms, and their castles and followings gave them capacities that kings had often to respect. Towns, for their part, had obtained charters from kings, emperors, or lords that conferred rights of self-government, courts, markets, and fiscal exemptions, and the great commercial cities of Italy, the Low Countries, and the Hanseatic world were nearly autonomous political entities. The church possessed its own legal system, canon law, and its own courts, tithes, and landholdings, and it educated and supplied many of the administrators on whom secular rulers depended. Chapter 44 examined how the investiture conflict sharpened the boundaries between ecclesiastical and secular authority, and these boundaries remained subjects of negotiation throughout the early modern period.

Law was another inheritance. Much of western and northern Europe lived under customary law, built on local practice and recorded in compilations that varied from region to region. In England the common law developed through royal courts, while on the continent the revival of Roman law in the universities provided a vocabulary of sovereignty, jurisdiction, and procedure that lawyers in royal service found useful. The result was an overlapping system of courts, including royal, seigneurial, municipal, and ecclesiastical, whose competence was frequently disputed, a situation examined in Section 74.20.

Medieval governments also bequeathed fiscal habits. Rulers lived chiefly on the revenues of their own domains, supplemented by feudal dues, customs, and judicial profits, and they requested extraordinary taxes for war. The idea that a king should "live of his own" and ask for aid only in emergencies remained a powerful constraint into the seventeenth century. When rulers began to need regular, large revenues, they were therefore asking subjects to accept an expansion of what had been understood as exceptional. This tension, between a medieval view of extraordinary taxation and an early modern need for permanent revenue, runs through the history of taxation discussed in Division II.

Section 74.3 · Division I

War, Religion, Commerce, and the Pressures for Stronger Government

Several pressures pushed governments toward greater capacity, and it is useful to separate them. The first was war. From the late fifteenth century, wars were fought with larger forces, more expensive weapons, and costly fortifications, and they were fought more often. Historians have counted the years in which major European powers were at war and found that in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries they were at war for a large majority of years. Each campaign demanded money, men, and supplies, and rulers responded by seeking new taxes, new credit, and new ways of mobilizing manpower. The second pressure was religion. After the Reformation, rulers who adopted or opposed new confessions needed to supervise churches, schools, and morals, to suppress dissent or to protect it, and to justify their policies to subjects and to foreign powers. Confessional divisions, treated in Chapters 62 through 67, also created international alignments that complicated diplomacy.

Commerce added a third pressure and a third opportunity. Expanding trade created taxable activity in ports and markets, and customs duties could be collected more easily from merchants passing through harbors than land taxes could from scattered villages. Commercial cities, such as Antwerp, Amsterdam, Genoa, and London, also supplied credit, shipping, and expertise to governments. The overseas commerce examined in Chapters 68 through 73 gave some states, such as Portugal and the Dutch Republic, a commercial basis for fiscal strength, and gave others, such as Castile, access to American silver whose effects on actual state capacity were mixed, as Section 74.9 explains. A fourth pressure came from subjects themselves. Merchants, landholders, and townspeople often wanted security, reliable courts, and orderly markets, and in times of religious violence or civil war many accepted stronger royal authority as a lesser evil than disorder.

Rulers also faced intellectual and cultural incentives. Humanist education, discussed in Chapter 57, trained a generation of lawyers, secretaries, and diplomats in rhetoric, Roman law, and classical political writing, and princes found that literate administrators could draft ordinances, conduct correspondence, and manage accounts. The printing press, as Chapter 55 explained, enabled governments to disseminate proclamations and to standardize forms, although it equally served critics and rebels.

But pressure for change was not the same as success. The same pressures that stimulated state growth could also undermine it. A government that borrowed heavily to fight a war and then failed to repay lost credit. A ruler who tried to impose a new tax without consent might provoke rebellion, as the sections on resistance show. An army that was not paid might mutiny, plunder, and alienate the population it was supposed to defend. Several of the greatest powers of the sixteenth century, including the Habsburg monarchy and the French crown, experienced severe fiscal crises and even civil war. Meanwhile some smaller, commercially wealthy states, such as the Dutch Republic, proved remarkably effective at mobilizing resources without a strongly centralized monarchy. The pressures were real, but the institutional responses varied widely, and the next sections explore why.

Section 74.4 · Division I

Composite Monarchies and the Problem of Governing Multiple Territories

Perhaps the most important structural fact about early modern European government is that many rulers governed several territories at once. Through marriage, inheritance, conquest, and diplomacy, dynasties accumulated realms, and the result was what historians, following H. G. Koenigsberger and J. H. Elliott, call the composite monarchy or composite state. In such a polity, a single ruler held several kingdoms, duchies, or provinces, each of which retained its own laws, privileges, representative institutions, and often its own language and fiscal arrangements. The Spanish crown joined Castile and Aragon under Ferdinand and Isabella, but Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia kept their own Cortes and laws. The Austrian Habsburgs ruled the archduchies of Austria, the kingdoms of Bohemia and Hungary, and other lands, each with its own estates. The British Isles, after 1603, had a single monarch for England, Scotland, and Ireland, but three distinct parliaments and legal systems. France, while more integrated, still contained provinces with different tax regimes and privileges.

Composite arrangements posed distinctive problems. A ruler who needed revenue from all of his territories had to negotiate with each set of estates separately, and the amounts that each would grant were limited by local law and by willingness to fund wars that might seem to serve the dynasty rather than the territory. A ruler might therefore be wealthy in the aggregate but unable to move resources from one region to another. Officials appointed to govern a territory were often required by its privileges to be natives, which restricted central control, and absentee rulers governed through viceroys, councils, or regents whose loyalties could be divided. Communication across distances, whether from Madrid to Brussels or from Vienna to Transylvania, took days or weeks, and decisions could be outdated by the time they were received.

Rulers developed several strategies. One was the creation of councils in the capital, such as the Spanish Council of Aragon, Council of Italy, and Council of Flanders, which advised the king on each territory while recognizing its distinctness. Another was the cultivation of aristocratic networks that crossed territorial boundaries, binding noble families to the dynasty through offices, pensions, and marriages. A third was the attempt to impose greater uniformity, as the Count-Duke of Olivares tried in the 1620s with his proposed Union of Arms, which would have required each of the Spanish monarchy's realms to contribute troops and money to a common defense. That project provoked resistance and contributed to the revolts of Catalonia and Portugal in 1640, a case treated in Chapter 75. The story illustrates a general point: attempts to turn a composite monarchy into a unified state could generate the very resistance they were intended to overcome.

Composite monarchy was not a sign of backwardness. For many subjects it offered advantages, since local privileges and institutions continued, and the ruler's distance could allow local elites considerable autonomy. For rulers it offered scale and access to diverse resources. The Spanish monarchy, the Austrian Habsburg lands, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Swedish crown with its Baltic provinces, and the Ottoman Empire's European provinces can all be understood as composite or multi-territorial political systems, each with its own balance between center and periphery. This chapter will return to the concept repeatedly, because it explains why central direction was so often limited, and Chapters 75, 78, and 79 will examine particular composite systems in depth.

Definition. A political formation in which one ruler holds several distinct territories, each retaining its own laws, privileges, and institutions, so that their union consists principally in the person and dynasty of the ruler.

Historical context. The term was developed by historians, especially H. G. Koenigsberger and J. H. Elliott in the 1970s and 1990s, to describe the typical political structure of early modern Europe, in contrast to the older assumption that most kingdoms were unitary. Contemporaries seldom used the phrase, but they understood the underlying reality, since lawyers and councilors constantly argued about the rights of particular kingdoms against the common monarch.

Why it matters. The concept explains why rulers of vast territories could not simply move resources or impose a single law. It also warns students against equating a ruler's list of titles with an integrated state. Modern equivalents such as "empire" or "country" can mislead, since a composite monarchy was neither a nation nor a centralized empire.

Division II: Taxation, Revenue, and Public Finance

The capacity to obtain and deploy resources was fundamental to early modern government. This division explains how revenue was raised, who collected it, how governments borrowed against it, and why raising a tax was never the same as successfully collecting it.
Section 74.5 · Division II

The Fiscal Problem: Why Early Modern Governments Needed Revenue

Early modern governments spent money in several broad categories. The first was the royal household and court, whose servants, buildings, ceremonies, and gifts were costly and also politically functional, as Section 74.24 explains. The second was administration, including salaries and fees for officials, judges, and secretaries, although many officials were paid partly through fees, perquisites, or the purchase price of their office rather than regular salaries. The third was diplomacy, including embassies, gifts, and subsidies to allies. The fourth, and by far the largest in most years, was war: the pay and supply of soldiers, the construction and maintenance of fortifications, the purchase of artillery and munitions, and the building and upkeep of fleets. A fifth category was debt service, as past borrowing generated interest obligations that sometimes consumed large portions of income.

Against these needs, rulers had a patchwork of revenue sources. Medieval rulers had lived chiefly from their own domains, the crown lands and rights, together with feudal dues and the profits of justice. These ordinary revenues were rarely sufficient for war. Rulers therefore sought extraordinary taxes, which they requested from assemblies or imposed under claims of emergency, and over time these extraordinary levies became recurrent. The distinction between ordinary and extraordinary revenue remained legally important, since it shaped who had to consent. A French king might impose the taille, a direct tax, without the consent of the Estates General in the sixteenth century, but an English monarch needed Parliament's grant for most taxes, and the Castilian crown negotiated certain grants with the Cortes while relying on other sources, such as ecclesiastical revenues authorized by the pope.

The difficulty of obtaining recurrent revenue was acute. The economy was predominantly agricultural, and incomes were uncertain and mostly unmonetized, which made assessment difficult. Governments possessed limited knowledge of subjects' wealth, since surveys, censuses, and registers were incomplete or outdated, a problem examined in Section 74.18. Transport of coin was slow and risky. Harvest failures, plague, and warfare itself reduced taxable capacity. And the privileged, who often had the most wealth, were frequently exempt. The result was that rulers' revenues often lagged behind their expenditures, and they bridged the gap through borrowing, the sale of offices and titles, the debasement of coinage, the sale of crown lands, and other expedients.

It follows that increases in nominal revenue did not necessarily indicate increased effectiveness. Prices rose across Europe in the sixteenth century, driven in part by population growth and by the inflow of American silver, so a larger nominal tax yield might represent a smaller real burden. Population growth, urbanization, and commercial expansion also increased the taxable base. A careful historian must therefore ask what revenue figures measure, in what currency, with what inflation, and per capita or in total, questions taken up in the Quantitative History Study below.

Section 74.6 · Division II

Direct Taxation, Land Assessments, and Fiscal Privilege

Direct taxes were levied on persons, households, or property. They took many forms. In France the taille, originally an extraordinary levy, became a permanent tax in the fifteenth century. In the north and center of the kingdom it was assessed on persons according to their estimated means (the taille personnelle), while in much of the south it was attached to land of non-noble status (the taille réelle). In England, medieval fifteenths and tenths were succeeded in the sixteenth century by subsidies based on assessments of land and goods, and later by direct levies such as the land tax created in the 1690s and the hearth tax introduced in 1662. Several continental territories levied hearth taxes, poll taxes, or assessments on landed property, and the Habsburg lands and the German principalities developed contributions based on the estimated productive capacity of farms.

Assessment was a social and political process rather than a purely technical one. A tax assessed by neighbors, such as the parish assessors who determined liabilities in English subsidy collection, could reflect local knowledge but also local favoritism and under-assessment of the powerful. In many French villages, communities collectively owed a fixed quota and chose their own collectors, who then apportioned the burden, producing endless disputes. Surveys such as cadastres in parts of Italy and southern France sought to establish more objective bases, but they were expensive and rarely complete. Governments seldom had accurate information, and the assessed valuations that did exist could become outdated, with the result that the real burden shifted in ways the central treasury did not control.

Fiscal privilege was a fundamental feature. Nobles and clergy were exempt from many direct taxes in France, in Castile, and in much of Central and Eastern Europe, on the theory that they served the realm in other ways, with arms or with prayer, and clerical wealth was taxed in part through separate grants negotiated with the church. Towns and provinces possessed their own exemptions or fixed contribution quotas. The effect was that direct taxes often fell most heavily on peasants and ordinary townspeople, who had the least political influence. Exemptions were not mere loopholes, but were rooted in legal and social understandings of status, and attempts to abolish them were politically explosive. In England, by contrast, nobility and gentry paid parliamentary taxes, although assessments were often light, an example of how different political bargains produced different incidence.

Collection was constrained by the limited reach of the central administration. Many direct taxes were collected through local intermediaries, including village officers, municipal councils, noble landlords, or estates committees, who retained a share, delayed payment, or negotiated reductions. Arrears were common, and governments frequently granted remissions after harvest failures or military devastation. The practical yield of a direct tax was thus less than its theoretical value, and the gap between assessment and collection is one of the most important themes of the chapter. Where central administrations could draw on dependable local institutions, as in the Dutch provinces or in parts of Sweden, yields were higher and resistance more manageable. Where they could not, governments turned to other sources.

Section 74.7 · Division II

Customs, Excise, Monopolies, and Indirect Taxation

Indirect taxes were levied on goods and transactions rather than directly on persons or property. They included customs duties on imports and exports, excises or sales taxes on consumption, tolls on movement, and revenues from state monopolies. For governments, indirect taxes had several attractions. They were collected at identifiable points such as ports, city gates, and markets rather than from every household. They tapped commercial wealth that land assessments missed. They were paid in small increments and could therefore be less visible than direct levies, and they could be applied to nobles and clergy who were exempt from direct taxation. For subjects, they could be nonetheless oppressive, because they raised the price of everyday necessities such as salt, wine, beer, and grain.

The forms varied across Europe. In France, the gabelle, a tax and monopoly on salt, was one of the most important and most resented revenues, and it varied sharply by region: some provinces paid heavy salt taxes, others paid little, and smuggling across fiscal boundaries was a major social and policing problem. The aides taxed beverages and other goods. In Castile the alcabala, a sales tax, was a mainstay of royal revenue, and in the 1590s the Cortes granted the millones, a tax on staple foods, to fund the crown's wars. In the Dutch Republic, excises on commodities such as beer, wine, grain, and peat provided the largest share of public revenue, and urban councils administered them. England introduced a parliamentary excise in 1643 during the Civil War, studied in the Primary Source Window below, and the excise became a permanent and growing element of English revenue after the Restoration. Customs duties formed a long-standing source for the English crown, and the grant of tonnage and poundage to monarchs for life, and its disputed collection under Charles I, became a flashpoint between king and Parliament.

State monopolies, in which the government claimed exclusive right to produce or sell a commodity, supplemented taxes. Salt monopolies existed in several states, and tobacco, playing cards, and stamped paper were later monopolized or taxed in various realms. Chartered trading companies, such as the English and Dutch East India Companies treated in Chapters 71 and 72, were not merely commercial enterprises but also political instruments that paid duties, supplied credit, and projected power, and their relation to state finances differed from place to place.

The comparative lesson is that fiscal systems were not interchangeable. Commercial and urban societies, where trade passed through compact ports and markets and where municipal institutions supported collection, were more suited to excise and customs. Predominantly agrarian territories with weak commercial networks found indirect taxation harder to administer and relied more on land and hearth levies, forced deliveries, and the dues of dependent peasants. Governments also learned that indirect taxes had political consequences, since consumption taxes provoked urban riots, such as the Revolt of the Gabelle in 1548 in southwestern France, and generated enormous incentives for smuggling and evasion. A fiscal system thus reflected the structure of the economy, the strength of local institutions, and the political bargain between rulers and subjects.

Section 74.8 · Division II

Tax Farming, Contractors, and the Administration of Revenue

Governments often lacked the personnel to collect revenue directly and therefore delegated collection to private individuals or groups. In a tax farm, or revenue lease, a financier agreed to pay the crown a fixed sum, usually in advance or in installments, in return for the right to collect a particular tax or group of taxes and to keep any surplus. The farmer took the risk of poor collection and received the profit of good collection. In France the General Farm, consolidating the leases of indirect taxes such as the gabelle and aides, became a powerful financial institution, especially from the seventeenth century. In the Ottoman Empire, tax farming (iltizam) expanded in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as the cash needs of the central treasury grew, and in 1695 the state introduced the life-term tax farm (malikâne) as a way of securing advance payments. In the Dutch Republic and in many Italian states, customs and excises were also let to contractors, although they were commonly supervised by urban or provincial authorities.

Why did governments farm taxes? Farming provided immediate cash, which was valuable when war demanded rapid funds and when direct collection would have required a large body of officials that did not exist. Farmers possessed local knowledge, credit, and commercial networks, and they were motivated by profit to pursue evasion and arrears vigorously. They also absorbed risk. Against these advantages stood heavy costs. The farmer's profit represented revenue that did not reach the treasury, and critics argued that the crown gave away much of the yield. Farmers could be harsh, since profit encouraged aggressive enforcement, and communities resented a private collector with the backing of the state. Farmers were often closely connected to officials and courtiers, and the opacity of farm contracts invited corruption.

Tax farming was one part of a wider system in which the line between public and private was drawn differently from modern practice. Governments also relied on financiers, such as the Genoese bankers who provided loans (asientos) to the Spanish crown, on office-holders who bought positions with fiscal responsibilities, and on local communities that collected their own quotas. It would be misleading to describe these arrangements simply as "corruption" or "failure." They represented the practical use of existing networks of credit and local knowledge, at a time when permanent bureaucracies of salaried officials were costly to build and difficult to supervise. Specialized fiscal courts and auditing bodies, such as the French chambres des comptes and the English Exchequer, tried to monitor accounts, but their reach was limited.

The relationship between farming and direct administration shifted over time and varied by place. Some governments tried to replace farmers with salaried officials when they could. In France, the finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert in the 1660s tried to reform the tax farms and to reduce corruption, though the system was restored and expanded later, and in England the excise was administered by commissioners and salaried officers, producing a relatively direct state fiscal bureaucracy by the early eighteenth century. The comparison shows that there was no single trajectory from private contracting to public administration. What mattered was the combination of political constraints, available personnel, and fiscal needs in each case.

Section 74.9 · Division II

Government Borrowing, Public Debt, and the Credibility of State Finance

When revenue could not keep pace with expenditure, governments borrowed. Borrowing was not new, since medieval monarchs borrowed from merchants and bankers, but its scale and organization grew significantly. Rulers used several instruments. Short-term loans, often with merchant bankers, provided immediate cash against anticipated revenue. Long-term annuities and bonds, sold to a wider public, converted part of the debt into a regular interest burden. In Castile, juros, annuities secured on particular revenues, became a major instrument of long-term finance, while in France, rentes on the Hôtel de Ville of Paris, begun in 1522, offered investors interest payments from designated revenues. Italian city-states such as Venice and Genoa had institutionalized public debt much earlier, and the Dutch provinces issued bonds and annuities that were widely held by townspeople.

The crown of Castile illustrates both the possibilities and the dangers. In the sixteenth century the Spanish Habsburgs commanded large revenues from Castile, the Church, and American silver, but the costs of the wars in Italy, the Netherlands, and the Mediterranean, treated in Chapter 75, exceeded those revenues. The crown borrowed from Genoese, German, and Castilian bankers through asientos, contracts for the delivery of funds at distant places, repaid from anticipated silver and tax revenues. When debts became unmanageable, the crown suspended payments and negotiated settlements with its creditors. Such suspensions, often called bankruptcies, are documented in 1557, 1560, 1575, 1596, 1607, 1627, and 1647. They did not end royal credit, since bankers returned on new terms, but they raised the cost of borrowing, destabilized the banking houses, and disrupted military finance. The case shows that silver imports did not automatically produce solid state finances, because high expenditure, forward commitments, and reliance on a limited group of lenders created fragility.

Other states developed more durable credit. The Dutch Republic benefited from a wealthy commercial society, from the authority of the provincial estates, which guaranteed loans, and from the Bank of Amsterdam, founded in 1609, which supported monetary stability. Creditors trusted that Holland and the other provinces would honor their obligations, because the creditors were also, in large part, the people who governed them. In England, after the Revolution of 1688 to 1689, the combination of parliamentary control over taxes and responsibility for debt service gave lenders confidence. The Million Act of 1693 created a long-term national debt, and the Bank of England, chartered in 1694, lent to the government and issued notes. Scholars have debated how much constitutional change contributed to the improvement in English credit, since some earlier Stuart monarchs had also borrowed on large scale and since by the Stop of the Exchequer in 1672 Charles II had suspended payments on certain debts, but the long-term trend after 1688 is clear: English governments borrowed larger sums at lower interest.

The chapter's central point is that credit depended on credibility, and credibility depended on political institutions as well as on revenue. Lenders needed confidence that the borrower would repay, that repayment could not be arbitrarily abrogated, and that debts were backed by identifiable and collectable revenue. Governments in which creditors had a voice or a stake in the political system, whether in a republic or a constitutional monarchy, often borrowed more cheaply. Absolute claims in principle did not guarantee fiscal strength; a ruler who claimed to be above the law might be viewed as a bad credit risk precisely because he could repudiate debts. This reverses a common assumption that unrestrained power necessarily meant greater financial capacity.

Section 74.10 · Division II

Taxation, Consent, Resistance, and Political Bargaining

The principle that taxes should be granted by those who paid them had medieval roots and persisted in different forms throughout the early modern period. In England, Parliament's right to consent to taxation was repeatedly asserted, from the Petition of Right in 1628 to the Bill of Rights in 1689, studied in a Primary Source Window below. In Castile, the Cortes' consent was required for certain taxes, though the crown found ways to supplement them. In the Holy Roman Empire, territorial estates in many principalities granted taxes to their princes and bargained for confirmation of privileges in return, and in Poland-Lithuania, the Sejm's agreement was needed for new taxes. In some monarchies, such as France, the Estates General were not summoned between 1614 and 1789, and the crown relied on other forms of consent, including negotiations with provincial estates, courts, and venal officeholders.

Bargaining was typically specific. In return for a grant, an assembly might request the confirmation of its privileges, the redress of grievances, control over how funds were spent, or concessions on religion. The French provincial estates of Languedoc and Brittany, in the pays d'états, negotiated the amount of their contributions and administered collection themselves, which gave them considerable influence. In Castile, the Cortes exchanged grants for the crown's agreement to certain petitions, though its leverage declined as the crown found other sources. Towns bargained collectively, securing exemptions or fixed sums. Nobles negotiated exemptions or compensations for service. The result was a web of agreements, not a uniform system.

When bargaining failed, resistance followed. Popular tax revolts were frequent: the Revolt of the Gabelle (1548), the Croquants of southwestern France in the 1590s and 1630s, the Nu-pieds of Normandy in 1639, and, in the Spanish monarchy, the revolt of Naples under Masaniello in 1647 over taxes on fruit. Elite resistance could be quieter but more damaging, including refusal to register royal edicts in sovereign courts, as in the French parlements, or refusal to vote supply in assemblies. Some resistance was explicitly constitutional, as in the English conflict over ship money, a levy extended inland by Charles I in 1635, whose legality was contested in Hampden's case in 1637 to 1638, and which contributed to the breakdown of relations that preceded the Civil War treated in Chapter 77.

Taxation therefore shaped the constitutional relationship between rulers and ruled. Rulers who obtained consent could often raise more revenue, with less coercion, because subjects accepted the legitimacy of the levy. Rulers who bypassed consent might raise money in the short term but at the cost of resistance and a legitimacy deficit. This helps explain why England, Holland, and Sweden, systems with strong representative institutions, developed efficient fiscal structures, while some apparently stronger monarchies struggled to collect. But the pattern was not universal. Some representative bodies, such as the Polish Sejm, eventually became so constraining that they hampered effective taxation. The relationship between consent and capacity, then, depended on how assemblies were organized and on whether they could deliver compliance.

Historical Vocabulary
Fiscal-Military State

Definition. A scholarly term for a state organized chiefly around the capacity to raise revenue, borrow, and sustain armed forces, in which the demands of war drive the development of taxation, credit, and administration.

Historical context. The phrase was popularized by John Brewer in The Sinews of Power (1989), a study of Britain from 1688 to 1783, and the idea has been extended by historians of other states and earlier periods. It draws on the earlier arguments of Charles Tilly, Michael Roberts, and Geoffrey Parker linking war and state formation.

Why it matters. The concept helps explain how governments acquired resources and why war was so closely tied to institutional change. It can mislead if taken as a universal model, since the form of fiscal and military organization varied widely, and since political bargaining, local intermediaries, and private contractors remained essential. It should not be confused with absolutism, because a fiscal-military state could be a parliamentary monarchy or a republic.

Cause and Effect

War, Taxation, Borrowing, Administration, and Political Resistance

Long-term causes

Medieval inheritances of corporate privilege and consent; the growing cost of gunpowder warfare and fortification; commercial expansion and the monetization of economies; religious division and the demand for order.

Short-term causes

Specific wars, such as the Italian Wars, the Dutch Revolt, the Thirty Years' War, and the wars of Louis XIV, that required immediate revenue, credit, and manpower beyond what existing arrangements could supply.

Event or transformation

Governments developed new taxes, expanded borrowing, used contractors and farmers, created offices and councils, and enlarged armies and navies, with different combinations in different states.

Immediate effects

Higher fiscal burdens on subjects, increased dependence on creditors and local intermediaries, new administrative offices, tax revolts, and, in some states, bankruptcies or constitutional conflicts.

Long-term consequences

Stronger fiscal and military capacities in some states, institutional layering rather than replacement of older structures, divergence between states with robust credit and those with fragile finance, and the foundation for eighteenth century wars.

Historical complexity

War did not by itself produce state building. It depended on whether rulers could secure consent, find credit, and rely on administrators and elites. Similar pressures led to different outcomes.

Why a single cause is insufficient: A war could strengthen a government that already had credit and consent, and could destabilize one that lacked them. The same conflict that helped create the English fiscal system in the 1690s contributed to Castilian exhaustion in the 1640s. Explanation must therefore attend to the political bargains and administrative capacities already in place.

This division explains the transformation of military institutions and its consequences for government. It examines how forces were raised, armed, fed, and paid, and does not retell the individual wars treated in other chapters.
Section 74.11 · Division III

From Feudal Obligations and Retinues to Larger Organized Armies

Late medieval armies were assembled from several sources. Nobles brought their retinues, often through contracts and indentures that specified service for pay rather than purely feudal obligation. Towns supplied militias and artillery, and monarchs hired mercenary companies, among them Swiss pikemen, Italian condottieri, and German soldiers. The Hundred Years' War, treated in Chapter 51, stimulated experiments with more continuous forces. In the 1440s the French crown created the compagnies d'ordonnance, cavalry companies maintained in peacetime and paid from a tax that became permanent, and in the following decades it added corps of archers and, later, infantry. These forces were small by later standards, but they established a precedent for armies supported by regular taxation.

From the late fifteenth century, the pressures of the Italian Wars and the spread of pike and firearms increased demand for infantry. Rulers recruited large numbers of soldiers for specific campaigns, usually by contracting with captains who raised companies from their own followers or from the open market. The German Landsknechte, organized under the Habsburg emperor Maximilian I, the Swiss, and Spanish infantry formed the basis of the larger armies of the sixteenth century. By the middle of the century, the Spanish crown maintained units that were relatively permanent, organized in tercios, and stationed in Italy, the Netherlands, and the Mediterranean. These units provided continuity of experience, but they were still raised and replenished by recruitment, and their upkeep depended on the crown's ability to pay.

Military contracting was fundamental. Rulers frequently relied on military entrepreneurs, commanders who raised and equipped regiments on their own credit and were reimbursed by the government, often with delay. The most celebrated example is Albrecht von Wallenstein, who in 1625 offered the Emperor Ferdinand II an army raised at his own expense and who financed it by contributions levied on occupied territory. Smaller entrepreneurs, such as colonels who recruited and clothed their regiments, were common across Europe, and historians such as Fritz Redlich and David Parrott have shown how thoroughly commercial the business of war had become. The system had advantages. It allowed governments to mobilize forces rapidly without large permanent bureaucracies, and it distributed risk. But it also created agency problems, as commanders could inflate muster rolls, drawing pay for soldiers who did not exist, or pursue their own interests.

During the seventeenth century many states shifted toward greater direct control. Sweden under Gustavus Adolphus and his chancellor Axel Oxenstierna developed a system of recruitment and administration, later formalized in the allotment system, in which rural districts supported soldiers. The Dutch Republic professionalized its army under Maurice and Frederick Henry of Nassau, and the French crown, under the secretaries of war Michel Le Tellier and his son Louvois, from the 1640s through the 1690s, imposed stricter supervision of officers, supply, and discipline. England's New Model Army, created by Parliament in 1645, was a national force with regular pay, though it was disbanded or reduced later. Yet even in the late seventeenth century, large armies of France or the Habsburgs included foreign regiments and depended on private contractors for supply, and it would be incorrect to regard early modern armies as simply the permanent, nationally recruited, centrally administered institutions of later centuries.

Section 74.12 · Division III

Gunpowder, Infantry, Artillery, and Fortification

Gunpowder weapons had been used in European warfare since the fourteenth century, but their effect on tactics and fortification became decisive in the decades around 1500. Improved cast-bronze cannon, mounted on mobile carriages, could batter medieval walls effectively, as the French army demonstrated in its swift campaigns in Italy beginning in 1494 and as the Ottoman cannon had demonstrated at Constantinople in 1453, examined in Chapter 41. Handheld firearms developed more slowly, from the matchlock arquebus to the heavier musket, and gradually became the primary infantry weapon, supported by pikemen who protected the shooters from cavalry. The combination of pike and shot, perfected by Spanish and later Dutch and Swedish commanders, dominated European battlefields for much of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Tactical development was accompanied by changes in drill and organization. Maurice of Nassau, in the 1590s, with the help of his cousins and scholars influenced by classical military writers, introduced standardized drill that broke the complex act of loading and firing into a sequence of simple movements, allowing troops to deliver volleys in rotation and to maneuver in smaller, shallower formations. Gustavus Adolphus later built on such techniques, adding mobile light artillery and cavalry that charged rather than caracoled. By the later seventeenth century, the flintlock musket and the plug bayonet began to replace the pike, and infantry formations became thinner and more reliant on firepower. These changes required more training and discipline and thereby increased the importance of regular, drilled forces.

Fortification responded to artillery. Medieval high walls were vulnerable to cannon fire, and Italian engineers in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries developed low, thick walls backed by earth, with angled bastions that allowed defenders to cover every section of the wall with fire. This style, called the trace italienne, spread across Europe and into the colonial world through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Its effect was to make sieges long and expensive: a fortress of this kind demanded large garrisons, extensive earthworks, and a prolonged investment by the attacker. Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban, the French engineer of Louis XIV, designed and reconstructed dozens of fortresses and perfected systematic siege techniques, including parallel trenches, in the later seventeenth century.

The consequences for government were significant. Constructing a ring of fortifications was a major financial and administrative undertaking, requiring surveyors, engineers, laborers, and materials. Armies needed to hold fortified towns and to besiege those of the enemy, and large garrisons tied down men and money. Fortified frontiers, such as those created by France in the north and east and by the Dutch in the southern Netherlands, became fixed elements of the political landscape, as examined in Section 74.31. It should be emphasized that the new fortification did not make war impossible or its outcomes predictable, since it mainly altered its character, and the cost made some small states vulnerable while favoring those that could mobilize large resources. The relationship between military technology and state capacity is examined critically in Section 74.16.

Section 74.13 · Division III

Recruitment, Discipline, Officer Corps, and Military Service

Armies were recruited in many ways. Voluntary enlistment was common, drawing on the unemployed, the poor, and adventurers, often with a signing bounty. Recruiters visited towns and fairs, sometimes using deception and drink, and soldiers' pay, though irregular, offered an income that many rural dependents lacked. Foreign recruitment was extensive: Swiss mercenaries served France, the papacy, and others under capitulations negotiated with the cantons, Germans served nearly everywhere, and Irish, Scots, and Italians appeared in many armies. Coercive recruitment grew in importance, including the drafting of vagrants and convicts, the impressment of sailors, and the levying of men from villages or districts under quota systems. In Sweden, as noted, rural communities were assigned to support soldiers. In Russia, Peter the Great introduced a recruit levy in 1705 that obliged communities to supply men for lifetime service, a case reserved for Russian history and for Chapter 79.

The officer corps was changing. In the sixteenth century, commissions were largely a function of noble birth, personal following, and the ability to raise a company or regiment. In many states the purchase of commissions became common, and in France and Britain officers frequently bought their posts, treating them as investments. At the same time, rulers sought to discipline the nobility by making military service a path of honor and royal favor. Louis XIV's army combined purchase with supervision by intendants and inspectors, and Prussia's later cantonal system, introduced in the 1730s, tied noble officers to a state-run army. Military education began in cadet schools and academies from the seventeenth century, although most training continued to be acquired in service. Professionalization, in the sense of formal qualifications and career structures, developed slowly and unevenly.

Discipline was enforced by harsh punishment, since early modern commanders feared mutiny and desertion. Articles of war, issued by Gustavus Adolphus and others, established codes of conduct and penalties, including flogging, running the gauntlet, and execution for serious offenses. Yet discipline was constrained by circumstances: unpaid or underfed troops mutinied, as the Spanish army in the Netherlands did dozens of times between the 1570s and 1600s, and many mutinies were organized, with elected representatives and negotiated demands. Desertion was endemic, especially in winter and during long sieges. Authorities tried to control it through patrols, identification, and rewards for the return of deserters, with limited success.

Relations between soldiers and civilian populations were often tense. Soldiers brought money and trade, but they also brought requisitions, billeting, violence, and disease. Contemporary writers, painters, and pamphleteers, such as the engravings of Jacques Callot depicting the miseries of war, recorded the damage, and historians use court records and town petitions to supplement such depictions. Soldiers were also themselves members of communities, with wives, children, and camp followers who accompanied armies and performed vital work, including laundering, cooking, nursing, and foraging. It would be wrong to regard the army as a purely male, military body separate from society. Governments gradually tried to reduce friction by building barracks, regulating supply, and prohibiting plunder, but these reforms were slow and applied unevenly, as the next section shows.

Section 74.14 · Division III

Military Logistics, Supply, Billeting, and the Burdens of War

An early modern army moved at the pace of its supplies. Soldiers needed bread or grain daily, and each horse required many times that weight in fodder. Armies of tens of thousands consumed enormous quantities, and transporting food by wagon over poor roads was slow and costly. Rivers and sea routes were preferable, which is one reason campaigns clustered along waterways and coasts. Governments established magazines, depots of grain, flour, and munitions, in fortified towns and along likely routes of advance, and commissaries and contractors purchased and delivered provisions. The extensive records of these arrangements, such as the correspondence of Le Tellier and Louvois, permit historians to reconstruct how armies were fed, though the actual deliveries often fell short of the plans.

Armies also lived off the countryside. Plunder was an old method of supply, and in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, particularly in the Thirty Years' War, commanders regularized it through a system of contributions. An army occupying a region demanded fixed payments in money or supplies from towns and villages in return for protection against plunder. This was in effect a form of taxation by force. Wallenstein's army, for example, supported itself largely by contributions from German territories, and other armies did likewise. The system fed the army without a central treasury but devastated populations, particularly where successive armies of different belligerents exacted payments, an experience described in Chapter 67. Later in the century, governments tried to regulate the practice, replacing arbitrary extortion with negotiated or administered contributions in occupied territory, and increasing the provision of supplies from magazines.

Billeting, or quartering, soldiers in the homes of civilians was a common and heavily resented burden. Barracks were few before the eighteenth century, so winter quarters were distributed among towns and villages, whose inhabitants were obliged to house and feed soldiers, sometimes with compensation and sometimes without. Billeting provoked disputes over food, behavior, and payment, and it spread disease, since armies carried typhus, dysentery, and other infections into communities. In England, the billeting of soldiers in private houses was a major grievance in the 1620s and contributed to the language of the Petition of Right of 1628. The Bill of Rights of 1689 and the Mutiny Acts later addressed the problem in a different way, as Section 74.36 and the Primary Source Window below discuss.

Disease was often more lethal than combat. Historians estimate that in many campaigns, deaths from sickness exceeded battlefield deaths by a considerable margin, though precise figures are uncertain and varied by campaign. Armies on the march and in siege lines suffered from poor sanitation and exposure, and the movement of troops spread epidemics through civilian populations. The logistics problem thus constrained strategy: commanders could not remain long in one place without exhausting supplies, and their ability to maintain an army in the field far from its base was limited. Governments able to organize reliable supply, through magazines, contractors, and credit, gained an advantage, which is one reason that fiscal and administrative capacity cannot be separated from military effectiveness.

Section 74.15 · Division III

Navies, Dockyards, Arsenals, and Maritime Administration

Naval power required distinctive institutions. Warships were among the most complex and expensive products of the early modern economy, needing large quantities of timber, iron, cordage, canvas, tar, and skilled labor. The Venetian Arsenal, founded in the twelfth century and expanded in later periods, was one of the largest industrial establishments in Europe, building and fitting galleys. Mediterranean powers, including Spain, Venice, and the Ottoman Empire, fought with oared galleys as well as sailing ships, as in the battle of Lepanto in 1571, while the Atlantic powers increasingly built sailing warships armed with broadside guns. The Portuguese and Spanish crowns operated institutions for oceanic navigation and trade, such as the Casa da Índia and the Casa de Contratación, discussed in Chapter 68, which integrated commerce, regulation, and naval protection.

The development of permanent naval administration was gradual. In England, Henry VIII created or expanded dockyards at Deptford, Woolwich, and Portsmouth, and established the Navy Board in the 1540s to manage ships, stores, and shipwrights. Under the Stuarts the navy depended on parliamentary supply and on controversial levies such as ship money, and the Commonwealth and later restoration governments expanded it significantly. Samuel Pepys, as Secretary of the Admiralty, led reforms in the 1680s concerning standards of officers' examination and records. The Dutch maintained five regional admiralties, a decentralized structure that reflected the federal nature of the Republic and that financed the fleet through customs duties collected locally. In France, Colbert in the 1660s and after built new arsenals at Brest, Rochefort, and Toulon and established a system of maritime conscription (classes) to register seafarers. Sweden established a naval base at Karlskrona in 1680.

Recruiting crews was a persistent problem. Seamen were drawn from the merchant marine and fishing communities, and their numbers were limited. Voluntary enlistment was supplemented by impressment, forcibly taking sailors into service, which was used in England and elsewhere and created conflicts with ports and merchants. The French system of classes obliged coastal inhabitants to serve in rotation. Naval pay was often in arrears, provisions of poor quality, and mortality from scurvy and disease high, which affected morale and recruitment. Dockyards also employed large civilian workforces, including shipwrights, ropemakers, and laborers, who formed distinctive industrial communities and sometimes struck over wages.

The link between naval organization and government revenue was close. A navy was expensive to build and maintain and benefited from steady funding and reliable credit. The Dutch Republic's maritime commerce supplied customs revenue for its fleet, and Britain's growth of naval power in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries was linked to parliamentary taxation, public borrowing, and the revenue of overseas trade. Conversely, naval power protected commerce and colonies, which generated revenue. This reciprocal relationship is one of the reasons that some historians describe the Dutch Republic and Britain as maritime fiscal states, though it must be remembered that both also maintained land armies, and that naval success depended on political, financial, and strategic circumstances and not on organization alone.

Section 74.16 · Division III

The Military Revolution Debate and the Growth of State Capacity

In 1955, the historian Michael Roberts delivered a lecture arguing that Sweden between 1560 and 1660 had undergone a "military revolution," in which new tactics, larger armies, more extensive strategy, and greater impact on society together required stronger government. Geoffrey Parker later extended and revised the thesis, emphasizing the fortification revolution of the trace italienne as the key innovation. According to Parker, defensive structures made sieges longer and more expensive, which required larger armies and more resources, which in turn drove states to expand taxation and administration. In this view, the states that could mobilize the necessary resources prospered, and those that could not fell behind, and the process helps explain the rise of the Western state and its eventual global military advantage. The thesis was elaborated by Charles Tilly and others, who proposed that war made states and states made war, and by those who emphasized the fiscal-military state.

The thesis has been extensively criticized and refined. Critics note that the chronology of change differs by region and that there is disagreement about which developments were decisive. Some historians, such as Jeremy Black, argued that the most important military transformations occurred later, in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and others, such as Clifford Rogers, proposed a series of "revolutions" beginning earlier. Studies of fortification show that the trace italienne was not universally adopted, nor always decisive in sieges. David Parrott and others have shown that military contracting, not centralized state administration, was central to armies for much of the period, and that states did not always become stronger as a result of war, since many were weakened or destroyed by it. The relationship between military change and political outcomes was also not uniform: the Dutch Republic and Britain developed strong fiscal capacity within representative frameworks, while Poland-Lithuania and the Holy Roman Empire, despite much warfare, did not develop highly centralized monarchies.

A further critique concerns causation. War could encourage state growth only when governments could persuade or compel subjects to pay, and that depended on existing institutions and political relationships. Some historians, such as Thomas Ertman, emphasize that the character of earlier local government and the timing of geopolitical competition influenced the form of states. Others, including Hendrik Spruyt and scholars of political coalitions, emphasize the roles of commercial interests, urban elites, and bargaining over taxation. Research on the early modern period has also pointed to nonmilitary activities, such as the administration of justice, the regulation of markets, and welfare provision, as part of state development, so that war-centered accounts may overlook important dimensions.

For students, the debate's value is in demanding careful comparison. A military innovation could increase the demands on a government, but whether it responded successfully depended on institutions, resources, political bargains, and geography. A strong army did not necessarily imply efficient administration, and a limited monarchy could mobilize resources effectively. The chapter will return to these questions in Section 74.37, and the Historical Debate below invites students to weigh the evidence.

Historical Debate

Did Military Innovation Drive the Rise of the Early Modern State?

The question: To what extent did changes in warfare compel European governments to expand taxation, credit, and administration, and to what extent were fiscal bargaining, political institutions, and other activities equally or more important?

Interpretation A: War and military innovation were the principal drivers. Scholars in the tradition of Roberts, Parker, Tilly, and Brewer argue that new weapons, fortifications, and larger armies created pressures that states had to meet by extracting more resources and building administrative capacity. States that could do so survived and expanded, and the competitive environment selected for fiscal and military effectiveness.
Interpretation B: Political bargaining, finance, and institutions were at least as important. Other historians argue that military change was uneven, that innovations were often adopted slowly, and that war sometimes weakened states. They emphasize the roles of existing institutions, local elites, commercial coalitions, military contractors, and political consent in determining whether governments could convert pressure into capacity, and they note nonmilitary activities such as justice, regulation, and welfare.

Evidence: Both sides use records of army size, fortification, and fiscal accounts, but they weigh them differently. Supporters of A point to the growth of armies and revenues and the correlation of warfare with fiscal expansion. Supporters of B point to states such as Poland-Lithuania and the Holy Roman Empire, which fought frequently without centralizing, to the importance of contractors, and to cases in which parliamentary or republican institutions supported large fiscal systems.

Why it matters: If war alone drove state formation, then the process might seem inevitable and uniform. If bargaining and institutions mattered as much, then different outcomes are explicable and the supposed superiority of any one model is more doubtful.

Student task: Choose one state discussed in this chapter and identify two pieces of evidence that support Interpretation A and two that support Interpretation B. Then explain what additional evidence would best help resolve the disagreement.

Division IV: Bureaucracy, Law, and Territorial Administration

This division addresses how rulers attempted to translate commands issued at court into action across geographically and legally diverse territories. The central analytical distinction is among issuing a law, communicating it, enforcing it, and obtaining compliance.
Section 74.17 · Division IV

Royal Councils, Secretaries, and the Development of Administrative Offices

At the center of most governments stood a council. The royal council, variously called the privy council, conseil du roi, consejo, or Rat, advised the ruler, prepared decisions, and supervised officials. Medieval councils were often large and included nobles and prelates. During the sixteenth century, rulers increasingly relied on smaller groups of trusted advisers and on specialized councils for particular functions or territories. The Spanish monarchy developed a system of councils, known as polysynody, including the Council of State, Council of Finance, Council of War, and territorial councils such as those of Castile, Aragon, Italy, and the Indies. The Habsburg lands in Central Europe had a Privy Council, a Court Chamber for finance, and a Court War Council. In France, the council system was complex, with divisions for state affairs, finances, and justice.

Secretaries became increasingly prominent. A secretary was a literate official who drafted correspondence, kept records, and conveyed the ruler's instructions, and the position could carry great influence through control of information and access. Secretaries of state in England and France evolved into heads of departments by the seventeenth century, with responsibilities for foreign affairs, war, or the household. The Spanish secretaries, notably under Philip II, handled a flood of paperwork, and the king's habit of reading and annotating dossiers personally has become emblematic of the "paper king." Secretaries often rose from legal or humanist training and from families of officeholders, and some, such as Cardinal Richelieu's collaborators and the Le Tellier family, built dynasties of administrators.

A gradual differentiation occurred between household service and public office. In the Middle Ages, the royal household supplied most of the ruler's administrators: the chamberlain managed finances, the steward the estates, and the chancellor the writing office. Over time, specialized bodies emerged. Exchequers and treasuries, chambers of accounts, and war departments developed their own procedures, staff, and archives. Nevertheless, the distinction remained blurred, since offices were often venal or hereditary, and officers regarded their positions as property or as a form of service to the ruler. The separation between the private household and public administration was incomplete in most states until the eighteenth century and later.

Administrative offices also reflected political compromise. Rulers created new offices to reward supporters, to raise money by selling them, or to bypass existing officers who were entrenched. This produced layers of overlapping institutions, a recurring pattern called institutional layering, rather than rational redesign. An observer might find in a single kingdom several competing councils, courts, and treasuries with imprecise boundaries. Reformers, from Colbert in France to Olivares in Spain, sought to streamline administration, but they met resistance from officeholders whose interests were at stake. The result was that the growth of administration did not necessarily mean the rise of modern bureaucracy, defined by salaried officials, formal qualifications, and impersonal rules, although elements of such bureaucracy appeared, particularly in the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Section 74.18 · Division IV

Records, Registers, Surveys, and the Information Needs of Government

Governing required information. A ruler who wished to tax, recruit, regulate, or judge needed to know who lived where, what they owned, and what they owed. Early modern administrations therefore produced increasing quantities of written records. Treasuries kept accounts; courts kept registers; councils kept minutes and correspondence. Written orders and replies traveled between capitals and provinces, and secretaries maintained copies. Governments established archives, such as the Spanish royal archive at Simancas, organized under Charles V and Philip II, which preserved a vast documentation of administration, and similar repositories grew elsewhere. The growth of paper has made the period unusually accessible to historians, but the records have to be read with attention to their purposes and limits.

Efforts to count and survey populations and property were widespread but partial. Fiscal registers listed households or hearths for tax purposes, and parish records of baptisms, marriages, and burials, whose systematic keeping was promoted by both Catholic and Protestant authorities after the sixteenth century, provided demographic information. Some governments conducted enumerations, such as the Castilian census-like surveys of the sixteenth century, the Venetian anagrafi, and the Ottoman tahrir registers, which recorded households and revenue sources in provinces and were compiled periodically from the fifteenth century into the seventeenth. In England, the Domesday survey of the eleventh century had no early modern equivalent, but local surveys and, later, the hearth tax assessments recorded households. Land surveys and cadastral maps were created in some regions, and maps, increasingly accurate, aided military planning and territorial administration.

Yet state knowledge remained limited. Surveys were expensive, easily outdated, and open to manipulation by those whose property was recorded. People had incentives to conceal wealth or population, and local officials shared those incentives. Many records used local measures and categories that were hard to compare, and the sheer diversity of weights, measures, currencies, and legal terms impeded standardization. Communication was slow: orders could take days or weeks, and officials in distant provinces acted on partial information. The French intendants' inquiries of the 1680s and 1690s, which sought information on provinces for the instruction of the heir to the throne and for fiscal reform, demonstrate both the ambition and the incompleteness of the project.

Historians therefore distinguish between the amount of paper a government produced and its real knowledge of society. Some scholars, influenced by James C. Scott's concept of "legibility," suggest that states sought to make their populations readable for administrative purposes, but they caution that early modern states achieved this only partially and that communities resisted or reshaped state categories. Records are best understood as products of interaction between officials and subjects, not as neutral snapshots. A tax register may reflect not simply what people owned but what they successfully negotiated or concealed, a limitation that students should keep in mind when using such sources.

Section 74.19 · Division IV

Provincial Government, Governors, Intendants, and Local Officials

Rulers needed agents in the provinces. In many states, the highest provincial authorities were governors, usually great nobles who held office as a mark of royal favor, with military and political responsibilities. In France, provincial governors, such as the Dukes of Guise and Montmorency, held substantial local power in the sixteenth century and were sometimes rivals of the crown during the religious wars. Rulers attempted to limit this power by appointing governors for short terms, by dividing authority, and by sending commissioners with specific tasks. In Spain, viceroys governed Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, Catalonia, and the American territories, and royal governors administered the Netherlands, but they had to balance the king's instructions against local privileges.

One notable innovation was the intendant, a royal commissioner sent to a province or generality with authority over justice, police, and finance. Commissioners had existed earlier, but under Richelieu and later in the 1630s and 1640s they were employed more regularly, and after the Fronde, the civil wars of 1648 to 1653, intendants were established permanently, particularly under Colbert and Louis XIV. They supervised tax collection, military supply, and public order, and reported to the central government. Historians once regarded the intendants as instruments of royal absolutism, sweeping aside local privileges. Recent scholarship, however, shows that they operated within a framework of provincial estates, sovereign courts, and venal officers, who retained much influence and whom the intendants often had to cajole rather than command. Their effectiveness varied by province and by individual. Chapter 76 will examine their role in the Bourbon monarchy.

Other states used different mechanisms. The Habsburg monarchy employed royal commissioners and later, in the eighteenth century, new provincial offices. Sweden developed a system of governors and county administration after Oxenstierna's reforms of 1634. Russia under Peter the Great created governorates in 1708. In England, by contrast, there was no equivalent of the intendant, and local government relied on justices of the peace, drawn from the gentry, who administered justice, regulated markets and wages, supervised poor relief, and collected rates, supported by sheriffs, constables, and churchwardens. This structure of unpaid local officeholders, often called "self-government at the king's command," allowed the English crown to govern with a small central staff, but it meant that national policy depended on the cooperation of the local elite.

Across Europe, the tension between central directives and local circumstances recurred. Local officials had loyalties to their communities, family networks, and interests, and they could delay, modify, or ignore instructions. Remote provinces were harder to supervise. Rulers responded by audits, inspections, rotation of officials, and the creation of overlapping supervisory bodies, none of which fully solved the problem. The result was that central authority was extended by negotiation with, and not simply imposed on, local societies, a process that varied with the density of local institutions, the status of local elites, and the ability of the center to offer rewards or impose punishments.

Section 74.20 · Division IV

Law, Courts, Custom, and the Uneven Expansion of Royal Justice

Early modern Europe contained many overlapping legal systems. Royal courts claimed supreme jurisdiction in principle, but local courts, seigneurial, municipal, and ecclesiastical, handled much of the everyday business of justice. Seigneurial courts, in which lords or their officials tried disputes among dependents, remained widespread in France, Central and Eastern Europe, and Scotland. Town courts judged commercial and criminal matters under municipal law. Church courts handled marriage, wills, and moral offenses, along with cases involving clergy, and in Catholic regions the Inquisition operated as a specialized tribunal. The competence of these courts was often contested, producing jurisdictional conflicts and the strategic use of appeals, forum shopping, and petitions to the king.

The bodies of law themselves differed. England's common law, administered by royal judges, developed through precedent and was distinct from the civil law tradition of the Continent, which drew on Roman law taught in universities. Within France, the north followed customary law (pays de droit coutumier) while the south adhered to written, Roman-influenced law (pays de droit écrit), and different provinces had their own codified customs. In the German lands, the "reception" of Roman law advanced in the sixteenth century, especially in princely courts, while local custom persisted. Scotland retained a mixed system, and the Ottoman Empire's European provinces combined the Islamic law of qadi courts, imperial regulations (kanun), and the autonomous legal institutions of Christian and Jewish communities, a pluralism that examples from Chapter 79 will develop.

Royal justice expanded unevenly. Rulers extended the reach of royal courts through appeals, the creation of new courts, and the codification of law. The French crown issued major ordinances, such as that of Villers-Cotterêts in 1539, examined in the Primary Source Window below, and Colbert's civil and criminal ordinances of 1667 and 1670, to regularize procedure. Castile developed chanceries and audiencias, and the Holy Roman Empire's supreme courts, the Reichskammergericht and the Reichshofrat, offered imperial adjudication of disputes among its members and between subjects and rulers. In England, the Crown's prerogative courts, such as Star Chamber, extended royal jurisdiction until they were abolished in 1641. Yet the establishment of royal courts did not eliminate local ones, and the French parlements, sovereign courts staffed by purchasers of office, claimed the right to review and register royal edicts, and sometimes delayed or protested them, an important brake on royal legislation.

Hence the difference between making a law and having it obeyed. A royal ordinance might state a rule for the whole kingdom while being implemented unevenly because of geography, competing jurisdictions, limited personnel, or local resistance. Courts also enforced law selectively, and the use of the courts by ordinary people, including women and the poor, to seek remedies or to protect interests is a significant theme of social history. Litigation could be a form of negotiation with authority: subjects used the language of royal justice to resist local lords, while lords used royal law against communities. The expansion of royal justice was therefore driven in part by demand from subjects who found it useful, which helps explain its gradual advance without implying central domination.

Section 74.21 · Division IV

Public Order, Policing, Regulation, and Social Discipline

Maintaining order depended overwhelmingly on local institutions. Early modern Europe had no professional police in the modern sense in most places. In towns, councils appointed watchmen, constables, and sergeants, regulated markets, and enforced rules about trade, building, and sanitation. In the countryside, village officers, parish constables, and manorial courts handled minor offenses and disputes, while lords and justices of the peace dealt with more serious matters. Royal officials intervened in extraordinary cases, such as riots and rebellions, often with the help of troops. Some capitals developed more organized forces: Paris acquired a lieutenant general of police in 1667, a post created under Colbert, with responsibilities for public order, markets, and sanitation.

Governments and local authorities also pursued what historians call social discipline, efforts to regulate behavior, morals, and poverty. Poor relief was reorganized in many towns during the sixteenth century, as in Ypres, Lyon, and later England's Elizabethan poor laws, which required parishes to support the impotent poor, set the able-bodied to work, and punish vagrants. Workhouses and houses of correction, such as the Amsterdam Rasphuis from the 1590s, aimed to reform idleness through labor. Sumptuary laws regulated dress and expenditure according to rank, and authorities attempted to control gambling, drunkenness, and sexual behavior, often in cooperation with church consistories and kirk sessions. Historians such as Gerhard Oestreich have used the term social disciplining for such developments, though more recent work stresses that regulations were frequently ignored and that communities also used discipline for their own purposes.

Criminal justice relied on public punishment and local prosecution. Many offenses were brought by private individuals, and procedures differed between the inquisitorial systems of the Continent, in which judges directed investigation and used torture under limits, and the English system of juries. Capital and corporal punishments were visible instruments of authority. Courts also frequently pardoned and mediated, since formal prosecution was costly and communities valued settlement. Witchcraft prosecutions, which peaked in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries and involved tens of thousands of trials across Europe, illustrate how local fears, ecclesiastical campaigns, and judicial procedures could interact, with enormous variation by region and in many places very few prosecutions.

Control of information was another aspect of governance. Governments and churches censored books through licensing and indices of prohibited works, as in the Catholic Index of Prohibited Books, and regulated the press, although enforcement was hampered by clandestine printing and cross-border circulation. In the Dutch Republic, relative freedom of publication made it a center of printing, which attracted authors from elsewhere, showing that policies differed even within a region. These examples show that the enforcement of public policy relied on a patchwork of local actors, whose interests and capacities determined its success. Where they cooperated, regulation could be effective; where they did not, central intentions came to little.

Section 74.22 · Division IV

Churches, Confessional Institutions, and the Administration of Society

Churches were among the most extensive administrative institutions of early modern Europe. Parishes covered nearly the whole population, and clergy kept records, taught doctrine, solemnized marriages, administered charity, and supervised morals. After the Reformation, as Chapters 62 through 65 explained, every confession built institutions to shape belief and behavior. Catholic reform, following the Council of Trent, strengthened the role of bishops, required seminaries for clerical training, and encouraged visitation of parishes. Lutheran and Reformed churches established consistories, synods, and visitations, and in the Church of England the Crown held supremacy. These institutions overlapped with, and in many cases supplemented, secular administration.

Religious institutions supplied records and services that governments found useful. Parish registers, whose keeping was mandated by many governments and church authorities in the sixteenth century, including in the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts for France and in England from the 1530s, became the basis for later demographic and legal identification of births, marriages, and deaths. Churches ran schools and universities, which trained clergy and officials, and managed poor relief and hospitals. Clergy often read royal proclamations from the pulpit, making the parish a channel of communication. Rulers thus had reason to seek cooperation with churches, and in confessional states, such as Lutheran Sweden and Denmark, the crown and church were closely integrated.

The relationship between confessional authority and the state is the subject of the concept of confessionalization, developed by Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard. They argued that the Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic churches in the later sixteenth and seventeenth centuries cooperated with princes to promote religious conformity, discipline, and loyalty, thereby contributing to state building. The thesis has been valuable, but it has been criticized for overestimating the degree of uniformity and for neglecting the persistence of religious diversity, local resistance, and lay piety. In many regions, communities remained mixed, as in the Holy Roman Empire, where the Peace of Augsburg of 1555 and the settlement of 1648 recognized several confessions, and in Poland-Lithuania, which had a reputation for toleration in the sixteenth century. The Dutch Republic had a privileged Reformed church but tolerated others in practice.

Religious minorities experienced these arrangements differently. Jews, Muslims, and dissenting Christians lived under varying degrees of toleration and restriction, and their own communal institutions, such as the Jewish kehillot and the Ottoman millet system's Christian ecclesiastical hierarchies, performed administrative functions, collecting taxes, running courts, and managing welfare within their communities. Governments that persecuted minorities, as in the expulsion of the Jews from Spain in 1492 and of the Moriscos between 1609 and 1614, or the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, also imposed economic and administrative costs. Churches, in short, were partners, competitors, and instruments of government, and they shaped the capacity and limits of state authority.

Division V: Dynasties, Court Society, and Negotiated Political Authority

Early modern government was conducted through people and relationships as well as formal institutions. This division examines dynasties, courts, nobilities, assemblies, and cities as parts of a system of negotiated authority.
Section 74.23 · Division V

Dynastic Government, Royal Households, and Personal Authority

Most European states were dynastic, meaning that political authority passed within a ruling family by inheritance, marriage, and agreement, with variations. In hereditary monarchies, succession rules specified who inherited: France applied the so-called Salic law, excluding female succession, while Castile and England permitted women to rule, as Isabella I and Elizabeth I demonstrated. In elective systems, such as the Holy Roman Empire and Poland-Lithuania, a body of electors or the nobility chose the ruler, though dynasties often exerted strong influence, and the Habsburgs held the imperial title almost continuously from 1438 to 1740. Because states were conceived in part as family property, dynastic events, including births, marriages, deaths, and minorities, had constitutional and international consequences.

Marriage was the principal instrument of dynastic politics. The Habsburg success in assembling territories through marriages, from the union of Maximilian I and Mary of Burgundy to the marriage of their son Philip to Joanna of Castile, was proverbial, and it produced the inheritance of Charles V, treated in Chapter 75. Disputed successions could provoke wars, as the Spanish succession crisis around 1700 would show in Chapter 80, because claims by rival dynasties rested on legal arguments about descent, renunciations, and treaties. Dynastic governance meant that rulers' personal characters, health, and fertility mattered, but it did not mean that rulers governed alone. They operated within structures of councils, courts, and families.

The royal household was both a domestic establishment and a political institution. It employed hundreds or thousands of servants, from great officers such as the chamberlain and steward to grooms and cooks, and it controlled access to the ruler. Positions in the household gave nobles proximity to the monarch and opportunities for influence, and they were prized. The household also managed royal finances and estates, and in many states it was initially the core of administration before differentiating into specialized departments. The Burgundian ducal household, a model for the Habsburgs, developed elaborate ceremonial and a hierarchical structure that was adopted in Spain and Austria. In the Ottoman Empire, the sultan's palace and household similarly combined domestic and governmental functions, staffed in part by slave-officials recruited through the devshirme and other means.

The balance between personal rulership and institutional government shifted over time but did not simply move from the former to the latter. Strong rulers could dominate councils, but they needed ministers, favorites, and secretaries to translate their wishes into action, and weak or minor rulers left government to regents, favorites, and factions. The role of the favorite, such as the Duke of Lerma and the Count-Duke of Olivares in Spain or the Duke of Buckingham in England, was a recurring feature of the seventeenth century, and the power of such figures, derived from the ruler's confidence, was often resented. Institutions provided continuity, but personal relationships determined their operation, and any account of government must consider both.

Section 74.24 · Division V

Court Society, Ceremony, Patronage, and Access to Power

The court was the political center of a dynastic state. It comprised the ruler, the royal family, the household, councilors, nobles in attendance, and a shifting population of petitioners, diplomats, artists, and clients. Norbert Elias, in his study of court society, argued that courts domesticated warrior nobilities by involving them in competition for status and favor, and while his thesis has been challenged and refined, it drew attention to the court as a social and political system. Courts differed widely. The Burgundian, Spanish, and later Versailles courts were elaborately ceremonial, whereas the English court under Elizabeth I and the Stuarts combined ritual with the constant presence of Parliament and city. The Ottoman court at Istanbul had its own hierarchies, and the courts of the German princes ranged from large to small.

Ceremony was not merely decorative. Rituals of precedence, dress, and access communicated rank and political favor. Who could enter the king's chamber, who held the royal garments, who sat or stood in the royal presence, were questions of importance because they showed who was close to the sovereign, and thus who could obtain favors. Disputes over precedence among nobles and among ambassadors were serious, as Section 74.28 notes. Royal entries, coronations, funerals, and festivals displayed the dynasty's power and legitimacy to subjects and foreigners, and printed descriptions and engravings extended their audience. Court culture also fostered patronage of artists, musicians, and scholars, which served political and symbolic purposes.

Access to the ruler was the currency of court politics. Rulers distributed offices, pensions, titles, lands, gifts, and marriages, and in return they received service, loyalty, information, and money. Petitions to the ruler were a primary route for seeking redress or favor. Patronage worked through chains of brokers: a great noble might secure a place for a client, who in turn rewarded relatives and dependents, and the resulting networks connected the center with provincial societies. A minister's power rested on his ability to dispense patronage and to protect clients, which is why ministers such as Cardinal Richelieu cultivated extensive networks of dependents. Such practice was widely accepted as normal and not necessarily corrupt, since early modern political morality prized loyalty, reciprocity, and obligation, even while contemporaries also condemned abuses and venality.

Court society should therefore be treated as an institution. It served as a forum for negotiation between ruler and elites, as a means of integrating nobles into royal service and thereby of containing aristocratic rivalry, and as a channel for information and appeals. It also had costs. Courts were expensive, and competition for favor could foster faction and intrigue. Courts could be isolated from the wider society, and rulers who relied too heavily on a narrow circle might misjudge provincial opinion. Louis XIV's Versailles, which transferred the court to a vast residence outside Paris in 1682, illustrates these dynamics, but its detailed history belongs to Chapter 76, and it should not be used as the model for all courts.

Section 74.25 · Division V

Nobility, Landed Elites, Officeholding, and Royal Service

Nobilities constituted the most important elite in nearly every European state, though they differed enormously. In England the peerage was a small group, with a larger gentry class beneath it. In France the noble estate was large, perhaps a few hundred thousand people, divided between the old sword nobility and the newer robe nobility, whose status came from judicial and administrative office. The Polish szlachta formed perhaps eight percent or more of the population, an unusually large proportion, though differences in wealth were vast. The Spanish hidalgos and grandees, the German imperial knights and territorial nobilities, the Hungarian and Bohemian magnates, and the Russian boyars and later service nobility had their own characteristics. These elites owned land, held local jurisdiction, and provided military leadership, and they were therefore indispensable to rulers.

Relations between crowns and nobilities were complex. Nobles were potential rivals, as in the French religious wars and the Fronde, but they were also collaborators. Rulers offered offices, honors, pensions, and military commands, and nobles offered service and legitimacy. Over time, many nobilities shifted from regional power based on private retinues to service in the army, court, and administration. In Russia, Peter the Great's Table of Ranks in 1722 formalized the link between service and status, and in Prussia and the Habsburg lands the nobility was integrated into officer corps and bureaucracies. Where nobles retained strong collective institutions and privileges, as in Poland-Lithuania, Hungary, and parts of the Holy Roman Empire, they could limit royal power.

Venal office, the sale of public offices, was a distinctive practice that linked the state to its subjects' wealth. In France, the crown sold offices in justice and finance on a large scale, and from 1604, under the system known as the paulette, officeholders could pay an annual fee to secure the right to transmit their offices to heirs. This produced a class of officeholders with property interests in their positions, which made them reliable creditors of the crown but difficult to dismiss or reform. Many offices conferred noble status, enabling wealthy commoners to rise socially. Sale of offices provided revenue without taxation, but it created vested interests and fragmented authority. In Spain, the crown sold municipal offices and titles, and in the Papal States and other Italian states, offices were commonly sold. England used venality more limitedly but permitted extensive fee-taking, and the Ottoman system used both appointment and purchase in various forms.

Historians no longer regard the relationship between crown and nobility as a simple contest in which the state defeated the aristocracy. Many nobles benefited from the growth of royal government, since it created careers and expanded patronage, and many also adapted to commercial and financial opportunities. The growth of royal service shifted the grounds of noble status without eliminating noble power. This is why scholars speak of the negotiation of status and loyalty. A ruler who tried to override noble privileges risked rebellion, while one who respected them could count on noble cooperation, and the balance varied with circumstances and over time.

Section 74.26 · Division V

Estates, Parliaments, Diets, and Representative Institutions

Representative assemblies were widespread in early modern Europe, though they differed in composition, powers, and fate. The English Parliament, composed of the House of Lords and the elected House of Commons, had the right to consent to taxes and to legislate with the Crown, and it met irregularly at the monarch's summons. The French Estates General, composed of clergy, nobility, and Third Estate, met rarely and ceased to be summoned after 1614, while provincial estates in Languedoc, Brittany, Burgundy, and other pays d'états continued to negotiate taxation. The Cortes of Castile, with representatives of the towns, met periodically and bargained over taxes, while the separate Cortes of Aragon, Catalonia, and Valencia protected their kingdoms' laws. In the Holy Roman Empire, the imperial Diet brought together electors, princes, and imperial cities and became permanent at Regensburg from 1663, while territorial diets or Landtage in many principalities represented prelates, nobles, and towns.

In Poland-Lithuania the Sejm, a diet of the nobility, held legislative power, and from 1652 the practice of the liberum veto, by which a single deputy's objection could dissolve a session, made decision-making increasingly difficult, a subject for Chapter 79. In Sweden the Riksdag of four estates, including a peasant estate, played a variable role, subordinate under Charles XI and Charles XII, but significant in the early eighteenth century. In the Dutch Republic, the States General and the provincial estates, especially the States of Holland, were sovereign bodies in which towns and nobles participated. Representation was therefore corporate and not individual: members spoke for orders, towns, or provinces, and not for a population of equal citizens.

The political authority of assemblies depended on their powers over taxation, their frequency of meeting, their ability to coordinate, and the willingness of rulers to respect them. Assemblies that controlled revenue and met regularly, as the English Parliament after 1688 and the Dutch provincial estates, acquired substantial influence. Assemblies that were summoned irregularly, divided by status, or bypassed through alternative revenue could decline. Historians have debated why some assemblies survived and others did not, with explanations including the strength of towns, the structure of noble privilege, the fiscal needs of rulers, and the effect of war. A once common view that representative institutions declined everywhere in the seventeenth century in the face of absolutism has been modified: many continued to function at provincial and local level, and some grew in importance.

Assemblies were also arenas of petition and negotiation. They provided the means by which subjects could express grievances, request legislation, and bargain for privileges, and thus they furnished legitimacy to taxation. They should not, however, be idealized as democratic. They represented privileged groups and often protected their narrow interests against both the crown and the unprivileged. Peasants, women, and the poor were largely excluded, and religious minorities were often barred or restricted. Understanding assemblies as institutions of corporate representation rather than modern democracy allows students to appreciate both their importance in limiting arbitrary taxation and their limits in representing the whole population.

Section 74.27 · Division V

Cities, Guilds, Provinces, and the Negotiated Exercise of Government

Towns were central to early modern government. Their wealth, markets, ports, and craft production were prime sources of revenue, and their councils administered local justice, regulation, and defense. Cities had charters that conferred rights of self-government and fiscal privilege, and some, such as the Hanseatic towns, the Italian republics, the free imperial cities of the Holy Roman Empire, and the cities of the Netherlands, were nearly autonomous. Urban governments were typically controlled by narrow oligarchies of merchants and landowners, who administered taxes, courts, and public works, and who negotiated with rulers on behalf of their communities. Rulers had good reasons to cultivate urban support: towns supplied credit, loans, and troops, and urban magistrates could enforce royal policy locally.

Guilds and corporations organized much urban economic and social life. Craft guilds regulated training, quality, prices, and membership, and also provided mutual aid, religious ceremonies, and political representation in some towns. Merchants' companies and corporations of officeholders, such as the lawyers or notaries, had comparable functions. Governments used such corporations as instruments, granting monopolies in exchange for payments or service, and they also tried to curb their privileges when these obstructed policy. The idea of society as a body of corporations, each with rights and duties, shaped the way government was understood, and it persisted until the revolutionary period, as Chapter 87 describes.

Provinces likewise had distinct institutions and privileges. Provincial estates, courts, and governors mediated between central government and localities. In France, the provinces differed in their tax regimes, with some negotiating their contributions through estates and others administered directly by royal officials, as noted in earlier sections. In the Habsburg lands, each province or kingdom had its own diet, whose consent was required for taxes and recruits. In the Dutch Republic, provincial sovereignty was a constitutional principle. The retention of provincial privileges was often a condition of union in composite monarchies, and attempts to override them risked rebellion, as the Catalan revolt of 1640 shows.

The dependence of rulers on influential communities meant that government was often a negotiated process. A ruler seeking taxes or troops had to bargain with the towns and provinces that would supply them, offering confirmation of privileges, commercial concessions, or political voice. Communities, in turn, used their leverage to protect their interests. This explains why governments that appeared strong on paper could be constrained in practice, and why some of the most effective states were those that institutionalized bargaining, as the Dutch Republic and England did. It also explains why local studies are so important to the history of the state, since they reveal how institutions worked in the communities that sustained or resisted them.

Division VI: Diplomacy, Sovereignty, and Territorial Relations

This division establishes the institutional foundations of early modern international politics, while reserving the detailed history of European wars and balance of power diplomacy for Chapter 80.
Section 74.28 · Division VI

Permanent Embassies, Resident Ambassadors, and Diplomatic Intelligence

Medieval rulers had exchanged envoys for specific missions, such as negotiating a marriage or treaty, but permanent representation developed in the fifteenth century in the competitive environment of the Italian states. Milan, Venice, Florence, Naples, and the papacy exchanged resident ambassadors who remained at foreign courts to report on conditions, negotiate continuing business, and defend their governments' interests. The Peace of Lodi in 1454 and the Italic League that followed formalized a system of balance among the Italian powers, and diplomatic practice flourished alongside it. Historians such as Garrett Mattingly have emphasized the Italian origins of resident diplomacy, though subsequent scholarship has shown that earlier and parallel practices existed elsewhere and that the system developed gradually.

The practice spread north and west. Ferdinand of Aragon maintained resident ambassadors in several capitals by the end of the fifteenth century, and during the sixteenth century France, England, the Habsburgs, and the papacy expanded their diplomatic networks. By the seventeenth century, resident embassies were standard among major European states, and by the middle of that century, the diplomatic congresses at Münster and Osnabrück brought together representatives from across the continent. The Ottoman Empire received resident ambassadors from European states, notably the Venetian bailo at Constantinople, long before it sent permanent ambassadors abroad, which it did extensively only from the late eighteenth century. In the east, Muscovy sent and received envoys, increasing exchanges with Western courts in the seventeenth century, and Russia established permanent missions from the time of Peter the Great.

Ambassadors performed multiple functions. They represented their rulers in ceremonial settings, negotiated treaties and trade matters, transmitted instructions, and gathered intelligence. Ambassadors' dispatches, especially the famous relazioni of Venetian envoys, which summarized conditions at foreign courts, are major sources for historians. Intelligence gathering involved correspondents, informants, and sometimes bribery, and cipher systems were used to protect messages and were deciphered by rival cryptographers. The post, and increasingly regular courier services, made communication faster, but delays remained: a dispatch from Constantinople or Madrid to London could take weeks, and ambassadors had to act on limited instructions and make decisions on their own initiative.

Diplomatic practice was shaped by questions of precedence and ceremony. Rulers and ambassadors insisted on rank, and disputes over precedence, such as that between French and Spanish ambassadors, could disrupt negotiations or lead to violence. Diplomatic immunity and the inviolability of ambassadors were gradually recognized as customary principles, formalized in later writings such as those of Hugo Grotius, although governments sometimes violated them. The development of diplomacy created a class of professional or semi-professional diplomats, trained in law and languages, and secretariats that supported them. But diplomacy remained closely tied to courts and dynasties, and ambassadors were often nobles chosen for rank as much as skill, so that diplomatic service overlapped with patronage and court society.

Section 74.29 · Division VI

Dynastic Claims, Treaties, Negotiation, and Political Legitimacy

Rulers justified their international ambitions in the language of law and dynasty. A claim to a territory might rest on inheritance, marriage, a previous treaty, a feudal relationship, a papal grant, conquest, or the consent of the inhabitants, and rulers or their lawyers produced elaborate justifications. The Valois kings of France based claims in Italy on Angevin inheritance, the Habsburgs on dynastic descent, and the English kings maintained, nominally into the eighteenth century, a claim to the French throne. Such claims were not simply pretexts, since they shaped the way legitimacy was perceived by subjects and other rulers, and they structured negotiation. Printed manifestos, legal treatises, and genealogies were used to publicize claims.

Marriage diplomacy was a standard tool. Alliances were cemented by marriages, and dowries, renunciations, and succession clauses were negotiated at length. The marriage of Louis XIV to the Spanish infanta Maria Theresa in 1660, accompanied by her renunciation of her inheritance, subject to the payment of a dowry that Spain was unable to pay, produced the basis for later French claims and wars, as Chapter 80 will explain. Marriage negotiations thus tied together dynastic strategy, finance, and international politics. Because such arrangements could produce unexpected results when births and deaths intervened, they contributed to the uncertainty that characterized early modern diplomacy.

Treaties were the means of settling wars and making alliances. They were drawn up in Latin or, increasingly in the seventeenth century, French, and signed and ratified with formal ceremonies, oaths, and exchanges of documents. Peace conferences, such as Cateau-Cambrésis in 1559, Vervins in 1598, Münster and Osnabrück in 1648, the Pyrenees in 1659, and Utrecht in 1713, brought together multiple parties. They resolved territorial, dynastic, and religious issues, and their provisions often included guarantees, which created ongoing obligations. Treaties were not always observed: rulers broke them when advantageous, but they also appealed to them to justify their positions, and the existence of a body of treaty law gradually shaped expectations.

Legal thought supplied a vocabulary for international relations. Humanist lawyers and theologians, among them Francisco de Vitoria, Alberico Gentili, and Hugo Grotius, discussed just war, the rights of ambassadors, and the law of nations. Grotius's On the Law of War and Peace (1625) became influential, though its immediate impact is easily exaggerated. These writings did not create a system of enforceable international law, but they influenced diplomatic discourse and provided standards of legitimacy. The international order thus rested partly on shared assumptions about dynastic right, treaties, and religion, and partly on power, so that legitimacy and force were intertwined.

Section 74.30 · Division VI

The Peace of Westphalia and the Problem of Sovereignty

The Peace of Westphalia refers to a group of treaties concluded in 1648 to end the Thirty Years' War, whose course is traced in Chapter 67. The negotiations took place in two Westphalian cities: Osnabrück, where the Holy Roman Emperor and the Protestant powers, notably Sweden and German Protestant estates, negotiated, and Münster, where the Emperor and Catholic powers, notably France, negotiated. Treaties were signed at both on 24 October 1648. Separately, Spain and the Dutch Republic concluded the Peace of Münster in January 1648, by which Spain recognized Dutch independence, ending the Eighty Years' War. Spain and France, however, continued to fight until the Peace of the Pyrenees in 1659.

The imperial settlement addressed specific problems. It confirmed and extended the religious arrangements of the Peace of Augsburg of 1555, extending recognition to Calvinists as well as Lutherans and Catholics, and it set 1 January 1624 as the "normative year" for determining the confessional status of territories and church property. It affirmed the rights of the imperial estates (the electors, princes, and cities) within the empire, including the right to participate in imperial deliberations and the right to make alliances, provided they were not directed against the emperor or the empire. It adjusted territories: Sweden obtained territory on the Baltic and North Sea coasts, including Western Pomerania, Wismar, and Bremen-Verden, with representation in the imperial Diet, while France received Habsburg rights and territories in Alsace in terms whose precise meaning was ambiguous and later disputed. Bavaria's elector kept the electoral dignity, and a new electorate was created for the Palatinate. The imperial courts continued to function, and the emperor retained significant prerogatives, though they were limited in practice by the estates' rights.

Later generations remembered Westphalia as the beginning of the modern international system of sovereign, territorial states. This interpretation, developed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and influentially formulated in the twentieth, for example by Leo Gross in 1948, treated the treaties as establishing the principle of state sovereignty, equality, and nonintervention. Many historians now reject this view as anachronistic. The treaties dealt with the constitution of the Holy Roman Empire, in which the estates remained subordinate members of a larger political order, and they contained provisions, such as the guarantees by outside powers and the continuing imperial structure, that contradict a simple model of absolute sovereignty. Scholars including Andreas Osiander and Derek Croxton argue that the concept of a "Westphalian system" is a later construction, and that the sovereign state system emerged gradually, with dynastic and composite structures persisting well beyond 1648.

It is therefore important to teach the settlement and the myth separately. The settlement was a negotiated resolution of imperial constitutional, religious, and territorial conflicts, important for stabilizing the empire's confessional relations and for reducing the scope of religious war among its members. The myth is the later narrative that the treaties founded modern sovereignty. Even so, the settlement matters for the history of the state: it illustrates how European powers negotiated a complex peace in a multilateral conference, how treaties could embody guarantees and balance, and how a composite, decentralized entity could survive by redefining the relationship between its members. The concept of sovereignty, as the Vocabulary box below explains, developed over time and was neither new in 1648 nor complete afterwards.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty

Definition. The claim to supreme and final authority within a political community, including the power to make law, to judge in the last instance, to tax, and to wage war, and, in later usage, the claim of states to be independent of external authority.

Historical context. Medieval lawyers developed ideas of supreme jurisdiction, and in the sixteenth century Jean Bodin gave sovereignty a systematic definition. The word came to be used in several overlapping senses: internal supremacy over subjects, external independence from other rulers, and, later, the authority of the people or nation. Early modern rulers frequently claimed sovereignty while sharing practical power with assemblies, courts, estates, and local elites.

Why it matters. Sovereignty named an aspiration and a legal claim, and not a description of how all governments worked. Applying a modern, territorially exclusive notion of sovereignty to early modern states, or to the settlement of 1648, produces anachronism. Students should ask who claimed sovereignty, against whom, and with what practical capacity.

It illustrates why historians hesitate to equate the treaty with the creation of sovereign states. It also reveals the primarily constitutional character of the settlement, because the article regulates relations within an existing political order. The treaty is the product of diplomats and represents the interests of the estates, Sweden, and the Emperor. It does not represent the views of ordinary subjects who lived through the war's devastation, nor does it itself show how the provisions were later interpreted and applied.

Section 74.31 · Division VI

Frontiers, Territorial Claims, and the Geography of State Authority

Early modern rulers claimed territories, but their effective authority was often weaker at the edges and complicated by overlapping rights. Boundaries were frequently zones, defined by local custom, geography, and the jurisdictions of lords and communities, and not by precisely surveyed lines. Enclaves and exclaves were common, as with the Papal enclaves in southern France at Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin, the scattered possessions of the Habsburgs, and the intermingled territories of the German principalities. A village might owe taxes to one authority, justice to another, and religious obedience to a third. Disputes over such rights were endemic and often resolved by negotiation, arbitration, or war.

Rulers sought to define and fortify frontiers. Fortress lines, such as Vauban's "pré carré" in northern France, were designed to secure a defensible boundary, and diplomats negotiated boundary commissions to settle disputes, as in the Pyrenees in 1659, which defined the Franco-Spanish frontier, and in later treaties. Surveys and maps assisted such efforts, and the growth of cartography in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries made territorial claims more visible. Yet clear linear boundaries were slow to become the norm. In eastern Europe and the steppe, the Ottoman, Habsburg, Polish-Lithuanian, and Russian frontiers were broad borderlands where authority was contested, and populations such as Cossacks, frontier soldiers, and nomadic groups were influential. The Habsburg Military Frontier, a belt of settlements of soldier-peasants along the Ottoman border, combined defense and settlement, illustrating how frontiers were administered.

A distinction between territorial claims and effective control is essential. A dynasty might claim a kingdom that it did not rule, or rule a region where its writ ran weakly. The kings of England claimed the French crown into the eighteenth century, and Spain claimed sovereignty over lands in the Americas far beyond its actual settlement. The Ottoman sultan claimed suzerainty over Moldavia and Wallachia, whose princes governed with considerable autonomy. Historical maps that color areas uniformly can obscure such distinctions. Students should therefore treat maps critically, asking which kind of authority is represented and how reliable the information is.

The geography of authority also involved communications and local power. A ruler's reach depended on roads, rivers, ports, postal systems, and the presence of loyal officials and garrisons. Mountain regions, forests, marshes, and remote islands often retained substantial autonomy. Seas could connect or divide, and maritime empires administered scattered territories by ship. These conditions meant that government differed from region to region, and that the idea of a uniform, territorially exclusive state was largely an aspiration, to be gradually approximated in some places, notably in western Europe by the eighteenth century, and not in others. Frontiers, in short, were places where states negotiated their limits.

Section 74.32 · Division VI

Diplomatic Coalitions and the Emerging European State System

Rulers formed alliances for security, expansion, and dynastic purposes, and these alliances shifted frequently. Fifteenth century Italian leagues, the Holy League against France in 1511, and the sixteenth century rivalry between Habsburgs and Valois illustrate the pattern. Confessional divisions added new alignments, such as the Catholic League and the Protestant Union, while states also crossed confessional lines for strategic reasons: Catholic France allied with Protestant Sweden and the Dutch against the Catholic Habsburgs during the Thirty Years' War, and France cooperated at times with the Ottoman Empire against the Habsburgs from the reign of Francis I. Such behavior shows that religious solidarity did not determine alliances.

Coalition warfare became characteristic. Wars of the later seventeenth century, including the Nine Years' War (1688 to 1697), involved large alliances against the expansion of Louis XIV's France, and the coordination of such coalitions required subsidies, joint planning, and diplomatic negotiation. The Dutch statesman William III and the English diplomats who worked with him, and later the Duke of Marlborough and Prince Eugene of Savoy, are associated with such coordination, though the full story belongs to Chapter 80. Subsidy treaties, by which wealthier powers paid poorer ones for troops, became common, linking fiscal capacity directly to military and diplomatic power, and helping explain why states with strong credit, such as Britain and the Dutch Republic, were especially effective coalition leaders.

The idea of a balance of power, meaning that no single state should be allowed to dominate, was familiar in Italy in the fifteenth century and grew in importance in the seventeenth. It was invoked by states resisting Habsburg and later French dominance, and it was written into treaties such as the Peace of Utrecht in 1713, which referred to the balance of power in Europe in connection with the Spanish succession. Historians debate how far the idea functioned as a guide to policy and how far it served as a rhetorical justification for particular interests, and they distinguish between the balance of power as a description, as an aim, and as a system. The concept is treated more fully in Chapter 80, and it should not be projected backward as a fixed system operating in the sixteenth century.

International relations remained dynastic, composite, and confessional, as well as strategic. The existence of an "emerging European state system" is therefore a modern analytical description, not something that contemporaries designed. Over the period, diplomatic institutions, treaty practice, and shared expectations accumulated, so that by the early eighteenth century, European rulers operated in a recognizable system of resident ambassadors, congresses, and treaties, yet it differed from the later nineteenth century concert and from modern state systems. Eastern and southeastern powers, including Russia and the Ottoman Empire, participated increasingly in this system, and their relationships with western states, examined in Chapter 79, were shaped by it and shaped it in turn.

Section 74.33 · Division VII

Monarchies, Republics, and Alternative Constitutional Arrangements

Early modern Europe contained a spectrum of political forms. Hereditary monarchies, such as France, Spain, England, Sweden, and Denmark, predominated, but they differed in the role of assemblies and law. Elective monarchies, including the Holy Roman Empire and Poland-Lithuania, made the throne subject to election by electors or by the nobility, with consequences for dynastic continuity and noble influence. Republics, including Venice, Genoa, the Swiss Confederation, and the Dutch Republic, were governed by councils of citizens or magistrates, though the "citizens" were a restricted group, and the Dutch Republic, a federation of provinces, combined republican institutions with the leadership of the House of Orange as stadholders. The Papal States were a monarchy governed by an elected, celibate ruler, and the Ottoman Empire was a hereditary dynastic empire with its own institutions.

Even among monarchies, constitutional arrangements varied. The English monarchy, after the conflicts of the seventeenth century treated in Chapter 77, became a constitutional monarchy in which Parliament had firm control of taxation and legislation. French kings claimed absolute authority while governing within a system of courts, privileges, and custom. In Sweden, authority oscillated between the crown and the Riksdag, and in Denmark, the crown declared royal absolutism in 1660 and the Royal Law of 1665, a notable case in which a monarchy formally removed the nobility's constitutional rights following a fiscal and political crisis after war with Sweden. Poland-Lithuania developed a "noble republic," often called the Commonwealth of Two Nations, with an elected king and powerful nobility.

Republics were not necessarily weak. Venice survived for centuries by a system of councils and balanced offices, and the Dutch Republic became a leading commercial and military power, financed by high taxes, strong credit, and a powerful navy. Federations and confederations, such as the Swiss and the Dutch, achieved cooperation among autonomous units, though they could be slow to decide. Republican systems often had advantages in credit and legitimacy, because those who governed were also those who paid and lent, but they faced challenges from oligarchic domination and from tension between regents and popular or princely factions. The existence of republics in a monarchical continent reminds us that monarchy was not the sole or necessary form of effective government.

The coexistence of these forms shows that European political authority did not develop along a single constitutional path. Some historians have identified two broad tendencies, a parliamentary or constitutional path and an absolutist path, as in the work of Perry Anderson and Thomas Ertman, but they stress many variants and intermediate cases. Others prefer to emphasize the continuum between negotiated and coercive government, and the similarity of fiscal-military developments across regimes. Whichever framework is used, the lesson is that constitutional form did not predict state capacity in a simple way, as the next sections show, and that it would be misleading to treat any one system as the norm or as the goal of European history.

Section 74.34 · Division VII

Regional Variations in Northern, Central, Eastern, and Southern Europe

Differences in geography, economy, and social structure produced distinctive patterns across Europe. In the west, commercial wealth and relatively dense urban networks supported fiscal systems based on customs and excise, and credit institutions developed in the Dutch Republic and England. In much of eastern Europe, where towns were smaller and the nobility dominated landholding, rulers' revenues depended more on landed resources and noble cooperation, and nobles often controlled the labor of dependent peasants, a system of enforced agrarian labor often called the second serfdom, which strengthened noble power in regions such as Poland-Lithuania, Brandenburg, Bohemia, and Russia. These contrasts should not be reduced to a simple division between a progressive west and a backward east, since the east was diverse and had effective institutions of its own.

Selective examples illustrate the variety. Poland-Lithuania, a large Commonwealth with an elected king and a powerful noble Sejm, had weak central taxation and a small standing army by the late seventeenth century, relying on noble levies and hired troops, and the liberum veto hindered reform, though the Commonwealth was a major European power in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. The Holy Roman Empire combined imperial institutions with hundreds of territorial states, some of which, notably Bavaria, Saxony, Brandenburg-Prussia, and the Habsburg hereditary lands, developed substantial administrations and armies, so that state building within the Empire occurred principally at the territorial level, a subject for Chapter 78. Scandinavia included Sweden, whose crown, through reforms, the allotment system, and conquests, created a strong fiscal-military state in the seventeenth century, and Denmark-Norway, which adopted absolutism in 1660.

Russia under the Romanovs and especially Peter the Great combined autocracy with state service and tax innovations: Peter's poll tax, introduced between 1718 and 1724, taxed male peasants directly to support the army, and the Table of Ranks of 1722 tied noble status to service. The Ottoman European order, covering the Balkans and Hungary for long periods, relied on a combination of provincial administration, timar landholding in return for military service, tax farming, the standing Janissary corps, and the autonomous religious communities, with changes over time as cash taxation and tax farming expanded. These systems are treated in more detail in Chapter 79. In southern Europe, Spain's fiscal and administrative structures were shaped by the composite nature of the monarchy, and Italian states ranged from republics such as Venice to the papal monarchy and Spanish-ruled territories.

The comparisons caution against universal models. The Ottoman Empire, Russia, and the Habsburg monarchy all maintained large armies with different financing and recruitment systems. Sweden mobilized large forces from a small population by remarkable administrative organization. Poland-Lithuania, with extensive territory and population, struggled to finance defense. Each system reflected the interplay of geography, economy, social structure, and political bargaining, and none was simply an imperfect copy of western models. The detailed national histories belong to later chapters, but this survey shows that varied institutions could produce effective or ineffective government, depending on circumstances.

Section 74.35 · Division VII

Tax Revolts, Resistance, Fiscal Crisis, and Administrative Failure

The history of state formation includes many failures. Governments overspent, defaulted, and provoked revolt. The Spanish crown's repeated suspensions of payment, noted earlier, were one form of fiscal crisis. The French crown experienced severe financial strain during the religious wars, in the 1630s and 1640s, and at the end of Louis XIV's reign, when debt and taxation reached burdensome levels. In the Ottoman Empire, fiscal difficulties in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, linked to inflation, military costs, and changes in warfare, led to the expansion of tax farming, the debasement of coinage, and unrest, including the Celali rebellions in Anatolia. These crises varied in cause and severity, but they reveal that expansion of resources did not guarantee stability.

Resistance took many forms. Open revolts, such as the French popular risings, the Catalan and Portuguese revolts of 1640, the revolt of Naples in 1647, and the Fronde, were often triggered or intensified by taxation, though they usually involved political, religious, and social grievances as well. Peasant communities, towns, and nobles used petitions, legal action, refusal to pay, flight, evasion, and violence. Soldiers' mutinies over unpaid wages were another form of resistance, and in the Spanish Netherlands, mutinies and the unpaid army contributed to the sack of Antwerp in 1576, the "Spanish Fury." Officials resisted too: corrupt or negligent administrators, local magistrates unwilling to enforce unpopular measures, and sovereign courts that refused to register edicts all limited government.

The limits of coercive enforcement are significant. Troops could be sent to collect taxes, as in the French dragonnades or military expeditions to rebellious provinces, but repeated coercion was costly and could provoke further resistance, and the soldiers themselves might be sympathetic to local populations or unpaid. Governments often had to compromise, granting remissions, reducing demands, or confirming privileges. Moreover, the corruption and privilege that burdened finance were not simply obstacles but part of how the system worked: exemptions rewarded elites, and venal officials had financial stakes in the regime. Reformers who challenged these arrangements faced determined opposition.

These cases support two points. First, state capacity was fragile and reversible: a government could lose ground in a crisis and recover or not. Second, the relationship between pressure and capacity was mediated by political legitimacy. Governments that secured the cooperation of key groups, whether through assemblies, privileges, or patronage, could survive heavy burdens, whereas those that alienated them risked collapse or civil war. The mid seventeenth century, with revolts and civil wars across Europe, led some historians to speak of a "general crisis," a thesis that remains debated but underscores the vulnerability of early modern states under fiscal and military strain.

Section 74.36 · Division VII

Ordinary Subjects and the Everyday Experience of State Authority

For most Europeans, the state was encountered through local figures and specific obligations. A peasant household paid direct taxes assessed by neighbors, dues to a lord, tithes to the church, and often indirect taxes on salt, wine, or beer when purchasing them. It might owe labor service for roads or fortifications, supply horses or carts for passing armies, and billet soldiers in winter. Men of military age faced the risk of recruitment, whether by lot, quota, or press gang, and families were affected by the loss of labor. The household economy, in which women, men, and children all contributed, had to absorb these demands, and women in particular managed the household and farm when men were absent or conscripted, and often interacted with officials, tax collectors, and courts.

Subjects used the institutions available to them. Petitions to authorities, from village communities to the monarch, sought tax relief, protection from billeting, or redress of abuses, and the survival of thousands of petitions allows historians to hear the voices of ordinary people, though filtered through the conventions of petitioning and scribes. Lawsuits in local and royal courts addressed disputes over taxes, tenure, and rights. Communities negotiated collective payments, hid assets, or sheltered neighbors from recruiters. Religious institutions provided both discipline and support: parish clergy recorded vital events and supervised morals, while confraternities and charities assisted the poor. Minorities such as Jewish communities negotiated their taxes and protections with rulers and local authorities, often through leaders who represented the community collectively.

Experiences differed by place and status. In western Europe, free peasants and tenants bargained with landlords and officials, while in parts of eastern Europe, serfdom limited mobility and subjected peasants to the demands of lords and the state through them. Townspeople experienced the state through guilds, markets, and urban regulation, and their tax burdens could be heavy, but they also had access to courts and urban privileges. Wartime brought exceptional burdens: soldiers' plundering, contributions, and disease, as in the Thirty Years' War, caused devastation in many German regions, though modern estimates of population loss vary widely by area, with some districts losing a large share of inhabitants while others were relatively spared.

State authority was thus experienced through negotiation and often through resentment. People distinguished between the king, whose justice might be invoked against local oppressors, and his officials, who could be blamed for abuses, a pattern visible in many petitions and rebellions that proclaimed loyalty to the monarch while attacking his agents. Over time, repeated interaction with courts, tax collectors, and recruiters increased familiarity with royal institutions, and gradually altered expectations of what authority could demand and what it owed. Yet the ordinary subject's world remained largely local, and the reach of the state, though growing, was mediated by lords, magistrates, clergy, and neighbors.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Rural Household Confronts the State

Consider a composite, representative household in a village of a European monarchy in the seventeenth century, constructed from patterns documented in many local studies and not from a single named family. The household farmed a modest holding, worked by a husband and wife, their children, and perhaps a servant. Each year, the village's assessors, chosen from among the inhabitants, determined the household's share of the direct tax quota that the community owed, and negotiated with neighbors over who was richer and who poorer. A bad harvest led the community to petition for a reduction, and the petition, composed with the help of the parish priest or a local notary, was sent to the provincial authorities.

When troops were quartered in the village for the winter, the household might be obliged to house and feed soldiers, supply fodder for horses, and pay a contribution toward their upkeep. The household's women managed the supplies and tried to protect stores, daughters, and property, while the village headman negotiated with the officers. Court days brought the household to the local seigneurial or royal court, as a litigant in a boundary dispute or as a witness. The parish priest read royal proclamations and recorded the family's baptisms and burials, and the village church or consistory might discipline members for moral offenses. When a recruiting party arrived, a son might be drawn by lot or sent as a substitute, or the family might pay to avoid the levy.

These experiences show the state not as a distant abstraction but as a series of local demands and relationships, filtered through neighbors, notables, and clergy. They also show agency: households petitioned, evaded, bargained, and sometimes rebelled, shaping the way policies were carried out. Historians reconstruct such lives from tax registers, court records, petitions, parish books, and diaries, which are unevenly preserved and often reflect the viewpoint of officials rather than villagers.

The ruler and ministers: A new tax is a means of financing a war the ruler regards as necessary to dynastic security and reputation. The concern is how to obtain revenue quickly without provoking resistance from assemblies, courts, or nobles, and how to keep creditors confident.

Section 74.37 · Division VII

Comparing State Capacity, Political Bargaining, and the Limits of Centralization

It is now possible to compare early modern states along several dimensions that are often confused. Government revenue measures the resources a state extracted, but it must be considered in relation to population, wealth, and prices. Military effectiveness concerns the ability to field, supply, and sustain forces and to win wars, and it depends on organization and strategy as well as size. Legal reach refers to the extent to which royal law and courts operated across territory, and administrative capacity to the ability to gather information and implement policies. Political consent describes the degree to which key groups accepted government demands, and legitimacy the broader sense that authority was rightful. These dimensions did not necessarily move together.

A government could maintain a large army while exercising weak control over provinces, as Habsburg Spain and arguably Louis XIV's France in some respects did. Another could have limited royal prerogatives but strong taxation, credit, and naval capacity, as the Dutch Republic and post-1688 England did. Poland-Lithuania had broad noble political participation but limited fiscal capacity. Sweden achieved high mobilization for a small population by integrating peasants and nobles into a militarized fiscal system. The Ottoman Empire sustained a large and effective administrative apparatus over a long period while adapting to changing fiscal conditions. Such examples show that classifying states as simply "strong" or "weak," or "absolutist" or "constitutional," obscures more than it reveals.

The concept of absolutism requires particular caution. Historians have long used it to describe monarchies, such as France under Louis XIV, in which the monarch claimed unlimited authority and ruled without representative institutions. Recent scholarship, however, emphasizes that even the most "absolute" monarchs depended on cooperation with nobles, officeholders, and local institutions, and that absolutism was a claim and a style of rule more than a description of reality. Some scholars, therefore, speak of "absolutism" as a political project and ideology, whose practical limits were substantial, and Chapter 76 will examine this debate for France. Others emphasize that rulers advanced by cooperation with elites in what has been called a "negotiated" absolutism, so that the difference between absolute and constitutional systems was one of degree and style.

Finally, the sections of this chapter caution against teleology. Stronger institutions did not necessarily entail unlimited royal power, and greater royal power did not entail greater capacity. England's parliamentary monarchy extracted more per head than many absolute monarchies, and Dutch republican institutions funded a vast fleet. The modern nation-state was not the inevitable result: many states remained composite, dynastic, and confessionally diverse into the eighteenth century and beyond, and the later uniform states arose from further transformations, notably those of the revolutionary era examined in Part XIV. The most helpful way to understand the early modern state is as an arena of continuing negotiation among rulers and the many groups on whom they relied.

Historical Debate

Was Early Modern State Formation Centralization, or Negotiated Reorganization?

The question: Did early modern governments primarily concentrate authority in central institutions, or did they reorganize authority by bargaining with entrenched institutions and local elites?

Interpretation A: Centralization was the dominant tendency. Some historians argue that rulers built larger administrations, armies, and fiscal systems that reduced the power of regional nobilities and corporate bodies, and that this movement toward centralization, although incomplete, characterized the period and laid the basis for later states, as reflected in the growth of intendants, standing armies, and royal courts.
Interpretation B: Authority was reorganized through negotiation. Other historians argue that rulers often extended their authority by securing the cooperation of nobles, officeholders, towns, and churches, offering patronage, privileges, and offices in return, so that state power grew through accommodation with existing institutions and not through their replacement. On this view, local intermediaries remained essential, and many apparently centralizing measures were negotiated or diluted.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite the growth of royal offices, revenue, and armies, and legislation extending royal jurisdiction. Supporters of B cite local studies of tax collection, venal office, litigation, and assemblies, which show how royal measures were adapted, and comparative cases such as England, the Dutch Republic, and the Habsburg lands. Many scholars now combine the interpretations, accepting growth in capacity while stressing its negotiated character.

Why it matters: If centralization was the norm, the nation-state may seem a natural outcome, but if negotiation was crucial, the variety of European political forms and the contingency of later developments become clearer.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Royal Authority, Taxation, Armies, and Local Privilege, c. 1500, c. 1600, and c. 1700

What Changed

  • Around 1500, armies were assembled campaign by campaign from retinues, mercenaries, and militias; by 1700 many states maintained large forces throughout the year, with regular supply, drilled infantry, and permanent fortifications and dockyards.
  • Revenue grew substantially in nominal terms, and public borrowing shifted from short-term loans from merchant bankers toward long-term debt, most evidently in the Dutch Republic and England.
  • Administrative offices, records, and diplomacy became more specialized: councils, secretaries, intendants, treasuries, and resident embassies were more numerous and more continuous than in 1500.
  • The theory of sovereignty developed, and royal courts and ordinances extended the formal reach of royal law, although variation remained.

What Stayed the Same

  • Rulers depended on nobles, towns, local officeholders, and creditors to collect revenue and enforce policy.
  • Corporate privilege, regional law, and exemption from taxation persisted widely, and venal office remained important.
  • Dynasty, marriage, and composite territories continued to shape politics and diplomacy.
  • Most subjects experienced authority through local institutions, and resistance, petition, and negotiation remained routine.

Who experienced change most directly? Peasants and townspeople in regions of heavy taxation and billeting, soldiers and sailors, and officeholders who gained or lost from reforms. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural communities, nobles whose local privileges persisted, and subjects in regions less touched by war. Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutional change was cumulative and layered, and that treating 1700 as a modern state in miniature overstates the transformation while ignoring it overlooks real expansion of capacity.

Division VIII: Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

Section 74.38 · Division VIII

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that between about 1450 and 1750, European governments substantially expanded their capacities to tax, borrow, wage war, administer territory, and conduct diplomacy, but that these changes occurred unevenly, through negotiation, and within persisting structures of dynasty, privilege, and local government. The early modern state was not a unitary national government. It was most often a composite of territories and jurisdictions held together by a ruler, a dynasty, a set of councils and officers, and a web of relationships with elites and communities.

Taxation and finance were central. Governments combined direct and indirect taxes, tax farming, venal office, and borrowing, and their success depended on the capacity of local intermediaries, the consent of assemblies and privileged groups, and the confidence of creditors. Large revenue did not guarantee solvency, as Castile showed, and constitutional limits did not preclude fiscal strength, as England and the Dutch Republic demonstrated. Military institutions grew through contracting and gradual administrative control, and the logistical and social burdens of war shaped both state and society, though the military revolution thesis requires qualification for chronology, region, and causation.

Administration, law, and the churches carried government into localities. Councils and secretaries enlarged the central apparatus, intendants and justices of the peace extended it locally in different ways, and courts, parishes, and corporations enforced rules and recorded lives. The difference between issuing and enforcing law was constant. Court society and patronage organized access to power, assemblies and diets provided consent, and cities and provinces defended liberties while supplying resources. Diplomacy acquired permanent institutions, yet remained dynastic and composite. The Peace of Westphalia settled imperial problems, and the later sovereignty myth must be separated from the historical settlement.

Taken together, these developments explain why different political systems produced different outcomes. A state's strength could be measured in revenue, military effectiveness, legal reach, administrative capacity, consent, or legitimacy, and these did not always coincide. Strong institutions did not necessarily mean unlimited royal power, and no single model, absolutist, parliamentary, republican, or imperial, was the destination of European history. This foundation prepares for Chapter 75, which examines the Habsburgs, Spain, and dynastic empire as a major composite system, and for Chapters 76 through 80, which examine other political systems and the international relations among them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how European governments between about 1450 and 1750 developed greater capacities to tax, borrow, fight, administer, judge, and negotiate with other powers, and why their authority nonetheless remained uneven, contested, and dependent on cooperation. Its central claim is that the early modern state was a process and a set of capacities, not a finished national government. Most states grew from dynastic territories with their own laws and privileges, and many were composite monarchies in which a single ruler governed several distinct polities.

Division I established the medieval inheritances of kingship, estates, towns, churches, and customary law, and identified the pressures of war, religious division, commerce, and the demand for order that encouraged stronger government. It distinguished pressure from success, since the same pressures that expanded capacity could also provoke bankruptcy, revolt, and civil war. Composite monarchy explained why a ruler's titles did not translate into uniform authority.

Division II treated taxation and finance. Governments combined domain revenue, direct taxes assessed through local intermediaries, customs and excise, monopolies, tax farming, venal office, and public borrowing. Privilege shaped who paid, and consent shaped how much could be collected. Castile's repeated suspensions of payment showed that large revenues and American silver did not secure stable finance, while the Dutch Republic and post-1688 England showed how representative institutions could support credit. Raising a tax was not the same as collecting it.

Division III explained military organization. Armies moved from retinues and short-term mercenary forces toward larger, longer-lived establishments, often raised through contractors and foreign recruitment. Gunpowder, pike and shot, drill, and the trace italienne increased costs, while logistics, billeting, and disease imposed severe burdens on soldiers and civilians. Navies required dockyards, timber, crews, and credit. The military revolution debate showed that military change influenced state growth but did not by itself determine its form.

Division IV turned to administration and law. Councils, secretaries, registers, surveys, governors, intendants, and local officeholders carried government into the provinces, but information was limited and orders were negotiated in practice. Overlapping courts and legal traditions limited royal justice, while local magistrates and churches enforced discipline, recorded vital events, and ran charity. The distinction among issuing, communicating, enforcing, and obtaining compliance with a law proved essential.

Division V examined the people and relationships through which government worked. Dynastic succession, marriage, households, courts, patronage, noble service, venal office, assemblies, cities, guilds, and provinces formed a system of negotiated authority. Court society was a political institution for distributing favor, organizing access, and integrating elites, and assemblies provided consent and a channel for grievance, though they represented privileged groups and not the whole population.

Division VI considered diplomacy and sovereignty. Resident embassies spread from Italy across Europe, treaties and congresses accumulated procedures, and dynastic claims supplied the vocabulary of legitimacy. The Peace of Westphalia was a settlement of imperial, religious, and territorial problems, and the later claim that it founded the sovereign state system is a myth that historians have challenged. Frontiers were often zones, and territorial claims differed from effective control.

Division VII emphasized variation and limits. Monarchies, elective monarchies, republics, and confederations coexisted, and regional contrasts in noble power, serfdom, urban strength, and fiscal structure produced different outcomes in Poland-Lithuania, the Empire, Sweden, Russia, and the Ottoman European order. Tax revolts, mutinies, and fiscal crises revealed fragility, and ordinary subjects experienced the state through taxes, billeting, courts, parish discipline, and recruitment, responding by petition, evasion, litigation, and sometimes rebellion.

The comparative synthesis distinguished revenue, military effectiveness, legal reach, administrative capacity, consent, and legitimacy as separate measures of capacity. A state could have a large army and weak provincial control, or limited royal prerogatives and formidable credit and naval power. Absolutism was better understood as a claim and a political project than as a description of unlimited power, and centralization was an aspiration pursued through negotiation.

The chapter thereby provides the framework for Part XII. Chapter 75 will examine the Habsburgs and Spain as a composite dynastic system, Chapter 76 will analyze Bourbon France, Chapter 77 the Dutch and British alternatives, Chapter 78 the transformation of central Europe, Chapter 79 the northern, eastern, and Ottoman European orders, and Chapter 80 the balance of power. In each, the concepts introduced here, composite monarchy, the fiscal-military state, venal office, patronage, and sovereignty, will help students distinguish what rulers claimed from what their governments could do.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Early modern state
A territorially organized political authority of roughly 1450 to 1750 that claimed supreme jurisdiction and exercised uneven capacities to tax, wage war, judge, and administer, usually through negotiated relationships.
Dynasty
A ruling family whose members inherited, exchanged, and contested claims to territories and offices, often across what later became separate states.
Composite monarchy
A polity in which one ruler held several territories that kept their own laws, privileges, and institutions.
Estates
The legally defined social orders, typically clergy, nobility, and towns, whose representatives met in assemblies to grant taxes and present grievances.
Privilege
A legal right or exemption belonging to a person, group, town, or province, such as freedom from a tax or the right to local jurisdiction.
Sovereignty
The claim to supreme and final authority within a political community, later also the claim of external independence.
Fiscal-military state
A state organized around raising revenue, credit, and armed forces, in which the needs of war drive institutional development.
Direct tax
A levy on persons, households, or property, such as the French taille or the English subsidy and land tax.
Excise
A tax on the sale or consumption of goods, such as beer, wine, or salt, collected at points of production or sale.
Gabelle
The French salt tax and monopoly, varying by region and widely resented.
Tax farming
The leasing of the right to collect a tax to a private contractor in return for a fixed payment to the government.
Venal office
A public office obtained by purchase, often heritable, treated by its holder as property.
Asiento
A contract by which bankers provided funds to the Spanish crown at specified places, repaid from anticipated revenues.
Juro
A Castilian annuity bond secured on royal revenues, a main instrument of long-term crown borrowing.
Military entrepreneur
A commander or contractor who raised and equipped troops on his own credit and was reimbursed by a government.
Trace italienne
A style of fortification with low, thick walls and angled bastions, designed to resist artillery and to give defenders overlapping fields of fire.
Billeting
The quartering of soldiers in civilian homes, a common and resented burden.
Contributions
Payments exacted from occupied or neighboring territory by an army in return for protection from plunder, in effect a form of taxation by force.
Impressment
The compulsory enlistment of sailors or other men into naval or military service.
Intendant
A French royal commissioner with authority over justice, police, and finance in a province, regularized in the seventeenth century.
Customary law
Law based on local practice and recorded custom, in contrast to Roman law or royal statute.
Parlement
One of the sovereign courts of France, staffed by venal officers, with the right to register and remonstrate against royal edicts.
Confessionalization
The interpretation that Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed churches cooperated with rulers to build religious conformity and discipline, contributing to state building.
Patronage
The distribution of offices, favors, and protection in return for loyalty and service, a basic mechanism of early modern politics.
Court society
The system of ceremony, access, and favor surrounding a ruler, which organized relations between sovereign and elites.
Liberum veto
The Polish-Lithuanian practice, first used in 1652, by which one deputy's objection could dissolve a Sejm session.
Resident ambassador
A diplomat stationed continuously at a foreign court, a practice developed in fifteenth century Italy.
Normative year
The date, 1 January 1624 in the Westphalian settlement, used to fix the confessional status of territories and church property.
Balance of power
The idea or policy that no single state should dominate, invoked in treaties such as Utrecht in 1713.
Absolutism
A claim to unlimited royal authority and the style of rule associated with it, whose practical limits are emphasized by recent scholarship.
Social discipline
Efforts by authorities and churches to regulate morals, poverty, and behavior, often enforced locally.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question students bring to the political history that follows. Instead of asking which states were strong and which weak, it asks what a government could actually do, through whom, at what cost, and with whose consent. That shift makes the great dynastic and constitutional narratives of Chapters 75 through 80 intelligible as variations on shared problems of revenue, force, administration, and legitimacy.

It also reveals processes that extend far beyond the chapter. The development of public credit, standing forces, written administration, and resident diplomacy shaped the eighteenth century wars and the fiscal crises that preceded the revolutions of Part XIV. The persistence of privilege and local intermediaries explains why attempts at reform in the later eighteenth century provoked such conflict, and the ideas of sovereignty and representation developed here would be transformed by the revolutionary generation.

The chapter connects regions that older narratives separated. Spanish silver, Genoese credit, Dutch excise, English parliamentary borrowing, Swedish allotment, Polish noble assemblies, Russian service, and Ottoman tax farming all addressed related problems in different ways. Seen together, they show Europe as a field of interacting, comparable, but distinct political experiments, with no single center and no single destination.

For Volume III, the chapter supplies the institutional counterpart to the cultural and religious transformations of the Renaissance and Reformation and to the overseas expansion of Part XI. Rulers who sought to govern confessional divisions, finance wars, and profit from empire did so through the institutions described here. The most important lesson is perhaps that the modern state was not the predetermined outcome of this history. It emerged, later and unevenly, from choices, bargains, and failures that students should continue to examine with the same attention to contingency.
