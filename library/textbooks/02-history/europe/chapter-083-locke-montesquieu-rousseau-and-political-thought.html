Chapter Opening

Every government asks to be obeyed. The thinkers examined in this chapter asked the question that such a demand invites: by what right, for what purpose, and within what limits? Their answers were not the same, and the differences among them remain the structure of modern political argument.

In 1689, two treatises on government appeared in London without an author's name on the title page. Their author, the physician and philosopher John Locke, did not acknowledge them publicly in his lifetime and listed them among his works only in a codicil to his will in 1704. His caution was reasonable. The first treatise ridiculed a theory of monarchy that many English clergy and royalists had defended, and the second argued that a people could lawfully remove rulers who betrayed their trust. Arguments of that kind had been dangerous under Charles II and James II. Locke's associate Algernon Sidney had been executed in 1683, and his unpublished manuscript on government had been used as evidence against him.

Locke was not the only writer to discover that political theory could be hazardous. In 1748 a French magistrate, Charles-Louis de Secondat, baron de Montesquieu, published The Spirit of the Laws at Geneva without his name. It was quickly known to be his, and it was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Prohibited Books in 1751. In 1762 Jean-Jacques Rousseau, a citizen of Geneva living in France, published The Social Contract in Amsterdam under his own name. Within weeks of the appearance of his educational treatise Emile that year, the Parlement of Paris ordered it burned and its author arrested, and the authorities of Geneva condemned both books. Rousseau fled. These episodes show how seriously governments took arguments about the sources of their own authority.

The questions these writers raised were not abstract. Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, Europeans lived through religious wars, civil wars, dynastic crises, fiscal pressure, and the growth of states that taxed and conscripted more effectively than their predecessors, as Chapters 66, 67, and 74 explained. Rulers claimed authority from God, from inheritance, from ancient custom, from conquest, or from the necessity of order. Subjects, parliaments, estates, cities, and churches replied with their own claims. Political thought was one of the ways in which these conflicts were argued, and in the work of a few authors it became systematic philosophy.

This chapter examines four principal answers to the problem of legitimate government. Thomas Hobbes argued that peace required an effective sovereign authorized by those it governed. Locke argued that government existed to protect rights that belonged to individuals before government was created, and that rulers who violated that trust could be resisted. Montesquieu asked how institutions, laws, and customs could restrain power and secure liberty, and distinguished among kinds of government rather than defending one universal model. Rousseau asked how citizens could live under common laws without being dominated by another person's will, and answered with the idea of a sovereign people expressing a general will. These theories overlapped in vocabulary, but they were neither identical nor easily combined.

The chapter does not treat them as steps in a march toward modern democracy. Locke did not propose universal suffrage. Montesquieu admired a mixed constitution in which a hereditary nobility had a protected role. Rousseau, who put popular sovereignty at the center of his theory, distrusted representative assemblies and excluded women from citizenship in his educational writings. Many authors who spoke of natural equality lived in societies that maintained serfdom, slavery, religious disabilities, and property qualifications, and some profited from them. Understanding these theories historically means taking both their ambition and their limits seriously.

Historians reconstruct these debates from printed treatises, manuscripts, pamphlets, correspondence, censorship records, trial evidence, publishers' decisions, translations, and the marginal notes of readers. This evidence is uneven. It is overwhelmingly the work of educated men, and it records what was argued far better than what ordinary people thought about obedience and rights. Where the chapter relies on a text, it identifies the text and distinguishes what an author argued from how later generations used and simplified it.

The chapter belongs to Part XIII, Science, Enlightenment, and European Society, within Volume IV. Chapter 81 examined changing knowledge of nature and Chapter 82 examined the networks of print, correspondence, academies, and salons through which ideas circulated. This chapter turns to the content of political argument. Chapter 84 will examine how reforming rulers and governments applied, resisted, or ignored such ideas, and Part XIV will examine the revolutionary crisis in which they acquired new and unforeseen consequences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why questions of sovereignty, obedience, and resistance became urgent in early modern Europe.
  2. Distinguish natural law, natural rights, civil law, and the law of nations, and explain how Grotius, Pufendorf, and Spinoza used them.
  3. Analyze Hobbes's argument from the state of nature to the authorized sovereign, including its limits.
  4. Trace Locke's reasoning from natural law to property, consent, representation, trust, and resistance, in the context of the Exclusion Crisis and the Glorious Revolution.
  5. Assess Locke's treatment of toleration and the limits of his liberalism, including property, religion, and colonial entanglements.
  6. Interpret Montesquieu's comparison of republics, monarchies, and despotisms and his analysis of political liberty and the distribution of power.
  7. Evaluate why the modern formula of three separate branches misrepresents Montesquieu's argument.
  8. Explain Rousseau's account of inequality, the social contract, the general will, and popular sovereignty, and the interpretive disputes they raise.
  9. Compare Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau on legitimacy, liberty, sovereignty, and representation.
  10. Evaluate criticisms of contract theory, especially Hume's, and the challenges posed by women, antislavery writers, and colonial subjects.
  11. Contextualize the reception of these ideas in Britain, France, the Dutch Republic, German lands, Italy, Poland-Lithuania, and the Atlantic world without assuming a single predetermined application.

Historical Context

By 1600 most Europeans lived under rulers who claimed authority that was in some sense given rather than chosen. The Reformation and the confessional wars examined in Part X had divided the loyalties of subjects and rulers and forced writers on all sides to ask when subjects might resist a ruler of another faith. Huguenot, Catholic League, Dutch rebel, and English Puritan writers all developed arguments about the limits of obedience, while royal apologists responded that kings answered to God alone. Jean Bodin's Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576) defined sovereignty as the supreme and perpetual power to make law, and the concept shaped nearly every later discussion, including those of Hobbes and Rousseau.

Political writers drew on a limited stock of inherited resources. Roman law supplied ideas of property, contract, and the common good. Scholastic natural law, developed from Thomas Aquinas and refined by Spanish theologians, taught that human law was answerable to a moral order accessible to reason. Scripture supplied examples of kingship, tyranny, and covenant. Aristotle and Polybius supplied classifications of regimes and the ideal of mixed government. Customary law and the practice of estates, parliaments, and corporations supplied a practical vocabulary of ancient rights. Much of the creative work of the period consisted of recombining these materials under new pressures.

The seventeenth century supplied the pressures. The Thirty Years' War devastated central Europe and ended in the settlements of 1648. In the same years the Fronde challenged the French crown, and the English Civil Wars led to the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649, treated in Chapter 77 alongside the Dutch Republic and the later British constitutional revolutions. Louis XIV's France, examined in Chapter 76, offered the most influential model of centralized monarchy, while the Dutch Republic, Venice, the Swiss cantons, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth offered different models of republican and noble self-government. Writers therefore had comparative evidence at hand, and they used it.

The thinkers of this chapter also wrote within the intellectual conditions described in Chapters 81 and 82. The methods of the Scientific Revolution encouraged attempts to reason systematically from first principles, and the Republic of Letters supplied readers, translators, and critics across state borders. These conditions did not produce agreement. Hobbes was influenced by geometry, Montesquieu by comparative observation, and Rousseau by moral psychology and classical republican models. Their works were published in a Europe where censorship, patronage, exile, and the book trade shaped what could be said and where. That commercial and legal setting is part of the evidence for the history of political thought.

Section 83.1

Political Authority, Sovereignty, and the Crisis of Legitimacy in Early Modern Europe

Early modern political argument began from a distinction that is easy to state and hard to apply: the difference between possessing power and having a right to exercise it. A conqueror, a usurper, a hereditary king, and an elected magistrate might all hold power. Which of them held it rightfully, and what made the difference? The question was pressing because the religious divisions of the sixteenth century had made it impossible to assume that rulers and subjects shared a single faith or a single account of authority.

The most influential royal answer was divine right monarchy. In its stronger forms, as in James VI and I's True Law of Free Monarchies (1598) or Jacques-Benigne Bossuet's later Politics Drawn from the Very Words of Holy Scripture, kings were God's lieutenants, accountable to God but not to their subjects, and resistance was sin. Not every defender of monarchy went so far. Many accepted that kings were bound by divine and natural law, by fundamental laws of succession, and by oaths, while denying that subjects could enforce those limits. Jean Bodin's concept of sovereignty gave this position its legal vocabulary, though Bodin himself acknowledged that sovereigns were bound by divine and natural law and by certain contracts.

Against such claims stood a rich tradition of constitutional and contractual argument. The Vindiciae contra tyrannos (1579), written in the Huguenot cause, argued that kings and people were bound by covenants and that magistrates might resist tyranny. Estates and parliaments in England, the Dutch provinces, Poland-Lithuania, Hungary, and the German principalities defended inherited liberties, and customary law offered a language of ancient rights. Republican institutions, from Venice and the Swiss cantons to the Dutch Republic, showed that substantial states could be governed without a single hereditary ruler, though each was dominated by a narrow elite.

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648, treated in Chapter 67, did not settle these questions. It strengthened the principle that territorial rulers governed religion within their lands, but it left open what rulers owed their subjects. Mid century revolts in England, France, and Naples, and the Dutch struggle with the Spanish crown that preceded them, kept the issue alive. By the time Hobbes and Locke wrote, political writers were not choosing between order and disorder but among several rival theories of order, each tied to particular institutions and interests.

It matters, however, that the legal shape of a polity could differ from the rhetoric used to describe it. In France, jurists spoke of a crown that endured beyond any one king, while provincial estates, municipal corporations, seigneurial jurisdictions, and the sovereign courts called parlements negotiated practical authority. In the Holy Roman Empire, imperial law and the rights of its estates survived alongside the prerogatives of territorial princes. The English monarchy was hereditary, but statute, common law, ecclesiastical authority, and parliamentary taxation complicated any picture of command flowing from a single source. Sovereignty was therefore a claim about ultimate authority, not a straightforward description of every institution through which people were governed. An account that equates the absolute vocabulary of royal writers with unlimited power in everyday life would miss why rulers repeatedly had to bargain for revenues, raise loans, and negotiate local cooperation.

The relationship between political theology and constitutional argument was especially unstable in an age of divided confessions. After the Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres of 1572, some French Protestants developed theories under which lesser magistrates could resist a ruler threatening the true church. Catholic League writers, confronting a Protestant claimant to the French crown, could reach analogous conclusions from a very different religious starting point. Appeals to the people's original authority, to divine law, or to the estates of a realm were instruments in immediate disputes, yet the arguments acquired lives beyond those disputes. A theory first used against one dynasty might later be employed against a different government or defended by a rival confession. Students should distinguish this transferable vocabulary from the particular political alliances in which it was invented.

The word consent could likewise refer to several different things. It might mean an oath taken by a corporate estate, the acceptance of a ruler in a coronation ritual, the approval of representatives in an assembly, or the voluntary agreement of individual persons. Political theorists would dispute which form, if any, was sufficient to transform coercive power into rightful government. A municipal charter or a parliamentary statute could therefore be cited as evidence of consent without implying that adult inhabitants individually participated. The difference between corporate consent and individual agreement is a crucial thread linking early modern resistance theory to Locke's later arguments and to Rousseau's rejection of any attempt to delegate the sovereign people's will.

Everyday government sharpened the stakes. A household might experience the state through a tax farmer, a justice of the peace, a village official recruiting soldiers, a toll collector, or a religious authority enforcing conformity, rather than through a published philosophical treatise. Complaints about arbitrary imprisonment, seizure of property, forced billeting, or conscience turned abstractions into grievances. Yet the same inhabitants could demand stronger magistrates to contain disorder or protect markets. Security and restraint were not simply rival principles held by separate political camps; they were needs that could conflict within the same community. The principal political writers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries offered different ways of explaining how those needs might be reconciled.

Historians reconstruct these debates from sermons, pamphlets, parliamentary speeches, legal briefs, and treatises. Such texts were written to persuade and often to defend a faction, so they show the arguments available to contemporaries rather than a neutral picture of how governments worked. Treatises written by clergy, lawyers, and officeholders also say little about the views of peasants, artisans, or women, whose political experience appears more often in petitions, riot records, and court cases than in philosophy.

Section 83.2

Natural Law, Natural Rights, and the Foundations of Political Obligation

Natural law was the central common language of European political thought. It held that there were moral standards binding on all human beings, discoverable by reason or revealed by God, against which human laws and governments could be judged. Scholastic writers had taught that human law derived its force from conformity to natural law. Natural rights language was related but distinct. Where natural law emphasized duties and a moral order, natural rights emphasized claims that individuals held, such as self-preservation, which governments might be bound to respect. In practice the two vocabularies overlapped, and historians still debate when and how the rights language became dominant.

Hugo Grotius (1583 to 1645), a Dutch jurist, wrote On the Law of War and Peace (1625) after escaping imprisonment in the political and religious struggles of the Dutch Republic and settling in France. Grotius argued that human beings are naturally sociable and that certain principles, such as keeping promises and respecting others' property, bind people independently of any particular church or government. He also distinguished natural law from the law of nations, which arose from the practice and consent of states. His work was a reaction to confessional war and to the need for common rules among rival Christian states, but it also served interests: Grotius had earlier defended the Dutch East India Company's claims to maritime trade.

Samuel Pufendorf (1632 to 1694), a German jurist, developed a more systematic and duty-centered theory in On the Law of Nature and of Nations (1672). He grounded natural law in human weakness and sociability and in God's command, and he taught that states arose from agreements that created obligations of obedience. His work became standard in universities across Protestant Europe. Baruch Spinoza (1632 to 1677), writing in the Dutch Republic in a Jewish dissenting tradition that had broken with his community, took a very different path. In the Theological-Political Treatise (1670) he argued that natural right extended as far as natural power, that peace required a sovereign with real authority, and that freedom of thought was compatible with and even beneficial to a stable state.

The argument drew on a much longer history than the seventeenth century alone. Medieval theologians and jurists had discussed whether a ruler who commanded injustice still produced a binding law, and scholastic writers distinguished an ordinance's coercive force from its moral legitimacy. Spanish and Portuguese thinkers confronting the conquest of the Americas examined the rights of Indigenous peoples, the legitimacy of empire, and the authority of Christian princes over non-Christian communities. Francisco de Vitoria, for example, rejected some straightforward claims of conquest based solely on religion while nevertheless offering arguments about travel, trade, and war that could support intervention. Natural law did not dictate one political policy. Its language could constrain an empire's pretensions, rationalize expansion, or expose the inconsistency of applying a general rule selectively.

Grotius made the distinction between kinds of legal obligation indispensable. He described duties rooted in the rational and social constitution of human beings, voluntary obligations derived from agreements, and practices of states that produced norms governing war and diplomacy. His famous conditional suggestion that natural law would retain a measure of validity even if one were to imagine the impossible and impious hypothesis that God did not exist was not an announcement of atheism. It was an effort to show that rational judgment could identify obligations independently of disagreement over religious authority. Grotius himself wrote within a Christian framework. His theory also allowed war under specified conditions and accepted forms of servitude that modern readers reject. The work is historically important because it joined a purported universal norm to detailed questions about property, promises, sovereignty, maritime access, and military conduct.

Pufendorf's emphasis on sociability offers a second way to understand why the law of nature mattered. Human beings were neither sufficient unto themselves nor reliably cooperative without rules. They needed social life to survive but could injure one another through ambition and fear. Duties toward other persons, duties toward oneself, and duties toward God formed an interconnected system. His account of states as artificial moral persons helped jurists think about authority as an institution that endured despite changes in individual officeholders. Spinoza reached different conclusions through his identification of right with power and his commitment to freedom of philosophizing. In his account, suppressing thought could produce instability, since officials could regulate outward conduct more readily than inward judgment. Comparing the two shows why no single definition of natural law adequately characterizes the period.

There was also a problem of evidence. Natural-law systems could reason from assumptions about human nature, but observers could point to remarkably different legal customs: communities recognizing communal land, kingdoms ruled by dynastic succession, aristocratic republics, and societies permitting bondage. Universal propositions therefore required arguments about which features of social life were genuinely natural and which arose from convention, force, or history. Authors frequently treated practices familiar in their own societies as general features of human life. Later critics would ask whether a theory was describing the world or silently reproducing the privileges of its author. This problem should follow students into every subsequent section, especially those concerning property, gender, colonial occupation, and the claimed consent of people who never had an opportunity to choose.

These theories should not be read as early statements of modern human rights. Natural rights in this period were usually tied to theological premises, to duties, and to particular legal contexts. They did not by themselves entail equal political participation, religious equality, or the abolition of slavery, and Grotius and Pufendorf both discussed circumstances in which slavery could be justified. Their significance was that they offered a standard of legitimacy independent of a ruler's will and a vocabulary that later writers would extend and sometimes turn against the societies that produced it.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Natural Law, Natural Rights, and Political Obligation

Natural law is the idea that a moral order binding on all people can be known by reason or revelation, and that human law is answerable to it. Natural rights are claims that individuals hold by nature, such as to self-preservation, and that governments should respect. Political obligation is the question of why subjects ought to obey a government.

In this period the three were interrelated but not identical. Natural law often emphasized duty, and natural rights emphasized claims. Modern human rights, in contrast, are usually understood as universal legal entitlements of all persons. Reading seventeenth century natural rights as the same thing is a common error.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Hugo Grotius, On the Law of War and Peace (1625)

Creator: Hugo Grotius, Dutch jurist and diplomat · Date: 1625, Paris · Type: Latin treatise on natural law and the law of war

Context: Grotius wrote after the Dutch Revolt, the Twelve Years Truce, and the political purge that led to his imprisonment and escape. The work appeared during the Thirty Years War, when rules governing conflicts between Christian states seemed both urgent and uncertain.

Paraphrase, not a translation, of the argument of the ProlegomenaGrotius argued that human beings have a natural desire for peaceful society, and that rules arising from that sociability, including honesty in agreements and abstention from others' property, would retain a measure of force even under the explicitly impious hypothetical supposition that God did not exist or govern human affairs; Grotius himself did not embrace that supposition. He added that the law of nations rested on the shared practice of states, so that warfare itself had to be conducted according to law.

Analysis: The passage shows an attempt to find principles acceptable across confessional lines. It also reveals limits. Grotius wrote from a learned European Christian perspective, his discussion of what natural law permitted in war and slavery now appears morally troubling, and his claims were shaped by Dutch commercial and political interests. A single text cannot show how widely these principles were accepted by rulers who invoked them selectively.

Questions for students:

  1. What is the connection between human sociability and Grotius’s claim about binding obligations?
  2. How does the qualified hypothetical concerning God differ from a profession of atheism?
  3. Why do Grotius’s Dutch commercial and diplomatic circumstances matter when reading his account of international law?
  4. What further evidence would be required to show that rulers followed the principles he described?
Section 83.3

Thomas Hobbes, the State of Nature, and the Sovereign State

Thomas Hobbes (1588 to 1679) wrote his major political works during and after the English Civil Wars. He had lived through the collapse of royal authority, the fragmentation of religious opinion, and a war in which Englishmen killed one another over competing claims to rightful authority. His Elements of Law and De Cive preceded Leviathan, published in 1651 while he was in exile in France and after the execution of Charles I, and he returned to England shortly afterward.

Hobbes began with a thought experiment. Suppose there were no common power able to keep people in awe. In this state of nature, he argued, individuals are roughly equal in vulnerability, because even the weak can kill the strong, and they compete for gain, safety, and reputation. Each has a right of nature to preserve himself and a judge of what preservation requires, so conflict becomes a war of all against all. Hobbes's famous description of life in this condition as solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short is a statement of what would follow without security, in which industry, navigation, learning, and trade are impossible because the fruits of labor are uncertain.

The escape, in Hobbes's argument, is not a natural community but an artificial one. Reason produces laws of nature, such as seeking peace and keeping covenants, but without a power to enforce them these laws do not secure peace. Individuals therefore covenant with one another to authorize a sovereign, whether a man or an assembly, to act on behalf of all, and each agrees to regard the sovereign's acts as his own. The resulting commonwealth is a person whose will counts as the will of its members. Because the point is peace, the sovereign's power must not be divided, and Hobbes identified the doctrine that subjects may judge good and evil for themselves, or that authority is split between crown and church, as a cause of civil war.

Hobbes's argument developed in a century when English politics displayed the very fragmentation he feared. Conflicts over taxation, episcopal government, and the authority of Parliament were intensified by the wars in Scotland and Ireland and the mobilization of rival armies in England. The collapse of a recognized arbiter meant that competing groups each claimed to defend legitimate law. Royalists invoked kingship and allegiance, while parliamentarians appealed to statute, ancient liberties, and the political nation. Religious bodies disputed which church discipline was divinely required. Hobbes viewed these overlapping claims as a recipe for conflict because opposing authorities encouraged individuals to decide which commands to obey. His concern was not that people were uniquely cruel but that mutually suspicious people, lacking enforceable rules, could become dangerous even while seeking only to protect themselves.

The state of nature was an analytical device, not a claim that every human society had once passed through exactly the same prehistoric stage. Its point was to isolate the consequences of living without a mutually recognized judge. People could make promises, but a promise made under fear, with no reliable enforcement, could not guarantee that others would comply. Even peaceful individuals would have reason to anticipate attack. Hobbes's list of laws of nature includes seeking peace, keeping covenants, and showing fairness, which demonstrates that his argument does not celebrate violence or selfishness as ideals. Those rational precepts become politically effective only when backed by a public authority. His distinctive move was to connect the necessity of coercive power to the equal vulnerability of ordinary persons rather than to the sacred ancestry of rulers.

Authorization is more complicated than mere submission. Hobbes argued that covenanters authorize a representative person, an individual or an assembly, whose acts count as theirs. The sovereign is not normally a party to the founding covenant in the same way that each subject is. This explains why subjects cannot ordinarily prosecute the sovereign for breaking the original agreement. It also helps explain why a democracy or aristocratic assembly could, in Hobbes's formal classification, exercise the sovereign authority that he considered indispensable, even though he thought monarchy preferable in practice. Sovereignty includes deciding controversies, judging threats to peace, making law, and determining public teaching on matters that could destabilize the commonwealth. Hobbes feared independent churches and divided military command for precisely these reasons.

The argument nonetheless leaves difficult questions. If subjects retain a natural freedom to resist death, how can a state mobilize soldiers or punish criminals who resist? If sovereign authority creates the public standards of justice, can the people meaningfully condemn a sovereign's arbitrary decisions? Hobbes answers some of these problems by distinguishing liberty as the absence of external impediments from a moral entitlement to disobey every disliked order. In other places his account of self-preservation remains an irreducible limitation. His critics identified a danger that the cure for civil conflict could expose subjects to other forms of domination. Hobbes replied that even severe authority was usually less destructive than civil war. Students should test that comparison against his lived circumstances rather than accepting or dismissing it as a universal historical law.

Hobbes is sometimes dismissed as a defender of tyranny, but this misses the structure of his argument. Legitimacy rests on authorization and on the sovereign's ability to protect. Subjects retain a right to resist threats to their lives, and Hobbes held that they could not be obliged to kill themselves or to accuse themselves in capital cases. Obligation to a sovereign ends when the sovereign can no longer protect, which made even a conquering power legitimate if it succeeded in securing peace. Hobbes defined liberty as the absence of external impediments, so that subjects were free where the law was silent. Contemporaries nonetheless attacked him, royalists for resting authority on consent rather than on divine right, and clergy for his materialism and his subordination of church to state. His book therefore met objections from every side.

Section 83.4

Republicanism, Mixed Government, Resistance, and Constitutional Alternatives

Hobbes's choice between an undivided sovereign and the war of all against all was challenged by writers who argued that stable government could rest on divided powers, popular participation, or the rule of law. The classical republican tradition, drawn from Aristotle, Polybius, Cicero, and Machiavelli's Discourses, held that free government required citizens who participated in rule and a constitution that balanced monarchic, aristocratic, and popular elements. It also stressed civic virtue, the willingness of citizens to place the common good above private advantage.

In England, republican ideas became politically active during and after the Civil Wars. John Milton and Marchamont Nedham defended the Commonwealth. James Harrington's Oceana (1656) argued that political power followed property, and that a stable commonwealth needed a distribution of land that prevented any individual or group from dominating, together with rotation in office and a written constitution. Algernon Sidney's Discourses Concerning Government, written in the early 1680s and published posthumously in 1698, argued against Filmer that governments originated in the consent of the people and that resistance to tyranny was lawful. After 1660 these ideas circulated among opponents of Stuart policy, and after 1688 they fed a tradition sometimes called commonwealth or country thought, which stressed the dangers of royal patronage and standing armies.

The Dutch Republic offered a living example of a republic in a major commercial state. Johan and Pieter de la Court's writings in the 1660s argued that the interests of Holland were best served by a regent republic without a stadtholder and by free commerce. Spinoza shared this republican environment, and his later Political Treatise, left unfinished at his death in 1677, examined the conditions under which monarchies, aristocracies, and democracies could be made stable. Dutch debate, like English debate, was bound up with practical conflict between the House of Orange and the merchant oligarchies of the provinces.

The word republican did not describe one settled arrangement. Venetian government was an elaborate oligarchy with councils, a doge, and a ruling patriciate. The Dutch provinces were represented through provincial estates, and Holland's urban regents held great influence within the States General. Swiss cantons combined different local structures, from urban councils to rural assemblies. The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth called its political order a republic while retaining an elective king and reserving much of political power for the nobility. Constitutional alternatives thus differed in scale, social inclusion, and their relationship to hereditary privilege. What united many writers was suspicion of a ruler who could act without effective institutional restraint, not commitment to an electorate embracing all inhabitants.

The English experience supplied a particularly concentrated laboratory for constitutional thinking. Parliament's defenders argued that royal taxation required consent and that courts and ancient law set boundaries around prerogative. The execution of Charles I in 1649 raised the opposite question: if Parliament could condemn a king, what made Parliament's own authority legitimate? The Commonwealth, Cromwellian Protectorate, and Restoration exposed the fragility of written constitutional schemes and the difficulties of maintaining civilian rule over an army. Harrington's proposed rotation of offices and constitutional assemblies sought to prevent the concentration of influence through landownership. His connection between the distribution of property and the structure of government would later resonate with republican arguments about corruption and dependence, even where writers disagreed about whether property should be redistributed.

Algernon Sidney, writing against Filmer and dynastic absolutism, emphasized the political community's right to judge the government it had established. Yet his posthumous publication complicates narratives of immediate influence. A manuscript written in one political crisis was printed in another and subsequently read by communities facing different conflicts. Books could acquire authority through the circumstances of a writer's prosecution or martyrdom as well as through the reasoning of their pages. The printed Sidney, the memories of the regicides, and the language of ancient English liberty did not form a single coherent doctrine. They furnished arguments that Whig reformers, opposition pamphleteers, and colonial readers could combine with Locke or with classical history.

Republicanism also raised an enduring practical problem: should citizens be free because they participate in power or because institutions protect them from its abuse? A small independent property holder might defend active political participation, while a merchant might prefer reliable contracts, secure courts, and protection against arbitrary interference. Some writers considered a standing army or a dependence on government office a danger to civic independence. Others feared that a politically mobilized populace could threaten order and property. These tensions complicate any direct line from classical republican virtue to later electoral democracy. They also prepare the distinctive positions of Montesquieu, who emphasized constitutional moderation, and Rousseau, who made participation in sovereign lawgiving central to political freedom.

The practical effect of these traditions was to make the choice between absolutism and anarchy look false. Constitutional alternatives, mixed government, and resistance theory could claim ancient precedent and living examples. They also had limits. Republican writers normally restricted participation to property owners, assumed male citizens, and defended liberty for the political nation rather than for all inhabitants. Understanding the republican alternatives is necessary to understand Locke, who borrowed from them while rejecting their emphasis on virtue and participation, and Montesquieu and Rousseau, who each reworked them in different ways.

Section 83.5

John Locke, the Two Treatises, and the Rejection of Absolute Monarchy

John Locke (1632 to 1704) was born in Somerset into a modest Puritan gentry family and educated at Oxford. He trained in medicine and entered the household of Anthony Ashley Cooper, later the first Earl of Shaftesbury, as physician and adviser. Through Shaftesbury he met politics at close range, including the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina (1669) and the opposition to Charles II's policy. Between 1679 and 1681, the Exclusion Crisis pitted those who wished to bar the Catholic James, Duke of York, from the succession against those who defended hereditary right. The crisis ended with royalist victory, and after the Rye House Plot of 1683, Shaftesbury died in exile, Sidney was executed, and Locke fled to the Dutch Republic, returning in 1689 after the Glorious Revolution.

The Two Treatises of Government were published in 1689, with a title page dated 1690. The First Treatise is a long refutation of Sir Robert Filmer's Patriarcha, written probably in the 1620s or 1630s but first printed in 1680. Filmer argued that kings derived authority from Adam's fatherly dominion over his descendants, and that all government was therefore patriarchal and absolute. Locke attacked this on scriptural and logical grounds, denying that Adam had been given such power, that it could be traced to present rulers, or that paternal authority resembled political authority. The Second Treatise, much more famous today, offers a positive account of legitimate government.

The date of composition has been disputed. Peter Laslett argued in the 1960s that most of the work was written between 1679 and 1683, as a response to the Exclusion Crisis, rather than as a justification of the revolution of 1688. Later scholars, including Richard Ashcraft and Mark Goldie, have emphasized that Locke revised the work and that the radical context of Whig opposition shaped it, while others have argued for earlier or more complex stages of composition. The disagreement is partly one of evidence, because Locke's manuscripts are incomplete, and partly one of interpretation, since it concerns whether the book was a revolutionary manifesto or a more moderate defense of constitutional government. The Preface of 1689 itself presents the work as a justification of William III's title, a claim historians treat cautiously.

Locke's political arguments emerged from the tensions of an expanding but unstable Protestant monarchy. The Restoration of 1660 had returned the Stuarts after the interregnum, but it had not removed disputes over parliamentary taxation, the established church, the succession, or the civil position of dissenters. Shaftesbury's circle feared that the accession of the openly Catholic James would strengthen absolutist government and reverse Protestant security. Petitions, elections, pamphlets, and rumors created an intense political contest. Locke did not compose a timeless exercise while standing outside it. His arguments about legitimate power engaged questions that could determine whether his political associates were prosecuted or whether an established dynasty could be excluded. That setting makes his discussion of resistance more pointed than a retrospective reading solely as a defense of a successful revolution.

The two treatises also answer two different kinds of claims. Filmer did not simply say that kings were strong or descended from kings. His theory proposed a genealogy of paternal authority: Adam's rule over his family furnished a model for the authority of rulers over subjects. Locke challenged the evidence that Adam received absolute authority, the inference from fatherhood to government, and the possibility of tracing a unique political heir even if the premise were granted. He repeatedly used biblical reasoning because his intended audience took scriptural argument seriously. The first treatise is therefore not incidental debris to be discarded before the supposedly modern second. It exposes what Locke thought was wrong with the claim that some human beings were naturally born rulers and others naturally born subjects.

The publication history underscores the distinction between a work's composition and its public function. Anonymous publication in the wake of the Glorious Revolution did not eliminate the dangers of identification, but it allowed arguments forged under Charles II to enter a changed political field. Locke's preface aligned the argument with William III's accession, while the bulk of the reasoning reached beyond the immediate settlement. The 1689 Bill of Rights constrained the monarchy and affirmed parliamentary claims but was not an enactment of Locke's entire theory, and it did not establish universal representation. Historians should therefore resist treating political philosophy as a literal blueprint immediately implemented by Parliament. The work supplied a principled way to defend government limited by rights, while actual political arrangements emerged from bargaining among political and confessional elites.

The history of the Carolina constitutions adds a separate complication. Locke worked as a secretary and official within Shaftesbury's proprietary enterprises and had documentary involvement with the drafting and revision of the Fundamental Constitutions. Surviving versions and amendments show his hand in portions of the manuscript record, but historians disagree over how much of the plan or particular clauses he personally authored. The proprietors' charter and their interest in a hierarchical plantation colony must be distinguished from Locke's own published claims. David Armitage has argued for substantial colonial connections, while more recent manuscript criticism has challenged some specific attributions. The evidence warrants serious discussion of Locke's connection to colonial governance, not the unsupported assertion that every provision was his personal political design. This controversy should remain visible when students later examine his theory of property.

Locke's rejection of absolute monarchy was radical in principle even if his practical aims were more limited. He denied that any ruler could hold power merely by inheritance or conquest without regard to the interests and consent of the governed. He therefore needed an account of what legitimate authority was, and he built it from a state of nature, natural law, property, and consent, the subjects of the following sections. His method was to move from a hypothetical account of natural human relations to the conditions under which political society could be justified, which gave later readers both a theory and a vocabulary.

Section 83.6

Locke's State of Nature, Natural Rights, Labor, and Property

Locke's state of nature differs sharply from Hobbes's. It is not a war but a condition of freedom and equality governed by the law of nature, which Locke identified with reason and with God's will that humankind be preserved. No one may harm another in life, health, liberty, or possessions, and in the state of nature each person has the right to enforce this law against offenders. This executive right, which Locke called a strange doctrine and defended at length, is precisely what makes the state of nature unstable: people judge their own cases, are partial to themselves, and lack a settled law, known judge, and power to enforce judgments. Civil government is the remedy for these inconveniences.

Property occupies a central place in Locke's theory. Locke used the word in a wide sense, covering life, liberty, and estate, but his argument is most developed for material possessions. God gave the earth to humankind in common, but each person owns his own person and labor, and by mixing his labor with something he makes it his own. This appropriation is limited by two conditions: a person may not take more than he can use before it spoils, and he must leave enough and as good for others. Money, according to Locke, changed the situation, because people could accumulate durable wealth without spoilage, and by tacit agreement came to accept unequal holdings.

The argument has been read in different ways. C. B. Macpherson's influential 1962 interpretation described it as justifying a class of capitalist appropriators, a reading many scholars have since challenged. James Tully and Jeremy Waldron, among others, stressed the theological and egalitarian premises of the argument and its limits on accumulation. A further line of criticism, advanced by Barbara Arneil and others, examines Locke's relation to colonization. Locke saw America as the place where one could observe the early stage of property, and he argued that land left uncultivated was effectively waste, a premise that could support claims against Indigenous inhabitants. Locke served as secretary within the Carolina proprietors' administration and participated in revisions of the Fundamental Constitutions, whose slaveholding provisions expressed a proprietary political project. The extent of his personal authorship of particular clauses remains disputed. He also held investments in the Royal African Company. Historians disagree about how far these facts shaped his theory, but they cannot be omitted from any account of its historical meaning.

The second treatise's account of nature begins with a theological premise frequently omitted from modern summaries. People are God's workmanship, so one individual cannot treat another as disposable property simply by virtue of superior strength. Equality here is a moral condition: no person comes into existence already subjected to another's political jurisdiction. Locke nevertheless does not say that all people possess equal wealth, capacity, or social authority. His state of nature permits peaceful families, exchanges, and the observance of natural law. It is the absence of a shared, publicly authorized judge, not an automatic appetite for warfare, that makes disputes dangerous. When each person can punish violations of natural law, a wronged individual may overreact and the offender may deny the charge. A common political authority promises relief from this insecurity.

Appropriation from the common earth poses a famous difficulty. If the world is given to humanity collectively, how can one person's labor create an exclusive claim without asking everyone else for permission? Locke argues that productive labor annexes something belonging to the worker, but this reasoning has limits built into its presentation. A person cannot reasonably claim a harvest that will rot unused, and appropriation must leave others adequate opportunity. These conditions are not peripheral formalities: they mark the distinction between improvement understood as beneficial use and appropriation that harms others. Writers have argued for centuries about whether money, increasing population, and commercial markets make the provisos ineffective, modify them, or require new forms of mutual obligation. None of these competing readings should be presented as uncontroversial fact.

Locke's language of improvement has a powerful colonial dimension. European writers often evaluated land use by forms of fencing, permanent cultivation, and commercial yield familiar in parts of Europe. Indigenous practices of seasonal movement, hunting, managed burning, communal tenure, irrigation, or mixed cultivation could be treated as insufficient use, even when they supported complex legal and ecological systems. That mismatch enabled colonizers to describe inhabited territory as underused. Locke's writings about American land and the comparative value of labor are consequently debated not only as abstract economics but as arguments available to English colonial enterprises. The presence of an argument in a treatise is not by itself evidence that a particular dispossession occurred because a colonial official read it; legal actions, treaties, settlement practices, and power must be examined alongside the text.

The slavery question is related but requires separate analysis. Locke's political writing vigorously condemns absolute arbitrary power over free persons, yet he allows a severely restricted form of enslavement following an unjust aggression and develops his theory in a world deeply shaped by Atlantic slavery. His historical connection with the Royal African Company and Carolina cannot be erased by citing the treatise's universal language, nor can a debated clause in a proprietary constitution alone establish everything he believed. Students should distinguish the moral logic of Locke's idealized account, his institutional roles and economic connections, the actual laws of colonial slave societies, and the uses that subsequent colonizers made of his vocabulary. Keeping those evidentiary layers separate makes the contradiction more, not less, serious.

Property is also the link between the state of nature and civil society. People leave the state of nature in order to preserve their lives, liberties, and estates more securely, and the protection of property is the main end of government. This does not mean that government exists only to protect the wealthy, since Locke defined property to include life and liberty, but it does mean that his theory placed the rights of owners at its center. Later radicals and conservatives both found support in that fact, the former by emphasizing the universality of the premises, the latter by emphasizing the priority of property.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Consent, Social Contract, and the State of Nature

The state of nature is a hypothetical condition without government used by theorists to ask what government is for. Consent is the voluntary agreement of those governed, which Locke treated as the basis of legitimate authority. A social contract is an agreement, real or hypothetical, by which people form a political community or authorize a government.

Theorists differed on whether the contract was an actual historical event, a useful fiction, or a test of legitimacy. They also differed on whether it created a sovereign who could not be resisted, a trust which could be revoked, or a collective will expressed in law. Equating all contract theory with democracy is therefore misleading.

Section 83.7

Consent, Legislative Authority, Representation, and the Right of Resistance

For Locke, political society begins when individuals agree to give up their executive power of enforcing the law of nature and to be governed by a common authority. The agreement is by consent, and since unanimity cannot be expected in a large body, the majority binds the rest, once the community is formed. Consent may be express, as when a person formally joins a commonwealth, or tacit, as when one enjoys the benefits of its laws by owning property or traveling on its roads. This doctrine of tacit consent was already controversial in Locke's time, because it appeared to make nearly everyone who lived in a country a consenting member, and Hume would later attack it.

Government, in Locke's account, is a trust. The community creates a legislative power, which he called supreme in the commonwealth, since it makes the laws that all must obey, but not absolutely arbitrary. The legislature cannot take property without consent, cannot rule by extemporary decrees, and cannot transfer its power to others, because it holds its authority in trust for the people's good. Executive power, which carries out the laws, and federative power, which conducts relations with other states, are distinguished from it, though Locke did not propose a formal separation of institutions as Montesquieu later did. His interest was in subordination, with the legislature supreme and the executive answerable.

When the trust is betrayed, the people retain a right to resist. Locke defined the dissolution of government, which occurs when the legislature is altered without the people's consent or when rulers act against the purposes for which they were trusted, and he argued that in such cases the people may remove and replace their governors. He insisted that this right was not a license for frequent rebellion, since people are slow to change governments, but he also declared that the appeal to heaven, the recourse to force, was the people's remedy against a ruler who places himself in a state of war with them. The argument was subversive in 1680 and adaptable in 1689, and it supplied later revolutionaries with a ready vocabulary.

For Locke, entry into political society involves a transformation of jurisdiction. Individuals who could previously enforce natural law for themselves agree to constitute a common body and allow it to act according to majority decisions. The majority principle solves a practical difficulty: unanimity at every subsequent decision would make a political society incapable of acting. Yet what constitutes the initial act of consent is less straightforward. Locke distinguishes explicit acceptance of membership from the tacit consent that may accompany enjoyment of territory, inheritance, or the protection of established law. Critics have long asked whether simply living in a place, especially when departure is difficult, can establish a morally binding choice. The force of the objection depends on whether consent is understood as a one-time historical founding event, an ongoing condition, or a justification for the type of authority institutions may exercise.

Law for Locke is neither an arbitrary command nor the disappearance of freedom. A stable, known, prospective legal rule can enable people to act without fearing an official's unpredictable will. The legislature holds supreme power in the ordinary working of government because it makes general rules for preservation of the community. Nevertheless it is limited by the ends for which society formed; it must not govern through arbitrary decrees, confiscate property without consent, or transfer its lawmaking authority to another body without proper authorization. The executive must apply law and preserve the state when emergencies arise. Locke also recognized a federative function concerning war, peace, and foreign relations, which did not correspond neatly to the later three-branch constitutional formula. The arrangement is a theory of public trust more than an institutional diagram dictated for every country.

Prerogative and resistance expose the difficulty of reconciling flexibility with constraint. Locke understood that a ruler may confront unexpected emergencies in which existing statutes are inadequate. He therefore recognized executive discretion for the public good, sometimes even beyond the ordinary letter of the law. This was not, in his intention, permission to treat the public good as whatever a prince found convenient. Persistent misuse of discretion could destroy the trust on which government rested. Yet deciding when a government had crossed that threshold posed a political problem: courts could be compromised, assemblies dissolved, and officials might deny that they had broken faith. Locke's ultimate appeal to the people implies that resistance can occur when ordinary remedies fail, but he also feared that frequent rebellion would undermine peace.

The right of resistance should therefore be distinguished from ordinary dissatisfaction with legislation. Locke's argument concerns a pattern of breaches that reveal a design against the rights and purposes of the community, such as arbitrary expropriation, attempts to replace law with force, or interference with representative institutions. A ruler who has forfeited political trust loses the right to govern, but the political community does not thereby disappear. This distinction between dissolution of government and dissolution of society permits people to establish a replacement constitutional order. It also helps explain how later authors could cite Locke both to defend an existing constitutional settlement and to justify its overthrow. Whether those readers faithfully extended his doctrine is a separate historical question.

Locke's theory should not be equated with democracy. Consent did not require universal suffrage, and Locke's discussion of representation assumed the English constitution of Parliament, in which participation depended on property and status. The people whose consent mattered were those who, in practice, were treated as members of the political community. Women, servants, the poor, and the enslaved were assumed to be covered by the consent of heads of households or were excluded altogether. The theory was open to wider readings, but it did not itself require them.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Can Consent Legitimize Government When Most Subjects Never Expressly Consented?

The question: If legitimate authority rests on consent, but almost no one has ever signed a contract or voted for the government that rules them, can the theory justify any actual government?

Interpretation A: Defenders of Locke argue that tacit consent is a reasonable account of how people participate in society. Interpretation B: Critics, beginning with Hume in 1748 and continuing in modern scholarship, argue that tacit consent is too thin to be consent at all, because most people had no realistic opportunity to leave and were never asked. They conclude that the real basis of obedience is custom, interest, or utility, and that the theory legitimizes whatever governments exist.

Evidence: The evidence includes the text of the Second Treatise, especially its discussion of tacit consent, Hume's essay, and historical records of how people actually acquired subjects' status through birth, residence, and oath-taking, including loyalty oaths demanded in the 1690s.

Why it matters: The answer determines whether contract theory is a standard for criticizing governments or a rationalization of existing ones, a distinction that mattered greatly when revolutionaries later invoked consent.

Section 83.8

Locke on Toleration, Conscience, Political Exclusion, and the Limits of Liberalism

Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration was published in Latin in Holland in 1689, as the Epistola de Tolerantia, and appeared almost at once in an English translation by William Popple. It argued that the state's authority concerns civil interests, such as life, liberty, and property, and does not extend to the care of souls, because true religion consists in inward conviction, which force cannot produce. Churches, in his account, are voluntary societies that may set their own rules and expel members, but may not use coercion against outsiders. Magistrates should therefore tolerate a wide range of religious opinions and practices.

The Letter belongs to a long European debate about toleration, which had involved Protestants persecuted by Catholic powers, Catholics persecuted by Protestants, dissenters in England, and Huguenots after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, a context in which Locke wrote. Pierre Bayle and others in the Dutch Republic had made similar arguments for liberty of conscience. Locke differed from some in basing toleration on the nature of church and state and on the political utility of peace, rather than on skepticism about religious truth.

The limits of Locke's toleration are important. He did not extend it to those who denied the existence of God, since oaths and promises, the bonds of society, could not hold them, nor to churches whose members owed allegiance to a foreign prince, which in the English context meant Roman Catholics. These exclusions reflected fears of political subversion in an England that had just removed a Catholic king, and they were common among Protestants of the period. The 1689 Toleration Act in England likewise excluded Catholics and Unitarians and left Anglican privileges intact.

Religious toleration was a practical problem in a state where civil rights depended partly on conformity to an established church. English dissenters faced restrictions on office, worship, education, and public standing; Catholic allegiance raised fears in a Protestant political nation; Jewish communities occupied an unequal and contested legal position. Locke had observed Catholic and Protestant conflicts in England and on the continent, and his Dutch exile exposed him to a comparatively pluralistic society that was not free of religious repression. His toleration argument should thus be read alongside questions about magistrates' competence and the social consequences of coerced belief. If an official threatens punishment for the wrong creed, Locke argued, the threat can induce compliance but cannot make inward conviction genuine. A church constituted as a voluntary society must rely on persuasion rather than on the state's sword.

The reasoning separates the purposes of political and ecclesiastical institutions, but not in a modern sense that removes religion altogether from public life. Locke remained a religious thinker and maintained that reason and revelation were compatible in important respects. The magistrate had to protect civil interests, not adjudicate salvation. Religious congregations could regulate their own membership, and individuals could choose or leave a community of worship. The challenge was to define which practices threatened civil order rather than merely offending the ruler's theological commitments. That question created a gap between the argument for a broad freedom of conscience and the exemptions or exclusions Locke was willing to defend in a society concerned with loyalty, promises, and public security.

Locke's treatment of atheists and groups thought to owe allegiance to a foreign jurisdiction makes that gap visible. He worried that those who denied divine sanctions might not regard oaths and promises as reliably binding, and he associated some Catholic claims with a competing political obedience. Historians disagree over how absolute these exclusions were in his broader corpus and how much they depended on specific English controversies. They nevertheless undermine any claim that his toleration argument already established a universally equal civil status. A useful classroom comparison is between Locke and Pierre Bayle, who advanced wider toleration arguments in some respects and disputed the connection between religious belief and morality. Neither author's position can be understood without attention to persecution and the religious-political context in which he wrote.

The limits of toleration were also limits of membership. A person could be tolerated in worship but excluded from public office; a woman could possess a conscience without independent political capacity; a wealthy Dissenter might have resources to maintain a congregation that a poor laborer lacked. The Anglo-Irish and Atlantic contexts make distinctions still sharper, because legal establishments and colonial governments did not all apply religious liberty in the same manner. The historical significance of Locke's argument lies partly in its refusal to identify civil peace with confessional uniformity. Its incompleteness lies in its reliance on criteria of trust and citizenship that could themselves reproduce unequal power. Students should ask whose conscience was protected in law, whose was defended in theory, and whose remained exposed to coercion.

The Letter also shows how Locke's political theory contained tensions that later generations would exploit. Natural equality sat beside property-based political participation, toleration beside the confessional exclusion of Catholics and atheists, and universal premises beside colonial and commercial practice. Historians use the term limits of liberalism, though anachronistically since the word liberalism did not yet exist, to describe how early modern arguments for rights coexisted with exclusion. The tensions were not merely hypocrisy, since they reflected genuine assumptions about who could be a responsible participant in political society, but they became apparent when later writers drew out the implications of Locke's premises.

Montesquieu, Historical Comparison, and the Study of Political Institutions

Charles-Louis de Secondat was born in 1689 near Bordeaux into a family of the robe nobility, magistrates whose offices gave them social standing. He inherited the title and the position of president in the Parlement of Bordeaux, a sovereign law court, in 1716, and sold the office in 1726. Parlements registered royal edicts and could remonstrate against them, so he knew from the inside the role of intermediary institutions in a monarchy. His estate also made him a wine producer, and he took part in the Academy of Bordeaux and the wider Republic of Letters.

His first major work, the Persian Letters (1721), appeared anonymously with the fictitious imprint of Cologne and Pierre Marteau; bibliographical research identifies the Amsterdam publishing house of Jacques Desbordes, then managed by Susanne de Caux. Written as the correspondence of two Persian visitors in France, it used the device of foreign observers to satirize French court life, the papacy, the Parisian world, and the arbitrary power of rulers, while also exploring despotism in the seraglio of one of the Persians. The device allowed a critique that could not safely be delivered directly, though it was not a serious study of Persia. His Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and Their Decline (1734) analyzed how institutions, military practice, and moral habits had shaped Rome's rise and fall, and began his effort to explain political change by general causes rather than the decisions of individuals.

The Spirit of the Laws (1748), the product of about two decades of reading and travel, including an extended stay in England from 1729 to 1731, was the larger attempt. Montesquieu proposed to study laws not as arbitrary commands but as relationships among a society's form of government, physical environment, religion, customs, commerce, and history. This was a comparative and historical method that drew on Greek and Roman sources, travel accounts, legal compilations, and reports of non-European societies. The method was ambitious but also uneven, since many of his sources were secondhand and some were unreliable.

Montesquieu's career shaped both his questions and his evidence. Born into a noble family near Bordeaux, he inherited a position connected to the Parlement of Bordeaux, one of the high courts that registered royal legislation and could protest royal measures. This did not make him an advocate of popular democracy. It placed him within a world of provincial institutions that defended their customary rights against centralizing claims. He also belonged to learned academies and traveled through much of Europe, including a prolonged visit to England. His notebooks and literary productions reveal a writer interested in practices as varied as commercial habits, legal procedure, the organization of households, and religious ceremonies. For him the question was not simply what constitution reason could invent, but why institutions suitable to one setting might fail in another.

The Persian Letters offered an early experiment in comparative observation through fiction. Two imagined Persian travelers, Usbek and Rica, comment on French institutions, religious practice, fashion, and monarchy; their apparent foreignness makes familiar European customs seem strange. Yet the novel also turns back on its narrators. The events in Usbek's seraglio reveal coercion inside the household of a man who criticizes political domination abroad. That double perspective means the book is not a straightforward statement that European rationality judges other societies. It invites readers to examine the arbitrariness of custom and the dependence of authority on perspective. The 1721 first edition circulated anonymously under a fictitious Cologne imprint associated with Pierre Marteau, while specialist bibliographical research identifies the Amsterdam house of Jacques Desbordes, then directed by Susanne de Caux. Such publication strategies belong to Chapter 82's history of print as well as to the history of political ideas.

The Considerations on the Romans and The Spirit of the Laws added historical inquiry to satire. Montesquieu sought the causes that made Rome powerful and later vulnerable, and asked how political forms related to the societies sustaining them. The distinction between a rule's written form and its operation was central. One might find a law that appeared harsh in isolation but served a moderating function within a particular set of customs, or a seemingly mild institution that became an instrument of arbitrary rule when effective restraints disappeared. Such reasoning demanded attention to comparison over time as well as across geographical settings. It did not eliminate bias. His judgments about Islam, Asian governments, and climate often drew on European travel accounts, conventional representations, and information of uneven reliability.

An explanatory history of Montesquieu should therefore distinguish three kinds of evidence: facts he could observe directly, reports he compiled from other writers, and interpretive models he constructed to make large comparisons possible. He was not conducting standardized social science in the modern statistical sense. His classifications clarified patterns but could oversimplify the cases examined. Modern scholars read him both as a pioneer of historical comparison and as an author whose analytical language perpetuated geographical stereotypes. Neither observation cancels the other. His importance for political thought lies in shifting attention from the moral authority of a ruler alone to the conditions under which institutional arrangements could sustain or undermine liberty.

The result is not a program but an analysis. Montesquieu aimed to understand why different societies had the laws they did and to show what conditions made liberty possible. He was not a democrat, and he was not a revolutionary, but he was an enemy of arbitrary power. His book was a landmark in the study of political institutions and a major influence on later legal, historical, and sociological writing, and it was read across Europe within a few years of publication. He has to be read as a magistrate of the old regime attempting to reform it by understanding it.

Section 83.10

Republics, Monarchies, Despotism, and the Spirit of Laws

Montesquieu divided governments into three kinds: republics, monarchies, and despotisms. A republic could be a democracy or an aristocracy, depending on whether the people as a whole or a part held sovereign power. A monarchy was a government of one person by fixed and established laws, and a despotism was the government of one person by will and caprice, without law. Each, he argued, had a nature, which is its structure, and a principle, which is the human passion that makes it work. The principle of a republic is virtue, a love of the laws and the country that places the common good above private interest. The principle of monarchy is honor, the sense of distinction attached to rank and office. The principle of despotism is fear.

This scheme was not meant to describe a progression or to recommend a single model. It was a tool for analysis, and Montesquieu's point was that laws suited to one form of government might destroy another. A monarchy that turned its nobles into courtiers without independent standing, or abolished the intermediate bodies that stood between crown and people, risked drifting into despotism. A republic depended on a degree of equality and small scale that was difficult to maintain. His account was therefore both descriptive and critical, and he used it implicitly to warn about the direction of French monarchy.

Montesquieu also argued that laws interacted with customs, religion, commerce, and physical circumstances. In Books XIV to XVIII he considered the role of climate and soil. Hot climates, he suggested, made people languid and encouraged despotism, while cold climates fostered vigor and liberty, and the fertility of a land affected whether it favored monarchy or republic. These passages are the most criticized part of his work. They rest on weak evidence and on stereotypes about Asia and Africa and the supposed contrast between the free North and the servile South. They also contradict his own insistence on the importance of laws and institutions, since he elsewhere argued that wise legislation could counteract the effects of climate.

Montesquieu's three principal governmental types are idealized categories rather than a list of the only constitutions that actually existed. In a republic, the sovereign authority belongs to the whole people or a portion of it; he accordingly distinguished democracy from aristocracy. A monarchy is rule by one person through established and relatively stable laws, supported by intermediate bodies. Despotism, by contrast, is rule by one person according to immediate will rather than law. The distinction between monarchy and despotism matters most: mere concentration of royal power did not automatically make every monarchy despotic, but erosion of legal and social constraints could move government in that direction. His terminology is evaluative, yet it also attempts to describe how different kinds of rule function.

Each governmental type was associated with a motivating principle. Democratic republics required political virtue, meaning a disposition to place the common good ahead of narrow advantage; aristocratic republics depended on moderation among those holding office. Monarchy was sustained by honor, the socially cultivated desire for distinction within a structured hierarchy. Despotism relied on fear. Montesquieu did not claim that every inhabitant of a monarchy acted honorably or that all republicans were virtuous. The principles describe what made a system work according to his model, and why its corruption might bring collapse. For example, a republic whose citizens ceased to value common institutions could preserve republican offices on paper while allowing a few people to monopolize their operation.

The relationship of political law to physical and social circumstances has been controversial. Montesquieu discussed climate, geography, size of territory, economic life, religion, population, commerce, and inherited manners as factors that shaped laws. He sometimes offered environmental explanations that later scholars have rejected as overgeneralized or prejudiced. Yet to dismiss the entire method as climatic determinism would ignore his insistence that no factor operated alone. A large empire might require administrative arrangements different from those appropriate to a city republic. A maritime commercial state could sustain habits and interests unlike those of an agrarian inland territory. Laws should therefore be assessed in relation to a broader social environment, not merely by whether an abstract formula appeared rational.

The comparative framework also illustrates the limits of European observers' sources. Descriptions of Ottoman, Persian, Chinese, and other non-European governments circulated through merchants, diplomats, missionaries, translated texts, and travelers. Some offered detailed information; others recycled stereotypes of an all-powerful oriental ruler. Montesquieu's use of eastern despotism became influential in European political vocabulary, but it could obscure real legal institutions, negotiated authority, provincial autonomy, and complex elites in those societies. The Ottoman Empire, for example, had courts, administrative rules, religious scholars, and powerful local interests; describing it solely as a despotism would not constitute adequate institutional history. Students should compare Montesquieu's analytical aims with the quality of the evidence he had and the political purposes served by a contrast between European moderation and imagined eastern arbitrariness.

Students should treat the book neither as a scientific system nor as a set of quaint errors. It was an attempt to make politics intelligible by locating institutions within social conditions, and it prompted later inquiries in history, geography, and sociology. Its weaknesses show how eighteenth century European writers often treated their own assumptions as universal when describing non-European societies, a theme that returns in the discussion of exclusion in Section 83.18 and in the examination of empire in Chapters 72 and 73.

Section 83.11

Political Liberty, the Separation of Powers, and the English Constitution

Book XI of The Spirit of the Laws, and especially its sixth chapter, is the most influential passage in Montesquieu's work. He began by defining political liberty not as doing whatever one wishes but as the right to do what the laws permit, together with a sense of security that arises from the belief that one is safe from abuse. Liberty, he argued, is found only where power is not abused, and the experience of ages shows that anyone who has power is apt to abuse it. The remedy is arrangement: it is necessary, in his words in the common English rendering, that power should be a check to power.

Montesquieu distinguished three kinds of power in every state: the legislative, the executive concerned with matters of the law of nations, and the executive concerned with matters of civil law, which he called the power of judging. Liberty is lost if the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person or body, because the same authority could make tyrannical laws and execute them tyrannically, and if the power of judging is not separate from the other two, because judges would then have the power of an oppressor. He added that judges should be drawn from the people and should apply the law strictly, and that the judicial power was in some sense invisible and null, because it was exercised by persons temporarily chosen rather than by a permanent body.

He presented these arrangements as the constitution of England. In his account, the legislature was divided between the House of Commons, representing the people, and the House of Lords, representing the hereditary nobility, with the king holding the executive and a veto over legislation. The Commons held the right to grant taxes, which allowed them to check the crown, and the nobility had a separate chamber to protect their interests. The system, he argued, secured liberty through a balance in which each part could restrain the others, and through the participation of different social orders.

A careful reading of Book XI begins with Montesquieu's definition of political liberty. Liberty is not the absence of all restraints or permission to do whatever one wishes. A person is politically free when laws and arrangements provide security against arbitrary interference. Fear of unpredictable arrest, punishment, or confiscation damages liberty even where some familiar freedoms remain. The possibility of judicial abuse is therefore as important as the formal relationship between a king and a legislature. Public offices must be established and limited so that no actor can treat a private command as law. This conception differs from Locke's emphasis on the justification of governmental authority by natural rights, though the two theories can be combined by later thinkers.

The celebrated separation-of-powers argument is often represented by a schoolbook diagram of three equal and isolated branches. Montesquieu's actual discussion is more intricate. Legislative power makes law; executive power concerns affairs of state, including war and diplomacy; the power of judging resolves legal disputes and punishes crimes. Concentrating them creates the opportunity for coercion without independent review. But effective liberty does not require that institutions never interact. In his account of England, the legislative authority itself contains distinct social components, and the monarch possesses certain powers to check legislative action. A constitution can prevent domination through reciprocal restraints and the different interests of institutions, not simply by drawing three boxes with no arrows between them.

His representation of the English constitution was selective. Eighteenth-century Britain included a hereditary House of Lords, a crown with ministers, an elected Commons chosen through unequal local franchises, and a political world marked by patronage. The growing connections between executive ministers and parliamentary majorities make it difficult to identify the system with strict separation. Montesquieu's English example was partly an ideal constructed from observation and political argument. His readers should distinguish that constructed model from the actual operation of Commons elections, aristocratic influence, jury practice, royal appointment, and the overlapping responsibilities of judges and officeholders. This distinction helps explain why different constitutional reformers later found support for different institutional designs in the same text.

The judiciary raises a further nuance. Montesquieu regarded the power of judging as dangerous if controlled by the same authority that made or executed the law, yet he also described judging in ways tied to temporary tribunals and particular legal procedures rather than a modern permanent supreme court with constitutional review. His discussion of criminal law, accusations, and punishments belongs to a wider concern that subjects should know the rules under which they live. Students should consequently read the separation-of-powers principle as one element of a broader project of political moderation. It is not a mechanical guarantee that any constitution bearing three branch names will protect individuals from arbitrary arrest, discrimination, secret administration, or violence.

It is a mistake to read this as a description of eighteenth century Britain or as a prototype of the modern separation of powers. The actual British system included ministers who sat in Parliament, a crown that managed members through patronage, and a House of Commons elected by a small and unequal electorate. Judges were not wholly independent until the Act of Settlement of 1701 began to secure their tenure. Montesquieu's analysis was a theoretical interpretation of English practice as he understood it, combined with his reading of Locke and of English commentators. The American founders later drew on it heavily, particularly in The Federalist, but they adapted it to a presidential and federal system that he did not describe. His argument is better understood as one about the distribution and mutual checking of powers than as a rule of three fully separate branches.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Constitutionalism, Mixed Government, and Separation of Powers

Constitutionalism is the principle that government is limited by fundamental laws, written or customary. Mixed government is a constitution combining monarchic, aristocratic, and popular elements, so that no one controls the state. Separation of powers is the distribution of legislative, executive, and judicial functions among different persons or bodies.

Mixed government is an older idea, associated with Polybius, and balances social orders. Separation of powers, as Montesquieu used it, distributes functions and also depends on social balance. Modern constitutions with three branches are later developments, and mixing the terms obscures this history.

Section 83.12

Intermediate Powers, Constitutional Moderation, Commerce, and Political Stability

Montesquieu's account of monarchy rested on what he called intermediate powers, the subordinate bodies that stand between the monarch and the people. The nobility, the clergy, the cities, and the Parlements each possessed privileges and a degree of independence that limited the crown's discretion. His formula, no monarch, no nobility, no nobility, no monarch, summarizes the claim that monarchy without such bodies would turn into despotism. This was a theory of moderate government, in which liberty depended on the dispersal of authority and the existence of laws that even rulers had to observe.

The position was politically conservative in one sense and critical in another. It defended aristocratic privilege, including the venality of judicial offices that Montesquieu's own family had held. It also implied that Louis XIV's policy of reducing the nobility to courtiers and ministers to executives of royal will weakened the constitution of France. His defenders presented him as a champion of liberty, and his critics, including later democrats, saw in him an apologist for a hierarchy of orders. Both readings capture part of his purpose.

Montesquieu also considered commerce a force for moderation. In Book XX he argued that trade softened manners, encouraged honesty and respect for contracts, and promoted peace among nations, an idea later called doux commerce. Commerce could be a check on arbitrary rulers, because capital was mobile and merchants could withdraw it. At the same time, he warned that the spirit of commerce could erode the virtue republics required, and he accepted that it brought inequalities. His account of commerce connected political theory to the economic changes treated in Chapter 85.

His treatment of slavery shows the strength and limits of his method. In Book XV he condemned slavery as contrary to natural law, and the famous chapter on the enslavement of Africans is written in ironic form that mocks the arguments used to justify it. Elsewhere, however, he discussed climate-based arguments for slavery in certain regions in ways that scholars interpret differently. Some read the work as a pioneering antislavery text, and others note its inconsistency. The disagreement illustrates how a single eighteenth century book could be used by opposed audiences, and why historians must read passages in context rather than quote them as the author's final position.

Intermediate powers were not an afterthought in Montesquieu's account. The nobility, local bodies, ecclesiastical institutions, and sovereign courts could possess rights and privileges that prevented a monarch's will from reaching every individual without mediation. In theory, their separate existence created points at which orders could be interpreted, resisted, delayed, or legally challenged. Montesquieu associated such institutions with lawful monarchy rather than with despotism. Yet the protections they afforded were uneven. Nobles might restrain royal taxation on their own estates while preserving dues and jurisdictions imposed on peasants. Courts might defend the formal rule of law but exclude people of low rank from meaningful remedies. Institutional limits on a ruler's power are not automatically the same as equality before the law.

Montesquieu also cared about how authority operated through the scale of political communities. A small republic could depend on direct relationships, common civic expectations, and participation by a relatively restricted citizen body. A larger monarchy required chains of administration and intermediary jurisdictions. An empire might employ structures that connected distant provinces to a center but risked losing the protections associated with established local law. These associations were neither universal predictions nor neutral descriptions, but they directed attention to practical questions often lost in abstract contract theory: how far is an official from the people affected, who can challenge an order, and what social interests support the survival of legal restraints?

Commerce provided Montesquieu with another test of political institutions. Exchange could connect different populations, reward predictable legal rules, and encourage habits of calculation and negotiation. In some passages he suggested that commercial relations could soften manners and diminish certain forms of violence. Elsewhere his analysis recognized the commercial exploitation and harsh inequalities that accompanied wealth. It would be misleading to present him as claiming that trade necessarily produced peace or liberty. Seaborne commerce could coexist with slavery, monopolies, conquest, and war; Europe's commercial empires provided evident examples. His larger claim was that economic practices helped shape the social conditions within which laws and governments operated. Chapter 85 will examine the material history of those practices in fuller detail.

The distinction between political moderation and popular rule should remain clear. A mixed constitution can restrain a crown by recognizing hereditary elites, as Montesquieu proposed, while preventing the vast majority of subjects from exercising political power. A state can protect some legal liberties and preserve rigid hierarchies. The decisive comparison with Rousseau is not simply that one author favored liberty and the other favored equality; both cared about arbitrary domination, but Montesquieu tended to seek its prevention through institutions and balancing social powers, while Rousseau worried that an elite structure might itself compromise the freedom of the collective sovereign. These positions could be combined in later constitutions, but they embody different diagnoses of political danger.

Compared with Locke, Montesquieu began not from natural rights and consent but from the observation of societies and the conditions of liberty. He cared less about the origin of government than about its working. For Locke, legitimacy depended on the people's consent and the protection of rights. For Montesquieu, liberty depended on laws and institutions that prevented the concentration of power. The two approaches were compatible, and later writers combined them, but they answered different questions. Neither required political or social equality.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Does Separating Powers Adequately Protect Liberty?

The question: Can dividing governmental functions among different bodies, as Montesquieu proposed, protect individual liberty by itself, or does it depend on other social and political conditions?

Interpretation A: Some interpreters argue that separation and balance of powers are the essential safeguard against arbitrary rule, because they prevent any one group from acting without the consent or scrutiny of others, and that the success of constitutional systems that adopted the principle supports the argument.
Interpretation B: Others argue that Montesquieu himself made liberty depend on social orders, customs, and laws, not on institutional design alone, and that formal division can fail where the same social elite controls every branch, or where the executive dominates by patronage as in eighteenth century Britain.

Evidence: The evidence includes the text of Book XI, accounts of the working of the British constitution in the eighteenth century, the practice of French Parlements, and later constitutional experience.

Why it matters: The debate affects whether institutional design or social and cultural conditions are treated as the primary guarantee of liberty, and it frames the reception of Montesquieu in later revolutionary and constitutional settings.

Student task: Evaluate one eighteenth century example in which institutional separation did or did not restrain power, using evidence from this chapter.

Section 83.13

Rousseau, Civilization, Property, and the Origins of Inequality

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712 to 1778) was born in Geneva, the son of a watchmaker, and left the city as a teenager. He lived for years in Savoy, Lyon, and Venice before settling in Paris, where he befriended Denis Diderot and contributed articles on music to the Encyclopedie. In 1750 his Discourse on the Sciences and Arts won a prize from the Academy of Dijon by answering in the negative the question whether the revival of the arts and sciences had improved morals. It argued that luxury, refinement, and learning had corrupted rather than improved human character.

The Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality Among Men (1755), written for the same academy though it did not win, developed the argument far more fully. Rousseau distinguished natural inequality, differences of age, health, and strength, from moral or political inequality, which depends on conventions. He argued that humans in a hypothetical state of nature were solitary, self-sufficient, and moved by self-preservation and pity, rather than by competition. He called this self-regarding impulse amour de soi, distinguishing it from amour-propre, the desire for esteem that develops in society and makes people dependent on others' opinions.

Inequality, he claimed, grew with the development of agriculture, metallurgy, and property. The person who first enclosed a piece of land and declared it his own, and found others simple enough to believe him, was the true founder of civil society. Property created dependence and conflict, and the rich, anxious to protect their gains, persuaded the poor to accept a political order that entrenched inequality in the guise of peace and law. In his account, political institutions were therefore initially created to consolidate unequal property, and they moved toward despotism, which was a new form of equality in which all were slaves to the master.

Rousseau's critique began with a challenge to the assumption that advances in learning and refinement necessarily improved human character. His response to the Academy of Dijon's 1750 question about the arts and sciences argued that cultivated brilliance could coexist with vanity, inequality, and dependence. He did not merely demand the destruction of learning, and the argument should not be assimilated to a modern rejection of scientific evidence. Rousseau asked what kinds of character were rewarded when people competed for distinction in polished society. A citizen could learn to imitate accepted manners while becoming more dependent on the approval of others. The problem was political because persons who needed patrons, fashions, or public admiration to establish their worth might struggle to make independent judgments about the common good.

The Second Discourse developed this problem through conjectural history. Rousseau contrasted an imagined early human condition, characterized by relative independence and limited needs, with increasingly complex associations in which cooperation, comparison, labor, and property altered human motivations. Two concepts are especially important: a natural concern for one's preservation and a socially intensified desire to be valued by others. The latter, often described by the French term amour-propre, need not be wholly destructive, but under unequal conditions it can produce rivalry, shame, and domination. Rousseau's account is not evidence that a particular prehistoric people lived exactly as he described. Its function is diagnostic: to show how apparently necessary social hierarchies may be products of historical institutions rather than fixed elements of human nature.

Property marks one important transformation, but the story is more complex than a simple attack on owning things. The emergence of cultivation, division of labor, permanent settlements, and legal rules could increase productive capacity while creating dependence on unequal access to resources. Rousseau imagined the establishment of civil laws under circumstances in which the wealthy could persuade others that rules defending their possessions protected everyone equally. The result was a political order that made contingent inequalities appear natural and legitimate. His criticism thus concerns the formation of relations in which some people command and others must seek permission to live securely. Students should compare this account with Locke's argument that labor can establish legitimate possession. Both begin with a prepolitical condition, but Rousseau treats the historical production of inequality as a central problem requiring explanation.

The Second Discourse does not by itself prescribe the institutional solution that The Social Contract would offer. Rousseau distinguished an account of how corrupting dependence might develop from the normative problem of how a free association should be organized. He also recognized that human capacities unfold through social life, so a return to isolated nature was neither a practical program nor a straightforward ideal. The transition from inequality to legitimate citizenship requires institutions that transform private dependence into a common relationship to law. The distance between those projects is why reading only his criticism of property or only his statement of popular sovereignty can misrepresent the whole political argument.

Rousseau was explicit that his state of nature was not an account of actual prehistoric societies. He asked readers to set aside the facts and treat his story as conditional reasoning. Historians therefore read it not as anthropology but as moral and political critique, which drew on travel literature about non-European peoples while using it for European argument. The Second Discourse also differs from Locke, who saw property as natural and a ground of government, and from Hobbes, who saw the state of nature as intolerable. For Rousseau, the problem lay not in nature but in the social development that made people unequal and dependent. That diagnosis set the question to which The Social Contract was addressed.

The Social Contract and the Reconciliation of Freedom with Political Authority

The Social Contract, or Principles of Political Right (1762), begins with a famous sentence: man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. Rousseau did not mean to call for immediate liberation. He asked how the chains might be made legitimate, and framed the problem as finding a form of association that defends and protects the person and goods of each member with the force of all, and in which each, uniting with all, nevertheless obeys only himself and remains as free as before.

His answer is the social contract. Each person gives himself and all his rights entirely to the community, and since the same condition applies to everyone, no one has an interest in making it burdensome to others. The act creates a moral and collective body, which Rousseau calls the sovereign when active and the state when passive. Each member is both a citizen, participating in sovereign authority, and a subject, obeying the laws. Because individuals obey laws they themselves, as members of the sovereign, have made, they obey only themselves, and civil freedom replaces natural liberty.

Rousseau distinguished several kinds of freedom. Natural liberty is an unlimited right to anything one wants and can get, and civil liberty is limited by the general will. Moral freedom, which he said only civil society confers, is obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself. This allowed him to argue that someone who refuses to obey the general will may be forced to be free, a phrase that has provoked much controversy. Defenders read it as saying that a citizen who violates laws he has himself shared in making is compelled to honor his own commitment. Critics argue that it licenses coercion in the name of an abstract freedom.

The opening problem of The Social Contract is often misunderstood as an explanation of how actual states historically began. Rousseau is principally asking for a form of association in which each person, joining others, remains subject to no other person's private dominion. He rejects the idea that force can turn power into moral right. Victory in war may create obedience, but not an obligation that deserves the name of legitimate authority. Nor can a person validly contract away the very freedom that constitutes moral agency. This makes Rousseau's contract unlike a bargain by which an isolated individual transfers unrestricted authority to a master. Political association requires an act that creates a collective body and transforms the position of everyone within it.

The central reciprocal arrangement is the commitment of each participant to the whole political community under the same condition as every other participant. No one is supposed to retain a private privilege to command others outside the common law. In Rousseau's ideal formulation, each citizen becomes both a member of the sovereign people, participating in the formation of general law, and a subject who must obey the law once validly enacted. The political community is not merely a collection of individuals pursuing separate advantages. It is a moral and legal association capable of having common interests. Whether that common interest is always identifiable, and how disagreements can be resolved without silencing legitimate differences, are questions Rousseau's theory repeatedly provokes.

Freedom changes meaning across this transition. Natural liberty, the ability to pursue what one can obtain, is limited by personal strength and circumstances. Civil liberty rests on laws protecting people against another individual's uncontrolled power. Moral freedom entails acting according to laws one has a rightful share in establishing, rather than being driven solely by appetite or dependence. Rousseau accordingly refuses to make the preservation of every private desire the test of a good political order. A free citizen may accept constraints that are justified as laws of the common association. Critics ask what prevents the common good from being defined by ambitious leaders or majorities that mistake their interests for everyone's. The theory's defenders answer that Rousseau's strict concept of general law itself excludes many forms of factional or personalized command.

Property is not abolished simply by entering the social pact. Rousseau argues that civil association can convert precarious possession into a legally protected property relation, but it also subjects private claims to the conditions of the community. A citizen should not be so wealthy as to be able to buy another person, nor so poor as to have to sell oneself. The familiar tension between legal equality and material dependence therefore remains in the center of his political theory. The problem is not settled by claiming either that the general will necessarily destroys property or that a formal vote guarantees substantive equality. The legitimate association must reconcile common authority with safeguards against relations of private domination.

The book rejects the justifications that other writers had offered for authority. Force creates no right, since a right based on force disappears when force fails. The family offers a model of natural society only while children need their father. Slavery cannot be legitimate, since no one can give up freedom, and a contract of unlimited obedience is void. In rejecting such foundations, Rousseau sought a basis for authority in the common participation of equals. His emphasis on legislation by the people, rather than the protection of prepolitical rights, marks the central contrast with Locke, and his demand that individuals be free through, not from, the collective is the point at which later readers divide.

Section 83.15

Popular Sovereignty, the General Will, and the Authority of Law

The most distinctive and most disputed idea in the Social Contract is the general will. Rousseau distinguished the general will from the will of all. The will of all is the sum of private wills, while the general will concerns the common interest, which is what remains when the differences among individual interests cancel one another out. A decision of the people may therefore be mistaken if citizens vote from private interest, and Rousseau argued that the general will is more likely to emerge when citizens decide without organized factions and with adequate information.

Sovereignty, for Rousseau, belongs to the people as a body and cannot be given away or divided. It is inalienable, because will cannot be transferred, and indivisible, because the general will is either whole or not. The sovereign's function is legislation, making general laws applicable to all and not directed at particular persons. Execution, the application of law to particular cases, belongs to a government, which is merely the sovereign's agent and may be changed by it. This distinction between sovereign and government separates Rousseau from Hobbes, for whom the sovereign is a distinct and permanent authority, and links him to Locke, though Rousseau denied that the people delegate sovereignty and rejected Locke's emphasis on a legislature holding a trust.

Law, in this theory, is the expression of the general will, and obedience to law is freedom. Laws must be general in object and source, since the same people who make them are subject to them. This is why Rousseau could say that the people cannot injure themselves. The theory does not provide a simple mechanism for identifying the general will, and he acknowledged that majority voting is a practical procedure that works if citizens are properly oriented to the common good, and that unanimity is required only for the original compact.

The distinction between a general will and the sum of private preferences is the first obstacle to understanding Rousseau's political vocabulary. A collection of individuals may agree to pursue advantages that benefit them as a faction while harming those outside it. Even a numerical majority can be organized around such a partial interest. Rousseau's general will instead concerns rules directed toward the common interest of the political body as a whole. This does not mean that every individual will immediately perceive that interest, or that one charismatic leader may simply announce it. The legitimacy of a law depends on its general character and its relation to the common association, and Rousseau feared that factions could transform deliberation into an exchange of particular favors.

His conception of sovereignty follows from that distinction. The sovereign is the people considered collectively as lawgiver, not a separate officeholder appointed to speak for them. Sovereignty cannot be alienated to a king, an assembly, or a minister without destroying the association's claim to self-government. Rousseau therefore distinguishes the people's sovereign legislative capacity from government, which executes laws and performs particular administrative acts. A government may be democratic, aristocratic, or monarchical in the sense of differing arrangements for the executive, while ultimate sovereignty remains with the citizens. Confusing those institutional levels can make Rousseau appear to reject the existence of representatives, administrators, or delegated responsibilities altogether, when his sharper objection concerns surrendering the authority to make the general laws.

Deliberation is nonetheless hazardous. Rousseau proposes that citizens should consider the general interest rather than vote simply as members of permanent factions. He worries that dependence on wealthy patrons, misinformation, and private associations can distort judgment. His ideal of a relatively cohesive polity raises difficult questions about pluralism, religious minorities, and social diversity. Historical societies contain conflicting interests that cannot always be reduced to an obvious common advantage. An account of popular sovereignty becomes incomplete if it assumes that disagreement always indicates selfishness or corruption. Students should examine both the aspiration to equal public standing and the dangers of treating dissenting voices as enemies of a supposedly self-evident national purpose.

Rousseau's notorious discussion of compelling an unwilling person to be free should be read within, but not excused by, this conceptual framework. He argues that a citizen who has entered a valid association may be required to obey its general laws; the enforcement of a law is not, in that sense, obedience to another private will. Critics argue that the language may enable coercive authorities to label their own commands as freedom. An historically responsible discussion distinguishes the claim that collectively authorized laws may be enforced from the further and far more dangerous claim that a ruler can know a person's true will better than the person does. The text leaves unresolved tensions about membership, coercion, deliberation, and institutional safeguards. Those tensions became central to later interpretations, but they should not be resolved through selective quotation.

Interpretations have varied widely. Some see Rousseau as a theorist of democratic self-government, whose idea of laws made by those who obey them anticipates later claims about political autonomy. Others emphasize the risks of a theory that treats dissenters as mistaken about their own will, and associate it with the Jacobin politics of 1793, a reading historians treat with caution because it reads later events back into an eighteenth century text and ignores his stress on small communities and strict limits. Philosophers continue to disagree about whether the general will is a procedure, a standard, or an ideal, and students should therefore treat any single summary with caution.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Sovereignty, Citizenship, and the General Will

Sovereignty is supreme authority within a political community. Citizenship is membership in a political community with rights and duties that varied widely by period and place. The general will is Rousseau's term for the collective will of citizens directed to the common good, distinct from the sum of individual preferences.

The general will is not the same as a majority vote, and popular sovereignty in Rousseau's sense does not equal representative democracy. Equating them is a common error.

Section 83.16

Citizenship, Representation, Civic Religion, and Republican Government

Rousseau applied his theory to practical questions in the later books of the Social Contract and in later works. Because sovereignty cannot be represented, he argued, citizens must legislate directly, and deputies can serve only as commissioners. In a passage criticizing the English, he claimed that the people of England believe themselves free but are so only during the election of members of Parliament, and that once the members are elected they are slaves. The criticism was aimed at the idea that representation could preserve sovereignty and is usually read as targeted at Montesquieu's model, though Rousseau applied it to the practice of English politics.

He also argued that republics need preconditions. They must be small so that citizens know each other and can assemble. They require relative equality of wealth, so that none is rich enough to buy another or poor enough to be forced to sell himself. They need civic education and public customs that bind citizens to the common good. The Social Contract invokes a legislator, an exceptional founder who frames laws for a people without holding power, and, in its final book, proposes a civil religion with a few simple dogmas, including the sanctity of the contract and laws, and the exclusion of intolerance, to be enforced by banishment. This proposal contradicts the liberal ideal of toleration and has been read as a mark of his concern for unity.

Rousseau also wrote practical proposals. In 1765 he drafted a constitutional project for Corsica, then in revolt against Genoa under Pasquale Paoli, advising an agrarian society of citizen-farmers who would avoid commerce and finance. In 1771 and 1772 he wrote Considerations on the Government of Poland at the request of a Polish noble, recommending civic education, a citizen militia, and reform of the elective monarchy, though his advice was never implemented, and the work was published in 1782. His writings on Geneva, including the Letters Written from the Mountain (1764), defended the rights of the general body of citizens against the small council.

The distinction between sovereign lawgiving and government helps explain Rousseau's institutional proposals. The people cannot permanently hand over the authority to decide the general rules, but administrative tasks necessarily require magistrates. Rousseau analyzes democracy, aristocracy, and monarchy as different ways of arranging executive government, not as three alternative sources of sovereignty. He even considered some forms of elective aristocracy administratively practical under suitable conditions, while insisting that officeholders remained subordinate to the sovereign body. Government had a tendency to develop interests of its own, which could gradually usurp the public's authority. His constitutional problem was therefore how to prevent magistrates from converting delegated administration into independent sovereignty.

The legislator is another difficult figure. Rousseau imagines a founder capable of designing institutions appropriate to a people, yet denies that the legislator can legitimately impose law merely by personal command. The person who proposes an institutional framework must persuade rather than rule. His examples from antiquity, and his occasional recourse to civil religion and public ceremony, show how seriously he regarded the formation of shared political habits. Citizens required more than a formal legal definition; they needed education and practices that taught them to see themselves as members of a common polity. The danger is evident as well: efforts to cultivate civic unity can become demands for conformity, and civic religion can create exclusion in the name of solidarity.

Geneva provided Rousseau with a concrete political language, but it was not a model of universal inclusion. Citizens, bourgeois, natifs, inhabitants, and foreigners occupied legally distinct positions, and only some categories enjoyed the relevant political rights. Conflicts between governing councils and citizen bodies shaped his writing about the proper place of popular assemblies. The fact that he praised aspects of Genevan citizenship does not mean he endorsed all its actual inequalities. He repeatedly used idealized republican examples to criticize practices in the cities and states he knew. A fair interpretation must distinguish the normative citizenry constructed in his theory from the historically restricted population recognized as sovereign by particular republican institutions.

His proposals for Corsica and Poland demonstrate that he did not imagine one identical constitution suitable for every territory. For Corsica he favored limited luxury, civic agriculture, and a social structure intended to sustain independence. For Poland-Lithuania he confronted a large and vulnerable commonwealth characterized by noble privileges, elective monarchy, and pressure from powerful neighbors. His recommendations for civic education, decentralized institutions, and a citizen militia responded to those conditions, even as they relied on a political nation far smaller than the full population. These works illustrate an intellectual movement from general principles to institutional adaptation. They also make clear why Rousseau cannot be reduced to the claim that all political functions must be carried out in one permanent mass assembly.

Rousseau's republic had exclusions. In Emile he assigned women a dependent domestic role, and his political writings assume male citizens. Geneva itself had graded categories of inhabitants, only a minority of whom were citizens with political rights. He condemned slavery as illegitimate but wrote little on colonial slavery. Historians therefore warn against two oversimplifications: that Rousseau was an unqualified founder of modern democracy, and that he was an inevitable precursor of totalitarian rule. His thought combined a radical claim about the sovereignty of the people with a conservative attachment to small, homogeneous, disciplined communities, and these elements pulled in different directions when later movements invoked him.

Critics of Social Contract Theory and Alternative Understandings of Political Legitimacy

Contract theory had critics from the beginning. David Hume (1711 to 1776), the Scottish philosopher, published his essay Of the Original Contract in 1748, and also dealt with the subject in earlier philosophical writings. He argued that no historical evidence supported the claim that governments originated in agreement, since nearly all began in conquest or usurpation, and that the doctrine of tacit consent was absurd, because a poor peasant who cannot leave his country can hardly be said to have chosen it. He compared such a person to one who is carried onto a ship while asleep and then told that he consents to be ruled by the captain because he did not jump overboard.

Hume offered an alternative account. People obey governments because of the advantages government provides and because of habit, which he called allegiance. Obligation is grounded in utility, the benefits of order and justice, and in the long-standing custom of obedience. He did not treat this as a defense of any particular regime. He argued that governments should be judged by their usefulness to society and that resistance could be justified in extreme cases of tyranny. His method was to rely on history and psychology rather than abstract right, and his History of England (1754 to 1761) applied it.

Other writers developed alternatives. Scottish thinkers such as Adam Ferguson and John Millar examined the development of societies through stages, relating political institutions to modes of subsistence and property, an approach that influenced later social theory. Civic republican writers continued to emphasize virtue and participation. Jeremy Bentham, in A Fragment on Government (1776), attacked the idea of an original contract as a fiction and proposed utility as the criterion of government. In Milan, Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764), written within a reforming circle, argued that punishments should be moderate, proportionate, and prescribed by law, and he opposed torture and capital punishment, thereby testing what legitimate state power over individuals could include.

Hume's critique attacked a persistent assumption of contract arguments: that a government's right to obedience could be explained by an original act of collective agreement. Most people, Hume observed, were born under governments whose foundations they never witnessed and whose laws they had little effective power to change. To describe habitual obedience as consent could turn the idea of consent into an empty fiction. A poor laborer who could not afford to leave a country did not meaningfully choose its government merely by staying. Hume's historical skepticism did not imply that any effective ruler was automatically legitimate. He grounded political obligation in the social value of stable rules, public security, and established expectations, while recognizing that extreme oppression could justify resistance. This helps distinguish utility from uncritical traditionalism.

For Hume, allegiance is a moral practice sustained by general social advantages rather than by a discoverable founding agreement. Property, promises, and justice depend on conventions through which people learn that restraint benefits them and others over time. An institution can acquire legitimacy from its operation and the stability it provides, even if its origins involved conquest, inheritance, or conflict. Critics reply that a purely consequential account may struggle to explain why subjects should obey when benefits are unequally distributed or when alternative arrangements are available. The debate requires evidence about actual governmental performance as well as principles of right. Students can use the English and Scottish experiences of dynastic change to ask whether a political settlement becomes rightful because its subjects consent, because it protects them, or because it has endured.

Other writers challenged social-contract reasoning for different reasons. Edmund Burke, near the end of the century, emphasized inherited political arrangements and the risks of reconstructing complex institutions from abstract principles; his major revolutionary critique belongs to the following historical period. Immanuel Kant retained the idea of an original contract as a standard by which laws might be assessed rather than requiring the recovery of an actual founding event. These are not simple variations on the same doctrine. One approach tests legitimacy through prudential judgment and institutional inheritance, while another uses a hypothetical standard of publicly justifiable law. Their presence demonstrates that criticism of literal historical contracts did not dispose of every possible contractual argument.

Reform of criminal justice offers a revealing test of this broader debate. Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments attacked torture, secret accusations, and punishments disproportionate to offenses. The argument rested on limits to the authority legitimately entrusted to the state and on the public purposes punishment could serve. A defendant facing an arbitrary tribunal might care less about the state's speculative founding contract than about whether legal evidence could be contested, whether rules were known, and whether punishments prevented harm. The connection between political obligation and legal institutions thus provides a bridge to Montesquieu's practical constitutional concerns. It also makes clear why debates about legitimacy had consequences outside the narrow circles of philosophers.

These critics matter because they show that the Enlightenment political tradition was not unified. Rights and contract theory competed with utility, historical development, custom, and virtue as foundations of legitimacy. Hume's criticism did not destroy contract language, which continued to serve political argument, but it made clear that the idea of consent needed care. Students should therefore see contract theory as one important strand within eighteenth century debate rather than as the defining form of Enlightenment political thought.

Section 83.18

Women, Slavery, Colonialism, and the Unequal Boundaries of Political Rights

The language of natural equality invited a question that its authors often avoided: equality among whom? The people who held formal political rights in eighteenth century Europe were few. Property qualifications limited the franchise in Britain, the Dutch Republic, and Geneva, serfdom bound much of the rural population of eastern Europe, and married women in England were subject to the common-law doctrine of coverture, while legal personality and marital property rules varied considerably across other European jurisdictions. Jews faced legal disabilities across much of Europe, though writers such as Christian Wilhelm Dohm, in his 1781 essay on the civil improvement of the Jews, and Moses Mendelssohn argued for change. Slavery in the colonies and the slave trade, discussed in Chapter 70, provided the starkest contradiction to the principle that all people are born free.

Some writers made the contradiction explicit. Mary Astell, in Some Reflections upon Marriage (1700), asked why women were subjected to arbitrary power in marriage when men were said to be free from it in government, and suggested that the arguments used against tyranny applied equally to husbands. Catharine Macaulay and, in 1792, Mary Wollstonecraft argued for women's rationality and education. Nicolas de Condorcet, in On the Admission of Women to the Rights of Citizenship, published on 3 July 1790, argued that women had the same natural rights as men, and that excluding them contradicted the principles on which the French Revolution was based.

Antislavery writers made parallel arguments. Quakers and others had condemned slavery on religious grounds since the seventeenth century. Ottobah Cugoano, a Fante man enslaved as a child and later freed in England, published Thoughts and Sentiments on the Evil of Slavery in 1787, denouncing the trade and arguing for its abolition, and Olaudah Equiano's autobiography followed in 1789. Their writings showed that those most affected by exclusion could speak for themselves in the political language of the time, and they countered the claim that Africans were unfit for liberty.

English coverture provides a specific example of the difficulty of treating natural equality as an accomplished social fact. At common law, marriage ordinarily merged a wife's separate legal identity with her husband's for many property and contractual purposes. Yet equity arrangements, trusts, settlements, widowhood, and business custom qualified the rule, and English married women's actual economic activity could be broader than a statement of formal disability suggests. It is inaccurate to project this doctrine unchanged across Europe. Continental civil-law and customary jurisdictions developed differing rules concerning dowries, community property, guardianship, married women's capacity, and inheritance. The legal history of women's exclusion must therefore be written comparatively, distinguishing formal marital power, control over property, actual economic activity, and political exclusion rather than collapsing them into one legal category.

Writers who challenged patriarchal power exposed tensions within the language of liberty. Mary Astell's argument that domestic subordination should be examined with the same skepticism applied to arbitrary monarchy did not automatically entail the same program as Locke's. Catharine Macaulay's republican historical writing contested assumptions about women's intellectual capacities and the political uses of history. Condorcet later argued explicitly that women possessed the rational and moral capacities on which political rights were grounded. His claim placed a burden of consistency on defenders of a political nation restricted to men. None of these interventions made women's political equality a settled norm of the Enlightenment. Their significance lies in demonstrating that contemporaries recognized the contradiction and offered arguments against it.

Slavery and colonial dispossession posed an even more violent challenge. Europeans who proclaimed natural liberty participated in economies structured by hereditary enslavement and military seizure of land. In colonial North America, Europeans frequently treated the absence of their preferred forms of cultivation or title as a justification for appropriation, while Indigenous nations maintained systems of territory, diplomacy, and collective sovereignty of their own. Atlantic plantation law, including French, British, Spanish, Portuguese, and Dutch colonial regulations, varied substantially; there was no single uniform European law of slavery. The political theory question was whether a right asserted as natural could be denied to an entire population by an institution designed for labor extraction. Arguments about just war, civilization, religious conversion, or commercial necessity were deployed to explain away the inconsistency.

Cugoano and other Black antislavery writers rejected the premise that the people most affected should remain only objects of European discussion. Cugoano combined Christian moral arguments with criticism of violence, commerce, and enslavement, calling readers to confront complicity as well as theory. His writing should be analyzed as an intervention in political argument, not merely as evidence that abolitionists elsewhere had sympathy for enslaved Africans. The same is true of Indigenous treaty making and petitions, which reveal claims to jurisdiction and reciprocity within imperial systems. By comparing such sources with Locke's and Rousseau's assertions about freedom, students can see both the reach and the historical limits of universalistic political language. The question is not whether later ideas can erase historical exploitation, but whether claims of legitimacy can be tested consistently against the experiences of people excluded from the institutions making those claims.

Exclusions were not identical. Women were subordinated within a household order that most societies treated as natural, though they had varied property and legal rights. Enslaved people were treated as property and subject to codes such as the French Code Noir of 1685. Serfs were bound to land and lords but possessed certain customary rights. Indigenous peoples were subject to conquest and dispossession, which writers such as Emer de Vattel treated through arguments about cultivation and use of land, though many Indigenous nations also acted as sovereign diplomatic partners in treaty relations. Propertyless laborers were legally free but excluded from the franchise. Historians therefore resist treating these cases as a single phenomenon, and they note that the same texts were used both to justify exclusion and to challenge it.

Section 83.19

The European and Atlantic Reception of Enlightenment Political Thought

Political ideas did not circulate evenly. Locke was read first in Britain and the Dutch Republic. A French translation of the Second Treatise appeared in Amsterdam in 1691, and Pierre Coste translated other works by Locke. Voltaire's Letters on the English (1733) popularized English constitutionalism and Locke's philosophy in France, though in ways that served French debates. Montesquieu was translated into English by Thomas Nugent in 1750 and was widely cited in Britain, France, and the colonies. Rousseau was read passionately but also condemned, and was more influential through his literary fame and his Geneva dispute than through the Social Contract before 1789.

In British lands, Locke's arguments became part of Whig defenses of the Revolution settlement and of dissenters' demands, while Hume, Smith, and Scottish writers developed alternatives. In the Dutch Republic, Patriot reformers of the 1780s drew on Locke, Montesquieu, and English and American examples to attack the Orange stadtholderate. In France, debates over the Parlements, royal power, and the nature of the monarchy made Montesquieu a reference point for those who defended intermediate bodies, while Rousseau stirred criticism of aristocratic society. In German-speaking lands, natural-law teaching in universities, deriving from Pufendorf and Christian Wolff, trained officials, and Kant's essay on enlightenment in 1784 applied the idea of public reason to the question of authority, a subject treated more fully in Chapter 84.

In Italy, Beccaria and the Milanese reformers, and writers in Naples such as Antonio Genovesi and Gaetano Filangieri, applied these ideas to law and economic reform, within states dominated by Habsburg or Bourbon rulers. In Poland-Lithuania, reformers such as Stanislaw Konarski criticized the liberum veto, and Rousseau's recommendations to Poland were requested by Polish nobles though not adopted. The Constitution of 3 May 1791 belongs to the later reform story. In the Atlantic world, colonial pamphleteers invoked Locke, Montesquieu, and English republicans in arguments over taxation and representation in the 1760s and 1770s, and the Declaration of Independence in 1776 and later state and federal constitutions drew on natural-rights and separation-of-powers language, a development reserved for Part XIV.

The movement of ideas between languages changed their meaning. A reader in France encountering Locke through David Mazel's French translation, Du gouvernement civil, published in 1691, did not necessarily encounter the paired treatises in the same form as an English reader. The translation foregrounded the second treatise and brought an anti-absolutist argument into Francophone Protestant networks. Editors wrote prefaces, publishers selected titles, and hostile readers excerpted passages to attack them. Similarly, translations of Montesquieu could emphasize constitutional moderation, while Rousseau's readers sometimes concentrated on civic equality, sentiment, or social criticism instead of his technical distinction between sovereignty and government. Intellectual reception thus requires the history of editions and reading communities developed in Chapter 82, not merely a catalogue of similar words appearing in different constitutions.

Different constitutional settings encouraged different uses. In Britain, a defense of parliamentary authority and established liberties could coexist with distrust of proposals to remake representation. In the Dutch Republic, arguments about the rights of citizens and the powers of the stadtholder interacted with long-standing provincial autonomy and the political struggles of urban regent elites. France's parlements and defenders of noble privilege could cite constitutional moderation against ministerial power while leaving large inequalities untouched. In the German principalities, discussions of natural law often took place in universities training civil servants who served dynastic governments. The same word liberty therefore might refer to a noble estate's right to resist centralization, an individual's protection from arbitrary imprisonment, or a people's right to govern itself.

Southern and eastern European examples reveal further diversity. Italian reformers translated philosophical concerns into debates over criminal procedure, property, commercial regulation, and the functioning of courts. In the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, reformers faced the unusual combination of an elective monarchy, a powerful noble political nation, and foreign intervention. The liberum veto and magnate influence made collective decision-making difficult, prompting constitutional proposals that neither simply copied Britain nor translated Rousseau into statute. In Habsburg territories, writers navigated composite legal traditions and the state's attempts to standardize administration. Some Orthodox and Greek intellectual settings developed political arguments through translations, education, and encounters with Ottoman and Habsburg authorities. Europe should not be reduced to a western center broadcasting theory into a silent periphery.

The Atlantic reception was similarly selective. Colonial settlers could claim the rights of English subjects against taxation while denying equal rights to Indigenous nations, enslaved Africans, or propertyless inhabitants. Antislavery writers could appropriate natural-rights vocabulary to condemn institutions defended by other people quoting the same authors. The American independence movement and the framing of written constitutions made ideas about consent and power especially visible, but they also drew from local charters, English legal practice, classical republican language, religious arguments, and immediate political negotiations. It would be anachronistic to claim that one philosopher produced an event or an institution by himself. The chapter's appropriate conclusion is that political ideas entered conflicts shaped by social and material conditions, and their meanings were repeatedly transformed by the actors who used them.

These uses show that the same ideas had different applications. Locke served conservative defenders of a settlement and radical critics, Montesquieu was cited by supporters of aristocratic constitutionalism and by republican founders, and Rousseau was read as a critic of civilization and as a theorist of popular sovereignty. Texts changed meaning as they were translated, excerpted, and adapted to local institutions. This variety explains why political theory did not have a single predetermined application and why historians of the Revolution debate the extent of its intellectual origins.

Everyday Life and Social History

Political Rights and Social Status: Five Lives in the Eighteenth Century

The abstract language of rights meant different things in different lives. An aristocratic officeholder in France or Britain could sit in a Parlement or the House of Lords, hold offices, and enjoy legal privileges such as tax exemptions or immunity from some penalties. A prosperous property owner in Britain might vote in a county or borough election and serve on juries or parish bodies, though the franchise was uneven and often controlled by patrons.

A rural laborer in England or France was legally free but usually without a vote, and depended on landlords and employers. In eastern Europe, peasants were often serfs, bound to estates and subject to the lord's courts. A married woman, whatever her rank, was usually subject to her husband's legal authority, though some had property protections under local custom, and widows or unmarried women sometimes held more independence. An enslaved person in a colony was treated as property and subject to a slave code, with almost no recognized legal protection, though enslaved people resisted and petitioned.

These differences depended on jurisdiction as well as status. Rights varied between England and Scotland, between French provinces, between Dutch cities, and between metropole and colony. Historians therefore study court records, petitions, tax rolls, franchise lists, and slave codes to understand how rights operated in practice, and they avoid assuming that the writings of philosophers described the legal experience of ordinary people.

Section 83.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The theories examined in this chapter should be compared directly. Hobbes asked how political order could overcome insecurity and answered that individuals must authorize an undivided sovereign. Locke asked how legitimate government could protect preexisting rights and answered with consent, a limited legislature, and a right of resistance. Montesquieu asked how institutions could restrain arbitrary power and answered by studying the structure of governments and the distribution of power among social orders. Rousseau asked how people could govern themselves collectively without surrendering their freedom to others and answered with popular sovereignty and the general will.

The disagreements were about fundamental matters. For Hobbes and Rousseau sovereignty was indivisible, though they located it in very different places. For Locke it was a trust, and for Montesquieu the more important question was how power was divided. Liberty meant freedom under law for Hobbes, the protection of natural liberty for Locke, security against arbitrary power for Montesquieu, and obedience to self-prescribed law for Rousseau. Property was a natural right for Locke, a source of inequality for Rousseau, and a factor in the balance of the constitution for Montesquieu. Locke and Montesquieu incorporated political representation in distinct ways; Rousseau rejected representation of sovereign lawmaking but permitted government and magistrates to administer law on behalf of the sovereign people.

A synthesis should also keep in view what these theories did not do. They did not establish universal suffrage, abolish slavery, secure religious equality, or end the political subordination of women. Their authors were embedded in societies that practiced such exclusions and often benefited from them. At the same time, the premises they supplied, that authority needs justification, that rights limit power, and that laws should be made by or for the governed, were open to uses their authors had not intended. Later critics such as Hume, Astell, Condorcet, and Cugoano show that these tensions were visible to contemporaries.

The lasting significance of the chapter is that it identifies a set of questions that remained open throughout the following centuries: whether legitimacy rests on consent, utility, tradition, or collective self-rule; whether liberty is best protected by rights, institutions, or participation; and whether sovereignty can be divided or represented. Chapter 84 turns to the governments that confronted such arguments, in the reforming monarchies of the later eighteenth century, and Chapters 87 to 92 examine the revolutionary crisis in which the questions were tested by events.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Arguments about Authority, 1625 to 1790

What changed?

  • Authority increasingly required justification by reason and consent rather than inheritance alone
  • Natural-rights vocabulary became a standard language of criticism
  • Institutional analysis and comparison became part of political writing
  • The public sphere of print made political argument widely accessible

What remained continuous?

  • Rulers still claimed authority from God, dynasty, and custom
  • Property, status, and sex continued to determine political participation
  • Slavery, serfdom, and colonial dispossession persisted
  • Censorship and exile continued to constrain writers

Who experienced change most directly? Educated men in towns, courts, and universities, who were the main audience and participants, and those in revolutionary or reform situations.

Who experienced greater continuity? Peasants, laborers, most women, enslaved people, and the poor, whose daily legal position changed little before 1789.

Why does the pattern matter? It prevents treating the history of political ideas as a simple story of progress, and shows how arguments changed faster than legal and social realities.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Emergence of Competing Theories of Legitimate Government

Long-term causes

Confessional division, the growth of fiscal-military states, inherited traditions of natural law, republicanism, and customary rights, and the expansion of print.

Short-term causes

The English Civil Wars, the Exclusion Crisis, the Glorious Revolution, the absolutism of Louis XIV, and the disputes in Geneva.

Event or transformation

The production of systematic theories of obligation, liberty, and sovereignty by Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau, with critics and alternatives.

Immediate effects

Debates over resistance, toleration, constitutions, and reform, censorship and exile for authors, and translation across Europe.

Long-term consequences

A shared vocabulary of rights, constitutionalism, and popular sovereignty used by reformers and revolutionaries, and persistent disputes about exclusion.

Why not one cause?

Ideas were shaped by crises but also by traditions, institutions, publishers, and personal circumstances.

Historical complexity: The theories cannot be explained solely by the events that provoked them, nor can later political events be explained solely by theory. Each influenced the other, and the same texts could support opposed causes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how European thinkers between the early seventeenth and late eighteenth centuries reconsidered the origin, purpose, and limits of political authority. The central problem was legitimacy: what makes government rightful, why subjects should obey, and when authority may be limited or replaced. The chapter presented several competing answers rather than a single Enlightenment doctrine.

Division I established the foundations. Religious conflict, civil war, and the growth of states made sovereignty and obedience urgent. Divine right monarchy, constitutional traditions, republican institutions, and customary rights offered rival accounts, and natural law supplied a shared vocabulary. Grotius, Pufendorf, and Spinoza developed natural law and natural right in ways that did not yet amount to modern human rights.

Hobbes, writing in the shadow of the English Civil Wars, argued that individuals in a state of nature faced perpetual insecurity and that peace required an authorized and undivided sovereign. His theory was a serious account of legitimacy based on authorization and protection, not merely a defense of tyranny, and subjects retained a right to preserve their lives. Republican writers such as Harrington and Sidney, and Dutch writers, offered alternatives based on balance, participation, and resistance to tyranny.

Division II examined Locke. Written in the context of the Exclusion Crisis and the Glorious Revolution, the Two Treatises rejected Filmer's patriarchal monarchy and grounded government in consent and the protection of rights, including property, within a law of nature. Government was a trust, and the people could resist rulers who violated it. His Letter Concerning Toleration defended liberty of conscience while excluding atheists and Catholics. Locke's thought contained tensions concerning property, colonial claims, and slavery, and consent did not mean universal suffrage.

Division III examined Montesquieu. Through the Persian Letters and The Spirit of the Laws he analyzed republics, monarchies, and despotisms, linked laws to customs, commerce, and environment, and argued that liberty depends on arrangements in which power checks power. His account of the English constitution was an interpretation, not a description, and the modern formula of three separate branches misrepresents it. His defense of intermediate powers reflected a vision of moderate monarchy and aristocratic privilege.

Division IV examined Rousseau. His Discourses criticized civilization and traced inequality to property and social dependence, and the Social Contract sought a form of association in which citizens obey only laws they prescribe themselves. Sovereignty, for Rousseau, belonged to the people, was inalienable and indivisible, and expressed the general will. The theory raised persistent interpretive disputes about coercion, majorities, and dissent, and was accompanied by civil religion, small republics, and exclusions, including women.

Division V placed these theories among their critics and exclusions. Hume attacked the historical and philosophical basis of contract theory and offered utility and custom as alternatives. Beccaria, Scottish writers, and Bentham pointed in other directions. Astell, Condorcet, Cugoano, and others tested natural equality against the subordination of women and the enslavement of Africans, while serfdom, coverture, property qualifications, religious disabilities, and colonial dispossession showed how unequal the boundaries of rights remained.

The reception of these ideas varied. Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau were translated, adapted, censored, and used differently in Britain, France, the Dutch Republic, German lands, Italy, Poland-Lithuania, and the Atlantic colonies. Political ideas had no single predetermined application, and conservatives, reformers, and revolutionaries all found resources in them.

The comparison of the four theories remains the chapter's main lesson. Hobbes emphasized security, Locke rights and limited government, Montesquieu liberty through moderated power, and Rousseau collective self-rule. These answers overlapped but were not identical or inevitably compatible. Their authors were embedded in societies of privilege and exclusion, and the history of their ideas is therefore a history of both ambition and limitation. Chapter 84 examines how governments responded to such ideas, and Part XIV examines their testing in revolution.

Absolutism
A theory and practice of monarchy in which the ruler claims supreme authority not subject to human legal limits, though in practice constrained by custom, law, and finance.
Civil religion
Rousseau's proposal for a minimal set of public beliefs supporting loyalty to the laws and the state.
Constitutionalism
The principle that government is limited by fundamental laws or practices.
Consent
Voluntary agreement of the governed, treated by Locke as the basis of legitimate government; it could be express or tacit.
Coverture
Under English common law, a doctrine that substantially merged a married wife's independent legal capacity with her husband's, although exceptions and equitable arrangements existed; other European jurisdictions followed different marital-property and legal-capacity rules.
Despotism
In Montesquieu, government by one person according to will and caprice, sustained by fear.
Divine right
The doctrine that monarchs derive authority directly from God and are accountable to Him alone.
Federative power
In Locke, the power to conduct relations with other states, including war and treaties.
Franchise
The right to vote, restricted in the eighteenth century by property, sex, religion, and local custom.
General will
In Rousseau, the will of the citizen body directed to the common good, distinct from the will of all.
Honor
In Montesquieu, the principle of monarchy, the sense of distinction attached to rank.
Intermediate powers
Bodies such as nobility, clergy, towns, and Parlements that stand between ruler and people and limit arbitrary power.
Law of nations
Rules governing relations among states, understood by Grotius as arising from the practice and consent of states.
Legislator
In Rousseau, an exceptional founder who frames laws for a people without holding sovereign power.
Mixed government
A constitution combining monarchic, aristocratic, and popular elements.
Natural law
A moral order binding on all people and knowable by reason or revelation.
Natural rights
Claims held by individuals by nature, such as self-preservation.
Patriarchalism
Filmer's theory that royal authority derives from the fatherly authority of Adam.
Political liberty
In Montesquieu, the right to do what the laws allow and security against abuse of power.
Political obligation
The question of why and when individuals must obey government.
Popular sovereignty
The principle that ultimate authority belongs to the people.
Property
In Locke, life, liberty, and estate; more narrowly, possessions acquired through labor.
Republicanism
A tradition emphasizing self-government, mixed constitutions, and civic virtue.
Right of resistance
The claim that subjects may lawfully oppose a ruler who violates the purposes of government.
Separation of powers
Distribution of legislative, executive, and judicial functions among different bodies.
Social contract
An agreement, real or hypothetical, forming a political community or authorizing government.
Sovereignty
Supreme and final authority within a political community.
State of nature
A hypothetical condition without government used to analyze its purpose.
Tacit consent
Locke's notion that continued residence and enjoyment of a country's benefits implies agreement.
Toleration
The policy of permitting religious difference; its extent varied widely among writers and states.
Utility
The principle that institutions are justified by their usefulness to society, central to Hume and Bentham.
Virtue
In republican theory, the willingness to place the common good above private interest.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it identifies the questions that organized modern political argument. Whether government rests on consent, utility, tradition, or collective self-rule, whether liberty is protected by rights, institutions, or participation, and whether sovereignty can be divided or represented were questions raised by actual crises in England, France, and Geneva, and they remained open after the authors who first posed them were dead.

It also shows how ideas and exclusion were intertwined. The language of natural equality and consent was developed by writers who lived in societies of privilege, serfdom, patriarchy, and slavery. Recognizing that does not diminish their achievement, but it prevents the error of treating them as simple founders of modern democracy, and it explains why critics from within and outside the tradition could turn their premises against their practices.

Within Volume IV, the chapter forms a bridge between the intellectual history of Part XIII and the political and economic transformations that follow. Chapter 84 will examine how monarchs and governments responded to such ideas, and Chapters 87 to 92 will show how revolutionaries adapted them under pressure. Nineteenth century liberals, conservatives, socialists, and nationalists, examined in Chapters 96 and 97, inherited and quarreled over the same vocabulary.

Finally, the chapter illustrates how historical knowledge of ideas is constructed: from texts and their contexts, from the conditions of printing and censorship, and from the reception that gave them life. Students should carry forward the habit of asking who wrote, for whom, under what pressures, and with what exclusions, and of distinguishing what a thinker argued from what later generations made of the name.
