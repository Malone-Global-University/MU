The Post-Roman Kingdoms of Western Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

When the last emperor recognized in Italy was set aside in 476, no single event announced the beginning of a new age. Roman law courts continued to sit, Roman taxes continued, in most places, to be collected, Roman senators continued to hold their estates and their titles, and Roman bishops continued to preside over their congregations. What changed, gradually and unevenly across the former western provinces, was who commanded the armies, who claimed the ultimate right to rule, and, in time, who the population understood itself to be governed by.

Picture, in the early sixth century, a Roman senator in Ravenna receiving a letter written in his king's name, drafted by a Roman civil servant, quoting Roman law, and addressed to him in the polished Latin rhetoric his education had trained him to expect from an emperor's chancery. The king who sent it, Theoderic, ruled as an Ostrogothic warlord who had conquered Italy with an army recruited largely outside the peninsula. Yet the letter he sent looked, on its surface, remarkably like the correspondence of any late Roman emperor. Picture, at roughly the same moment, a landholder in southern Gaul watching the political center of gravity shift, within a single generation, from a Visigothic king ruling from Toulouse to a Frankish king whose armies had just destroyed that same Visigothic kingdom north of the Pyrenees. Picture, finally, a farming community in lowland Britain, perhaps two or three generations after the last Roman administrators had withdrawn, no longer served by an imperial governor, no longer paying an imperial tax collector, and increasingly ruled, at the local level, by warrior leaders whose names, burial customs, and material culture look less Roman with each passing decade.

These three scenes, invented here only as composite illustrations rather than as documented individual lives, nonetheless represent three genuinely different historical experiences of the same broad transformation: the end of a single, unified western Roman imperial government and its replacement, across the former western provinces, by a patchwork of new political communities. Chapter 28 explained how and why the machinery of western imperial rule broke down over the course of the fifth century, a process of fragmentation rather than a single catastrophic collapse, and one that left the eastern Roman Empire, governed from Constantinople, very much intact. This chapter turns from that story of ending to a necessary story of beginning: what kinds of political order actually replaced western imperial government, region by region, and how much of the Roman world these new rulers preserved, adapted, or discarded.

The temptation, when narrating this transition, is to reach for a simple and satisfying formula: Rome fell, and barbarians took its place. That formula is not so much wrong as radically insufficient. It flattens four or five genuinely distinct political trajectories into a single undifferentiated story, and it obscures the fact that the people historians conventionally call Goths, Franks, or Anglo-Saxons were not simply foreign invaders who arrived, conquered, and then governed according to customs wholly separate from Rome. Many of the men who became kings in the post-Roman west had themselves served, at some point, as Roman military officers or allied commanders. Many of their followers had lived for generations inside or alongside the Roman frontier system, adopting Roman military equipment, Roman coinage, and, in some cases, Roman Christianity, well before they became rulers of Roman provinces. The new kingdoms this chapter examines were not simply the negation of Rome. They were new political creations built, in varying proportions depending on region, from Roman institutions, Roman law, Roman landholding patterns, Roman Christian church structures, and inherited military and customary traditions carried by the new ruling groups themselves.

This chapter therefore asks a comparative question rather than telling four separate national stories: how did rulers who emerged from the fragmentation of the western Roman Empire convert military power into durable government, and what did they keep, change, or abandon from the Roman world they now ruled? Answering that question requires examining Ostrogothic Italy, where governmental continuity with the Roman past was unusually strong; Visigothic Iberia, where a kingdom that began in southern Gaul relocated its center of gravity across the Pyrenees and gradually built a monarchy resting heavily on Roman provincial society; Anglo-Saxon Britain, where Roman institutional structures collapsed more completely than almost anywhere else in the former western provinces; and Frankish Gaul, where Clovis built a rapidly expanding kingdom on a foundation of substantial Roman social and ecclesiastical continuity.

None of these kingdoms, it is worth emphasizing at the outset, was the inevitable outcome of some deeper historical logic. Each depended on specific military campaigns, specific alliances, specific religious choices, and specific relationships between new rulers and the Roman populations, aristocracies, and churches they now governed. Some of these kingdoms proved durable. Others, as later chapters will show, did not survive the century that followed their founding. This chapter's task is not to explain why one kingdom eventually triumphed over the others, since that outcome was not yet visible to anyone living through the events described here. Its task is to explain, region by region and then comparatively, how the post-Roman political map of western Europe actually took shape.

Finally, this chapter previews a methodological caution that will matter throughout this textbook's treatment of the early medieval period. The very names historians use for these peoples and kingdoms, Goth, Frank, Saxon, Roman, Briton, can suggest fixed, bounded, biologically continuous populations moving through history like chess pieces. The evidence available to historians, drawn from archaeology, law codes, chronicles written well after the events they describe, and the scattered documentary residue of early medieval government, tells a more complicated story of shifting political identities, mixed populations, and categories that were often more legal and political than what a modern reader might assume from the word "ethnic." This chapter introduces that complication carefully rather than resolving it, since the fuller historiographical treatment belongs to a dedicated section near the chapter's close.

Historical Context

Chapter 28 traced the fifth-century fragmentation of western Roman imperial authority, a process driven by recurring civil war, the loss of tax-paying provinces, the growing independence of regional military commanders, and the settlement of allied and invading groups within the empire's western territories. That chapter established several conclusions this chapter now builds upon directly. First, the western Roman Empire did not fall in a single dramatic moment. The conventional date of 476, when the Roman general Odoacer deposed the child emperor Romulus Augustulus and declined to appoint a further western emperor, marked the end of a separate western imperial office that had, in practice, already lost most of its effective authority over the preceding decades. Second, the eastern Roman Empire, governed from Constantinople, survived this crisis largely intact and continued to regard itself, and to be regarded by many contemporaries, as the sole legitimate Roman imperial government, a claim with real consequences for how western rulers such as Theoderic sought to present their own authority. Third, Roman provincial society, its landholding aristocracies, its cities, its churches, and its administrative habits, did not disappear when imperial government did. It persisted, in varying degrees of strength, as the social and institutional material out of which new political orders were built.

By the time this chapter's narrative opens, in the final decades of the fifth century, several military and political groups had already established durable footholds within the former western provinces. Visigothic forces, originally settled in southwestern Gaul under a treaty arrangement with the Roman state earlier in the fifth century, had built a substantial kingdom centered on Toulouse and extending, at its height, across much of Gaul south of the Loire and into Iberia. Ostrogothic forces, under the command of Theoderic, remained, as the fifth century closed, formally in the service of the eastern Roman emperor, who in the late 480s encouraged Theoderic to lead his people into Italy and displace Odoacer, a solution that removed a difficult and potentially dangerous Gothic army from territory closer to Constantinople while leaving the eastern emperor's precise relationship to a restored Italian kingdom deliberately ambiguous. In Gaul north of the Loire, a Roman military commander named Syagrius maintained a rump Roman administrative territory around Soissons, one of the last directly governed remnants of Roman Gaul, while Frankish groups, settled for generations along and within the Rhine frontier, expanded their military and political presence in northern Gaul. In Britain, Roman administrative withdrawal had already proceeded further and faster than almost anywhere else in the western provinces, a process examined more fully in this chapter's dedicated section on post-Roman Britain.

Several structural features of the late Roman world shaped what all these new political communities inherited. Roman provincial government had long relied on a combination of imperial officials, local municipal councils dominated by landowning elites, and, increasingly from the fourth century onward, Christian bishops who exercised growing civic as well as religious authority in their cities. Roman law, codified and elaborated over centuries, governed property, inheritance, contract, and status in ways deeply embedded in provincial society. Roman taxation, though it had become increasingly difficult to collect efficiently as the fifth century progressed, had underwritten a professional army and an extensive administrative apparatus, and the capacity, or incapacity, of a new ruler to maintain some version of this fiscal system shaped how much continuity his government could sustain. Roman landholding patterns, dominated in many regions by large aristocratic estates worked by tenant farmers and, in some areas, enslaved laborers, provided the economic foundation on which both the old Roman aristocracy and the new ruling groups depended.

It is also essential to recognize, before turning to the individual kingdoms, that the groups conventionally identified as Goths, Franks, and Saxons were not simply foreign populations transplanted wholesale from distant homelands into Roman territory. Many had lived for generations along or within the Roman frontier, serving as Roman soldiers, allied troops, or frontier settlers, absorbing Roman military technology, administrative vocabulary, and, in some cases, Christianity, well before their leaders became kings of Roman provinces. The relationship between these groups and the Roman world they eventually governed was therefore one of long and complicated interaction rather than sudden, unmediated conquest by wholly alien outsiders, a point developed further in this chapter's closing section on identity and ethnogenesis.

Section 29.1

From Western Empire to Post-Roman Kingdoms

Chapter 28 closed with the western Roman Empire's central imperial office effectively extinguished, its former provinces divided among several regional powers, and the eastern Roman Empire, ruling from Constantinople, continuing to claim, in principle, sovereignty over the entire Roman world. This chapter begins from that point and asks what actually took its place. The phrase "post-Roman kingdoms," used throughout this chapter and this Part, is a deliberate historical label rather than a term contemporaries themselves would necessarily have used. It signals two things simultaneously: that these kingdoms emerged after, and out of the fragmentation of, western Roman imperial government, and that they were not simply Rome under a different name, nor were they a wholesale rejection of everything Roman. They were new political formations, built in each region from a distinctive combination of surviving Roman institutions, Roman social structures, and the military and customary traditions of the peoples who now furnished their kings.

To compare these kingdoms usefully, this chapter organizes its analysis around several recurring criteria. The first is kingship itself: how a ruler claimed legitimacy, whether through military success, negotiated arrangements with Rome or with local elites, hereditary succession, or some combination of these. The second is administration: whether a kingdom retained anything resembling the late Roman offices of provincial and civic government, and whether it employed Roman-trained civil servants. The third is law: whether a kingdom issued new law codes, preserved Roman law for its Roman subjects, applied different law to different populations, or moved toward a single body of law for all its inhabitants. The fourth is taxation: whether a kingdom continued to collect anything resembling the Roman land tax, and what that continuity or discontinuity reveals about the strength of its fiscal administration. The fifth is the position of the landed aristocracy, both the old senatorial and curial elites inherited from Rome and the new military elite associated with the incoming rulers. The sixth is religion, particularly the frequently significant division between Arian Christianity, professed by several of the new ruling groups, and the Nicene Christianity of most of the Roman population and its bishops. The seventh is military organization, since every one of these kingdoms rested, in the end, on its capacity to raise and sustain effective armed force. The eighth, and in some ways the most difficult, is identity: how contemporaries understood the difference, if any, between "Roman" and the various peoples now ruling them, a question this chapter treats at length in its closing sections.

Applying these criteria across Italy, Iberia, Britain, and Gaul reveals a spectrum rather than a single pattern. At one end stood Ostrogothic Italy, where Theoderic inherited, and largely preserved, an unusually intact Roman administrative and senatorial apparatus. At the other end stood post-Roman Britain, where Roman civil administration, taxation, and urban life had contracted so severely by the time new political communities formed that continuity with the Roman past operated very differently, and far more faintly in institutional terms, than anywhere else examined in this chapter. Visigothic Iberia and Frankish Gaul occupied intermediate positions, each combining substantial Roman social and legal inheritance with new and evolving forms of royal authority. Holding these four cases in comparative view, rather than narrating them as four disconnected stories, is the organizing purpose of this chapter.

Section 29.2

Roman Institutions after Roman Imperial Government

Before examining each kingdom individually, it is necessary to establish what, in general terms, the post-Roman rulers of western Europe actually inherited from the Roman world, since every subsequent regional discussion in this chapter measures its subject against this common baseline. Late Roman provincial society rested on several interlocking institutions. Cities, though many had shrunk considerably from their earlier imperial size, remained centers of administration, commerce, and, increasingly, ecclesiastical authority. Each city of any significance had traditionally possessed a municipal council, the curia, composed of local landowning notables responsible, among other duties, for local tax collection, a responsibility that had become an increasingly unwelcome burden by the late imperial period as fiscal demands rose and central enforcement weakened.

Roman law, elaborated over centuries through imperial legislation, juristic commentary, and provincial custom, governed property, inheritance, contracts, slavery, and personal status for the empire's Roman inhabitants. By the late fourth and fifth centuries, this law had been partially systematized in various compilations, a process that would culminate, for the post-Roman kingdoms specifically, in codifications such as the Visigothic Breviary of Alaric, issued in the early sixth century as a simplified digest of Roman law, a development discussed further, including debate over its precise intended constituency, in Section 29.6. Roman taxation rested primarily on land, assessed according to complex and regionally variable formulas, and had financed both the professional Roman army and an extensive administrative bureaucracy organized around provinces and, above them, larger administrative units called dioceses and praetorian prefectures.

The late Roman landholding aristocracy, whether the senatorial elite of Italy or the comparable provincial nobilities of Gaul, Iberia, and, to a lesser extent, Britain, controlled vast agricultural estates, called villas, worked by a combination of free tenant farmers, known as coloni, and enslaved laborers. This aristocracy's wealth, education, and local social authority made it an indispensable partner, or a dangerous rival, for any new ruler seeking to govern effectively. Finally, the Christian church, and particularly its bishops, had by the fifth century become central figures in urban civic life, exercising judicial, charitable, and increasingly political functions that, in many cities, outlasted or absorbed functions once performed by Roman civil officials.

Every kingdom examined in this chapter had to decide, in practice rather than through any single deliberate policy, how much of this institutional inheritance to preserve, how much to adapt, and how much to allow to lapse. As the following sections show, the answer varied enormously depending on the strength of the incoming military and political leadership, the depth of prior interaction between that leadership and the Roman world, the willingness of Roman elites to cooperate with new rulers, and the sheer scale of the disruption that had preceded a given kingdom's formation.

Section 29.3

Ostrogothic Italy and Theoderic

Theoderic, who led the Ostrogothic people into Italy between 488 and 493, offers the clearest example, among the kingdoms examined in this chapter, of a ruler who inherited an unusually intact Roman institutional structure and chose, deliberately, to preserve it. Theoderic had spent part of his youth as a hostage in Constantinople, where he received a Roman-influenced education and later served, for a time, as a Roman military officer allied to the eastern emperor. This background gave him a familiarity with Roman administrative practice, and with the political value of appearing as a legitimate governor rather than a foreign conqueror, that shaped his entire approach to ruling Italy.

The eastern emperor Zeno, seeking to remove a powerful and potentially dangerous Ostrogothic army from territory closer to Constantinople while simultaneously unseating Odoacer, whose own constitutional relationship with Constantinople had remained ambiguous, reached an arrangement with Theoderic by which Theoderic would lead his people westward and remove Odoacer from power. The precise constitutional status Zeno and Theoderic envisioned for Theoderic's subsequent rule of Italy is difficult to reconstruct from the surviving evidence and remains a long-standing scholarly problem. Theoderic's campaign against Odoacer lasted several years and culminated, in 493, in a negotiated agreement for joint rule that Theoderic almost immediately violated, killing Odoacer at a banquet and eliminating him as a rival. From that point until his death in 526, Theoderic ruled Italy as king of the Ostrogoths, while maintaining a carefully ambiguous relationship with Constantinople that avoided outright rejection of eastern imperial sovereignty while, in practice, exercising fully independent authority.

Theoderic's government rested on a deliberate division of function between Goths and Romans, at least in principle. Gothic soldiers, settled across Italy under arrangements historians debate in some detail, discussed further in the Historical Debate feature below, provided military manpower and formed the core of Theoderic's armed forces, while Roman civil administration, staffed very largely by the existing senatorial and curial elite, continued to manage taxation, law, and the ordinary business of provincial government. Theoderic retained the Roman Senate in Rome, preserved existing Roman offices such as the praetorian prefecture, and issued edicts that, in form and legal reasoning, drew heavily on Roman legal tradition. He presented himself, in his own correspondence and public inscriptions, not as a foreign usurper but as a ruler restoring order, prosperity, and the rule of law to a Roman Italy that had suffered under Odoacer's less settled regime.

This self-presentation should not be mistaken for the whole reality of Theoderic's rule. His authority rested, in the last instance, on Gothic military force, and Goths and Romans remained, for most purposes, distinct legal and social populations under his government, a distinction examined further in Section 29.11. Nevertheless, the degree of institutional continuity Theoderic achieved, and deliberately cultivated, remains historically remarkable. Few other post-Roman rulers governed a former Roman province with a comparably intact senatorial aristocracy, a comparably functioning fiscal administration, or a comparably self-conscious rhetoric of restoring, rather than replacing, Roman order.

Section 29.4

Government and Society in Ostrogothic Italy

Beyond Theoderic's personal biography, Ostrogothic Italy's institutional structure repays closer examination, since it illustrates in unusual detail how a post-Roman kingdom could combine new military rule with sustained Roman civil government. Roman civil offices, including the praetorian prefecture, the office responsible for finance and general administration, and the office of master of offices, responsible for the royal chancery and court ceremonial, continued to function under Theoderic, staffed by Roman aristocrats trained in the traditions of late Roman government. Cassiodorus, whose career and writings are examined more fully in the Primary Source Window below, held several of the kingdom's highest civil offices across a career spanning multiple reigns, illustrating the continuing importance of educated Roman administrators to Ostrogothic government.

Roman law continued to govern the kingdom's Roman population, and Theoderic issued his own edicts within a recognizably Roman legal framework, addressing matters such as property disputes, public order, and the regulation of urban life in language and reasoning drawn directly from Roman legal tradition. Roman taxation, though it faced the same practical difficulties of assessment and collection that had troubled the late western empire, continued to be levied, and Theoderic invested visibly in the physical infrastructure of Roman Italy, including aqueducts, public buildings, and the fortifications and palace complex at Ravenna, projecting an image of restored Roman prosperity.

At the same time, religious division introduced a significant and, in the long run, corrosive tension into Ostrogothic society. Theoderic and most of his Gothic followers adhered to Homoian Christianity, traditionally labeled "Arian" in much historical writing after the fourth-century theologian Arius, though modern scholarship distinguishes the specific Homoian creed actually professed by Gothic churches from the broader and more varied set of positions historically grouped under that older label. This chapter uses the familiar term "Arian" for readability in what follows, while noting that the underlying theology was more specific than that label alone suggests. Homoian Christianity differed from the Nicene theology professed by the overwhelming majority of Italy's Roman population and clergy; Nicene church authorities regarded Homoian doctrine as heretical, a judgment rooted in the fourth-century Council of Nicaea of 325 and elaborated in subsequent church councils. Theoderic pursued a generally tolerant policy toward Nicene Christianity throughout most of his reign, avoiding forced conversion and permitting the Nicene church to function largely undisturbed, a policy that helped sustain cooperation between Gothic rulers and Roman Catholic elites. In the final years of his reign, however, relations deteriorated, coinciding with rising suspicion of senatorial loyalty to Constantinople and culminating in the execution of prominent Roman aristocrats, including the philosopher and statesman Boethius, on charges of treasonous correspondence with the eastern empire. This episode illustrates that the accommodation between Gothic rulers and Roman elites, however successful for much of Theoderic's reign, remained fragile and dependent on continued mutual trust rather than resting on fully integrated or permanently stable institutions.

This chapter does not follow Ostrogothic Italy's history beyond Theoderic's death in 526, since the destructive wars of reconquest waged by the eastern Roman Empire under Justinian, which eventually destroyed the Ostrogothic kingdom, belong to the Byzantine history examined in Part VI. What matters here is the model Ostrogothic Italy offers for comparison: a post-Roman kingdom in which Roman civil, legal, and senatorial institutions survived to an unusually high degree, coexisting, not always comfortably, with a distinct Gothic military and religious identity.

Section 29.5

The Visigoths from Gaul to Iberia

The Visigothic kingdom's early history followed a markedly different geographic trajectory from that of the Ostrogoths in Italy. Visigothic forces had been settled in Aquitaine, in southwestern Gaul, since 418, under an arrangement with the western Roman state that historians continue to debate in its precise legal and practical details, discussed further in the Historical Debate feature. Over the following decades, Visigothic kings expanded their territory well beyond the terms of that original settlement, extending their authority across much of southern Gaul and, increasingly, into Iberia, where Roman provincial government had already weakened considerably under pressure from other groups, including Suevi and Vandals, who had entered the peninsula earlier in the fifth century.

By the later fifth century, the Visigothic kingdom, ruled from Toulouse, controlled much of southern Gaul and had extended its authority into substantial, expanding, but unevenly and often loosely held portions of Iberia, making it, for a time, the largest and most powerful of the western successor kingdoms. This position proved unsustainable once a serious rival emerged to its north. Clovis, whose rise in Gaul is examined in Section 29.9, defeated and killed the Visigothic king Alaric II at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, a defeat that ended Visigothic dominance over most of Gaul. Surviving Visigothic forces and their king's young successor withdrew primarily into Iberia, retaining a reduced but still substantial foothold north of the Pyrenees in the region historians call Septimania, or Gallia Narbonensis, centered on Narbonne, that would persist for centuries before eventually passing to Frankish and later Arab control.

This defeat at Vouillé should be understood as a genuine turning point rather than a mere setback. It redirected the entire subsequent history of the Visigothic kingdom toward Iberia, where its rulers would spend the following two centuries building a monarchy resting increasingly on Hispano-Roman provincial society, a development examined in the following section. The shift also illustrates a broader theme of this chapter: the boundaries and centers of the post-Roman kingdoms were not fixed at their founding but continued to shift, sometimes dramatically, as a result of specific military and political contests among the new ruling powers themselves, not only in confrontation with a fading Roman imperial authority.

Section 29.6

Visigothic Iberia: Law, Kingship, and Roman Inheritance

As the Visigothic kingdom's center of gravity shifted toward Iberia after 507, its rulers governed a population overwhelmingly composed of Hispano-Roman provincials, whose social structures, landholding patterns, cities, and Christian church organization had developed over centuries of Roman rule and had already absorbed the earlier fifth-century disruptions caused by Suevic and Vandal movements through the peninsula. This chapter's treatment of Visigothic Iberia is deliberately confined to the kingdom's formative sixth-century development. The later sixth- and seventh-century development of Visigothic monarchy lies beyond this chapter's comparative focus; it is enough here to note King Reccared's personal conversion to Nicene Christianity, conventionally dated 587, and the kingdom's formal Nicene settlement at the Third Council of Toledo in 589, when the Gothic population as a body was received into the Nicene church, a development beyond this chapter's comparative scope.

Even in its earlier, formative period, however, Visigothic kingship in Iberia depended heavily on Roman legal and administrative inheritance. Older scholarship generally held that Visigothic kings issued law intended for their Gothic subjects specifically, while permitting Roman law to continue governing the kingdom's Roman population, a pattern known to historians as the personality of law, under which an individual's applicable legal code depended on ethnic or political affiliation rather than territorial residence alone. Current scholarship treats this model as considerably less settled than earlier textbooks often suggested: whether Visigothic royal law applied only to Goths, whether Roman law applied only to Romans, and whether either body of law in practice operated territorially rather than personally, remain genuinely disputed questions among historians of post-Roman law, and this chapter does not resolve them. What can be said with confidence is that the early sixth century produced the Breviary of Alaric, issued under King Alaric II shortly before his death at Vouillé, a compilation and simplification of Roman imperial law drawing on earlier Roman legal codes. The Breviary is strong evidence for the continuing vitality of Roman legal culture within the kingdom, but its precise intended constituency, whether it was meant strictly for Roman subjects or served a broader administrative purpose, is itself part of the wider scholarly debate over personal and territorial law in this period.

Hispano-Roman provincial elites, including landowning aristocrats and, increasingly, Nicene Christian bishops, remained essential to Visigothic government throughout this period, much as their counterparts did in Ostrogothic Italy and Frankish Gaul. Provincial councils, drawing on the tradition of Roman civic government and increasingly organized around the church, continued to manage local affairs in many cities, while royal authority, exercised through officials whose Latin titles descended directly from late Roman administrative vocabulary, oversaw taxation and justice at a broader level. Religious division, as in Ostrogothic Italy, complicated this arrangement, since the Visigothic royal house adhered to Arian Christianity throughout most of this period, creating a persistent gap between the Arian ruling minority and the overwhelmingly Nicene Hispano-Roman population, a division that would not be resolved until the later sixth-century conversion mentioned above.

The Visigothic case illustrates, alongside Ostrogothic Italy, that substantial Roman institutional and legal inheritance could survive the transition to a new post-Roman monarchy even when that monarchy's ruling dynasty and core military following remained ethnically and religiously distinct from the population it governed. It differs from the Ostrogothic case, however, in the absence of anything comparable to the intact Roman Senate of Italy, and in the kingdom's own internal instability, since Visigothic succession throughout this period was frequently contested and occasionally violent, a pattern of elective and often disputed kingship that would remain a persistent feature of Visigothic political life.

Section 29.7

Britain after Roman Imperial Government

Post-Roman Britain presents the sharpest contrast to the pattern of substantial institutional continuity examined thus far in this chapter, and for that reason it deserves particularly careful treatment. Roman military and administrative withdrawal from Britain had begun already in the early fifth century, well before the events narrated in Chapter 28 concerning the wider western empire, as usurping commanders removed troops from the island to pursue continental ambitions and central authority proved unable, or unwilling, to reverse the process. By around 410, a frequently cited date drawn from a later account describing an imperial response to British appeals for help, effective Roman administrative government in Britain had ended, leaving local communities to organize their own defense and governance.

What followed is considerably harder for historians to reconstruct than the roughly contemporary developments in Gaul, Italy, or Iberia, both because surviving written sources for post-Roman Britain are exceptionally scarce and because those that do survive, most notably the polemical religious tract of a British cleric writing perhaps a century after the events he describes, are difficult to use as straightforward historical narrative. Archaeology, examined further in the Map Study and Timeline Study features below, therefore carries an unusually heavy share of the evidentiary burden for this period. That archaeological evidence shows a striking and rapid decline in the material markers of Roman provincial life: coin use largely ceased, mass-produced Roman pottery disappeared from most sites within a few decades, villa estates were abandoned or repurposed, and urban centers, never as extensively developed in Britain as in Gaul or Italy, contracted sharply or were abandoned altogether.

Alongside this decline, evidence for new settlement, primarily in eastern and southern Britain, associated with groups conventionally called Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, becomes increasingly visible from the fifth century onward, through distinctive burial practices, material culture, and, eventually, place names and linguistic evidence pointing to the spread of Old English. Historians debate intensely, and this chapter does not attempt to resolve, the relative scale of actual population movement into Britain during this period as opposed to cultural and political transformation among populations already present, a debate examined further in the Historical Debate feature below in connection with the wider question of how post-Roman political identities formed. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that political authority in lowland Britain fragmented into numerous small, competing units, some led by figures bearing British names and apparently continuing, in modified form, elements of late Roman provincial leadership, others increasingly identified with the incoming Germanic-speaking groups, and that this process unfolded with far less institutional continuity, and far less surviving documentation, than the roughly contemporary consolidation of royal power in Italy, Gaul, or Iberia.

British political and military resistance to this Anglo-Saxon expansion is attested, if only faintly and through sources of uncertain reliability, including later traditions concerning a British military victory at a site called Mons Badonicus, sometime around the late fifth or early sixth century, which appears to have checked, at least temporarily, the westward spread of Anglo-Saxon political control. This chapter treats such traditions with appropriate caution, distinguishing what later medieval legend, including the much later stories associated with a figure eventually known as Arthur, built upon this episode from what contemporary or near-contemporary evidence can actually support, a distinction elaborated further in the chronology check and source-analysis exercises at the chapter's end.

Section 29.8

Anglo-Saxon Kingdom Formation

Rather than presenting the emergence of Anglo-Saxon England as a neat progression from invasion to a tidy arrangement of seven kingdoms, a later medieval simplification historians generally now avoid, this chapter emphasizes the gradual, regionally uneven, and archaeologically documented process by which small-scale political units in lowland Britain coalesced, over the fifth and sixth centuries, into a shifting patchwork of larger and more durable kingdoms. Early Anglo-Saxon political organization appears, from burial and settlement evidence, to have centered on relatively small territories controlled by local warrior leaders, whose authority rested on military success, control of agricultural resources, and the ability to reward followers, rather than on anything resembling the fiscal and administrative apparatus that, however diminished, still underpinned kingship in Italy, Gaul, or Iberia.

The relationship between these emerging Anglo-Saxon polities and the surviving British population of lowland Britain remains one of the more actively debated questions in early medieval British history, and recent scientific evidence has reshaped that debate significantly. Older historical narratives, drawing heavily on the British cleric's polemical account mentioned in the previous section, described near-total displacement or extermination of the British population by invading Anglo-Saxons, while a later generation of archaeologists, reacting against that account, tended instead toward a model of small-scale elite migration with substantial underlying population continuity. Large-scale ancient DNA studies published in the early 2020s have complicated both positions. Sampled early medieval burial populations in eastern England show substantial continental northern European ancestry, on the order of three-quarters in some sampled communities, indicating that migration across the North Sea during this period was considerably larger in scale than the small-elite-migration model proposed. At the same time, the same evidence shows pronounced regional variation, meaningful surviving local British ancestry, and admixture between newcomers and existing populations rather than uniform replacement. This chapter therefore presents neither the older extermination narrative nor a minimizing elite-migration narrative as adequate; both large-scale migration and substantial local continuity, unevenly distributed across regions, appear to be part of the historical picture, and further scientific evidence continues to refine this understanding.

By the end of the period this chapter examines, several more substantial Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, including Kent, the East Saxons, the South Saxons, and emerging polities in what would become East Anglia, Mercia, and Northumbria, had begun to take recognizable shape, though their internal organization, territorial boundaries, and relative power continued to shift considerably into the following centuries. Later chapters return to the subsequent political consolidation of England, including Chapter 33's treatment of the formation of England, France, and the German kingdom. What distinguishes this Section 29.8 account from the Ostrogothic, Visigothic, and Frankish narratives examined elsewhere in this chapter is the near total absence, in Britain, of the kind of surviving Roman civil administration, senatorial aristocracy, and functioning fiscal apparatus that shaped political development elsewhere in the former western provinces. Anglo-Saxon kingship, in its formative period, therefore developed along lines considerably more independent of direct Roman institutional inheritance than the kingdoms examined in the preceding sections of this chapter, even as elements of the surviving British Christian church, particularly in the western regions of the island less affected by Anglo-Saxon settlement, preserved their own distinct connection to the wider late antique Christian world.

Section 29.9

Frankish Gaul: Clovis and the Expansion of Frankish Rule

The Franks, a confederation of peoples settled for generations along and within the Rhine frontier, had long maintained a complex relationship with Roman Gaul, at times serving as Roman allies and frontier troops, at other times raiding or contesting Roman territory. By the later fifth century, Frankish political leadership had begun to consolidate under the Merovingian dynasty, named for a semi-legendary early ancestor, Merovech, and it was Clovis, who came to power around 481, who transformed scattered Frankish political authority into a substantial and rapidly expanding kingdom.

Clovis's first major achievement was the defeat, in 486, of Syagrius, the Roman commander who had maintained a rump directly governed Roman territory around Soissons in northern Gaul, one of the very last fragments of directly administered Roman government west of Italy. This victory brought a substantial Gallo-Roman population, along with its existing administrative and ecclesiastical structures, under Frankish rule, and it illustrates a pattern that would recur throughout Clovis's reign: Frankish expansion proceeded not by wholesale destruction of Roman provincial society but by its absorption into an expanding Frankish political framework, generally with considerable continuity in local administration, landholding, and church organization.

Clovis's conversion and baptism, traditionally dated around 496 largely on the strength of Gregory of Tours's later narrative but of genuinely disputed chronology, with some scholars arguing for a considerably later date, possibly as late as 508, proved to be a decision of exceptional long-term significance, distinguishing the Franks from the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, and other Arian-ruled kingdoms examined elsewhere in this chapter. Later Frankish tradition, recorded in particular by the sixth-century bishop and historian Gregory of Tours writing decades after the event, attributed Clovis's conversion to a vow made during a difficult battle, in which he prayed to the God of his Christian wife, Clotild, and attributed his subsequent victory to divine favor. Historians treat this narrative with appropriate caution as a later, theologically shaped account rather than a straightforward contemporary record, while recognizing that the conversion itself, whatever its precise circumstances, had immediate and lasting political consequences. By adopting the same Nicene Christianity practiced by the overwhelming majority of the Gallo-Roman population and its influential episcopate, Clovis positioned himself, in a way the Arian Visigothic and Ostrogothic kings could not, as a natural ally of the Roman Catholic church within his kingdom, a relationship of profound importance for the political and religious history examined further in Chapter 30.

Clovis's defeat of the Visigothic king Alaric II at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, discussed already in Section 29.5, eliminated the most powerful rival kingdom in Gaul and extended Frankish control across most of the region, with the notable exception of the Burgundian kingdom in the southeast, which would itself be absorbed by Clovis's successors in subsequent decades, and a residual Visigothic foothold in the extreme south. By the time of Clovis's death in 511, the Franks possessed a combination of advantages not fully matched by any of the other kingdoms examined in this chapter: recent military success, religious alignment with the Gallo-Roman Nicene episcopate, and substantial cooperation with existing Gallo-Roman aristocratic and administrative structures. Whether and how these advantages would translate into lasting power was not yet settled in 511, and depended on the specific choices, conflicts, and contingencies of the generations that followed. This chapter deliberately stops its narrative of Frankish history at this point, since the internal organization of Frankish kingship, the later territorial divisions among Clovis's heirs, the structure of Merovingian aristocracy and monasticism, and the kingdom's subsequent regional politics form the dedicated subject of Chapter 30.

Section 29.10

Roman Elites, Bishops, and New Kings

Across every kingdom examined in this chapter, a common structural pattern recurs beneath the surface of considerable regional variation: new rulers depended, to a greater or lesser degree, on the cooperation of existing Roman elites, and particularly on Roman landowning aristocrats and Christian bishops, to govern effectively. This section draws that comparative pattern together explicitly, since it helps explain both the degree of institutional continuity examined in the preceding sections and the limits of that continuity.

Roman aristocrats brought to these new kingdoms literacy, administrative experience, familiarity with Roman law, and, often, extensive local landholding and social influence that no newly arrived ruling group could easily replace. Figures such as Cassiodorus in Ostrogothic Italy illustrate the career opportunities this dependence created for educated Roman elites willing to serve new rulers, while the poet and bishop Sidonius Apollinaris, writing in Visigothic-controlled Gaul somewhat earlier in the fifth century, illustrates the more ambivalent position of a Gallo-Roman aristocrat navigating a political world in which Roman imperial authority had already receded but a fully consolidated alternative had not yet stabilized. Such correspondence and literary evidence, produced by and for a narrow, highly educated aristocratic circle, must be read with an awareness of whose perspective it represents and whose experience it necessarily leaves out, a caution developed further in the Everyday Life feature below.

Bishops occupied an increasingly central position in this transitional world, since the late Roman trend toward growing episcopal authority in civic life, discussed in Section 29.2, accelerated rather than reversed as imperial administrative structures weakened. Bishops frequently served as intermediaries between new rulers and urban populations, as guardians of church property and charitable institutions, and, in some cases, as chroniclers of their own turbulent times, most notably Gregory of Tours, whose History of the Franks, composed later in the sixth century, remains an essential, if not unproblematic, narrative source for Frankish political history under Clovis and his immediate successors. The religious alignment or division between a kingdom's ruling dynasty and its bishops, examined throughout this chapter in connection with the Arian and Nicene divide, shaped the depth and character of this cooperation considerably: Clovis's Nicene conversion gave him ready access to the political and moral authority of the Gallo-Roman episcopate in a way that Arian rulers in Italy and Iberia, however administratively sophisticated their governments, could not as easily replicate.

This pattern of elite mediation should not be mistaken for equality of power or interest between Roman elites and new royal authority. Roman aristocrats and bishops cooperated with new kings because doing so preserved their own property, status, and local influence, not necessarily out of any deep loyalty to their new rulers, and cooperation could give way to suspicion or violence when political circumstances shifted, as the execution of Boethius under Theoderic's later reign, discussed in Section 29.4, illustrates starkly. Recognizing both the genuine dependence of new rulers on Roman elite cooperation and the fragility of that cooperation helps explain why institutional continuity, even where it was substantial, remained a negotiated and sometimes unstable arrangement rather than a settled, permanent feature of any of these kingdoms.

Section 29.11

Law, Identity, and the Problem of "Roman" and "Barbarian"

This chapter has repeatedly used labels such as Goth, Frank, Saxon, Briton, and Roman, and it is necessary, before drawing this chapter's comparative conclusions, to examine those labels critically rather than treating them as self-evident descriptions of fixed, biologically continuous, and mutually exclusive populations. Older historical writing, particularly from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, often described this period through a framework of discrete peoples, each possessing a stable ethnic identity, language, and culture, migrating across the map of Europe and displacing or conquering one another in relatively straightforward fashion. Much of this older framework was shaped, directly or indirectly, by later nationalist historical traditions eager to trace modern nations back to supposedly ancient and continuous ethnic populations, a tendency this chapter treats with considerable caution.

More recent scholarship, associated particularly with research into what historians call ethnogenesis, the process by which peoples and political identities form, dissolve, and reform over time rather than persisting unchanged from some ancient origin, presents a considerably more fluid picture. Groups such as the Goths or the Franks were not fixed populations with unchanging membership. They were political and military confederations that could absorb outsiders, including Romans, and that redefined their own core traditions and even their origin stories over time. One influential school within this scholarship has proposed that such confederations coalesced around a relatively small core of tradition-bearing leadership, sometimes called a "nucleus of tradition," surrounded by a much larger and more heterogeneous following that might include Romans, other Germanic-speaking groups, and various local populations who joined for military, economic, or political reasons rather than through shared ancestry. This specific model remains actively contested, and other historians have challenged both its evidentiary basis and its assumptions about how such a "nucleus" could be identified or how much explanatory weight it can bear. What commands broader agreement across this scholarship, even among those who reject the nucleus-of-tradition model specifically, is the underlying anti-essentialist argument: early medieval ethnic and political identities were constructed, adaptable, and capable of absorbing diverse populations, rather than fixed inheritances from a stable ancient past.

Law in this period is often invoked to illustrate this complexity, though here too caution is warranted. As Section 29.6 explained in connection with Visigothic Iberia, historians have traditionally described several of these kingdoms as practicing a personality of law, under which an individual's applicable legal code depended on political or legal affiliation, Roman or Gothic, for instance, rather than on territorial residence. Current scholarship treats the actual scope and operation of this model as considerably less certain than older textbooks suggested, and whether particular kingdoms applied law personally, territorially, or through some combination of the two remains debated. What can be said with more confidence is that, over time, in most of these kingdoms, distinct legal treatment of different populations gradually gave way to more unified legal systems, a long process this textbook traces across subsequent chapters rather than resolving here.

None of this scholarly correction means that meaningful cultural, religious, and political distinctions between Roman populations and incoming ruling groups did not exist or did not matter to contemporaries. Arian and Nicene Christians genuinely understood themselves as belonging to different, and to some degree opposed, religious communities. Roman aristocrats genuinely valued a cultural and educational tradition, sometimes called Romanitas, that they distinguished from the customs of their new rulers, even while serving those rulers loyally. Gothic or Frankish military elites genuinely maintained distinct legal privileges, weapon-bearing customs, and, in some cases, distinct dress and burial practices that marked them as different from the Roman populations they now governed. The historically responsible position, and the one this chapter adopts, recognizes real and historically significant distinctions between these groups while rejecting the notion that those distinctions correspond to fixed, biologically bounded, unchanging peoples moving through history like separate chess pieces. Political identity in the post-Roman west was constructed, contested, and historically contingent, not a timeless given.

Section 29.12

Comparing the Post-Roman West

Drawing together the regional narratives examined throughout this chapter, a clear comparative pattern emerges, one that will recur, in modified form, throughout this textbook's subsequent treatment of medieval European state formation. Italy, under Theoderic, achieved the strongest governmental continuity with the Roman past among all the kingdoms examined here, preserving an intact Roman Senate, a functioning fiscal and administrative apparatus, and a deliberate royal rhetoric of restored Roman order, even as Gothic military settlement and Arian religious identity marked a persistent internal distinction between rulers and ruled.

Gaul, under the early Frankish kings and particularly under Clovis, combined strong Roman social and ecclesiastical continuity, especially through the cooperation of the Gallo-Roman aristocracy and, crucially, the Nicene episcopate, with an increasingly powerful and expansionist Frankish kingship whose legitimacy rested more heavily on military success and religious alignment than on any elaborate claim to restored Roman administrative order of the kind Theoderic cultivated in Italy. Iberia, as Visigothic political power relocated there after 507, developed an evolving monarchy that worked through substantial Hispano-Roman legal and social inheritance, formalized in compilations such as the Breviary of Alaric, even as persistent religious division between an Arian ruling minority and a Nicene majority, together with recurring succession disputes, gave the kingdom a more unsettled character than either Ostrogothic Italy or early Frankish Gaul. Britain, finally, experienced by far the deepest institutional fragmentation of any region examined in this chapter, with Roman civil administration, fiscal structures, and urban life contracting so severely, and so early, that the emerging Anglo-Saxon kingdoms developed political structures considerably more independent of direct Roman institutional inheritance than their continental counterparts, even as elements of British Christianity and, in places, British political leadership persisted alongside this new political formation.

This comparative picture yields the chapter's central conclusion. The end of western Roman imperial government did not produce a single uniform outcome across the former western provinces. It produced a range of distinct political experiments, each shaped by the specific balance, in that region, between surviving Roman institutions and the military, religious, and customary traditions of new ruling groups, and each shaped by the specific individuals, alliances, and conflicts that determined how that balance was struck. None of these outcomes was predetermined. Ostrogothic Italy's apparently successful synthesis of Gothic military power and Roman civil government did not prove durable, as later chapters covering the Byzantine reconquest will show. The Visigothic kingdom, having lost most of Gaul in 507, would continue to develop for nearly two more centuries in Iberia before facing its own eventual crisis. Anglo-Saxon Britain's fragmented early kingdoms would gradually consolidate, over subsequent centuries, into fewer and larger political units. The Frankish kingdom, built by Clovis on a foundation of military success, Gallo-Roman cooperation, and Nicene religious alignment, possessed, at his death in 511, a distinctive combination of advantages among the kingdoms examined in this chapter. What became of those advantages, and how the Frankish world subsequently developed, is a question this chapter leaves open, to be answered by the more detailed treatment of Frankish kingship, aristocracy, and society that follows in Chapter 30.

Historical Vocabulary
Foederati and Hospitalitas

Foederati refers, in late Roman usage, to allied peoples settled within or alongside Roman territory under treaty arrangements, providing military service in exchange for land, subsidies, or recognized status. Hospitalitas, sometimes translated as "hospitality" or "billeting," describes the legal and practical mechanisms, debated in some detail by modern historians, through which such allied groups, including the Visigoths settled in Aquitaine in 418 discussed in Section 29.5, received access to land or its revenues, whether through direct land grants, a share of tax revenue, or the requisitioning of a portion of existing estates. Historians disagree about exactly how these arrangements worked in practice, and this uncertainty matters because it shapes how continuous or disruptive scholars judge the settlement of groups such as the Visigoths to have been for existing Roman landholding society.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnogenesis

Ethnogenesis refers to the historical process by which a people's collective identity forms, changes, and is transmitted over time, rather than existing as a fixed, unchanging inheritance from a distant origin. One influential school of scholarship associated with this approach has argued that groups such as the Goths or the Franks coalesced around a relatively small core of leaders and traditions, a "nucleus of tradition," that could attract and absorb a much larger and more diverse following, including Romans and members of other groups, over the course of migration and settlement. This specific model remains contested among historians, who debate its evidentiary support, while the broader claim that early medieval ethnic identities were constructed and adaptable, rather than fixed, commands wider acceptance. Section 29.11 develops this concept, and its ongoing debates, at greater length.

Primary Source Window

Cassiodorus, the Variae, Early Sixth Century

Creator: Flavius Magnus Aurelius Cassiodorus, Roman aristocrat and senior civil servant under Theoderic and his immediate successors · Date: letters composed across Cassiodorus's official career, collected and published around 537 · Type: A collection of official royal correspondence, edicts, and administrative documents drafted by Cassiodorus on behalf of the Ostrogothic kings he served

Context: Cassiodorus served Theoderic and his successors in a series of senior civil offices, including quaestor, the official responsible for drafting royal correspondence in polished Latin, and later praetorian prefect. The Variae collects several hundred of these official letters, addressing subjects ranging from tax disputes and building projects to diplomatic correspondence and appointments, and offering historians an unusually direct window into the daily operation of Ostrogothic government.

Paraphrase of themes recurring across the Variae Across many of these letters, Cassiodorus presents Theoderic's government in the traditional language of Roman imperial administration, describing the king's concern for justice, the maintenance of public buildings and infrastructure, the fair treatment of both Goths and Romans before the law, and the restoration of order and prosperity to a Roman Italy that, in this presentation, had suffered under less legitimate rule before Theoderic's arrival. This is a synthesis of recurring themes across many letters rather than a translation of any single passage.

Analysis: The Variae is invaluable evidence for the institutional continuity examined in Sections 29.3 and 29.4, since it shows Roman administrative language, Roman legal reasoning, and Roman civic values being actively employed in the service of a Gothic king. At the same time, students should recognize that these letters were composed by a Roman aristocrat with every professional and personal incentive to present his royal employer favorably, and that official correspondence of this kind, however administratively informative, cannot by itself reveal how ordinary Goths or Romans outside the court actually experienced Ostrogothic rule.

Historical Debate

Were the Successor Kingdoms "Barbarian Kingdoms"?

The question: Should historians describe the political formations examined in this chapter primarily as barbarian kingdoms, conquest states imposed on Roman populations by ethnically distinct outside invaders, or primarily as new political formations arising within a continuing, if transformed, late Roman world, in which incoming military leaders and Roman elites jointly constructed new forms of government?

Interpretation A — Emphasis on conquest, rupture, and ethnic distinction: Some historians argue that the arrival and settlement of groups such as the Goths and Franks represented a genuine and significant rupture in western Roman history, driven by population movement, military conquest, and the imposition of new ruling elites over subject Roman populations. This interpretation emphasizes the real violence, displacement, and loss of political autonomy that accompanied these transitions, and cautions against allowing an emphasis on institutional continuity to soften or obscure that Roman populations across the former western provinces had, in a very real sense, lost self-government to conquering outsiders.
Interpretation B — Emphasis on continuity, negotiation, and shared construction: Other historians, drawing on the scholarship of ethnogenesis discussed in Section 29.11, argue that this framework overstates the foreignness and unity of the incoming groups and understates the degree to which Roman elites actively participated in constructing the new political order, often to preserve their own property and status. This interpretation emphasizes negotiated settlement, extensive institutional continuity, particularly in Italy and, to a lesser degree, Gaul and Iberia, and cautions against treating "Roman" and "barbarian" as two fixed, opposed, and internally uniform populations rather than overlapping and evolving political identities.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying evidence, including law codes, administrative correspondence such as the Variae, archaeological settlement patterns, and narrative sources such as Gregory of Tours, but differ in how much weight to assign to conquest and rupture as opposed to negotiation and continuity, and the balance struck often depends heavily on which specific kingdom, Italy, Gaul, Iberia, or Britain, a historian is examining most closely.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes the language used throughout the remainder of this textbook to describe early medieval political change, and cautions against assuming that either a purely triumphalist narrative of Roman institutional survival or a purely catastrophist narrative of barbarian conquest accurately captures the varied regional experiences examined in this chapter.

Student task: Using evidence from at least two of the four kingdoms examined in this chapter, identify support for each interpretation, then explain whether you think a single interpretation should apply uniformly across Italy, Gaul, Iberia, and Britain, or whether the appropriate interpretation should vary by region.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Roman Government under Kings

What Changed

  • Ultimate political and military authority passed from Roman emperors and their appointed officials to new royal dynasties, whose legitimacy rested on conquest, negotiated settlement, and dynastic succession rather than imperial appointment
  • Religious division between Arian ruling groups and Nicene Roman populations introduced a new axis of political and social tension largely absent from earlier Roman provincial government
  • In Britain especially, Roman civil administration, taxation, coinage, and urban life contracted dramatically, producing a much more thoroughgoing institutional discontinuity than elsewhere in the former western provinces
  • Political identity increasingly came to be expressed through categories such as Goth, Frank, or Roman, categories that carried legal as well as cultural significance under legal arrangements, debated in their precise scope, that historians have traditionally described as a personality of law

What Stayed the Same

  • In Italy, Gaul, and Iberia, Roman legal tradition continued to shape government and, for at least part of the population in most kingdoms, continued to apply directly, often through explicit compilations such as the Breviary of Alaric, though the precise scope of that application remains debated
  • Roman landholding aristocracies retained substantial estates, wealth, and local social influence in most regions, even as their political relationship to central authority changed
  • Christian bishops continued, and in many places expanded, their civic and religious authority, serving as crucial intermediaries between new rulers and urban populations
  • Latin remained the primary language of administration, law, and the church across every kingdom examined in this chapter except, in modified form, in parts of Britain

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both substantial continuity and genuine change prevents two opposite errors: assuming that "Rome" simply continued under new management everywhere, or assuming that the end of imperial government erased Roman institutions and populations wherever new kings took power.

Cause and Effect

Why Institutional Survival Varied by Region

Long-term causes

The depth of prior interaction between a given incoming ruling group and the Roman world, the strength and density of existing Roman administrative and urban infrastructure in a given region, and the degree to which a region's fiscal system remained functional into the late fifth century.

Short-term causes

The specific circumstances of conquest or settlement, including whether a new ruler actively sought Roman aristocratic cooperation, as Theoderic and Clovis both did in different ways, or whether existing Roman administration had already collapsed before a new political authority could absorb or replace it, as occurred in much of Britain.

Effect

Italy and, to a lesser extent, Gaul and Iberia retained substantial Roman civil, legal, and ecclesiastical institutions under new royal authority, while Britain developed political structures considerably more independent of direct Roman institutional inheritance.

This chapter's caution

Institutional survival was not simply a matter of how "civilized" or "barbaric" a given ruling group supposedly was; it depended on specific historical circumstances, including the prior strength of Roman infrastructure and the deliberate choices new rulers made about whether and how to preserve it.

Historical complexity: Even within a single kingdom, institutional continuity was rarely uniform. Ostrogothic Italy preserved the Senate and civil administration while maintaining sharp religious and legal distinctions between Goths and Romans, showing that continuity and rupture could coexist within the same political system.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life amid Political Transformation

For most inhabitants of the former western Roman provinces, the political changes examined throughout this chapter unfolded gradually and unevenly rather than as a single dramatic rupture experienced uniformly across society. A tenant farmer working an estate in Gaul or Italy likely continued to owe rent or labor obligations to the same landowning family, or its heirs, regardless of whether that region's ultimate political authority now rested with a Roman-appointed official, a Gothic king, or a Frankish ruler. Urban craftsmen and merchants in Italian cities such as Ravenna or Rome likely continued their trades much as before, even as the coinage they used now circulated under a Gothic king's name, since Ostrogothic coinage deliberately preserved pseudo-imperial conventions, at times combining imperial imagery with royal monograms rather than replacing Roman coin design outright. Village communities in Iberia continued to bring disputes before local courts operating, in most cases, according to familiar Roman legal principles, even as the ultimate royal authority behind those courts had changed.

At the same time, the scale of disruption varied considerably by region and by social position. In post-Roman Britain, discussed in Sections 29.7 and 29.8, the collapse of Roman coinage, mass-produced goods, and villa-based estate agriculture meant that ordinary economic and material life changed far more dramatically than in Italy or Gaul, where market networks, coinage, and estate structures survived with considerably greater continuity. Enslaved laborers and dependent tenant farmers across all these kingdoms, whose labor sustained the landholding aristocracies discussed throughout this chapter, experienced these political transitions primarily through continuity in their own subordinate status, regardless of which ruling group ultimately controlled the estates on which they labored. Recognizing these differing experiences helps ensure that this chapter's necessarily aristocratic and administrative sources, including royal correspondence and episcopal chronicles, do not stand in, unexamined, for the experience of the broader population.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the fragmentation of western Roman imperial government, traced in Chapter 28, gave rise not to a single uniform "barbarian Europe" but to a range of distinct post-Roman kingdoms, each combining inherited Roman institutions, Roman social structures, and the military, religious, and customary traditions of new ruling groups in different proportions. Rather than telling four disconnected national stories, this chapter has organized its account around a comparative question: how did rulers who emerged from imperial fragmentation convert military power into durable government, and what did they preserve, alter, or abandon from the Roman world they now governed?

Ostrogothic Italy, under Theoderic, offered the clearest example of sustained governmental continuity, preserving the Roman Senate, civil administration, and legal tradition even as Gothic military settlement and Arian religious identity marked a persistent distinction between rulers and ruled, a distinction that contributed to the instability and violence that marked the final years of Theoderic's reign. Visigothic power, originally centered on Toulouse in southwestern Gaul, shifted decisively toward Iberia after the kingdom's defeat by Clovis at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, where it developed an evolving monarchy resting heavily on Hispano-Roman legal and social inheritance, formalized in compilations such as the Breviary of Alaric, even as religious division and recurring succession disputes gave the kingdom a more unsettled character than Ostrogothic Italy.

Britain presented the sharpest contrast among the cases examined in this chapter. Roman administrative withdrawal, beginning already in the early fifth century, proceeded further and faster there than anywhere else in the former western provinces, and the small, competing Anglo-Saxon and British political communities that gradually emerged developed with far less institutional continuity, and far less surviving documentation, than their continental counterparts. The Franks, by contrast, built under Clovis a rapidly expanding kingdom that combined substantial Gallo-Roman social and ecclesiastical continuity with a distinctly Frankish and increasingly dominant royal authority, a combination made especially effective by Clovis's conversion to Nicene Christianity, which aligned him with the Gallo-Roman episcopate in a way Arian rulers elsewhere could not easily match.

Throughout every regional case, this chapter has emphasized the essential mediating role played by Roman aristocrats and bishops, whose cooperation, however conditional and sometimes fragile, allowed new rulers to govern populations, institutions, and legal traditions considerably more complex than their own military organizations could otherwise have administered. The chapter has also insisted on a critical historiographical point: the ethnic and political labels this period's history requires, Goth, Frank, Saxon, Roman, and others, should not be mistaken for fixed, biologically bounded, unchanging populations. Scholarship on ethnogenesis has shown that these were constructed and evolving political identities capable of absorbing diverse populations, including Romans, rather than stable peoples moving through history unchanged; historians continue to debate the specific mechanisms through which such identities formed.

Comparing these four regional trajectories yields the chapter's central conclusion: institutional continuity with the Roman past varied enormously across the post-Roman west, shaped by the depth of prior interaction between incoming rulers and Roman society, the strength of existing Roman infrastructure in a given region, and the specific choices made by particular rulers and elites. None of these outcomes was inevitable, and none of these kingdoms' futures was yet settled by the point at which this chapter's narrative closes. Among them, the Frankish kingdom built by Clovis possessed a distinctive combination of advantages, whose consequences for the subsequent political history of western Europe this textbook now examines directly.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Successor kingdom
A modern historical term for the new political formations that replaced western Roman imperial government in a given region, built from varying combinations of Roman and non-Roman institutions.
Foederati
Allied peoples settled within or alongside Roman territory under treaty arrangements, providing military service in exchange for land, subsidies, or recognized status.
Hospitalitas
The debated legal and practical mechanisms through which settled allied groups received access to land or its revenues within Roman territory.
Arianism (Homoian Christianity)
A traditional label, after the fourth-century theologian Arius, for the non-Nicene Christianity professed by several post-Roman ruling groups including the Ostrogoths and Visigoths; modern scholarship identifies the specific creed involved more precisely as Homoian Christianity, distinct from mainstream Nicene Christianity.
Nicene Christianity
The mainstream Christian doctrine affirmed at the Council of Nicaea in 325, held by the overwhelming majority of the late Roman population and, notably, adopted by Clovis (traditionally dated c. 496, though the exact date is disputed).
Ethnogenesis
The historical process by which peoples and political identities form, change, and are transmitted over time, rather than persisting unchanged from a fixed ancient origin.
Gens
A Latin term, often translated as "people" or "nation," used in this period to describe a political and military community such as the Goths or the Franks, understood historically as a constructed rather than fixed category.
Romanitas
A cultural and educational tradition associated with Roman identity, valued and cultivated by Roman aristocrats even while they served non-Roman rulers.
Curia
A late Roman municipal council composed of local landowning notables, traditionally responsible for local civic administration and tax collection.
Comes
A Latin administrative and military title, often translated as "count," used across several post-Roman kingdoms for royal officials with regional authority.
Dux
A Latin military title, often translated as "duke," originally denoting a Roman military commander and later adapted by post-Roman kingdoms for regional military leaders.
Municipium
A self-governing Roman city with its own local council and administrative privileges, a category of urban organization inherited, with varying continuity, by the post-Roman kingdoms.
Villa
A large Roman agricultural estate, typically worked by tenant farmers and enslaved laborers, forming the economic foundation of the late Roman and post-Roman landholding aristocracy.
Colonus
A tenant farmer cultivating land on a Roman or post-Roman estate; some coloni were free tenants, while others, sometimes called adscripticii, were legally bound to the land they worked, a status that varied by period and region.
Wergild
A customary payment, prescribed by law among several Germanic-speaking peoples, owed as compensation for injury or death, varying according to the status of the victim.
Personality of law
A model, traditionally used to describe several post-Roman kingdoms, under which an individual's applicable law depends on ethnic, political, or communal affiliation rather than territorial residence; the actual scope and operation of this model in specific kingdoms remains debated among historians.
Territoriality of law
A legal system under which law applies uniformly to all inhabitants of a given territory regardless of ethnic or communal affiliation, a pattern that gradually developed in most post-Roman kingdoms over time.
Episcopate
The collective body of bishops within the Christian church, whose civic and religious authority expanded significantly during the period examined in this chapter.
Senate (late Roman)
The assembly of Rome's senatorial aristocracy, preserved as an institution under Ostrogothic rule in Italy, though its political power had already diminished considerably from earlier periods.
Praetorian Prefecture
A senior late Roman administrative office responsible for finance and general provincial government, retained as an institution in Ostrogothic Italy and staffed by Roman officials such as Cassiodorus.
Barbarian (as historical category)
A Roman term for peoples outside the empire's cultural and political boundaries, used historically with connotations of foreignness that modern scholarship treats critically rather than as a neutral descriptive category.
Migration Period
A modern historical term for the era, roughly the fourth through sixth centuries, characterized by the movement and settlement of various peoples across and within the Roman world.
Gallo-Roman
A term describing the Romanized population and culture of Gaul, whose landholding aristocracy and church institutions remained central to political life under both Visigothic and Frankish rule.
Hispano-Roman
A term describing the Romanized population and culture of Iberia, whose social and legal structures shaped the development of the Visigothic monarchy.
Breviary of Alaric
A compilation and simplification of Roman imperial law, issued under the Visigothic king Alaric II in the early sixth century; its precise intended constituency, and its relationship to the broader debate over personal versus territorial law, remains disputed among historians.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it answers a question Chapter 28 deliberately left open: once western Roman imperial government had fragmented, what actually replaced it? The answer this chapter has developed is neither a single new empire nor a simple collection of foreign conquest states, but a comparative range of new political societies, each combining Roman inheritance and new leadership in its own distinctive proportion, shaped by specific regional circumstances, specific rulers, and specific choices made by both incoming leaders and existing Roman elites.

The larger processes this chapter reveals will recur throughout the remainder of Part V and, in modified form, throughout this textbook's treatment of medieval state formation more broadly. The negotiation between new rulers and inherited institutions, the mediating role of aristocrats and bishops, the significance of religious alignment or division, and the constructed, contested nature of political identity are themes that this textbook will meet again in the formation of England, France, and the German kingdom in Chapter 33, in the Christianization of Scandinavia and central Europe in Chapter 35, and, at a larger scale, throughout the entire history of European state formation examined across later Parts.

This chapter also connects different regions of Europe by showing that a single underlying transformation, the end of unified western Roman government, produced genuinely different outcomes in Italy, Gaul, Iberia, and Britain, a reminder against treating any one region's experience, whether the strong continuity of Italy or the sharp discontinuity of Britain, as representative of the whole. It contributes to Volume II's larger argument by establishing the political foundations, Ostrogothic, Visigothic, Anglo-Saxon, and above all Frankish, on which the more fully developed medieval societies of Latin Christendom, examined throughout the remainder of Part V, would be built.

Chapter 30 now turns from this comparative survey to a sustained, focused examination of the Frankish world, the successor kingdom that emerged from this chapter's narrative as the most durable and consequential of the four, tracing Merovingian kingship, aristocracy, Christianity, regional power, monasteries, and relations with neighboring societies in the depth this chapter's comparative structure did not permit. Understanding how Clovis built the foundation examined here is the necessary starting point for understanding what his successors, and the Frankish world more broadly, would go on to build.
