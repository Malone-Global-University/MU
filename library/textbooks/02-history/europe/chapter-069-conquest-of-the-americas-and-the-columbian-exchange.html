Conquest of the Americas and the Columbian Exchange

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 1492 and the end of the sixteenth century, European conquest, Indigenous resistance, and the movement of organisms across the Atlantic remade the Americas and altered the histories of Europe, Africa, and Asia. The conquest was neither a single event nor an inevitable European victory, and conquest did not erase the peoples it attempted to rule.

In 1514, royal officials on the island of Hispaniola carried out a distribution of Indigenous people among Spanish settlers, recording how many remained in each district and which colonist would receive their labor. The repartimiento record, associated with the official Rodrigo de Alburquerque, listed roughly twenty six thousand Indigenous people assigned within the colonial labor system; it was not a complete census of the island's population. The island had been home to a Taíno population that Spanish observers, and later historians, estimated at anywhere from tens of thousands to well over a million, and the survivors recorded in 1514 had already endured two decades of warfare, tribute demands, forced labor, enslavement, famine, and introduced disease. The document is a bureaucratic record, drawn up by the colonizers for the purposes of colonial administration, and it is one of the few quantitative traces from the earliest stage of the Spanish conquest. It records a catastrophe in the language of administrative convenience.

Seven years later, in August 1521, the island city of Tenochtitlan, capital of the Mexica and one of the largest cities in the world, fell after a siege fought by an army that was overwhelmingly Indigenous and that included many thousands of warriors from Tlaxcala, Texcoco, and other communities with grievances against Mexica rule. Eleven years after that, in November 1532, Spanish forces led by Francisco Pizarro seized the Inca ruler Atahualpa at Cajamarca in the northern Andes. Popular memory has often compressed these episodes into a single story in which a handful of Spaniards defeated vast empires through courage, steel, horses, and germs. The scholarship of recent decades has substantially complicated that story. The conquest of the Americas was made up of many different campaigns, fought in different environments against societies with different political structures, and its outcomes depended on Indigenous alliances, rivalries, and strategies at least as much as on European weapons.

This chapter argues that the conquest of the Americas depended on the interaction of several forces. Indigenous political divisions and the decisions of Indigenous leaders created openings that Spanish commanders exploited and, in many cases, depended on. European maritime capacity, firearms, steel, horses, and above all the ability to draw reinforcements and supplies from a Caribbean base gave the invaders advantages in particular settings. Introduced diseases killed on a scale that no contemporary society had experienced, and they did so unevenly, interacting with warfare, hunger, forced labor, and the disruption of food systems. Legal and religious institutions, from the encomienda to the mission, converted episodes of plunder into durable systems of rule. None of these forces acted alone, and none operated identically in the Caribbean, Mexico, the Andes, the Chilean frontier, or coastal Brazil.

The chapter also argues that conquest did not mean disappearance. Nahua communities continued to govern themselves through their own municipal institutions, Andean communities preserved ayllus and reorganized obligations of labor, Maya polities held out for generations or negotiated their own terms, and the Mapuche of southern Chile fought Spanish armies for centuries. Indigenous elites wrote petitions, litigated in colonial courts, became Christians on their own terms, and composed some of the most important historical sources discussed in this chapter. Colonial societies were built on violence, but they were also built by negotiation, and Indigenous people were historical actors in every stage of that process.

The chapter draws on an unusually varied body of evidence. Spanish sources include the letters Hernán Cortés addressed to Charles V, the chronicle of Bernal Díaz del Castillo, royal decrees, legal treatises, tribute registers, and the vast administrative records preserved in the Archivo General de Indias in Seville. Indigenous sources include the Nahuatl accounts compiled in the Florentine Codex, pictorial histories such as the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, Maya annals, and the illustrated letter of Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala from the Andes. Archaeologists, paleopathologists, and ecologists add evidence of settlement patterns, burials, pollen, and animal remains. Each category has its own purposes and limits, and a recurring task of the chapter is to ask whose account survives and why.

Chapter 68 examined how Portuguese and Spanish voyages created the maritime connections that made colonial conquest possible. Chapter 69 follows those connections into the Americas and examines what Europeans did, and what Indigenous peoples did in response, once the voyages had become settlements. It also begins to trace the movement of crops, animals, pathogens, and people that transformed both sides of the Atlantic. Chapter 70 will examine the Atlantic slave trade and plantation economies, Chapters 71 and 72 will follow Portuguese, Dutch, British, and French expansion, and Chapter 73 will analyze how overseas empire changed European societies. The present chapter provides the foundation on which those later chapters build, and it returns repeatedly to a theme central to Volume III: the way in which dynastic states, religious institutions, and commercial networks drove Europe outward while the consequences returned to reshape Europe itself.

Students should also note the difficulty of language. The Spanish term indio was a colonial category that lumped together peoples as different as the Taíno, Mexica, Maya, Inca, and Mapuche, and this textbook uses "Indigenous" and specific names wherever possible. The term "conquest" itself is a convenient label for a process that was often incomplete, negotiated, and contested, and the chapter will repeatedly note where Spanish claims to have conquered a territory outran the reality of their control.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the transition from Atlantic encounter to colonial conquest in the Caribbean and explain how Caribbean institutions and practices shaped later mainland expeditions.
  2. Explain why the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 cannot be understood as a small Spanish force simply defeating a larger Indigenous empire, with specific attention to Indigenous alliances and political divisions.
  3. Analyze the political circumstances of the Inca Empire, including the Huáscar–Atahualpa conflict, and explain how Spanish forces captured Atahualpa and why organized Andean resistance continued until 1572.
  4. Compare conquest patterns in the Caribbean, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Central America, northern New Spain, Chile, and Portuguese Brazil, and explain why outcomes differed.
  5. Describe the principal institutions of Spanish colonial rule, including the Council of the Indies, audiencias, viceroyalties, encomienda, repartimiento, and mita, and the limits of metropolitan authority.
  6. Assess the legal, theological, and moral debates over the legitimacy of conquest, including the Requerimiento, Las Casas, Sepúlveda, and the New Laws of 1542.
  7. Evaluate the roles of introduced disease, warfare, forced labor, hunger, and displacement in the demographic collapse of Indigenous populations, and explain why estimates remain uncertain.
  8. Distinguish the introduction of American and Afro-Eurasian organisms from their widespread adoption, and trace the Columbian Exchange across Europe, Africa, and Asia.
  9. Interpret Spanish, Indigenous, and mixed ancestry sources critically, identifying the purposes, perspectives, and limits of each.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the success of European conquest campaigns and articulate why single cause explanations are inadequate.
  11. Connect the conquest and early colonial order to developments in Habsburg Spain, European finance, religion, and commerce, and to the later Atlantic systems examined in Chapters 70 to 73.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a pattern of settlement, tribute, and forced labor develop in the Caribbean, and why did it become a template for later conquests?
  2. Why did Indigenous political divisions and alliances matter so much in the conquest of Mexico, and how should historians describe the motives of Tlaxcalans, Totonacs, and other allies?
  3. In what ways did the Andean conquest differ from the Mesoamerican, and why did Inca resistance continue long after the capture of Atahualpa?
  4. What evidence do historians use to reconstruct the conquest, and how do Spanish conquest narratives, Indigenous accounts, and later retrospective sources differ in perspective and reliability?
  5. How did legal and religious arguments justify, constrain, or fail to constrain conquest and forced labor, and what did the Crown gain from allowing such debates?
  6. Why is it historically inadequate to attribute Indigenous population collapse to epidemic disease alone, and how do historians estimate losses when sources are fragmentary?
  7. How did the Columbian Exchange change agriculture, diet, landscapes, and economies in the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia, and why did adoption of new species proceed at different speeds?
  8. Which Indigenous institutions, languages, and religious practices survived the sixteenth century, and how did Indigenous communities reshape colonial society?

Historical Context

The Spanish expeditions that crossed the Atlantic after 1492 set out from kingdoms shaped by centuries of frontier warfare and settlement within the Iberian Peninsula. The Castilian conquest of Granada, completed in January 1492, brought to a close the long process often described as the Reconquista, discussed in Chapter 47 in connection with the crusading movement and Iberian expansion. That process had produced practices that conquerors carried to the Caribbean: royal licenses for private expeditions, grants of authority over conquered populations to military leaders, the founding of municipalities as instruments of legal authority, and an expectation that military service should be rewarded with land, labor, and tribute. The conquest of the Canary Islands, begun in the fifteenth century and finished by Castile in 1496, provided a more recent Atlantic precedent, including the enslavement of Indigenous Guanche communities, experiments with sugar, and contests between Castile and Portugal.

Chapter 68 examined how Portuguese voyages along the African coast, Atlantic islands, and navigation techniques made Columbus's crossing possible, and how the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 divided the claims of Castile and Portugal along a line in the Atlantic. Papal bulls issued by Alexander VI in 1493 granted the Castilian Crown rights in the lands it discovered, on the understanding that its rulers would support the conversion of the peoples they found. These documents were later invoked as the legal foundation of the Spanish presence, though they were never accepted by other European monarchs as limiting their own activities, a point that matters for Chapter 72. Spain in these decades was a dynastic composite of Castile, Aragon, and other territories. After 1516 the Crown passed to Charles, a Habsburg who in 1519 also became Holy Roman Emperor as Charles V, so that colonial conquest took place within a monarchy whose European commitments and financial needs, examined in Chapter 75, strongly influenced colonial policy.

The Americas into which Europeans arrived were not an empty or undifferentiated continent. Their populations spoke hundreds of languages and lived in societies ranging from small mobile bands and dispersed agricultural villages to densely populated city states and large tributary empires. The Caribbean was home to Taíno and other Arawakan speaking communities, as well as to peoples whom Europeans called Caribs. Mesoamerica contained the Mexica and their allies of the Triple Alliance, rivals such as Tlaxcala, the Tarascan state of western Mexico, and dozens of Maya polities. The Andes were ruled by the Inca state, which had expanded over roughly the previous century. Elsewhere, from the Muisca of the Colombian highlands to the Mapuche of southern Chile and the Tupi speaking communities of coastal Brazil, societies possessed their own political systems, agricultural economies, religious institutions, and histories of conflict. Much of this diversity is recoverable only through a combination of archaeology, Indigenous writing and oral tradition recorded after conquest, and the biased but valuable accounts of Europeans.

Students must also be aware of chronological conventions. Dates in this chapter follow the Julian calendar, which was in use in Spain and its colonies until the Gregorian reform of 1582, so that the date of the fall of Tenochtitlan, 13 August 1521, is a Julian date. Indigenous societies used their own calendars, and the Mexica recorded years and ritual cycles in a calendar system that was correlated with the Christian calendar only after the conquest, with some uncertainty. Spanish regnal numbers also require care: the ruler who authorized the New Laws was Charles I of Castile and Aragon, who is known as Charles V in his capacity as emperor, and this textbook uses Charles V for convenience while identifying his Spanish titles where they matter.

The evidence for the conquest is dominated by conquerors' narratives. Cortés wrote his letters to justify his own insubordination, and many other Spanish accounts were composed as petitions asking the Crown for rewards on the strength of services rendered. Chroniclers such as Francisco López de Gómara wrote about events they never witnessed, and Bernal Díaz, who participated in the Mexican campaign, wrote his history decades later. Indigenous testimony exists, but nearly all of it was recorded after the conquest, in alphabetic script, often under the direction of friars, and it bears the traces of the circumstances in which it was produced. Historians therefore read these sources against one another, searching for corroboration, noting silence, and trying to distinguish documented events from the narrative frames that later writers imposed.

Division I · From Atlantic Encounter to Caribbean Conquest · Section 69.1

From Encounter to Conquest, 1492–1502

Chapter 68 traced the voyages that carried Columbus across the Atlantic. This section begins where that narrative leaves off, with the decision to convert a handful of landfalls into permanent colonial possessions. At the end of the first voyage Columbus left a garrison of thirty nine men at La Navidad on the northern coast of Hispaniola. When he returned in 1493 with a fleet of seventeen ships carrying well over a thousand men, together with horses, cattle, pigs, seeds, and tools, the character of the enterprise had changed. The expedition was no longer a reconnaissance that might discover a route to Asian markets. It was a colonizing project backed by the Castilian Crown, and its principal immediate source of wealth was not the spices Columbus had promised but gold, which Indigenous communities worked in modest quantities, and the labor of the people who lived on the island.

The Portuguese model of fortified trading posts, which had worked along the African coast by exchanging goods with existing commercial networks, did not translate to the Caribbean. Communities of the Greater Antilles had little to offer Europeans in the quantities Columbus needed, and there was no large scale existing commerce to attach to. The colonists therefore turned to extraction through domination. Spanish leaders demanded food, labor, and gold, seized women and captives, and answered resistance with force. In 1494 and 1495 Columbus shipped Indigenous captives to Spain to be sold, an action that the Crown, and Isabella in particular, regarded with ambivalence, since Indigenous people were nominally the Crown's subjects. In 1495 a tribute system required adult residents of the gold producing districts to deliver a fixed quantity of gold dust, or in other districts cotton, at regular intervals.

Royal authority in these years was uncertain and personal. Columbus held extensive privileges under the agreements reached at Santa Fe in 1492, including titles and a share of revenues, and he governed the colony poorly. Colonists faced disease, hunger, and disappointment. In 1497 and 1498 the Spanish alcalde Francisco Roldán led a rebellion against the Columbus brothers, and in 1500 the Crown sent Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrested Columbus and sent him to Spain. The Crown thus concluded that the enterprise could not remain a private monopoly of the Admiral and his family, and it began to assert direct royal control over the Indies, a development that anticipated the growth of colonial administration discussed in section 69.25.

By 1502, when Nicolás de Ovando arrived as governor, the outlines of colonial domination were visible. Spanish authority rested on garrisons, tribute demands, and military campaigns against caciques who refused to cooperate. Colonists regarded labor and land as rewards for service. Royal officials gradually accepted that Indigenous people should be organized into labor drafts. The Crown retained theoretical protective responsibilities, and Isabella's will of 1504 expressed concern for the welfare of Indigenous subjects, but the practice on the ground favored colonists. The first decade thus created a model in which royal sponsorship, private armed enterprise, a claim to evangelize, and the expectation of tribute combined to produce colonial rule.

Section 69.2

Taíno Societies and the First Spanish Settlements

The people whom Columbus met in the Greater Antilles are known as the Taíno, a name that encompasses Arawakan speaking communities of Hispaniola, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and parts of the Bahamas. The word itself became a general label only in later centuries, and the people did not necessarily use it for themselves. Archaeological evidence, linguistic analysis, and the writings of early Spanish observers show a society organized into chiefdoms, called cacicazgos, each led by a cacique and sometimes a female cacica. Hispaniola contained several large cacicazgos, often listed by Spanish writers as Marién, Maguá, Maguana, Jaragua, and Higüey, and each had its own territory, leadership, and alliances. The island was not politically unified, which both complicated and enabled Spanish strategy.

Taíno agriculture centered on cassava, a root crop planted in mounds called conucos, supplemented by maize, sweet potatoes, beans, squash, peppers, fruit trees, and fishing. Communities produced cotton textiles, worked wood and stone, and traded goods among islands. A noble class, whose members included the caciques, held status through lineage, and commoners provided labor. Religion centered on spirits and ancestral objects called zemís, carved and venerated, and on ritual practices including a ball game, communal dances called areítos, and ceremonial use of hallucinogenic snuff. The Hieronymite friar Ramón Pané, who lived among Taíno communities in the 1490s at Columbus's request, wrote the earliest European account of an Indigenous American religion. His work survives only in later versions, and it reflects the limitations of his understanding, yet it remains a valuable source.

The first Spanish settlements show how fragile the initial colonial enterprise was. La Navidad, built partly from timbers of the wrecked flagship in December 1492, had been destroyed by the time Columbus returned in November 1493. Accounts differ on how the garrison perished, though they point to conflict arising from seizures of food and women and to the involvement of Indigenous leaders including Caonabó. In January 1494 Columbus founded La Isabela on the island's northern coast, in a poorly chosen location where disease and hunger afflicted the colonists. The settlement was largely abandoned after the founding of Santo Domingo on the southern coast in 1496 by Columbus's brother Bartholomew, which Ovando relocated in 1502 to the western bank of the Ozama River after a hurricane. Santo Domingo became the administrative center of the Spanish Caribbean and the oldest continuously occupied European city in the Americas.

Indigenous responses varied. Some caciques, notably Guacanagarí, who had hosted the first garrison, tried to use the newcomers as allies or to maintain accommodation. Others, including Caonabó of Maguana and Behechio of Jaragua, resisted or avoided the Spaniards. Many communities attempted to flee into mountainous interiors, withdraw food supplies, or abandon cultivation to deprive colonists of provisions. These responses are sometimes described as unsuccessful, but they illustrate that Taíno leaders weighed options in a political landscape of rivalries, and that they were not united against Europeans as a single people. The Spanish exploited those divisions, though they could not control them completely.

Section 69.3

The Conquest of Hispaniola and the Imposition of Forced Labor

The military subjugation of Hispaniola took place in stages. In March 1495, in the Vega Real, a Spanish force of a few hundred men with cavalry and war dogs, supported by Indigenous allies, defeated a much larger Taíno force. The battle did not end resistance, but it demonstrated how horses, steel weapons, crossbows, and terror could be effective in particular engagements, especially when opponents lacked armor, cavalry, or any experience with such tactics. The campaign was followed by the tribute system, and Columbus's brother Bartholomew pursued caciques who refused it. Under Roldán and later officials, caciques who had been allowed to remain in place were required to provide laborers to Spanish settlers in exchange for protection.

The arrival of Ovando in 1502 with a fleet carrying roughly two thousand five hundred colonists intensified the process. His instructions from the Crown envisaged Indigenous people living in towns, being instructed in Christianity, and working for wages. In practice, Ovando distributed Indigenous communities among Spanish colonists, who received the right to demand labor and services, a system that became known as the encomienda. Many caciques were brought into subordinate positions, and some were killed. The conquest of the eastern province of Higüey in 1503 and 1504, and the destruction of the leadership of Jaragua in 1503, completed the Spanish occupation of the island's principal districts.

The labor demanded of the Taíno was brutal. Men were assigned to panning gold, to agriculture on Spanish estates, and to building. Communities were often moved far from their homes, families were separated, and subsistence agriculture suffered because men were absent during planting and harvest. Spanish livestock, particularly pigs, and the colonists' demands for food devastated the cultivated fields. Recorded mortality was extraordinary. The reasons were many: introduced diseases, especially an epidemic that Spanish observers reported in the 1490s and the smallpox epidemic that reached Hispaniola in 1518, violence, enslavement, famine, flight, and a collapse in birth rates produced by hardship and separation. When labor demands outstripped the available population, Spanish slaving expeditions raided other islands, particularly the Bahamas, whose Lucayan population was almost entirely removed within a generation.

By the time of the 1514 distribution, roughly twenty six thousand Indigenous people were recorded, and by the middle of the century the Indigenous population of Hispaniola was a small remnant of its pre contact size. Estimates of that original population range widely, from fewer than a few hundred thousand to over a million, with some older estimates much higher, a debate considered in section 69.31. The scarcity of Indigenous labor led colonists and the Crown to look elsewhere: to slaving raids across the Caribbean, to expeditions to the mainland, and to the importation of enslaved Africans. The system of forced labor and tribute that the Spanish had assembled in Hispaniola would be exported to Mexico, Central America, and Peru.

Historical Vocabulary
Encomienda

The encomienda was a grant by the Crown to a Spanish colonist, the encomendero, of the right to receive tribute and labor from a specified group of Indigenous people, in exchange for an obligation to protect them and arrange their instruction in Christianity. The word derives from the Spanish verb encomendar, to entrust. The institution drew on medieval Castilian practices of entrusting frontier populations and territories to military orders and nobles, but in the Americas it became a mechanism for extracting labor from Indigenous communities. Under the encomienda, the land was not formally granted, and Indigenous people were not legally enslaved, although in practice the distinction was often blurred.

The term matters to this chapter because the encomienda shaped the relationship between conquerors, Indigenous communities, and the Crown. Encomenderos sought to turn grants into hereditary, quasi feudal rights, while the Crown sought to preserve its own authority and prevent the emergence of a powerful colonial aristocracy. That conflict produced the Laws of Burgos in 1512 and the New Laws of 1542, and, in Peru, a series of rebellions. Students should avoid translating encomienda directly as "feudal" or "serfdom," since it was a colonial institution with its own legal characteristics and sixteenth century variations.

Section 69.4

Indigenous Resistance in the Caribbean

Resistance in the Caribbean ranged across armed rebellion, flight, negotiation, and the preservation of community life under colonial pressure. Three names recur: Caonabó, Anacaona, and Enriquillo. They belonged to different decades, and the circumstances in which each acted should not be confused. Caonabó, the cacique of Maguana, was identified by Spanish accounts as an opponent of the colonists in the early 1490s and was linked, though the evidence is not unequivocal, to the destruction of La Navidad. Spanish sources report that around 1494 Alonso de Ojeda captured him, apparently by deception, and that he died in captivity on a ship bound for Spain. His wife Anacaona, a cacica from the Jaragua cacicazgo and sister of Behechio, remained a figure of political and cultural prominence after his capture.

Anacaona was killed during Ovando's governorship. In 1503 Ovando visited Jaragua and, according to Spanish accounts including that of Las Casas, assembled the principal nobles, then had them seized, killed, or burned, and had Anacaona taken to Santo Domingo, tried, and hanged. Sources vary in detail, and Las Casas, who wrote some decades later, had reasons to emphasize Spanish treachery, but there is no doubt that the event took place and that it eliminated the leadership of one of the island's principal polities. Cotubanamá, the cacique of Higüey, was captured and executed in 1504 after the war in the eastern region.

Enriquillo's rebellion belongs to a later generation. Raised by Franciscan friars in a Spanish setting, Enriquillo was a cacique whose people had been assigned to Spanish encomenderos and who, following mistreatment by an encomendero, withdrew in 1519 into the Bahoruco mountains with followers. For about fourteen years, he conducted a guerrilla war, attracting runaways, including some enslaved Africans. Spanish expeditions failed to defeat him. In 1533 a negotiated settlement recognized his freedom and that of his community, in a rare case of the Crown conceding to an Indigenous leader in arms. Enriquillo died shortly afterward, and the community's later history is poorly documented.

It would be a serious error to treat these figures as evidence that Indigenous resistance in the Caribbean was always military or always unsuccessful. Communities also resisted by moving to remote areas, by hiding crops, by continuing religious practices, and by marrying into Spanish households and shaping the culture of the emerging colonial society. Older narratives, repeated in some Spanish chronicles and nineteenth century histories, claimed that the Taíno were exterminated by the mid sixteenth century. Research in documentary history, language, and genetics has complicated that conclusion. Taíno vocabulary survived in Spanish, including words such as hammock, canoe, hurricane, and tobacco. Studies of ancient and modern DNA show that Indigenous ancestry persists in some Caribbean populations. Communities in Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Dominican Republic have continued to identify with Taíno heritage, and the nature and extent of that continuity remains an active and sometimes contested area of research.

Section 69.5

Cuba, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and the Expansion of Caribbean Colonization

As Hispaniola's Indigenous population declined, Spanish expeditions moved outward. Juan Ponce de León led an expedition to Puerto Rico in 1508, and colonization intensified over the next several years. In 1511, Taíno communities led by caciques rose against Spanish settlers, and, after initial setbacks, Spanish forces with Indigenous allies suppressed the revolt, a pattern repeated elsewhere. The island's gold deposits were modest, and the colonists increasingly turned to agriculture and livestock. Jamaica was occupied under Juan de Esquivel beginning in 1509, and its Indigenous population also declined rapidly.

The conquest of Cuba, begun in 1511 by Diego Velázquez with Pánfilo de Narváez among his captains, followed a familiar course. Velázquez established towns, among them Baracoa in 1511 and Santiago de Cuba in 1515, and Havana was founded on the southern coast in 1515 and relocated to its northern harbor in 1519. Hatuey, a cacique who had fled from Hispaniola, organized resistance and was burned at the stake in 1512. Las Casas, who accompanied the expedition as a priest and encomendero, later described the violence he witnessed in Cuba and became one of its most powerful critics. His testimony will be examined in section 69.24.

The Caribbean islands were becoming a strategic base. Cuba provided the starting point for the expeditions to the Yucatán coast in 1517 and 1518, and for Cortés's expedition in 1519. The island of Hispaniola housed the first audiencia, established at Santo Domingo in 1511, the institution that would later extend across Spanish America. Havana, positioned at the exit of the Gulf Stream, became a gathering point for Spanish ships returning to Europe. Livestock, sugar cane, wheat, and other plants were multiplied in island environments before being exported to the mainland, and expeditionary forces were recruited among colonists who had already acquired experience in warfare and exploitation.

The Caribbean also saw the early presence of Africans. Some had been in Spain before 1492, and individuals of African descent, enslaved and free, accompanied Spanish voyages and conquests. Royal licenses permitted the importation of enslaved Africans to Hispaniola from the early sixteenth century, and in 1518 Charles V's government authorized direct shipments, an arrangement that expanded as Indigenous labor declined. In December 1521, enslaved Africans rebelled on a sugar estate in Hispaniola, one of the earliest recorded uprisings in the Americas, and runaway communities, later called maroons, formed in mountain refuges. These developments introduced themes that Chapter 70 will develop. The Caribbean thus combined the first sustained pattern of Indigenous exploitation with the emergence of African slavery, and the interaction between them shaped later colonial societies.

Division II · The Conquest of Mexico and Mesoamerica · Section 69.6

Mesoamerica before the Spanish Invasion

When Spanish ships first appeared off the coast of Mesoamerica, the region had a history of urban civilization stretching back millennia. Its peoples shared elements of a common cultural tradition, including a ritual calendar, systems of writing and record keeping in pictorial or hieroglyphic form, maize based agriculture, a ball game, and elaborate temple architecture, while speaking many languages and maintaining distinct political systems. The most powerful state in the early sixteenth century was the Triple Alliance, a coalition of the city states of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan, formed around 1428 after a war against Azcapotzalco. Tenochtitlan, built on an island in Lake Texcoco and connected to the shore by causeways, emerged as the dominant partner. Its people, the Mexica, are often called Aztecs in popular usage, a term discussed in the vocabulary box below.

The Triple Alliance was a tributary empire rather than a centralized state. Conquered city states, called altepetl, typically retained their local rulers, languages, and institutions, so long as they delivered tribute in goods, labor, and military service and acknowledged Mexica religious and political superiority. The tribute lists preserved in the Codex Mendoza, a post conquest document drawing on earlier records, show enormous quantities of cotton cloth, feathers, cacao, maize, and warrior costumes flowing into the capital. Tenochtitlan had a population that scholars estimate in the range of one hundred thousand to over two hundred thousand, with markets, such as the great market at neighboring Tlatelolco, and agricultural systems based on chinampas, raised fields built in the shallow lake beds. Specialists regard it as one of the largest cities in the world at the time.

The system generated resentment. Subject city states resented tribute burdens and the demands for captives for ritual sacrifice. Some neighboring states remained independent and hostile, among them Tlaxcala, a confederation of four lordships east of the Valley of Mexico that resisted Mexica pressure and maintained its autonomy, though economically squeezed by the blockade of imported goods such as salt and cotton. To the west, the Tarascan state of Michoacán was an expanding rival that had defeated Mexica attacks. The Mexica ruler, Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin, known as Motecuhzoma II, who took power in 1502, had consolidated authority, but conflicts in succession and unrest in the provinces continued. Ritual violence, including human sacrifice, was a significant element of Mexica statecraft and religion, supported by archaeological discoveries at the Templo Mayor and by Indigenous and Spanish accounts, though Spanish writers had incentives to exaggerate it to justify conquest.

Beyond central Mexico, Maya societies in Yucatán, Chiapas, and Guatemala were organized in many independent polities, some governed by lineages claiming ancient authority, others by councils. The great Classic period cities had been abandoned centuries earlier, but Postclassic Maya towns such as Mayapán, Chichén Itzá in its later phases, and the K'iche' center of Q'umarkaj were vibrant. Maya political fragmentation shaped the course and duration of Spanish campaigns in the region. Mesoamerica in 1519 was thus a patchwork of rivals, tributaries, and independent states, where the arrival of foreign armies could be interpreted as an opportunity to change the balance of power.

Historical Vocabulary
Mexica, Aztec, and Nahua

Mexica was the name used by the people of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco for themselves. Aztec, derived from a Nahuatl word referring to Aztlan, an origin place in Mexica tradition, was rarely used as a self designation and became a common name in European writing after the conquest. Historians now generally prefer "Mexica" for the people of Tenochtitlan, "Triple Alliance" for the political system, and Nahua for the larger population of Nahuatl speakers, who included the Mexica, Tlaxcalans, Texcocans, and many others. These distinctions matter because the Nahua world was politically divided. When this chapter says that Nahua allies fought against the Mexica, it is describing a conflict among Nahuatl speaking communities with different histories and interests, not a clash between "Aztecs" and an undifferentiated Indigenous population.

Section 69.7

Hernán Cortés and the Expedition of 1519

Spanish contact with the Mexican mainland grew from expeditions launched from Cuba. In 1517 Francisco Hernández de Córdoba reached the Yucatán coast and was driven off by Maya forces at Champotón. In 1518 Juan de Grijalva explored further along the Gulf Coast, obtaining gold through trade and hearing of a powerful ruler inland. Governor Velázquez then commissioned Hernán Cortés, a landholder and official in Cuba, to lead a third expedition, but, doubting his loyalty, attempted to revoke the commission. Cortés sailed in February 1519 with about eleven ships, several hundred men, around sixteen horses, and some artillery. The first stage of the enterprise was thus an act of defiance against the colonial governor, and Cortés's later letters to Charles V were in part a legal strategy to justify that defiance.

At Cozumel, Cortés found Gerónimo de Aguilar, a Spaniard who had been shipwrecked in 1511 and had learned a Mayan language. At Potonchán, on the coast of Tabasco, Spanish forces fought a battle with local forces, after which the local rulers gave gifts, including twenty women. One of them, a Nahuatl speaking woman baptized as Marina and known in Nahuatl sources as Malintzin, could translate between Nahuatl and the Mayan language Aguilar spoke. A chain of translation thus connected Nahuatl, Mayan, and Spanish, until Malintzin learned Spanish and became Cortés's principal interpreter and, according to many sources, an influential adviser. Her origins are reported differently in different sources, but she was probably from a Nahuatl speaking community and had been enslaved or given as tribute. She bore Cortés a son, Martín, in 1522. Later memory of her has been contested, as "La Malinche," a symbol alternately of betrayal, survival, and the origins of a mixed society. Historians emphasize that she was a young woman with limited power, acting under coercion, who nevertheless shaped negotiations.

On the coast of what is now Veracruz, Cortés founded the town of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz in the spring and summer of 1519 and arranged for its newly constituted town council to elect him as captain general and chief justice, deriving his authority directly from the municipality and, by extension, from the Crown rather than from Velázquez. The maneuver drew on Castilian municipal tradition and was legally ingenious. To eliminate possible dissent, Cortés disabled his ships, a deed often described as burning them, though the sources suggest he scuttled most of them. Messengers from Motecuhzoma met the Spaniards with gifts and instructions to leave, but the gifts of gold further aroused Spanish interest.

Students should be careful with sources for this stage. Cortés's own letters present the expedition as an act of loyal service. Bernal Díaz del Castillo, who completed his True History of the Conquest of New Spain in the 1560s, wrote partly to correct what he saw as exaggerated credit given to Cortés. Francisco López de Gómara, who was Cortés's chaplain in Spain and never visited the Americas, wrote a laudatory biography. Nahua sources, including the Florentine Codex and the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, preserve other perspectives. Together they show a campaign that depended less on a single commander's genius than on translators, local political contacts, supply lines, and good fortune.

Section 69.8

Indigenous Alliances and the Road to Tenochtitlan

Cortés's expedition advanced inland through a political landscape of communities with their own agendas. At Cempoala, the capital of the Totonacs, local leaders complained of Mexica tribute collectors and troop demands. When Mexica officials arrived, Cortés persuaded the Totonac rulers to arrest them, committing them to rebellion and making Spanish support essential. Totonac participation proved valuable, providing food, porters, and warriors, and helping Cortés understand the political geography of the highlands. The Totonac decision was not a naive surrender to a stronger power. It was a calculation that a new ally might help weaken a hegemonic and costly overlord.

In September 1519 the Spanish entered the territory of Tlaxcala. The Tlaxcalans, accustomed to defending themselves against the Mexica, initially attacked the strangers and fought a series of battles, in which Spanish and Totonac forces confronted Tlaxcalan and Otomí warriors. After heavy fighting, Tlaxcalan leaders, among them Xicotencatl the Elder and Maxixcatzin, concluded that an alliance offered advantages. Their goals were their own: to destroy or weaken Tenochtitlan, to obtain access to trade goods, to preserve autonomy, and to secure advantages within any new order. Tlaxcalan accounts later emphasized their role, in petitions asking Spanish authorities for privileges as faithful allies, and these documents must be read as both history and advocacy. Even so, the Tlaxcalans were the largest and most persistent Indigenous allies of the Spaniards, and the campaign could not have succeeded without them.

Their alliance was not unanimous or unconditional. Within Tlaxcala, factions disagreed, and the younger Xicotencatl was reported to oppose the alliance. The allies also had distinct expectations of the outcome. Cortés and his men sought gold, titles, and glory. The Tlaxcalans sought vengeance, territory, and security. Totonacs, Texcocans, and others sought relief from tribute. Spanish writers, who dominated early accounts, tended to present these allies as auxiliaries, but the numerical balance and the logic of the campaigns show that Indigenous leaders were the principal partners in the coalition, and Spaniards a small but critical element, controlling firearms, horses, and the legal claims that would later benefit them.

The Spanish advance into the Valley of Mexico passed through Cholula, a major religious center allied with Tenochtitlan, in October 1519. There, Spanish and Tlaxcalan forces carried out a massacre of Cholulans, killing thousands in a few hours. The Spanish justified the attack as a response to a plot to ambush them, and some Indigenous sources give a different account, in which Cortés planned the killing as a demonstration of terror. The number killed is estimated at several thousand, though sources diverge. The event shows how violence functioned as a strategy, designed to intimidate potential opponents and to eliminate a strategic point. It also reveals the interests of the Tlaxcalans, who had long hostile relations with Cholula. After Cholula, the expedition continued toward Tenochtitlan with little opposition.

Section 69.9

Motecuhzoma II, Tenochtitlan, and the Crisis of Imperial Authority

On 8 November 1519 the Spanish and their Indigenous allies entered Tenochtitlan along a causeway from the south. Spanish writers were astonished by the city's size, canals, temples, markets, and organization, and Bernal Díaz compared the view to enchantments described in chivalric romances. Motecuhzoma met them in a formal reception, and the Spaniards were lodged in a palace near the center of the city. The Mexica ruler, a hereditary leader who held religious and political authority and whose power depended on negotiations among nobles, priests, and allied rulers, had to decide how to respond to the strangers and to their growing Indigenous coalition.

His motives and understanding are matters of debate. A widely repeated story holds that Motecuhzoma believed Cortés to be the returning god Quetzalcoatl and therefore treated him with deference. Most modern scholars regard that account with skepticism, since it appears in sources written after the conquest, when Indigenous writers and Spanish authors had reasons to explain the Mexica defeat in terms of supernatural error. A more convincing interpretation is that Motecuhzoma followed standard diplomatic practice, receiving guests with gifts and ceremony while assessing their strength. Mexica rulers often permitted foreign embassies, and the strangers' behavior, including the massacre at Cholula, may have led him to combine hospitality with caution.

Within days the Spaniards seized Motecuhzoma and held him under guard in their quarters, a bold move that gave them leverage but also exposed them to enormous risk. Spanish accounts report that Motecuhzoma accepted Spanish authority and recognized Charles V as sovereign, in speeches that Cortés included in his letters. Historians question how far these statements reflect Nahua diplomatic language or later Spanish legal needs. During his captivity, the ruler continued to govern in some measure, collected tribute that was shared with the Spaniards, and facilitated meetings with other rulers. The confinement, however, weakened his credibility. Mexica nobles, including his brother Cuitláhuac, rejected the arrangement, and Mexica society faced the problem of an authority compromised by foreign pressure.

The weeks that followed show a political crisis. Spanish demands for gold and attempts to remove or desecrate religious images provoked tension. The presence of thousands of Spanish and Tlaxcalan troops in the city strained supplies. The Mexica elite was divided between those who counseled accommodation and those who wanted to expel the intruders. The Spaniards, for their part, were isolated in a city whose population could cut off food and water and destroy the causeways. The balance of power was unstable, and the situation changed dramatically in spring 1520 when events elsewhere drew Cortés away from the capital.

Section 69.10

The Crisis of 1520: Revolt, Retreat, and Epidemic Disease

In April 1520, a Spanish force sent by Governor Velázquez under Pánfilo de Narváez landed on the Gulf Coast with the aim of arresting Cortés and taking control of the expedition. Cortés left Tenochtitlan with part of his men, defeated Narváez in a night attack at Cempoala in late May, and incorporated many of Narváez's soldiers into his own force. During his absence, Pedro de Alvarado remained in the capital. In May 1520, during the festival of Tóxcatl, a major religious celebration, Spanish soldiers attacked Mexica nobles and dancers gathered in the sacred precinct. The Spanish later claimed they feared an uprising. Nahua accounts, such as the one preserved in Book 12 of the Florentine Codex, describe an attack on unarmed celebrants and many deaths. The Mexica rose in fury and besieged the Spaniards in their quarters.

When Cortés returned in late June, he found the city in revolt. Attempts to restore order failed. Motecuhzoma, brought out to address his people, was reportedly wounded, and he died in late June 1520. How he died is contested. Spanish accounts say that Mexica warriors stoned him, while some Nahua accounts claim that the Spaniards killed him, and the evidence does not permit certainty. Cuitláhuac was chosen ruler. On the night of 30 June to 1 July, the Spanish and their allies attempted to withdraw along the Tlacopan causeway and suffered heavy losses, an episode remembered as the Noche Triste, the "sad night." Accounts of casualties vary. Several hundred Spaniards were killed, and many more Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous allies, and much of the treasure collected was lost in the lake. A week later the survivors defeated a Mexica force at Otumba and reached Tlaxcala, where the allies sheltered them.

The same months brought an epidemic. Smallpox, previously unknown in Mesoamerica, arrived with the Narváez expedition, reportedly carried by an infected member of the party, and spread rapidly among communities in the Valley of Mexico and beyond in the second half of 1520. Nahua accounts describe a disease that covered people's bodies with sores and left many unable to move, so that those who were ill could not obtain food or water. Spanish observers reported enormous mortality. The epidemic killed Cuitláhuac, who had ruled for only a few months, and was succeeded by Cuauhtémoc, a young relative. Historians believe that mortality was substantial, though no reliable count exists, and that disease killed political and military leaders, disrupted harvests and food supplies, and weakened the capacity of Mexica and other polities to organize. At the same time, smallpox also struck the Tlaxcalans and other allies.

The crisis of 1520 shows why simple explanations fail. The Spanish retreat was a defeat that nearly destroyed the expedition and could have ended Spanish presence in Mexico. Cortés survived because Tlaxcalan allies sheltered and supported him, because he secured reinforcements from the Narváez expedition and later from the Caribbean, and because the epidemic disrupted the Mexica leadership. The result was not inevitable. If the Tlaxcalans had decided to turn on the Spaniards, or if Cuitláhuac had had time to consolidate an anti Spanish coalition in the Valley, the outcome might have been very different.

Section 69.11

The Siege and Fall of Tenochtitlan, 1521

During the second half of 1520 and early 1521, Cortés rebuilt his coalition. Reinforcements arrived from Hispaniola and Cuba, including soldiers, horses, and supplies. Spanish forces and their allies reconquered communities that had joined the Mexica, such as Tepeaca, where captives were branded and enslaved. Texcoco, a member of the Triple Alliance, fell under the influence of a ruler favorable to the Spanish, who sought advantage in the changing order. Allied communities provided warriors, food, porters, and labor. The Spaniards built thirteen brigantines, small sailing vessels with oars, in Tlaxcala, under the direction of the shipwright Martín López, and carried them in pieces across the mountains to Lake Texcoco. This was a major logistical and diplomatic undertaking requiring the labor of thousands of Indigenous porters.

The siege began in late May 1521. The Spanish and allied forces cut the aqueduct that carried fresh water from Chapultepec, occupied causeways, and used the brigantines to defeat the Mexica fleet of canoes and control the lake. Cuauhtémoc organized a determined defense, and the Mexica fought effectively. In late June, a Spanish assault on the causeways went badly, and some Spanish prisoners were captured and sacrificed, a defeat that nearly demoralized the allies. The fighting that followed took place street by street, as allied forces systematically destroyed buildings and filled canals with rubble. The city was cut off from food and fresh water. Starvation, thirst, and disease, probably including the continued effects of smallpox, spread through a densely crowded population swollen by refugees.

The scale of the allied army is crucial. Cortés claimed a force of tens of thousands, and some Spanish sources claimed over a hundred thousand. Modern scholars treat such figures with caution but agree that Indigenous allies outnumbered the Spaniards by a very large margin. Cortés's own force numbered several hundred Spaniards. Without Tlaxcalan, Texcocan, and other allied armies, the siege could not have been maintained. The allies also provided the manual labor, intelligence, food, and local knowledge on which the Spanish depended. For the allies, the siege was a war of vengeance against a tributary overlord. The violence inflicted on the city's population, including civilians, was often committed by allied forces, and Cortés wrote that he was unable to restrain them.

On 13 August 1521 Cuauhtémoc was captured while attempting to escape by canoe, and the city surrendered. Spanish and allied soldiers plundered the ruins. Estimates of the number killed in the siege vary widely and are uncertain, but all sources agree that mortality was massive, and the survivors were allowed to leave the devastated city. Cortés later constructed a Spanish capital on the site, and Mexico City became the center of the colonial administration of New Spain. The fall of Tenochtitlan ended the Triple Alliance as a political entity and gave the Spanish and their allies control of the Valley of Mexico. Cuauhtémoc remained in captivity and was executed in 1525 during Cortés's expedition to Honduras.

Primary Source Window

Nahua Accounts of the Conquest in the Florentine Codex, Book 12

Creators: Nahua scholars and elders of Tlatelolco and Tenochtitlan working with the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún · Date: Date: first accounts gathered around 1553–1555; the Florentine manuscript completed in 1577, followed by a separate revision in 1585 · Type: Bilingual Nahuatl and Spanish prose history, compiled in Mexico and preserved in a manuscript now in Florence

Context: The Florentine Codex is a twelve book encyclopedic account of Nahua life compiled under Sahagún's direction in the sixteenth century. Book 12 narrates the arrival of the Spaniards and the conquest of Mexico from the perspective of Nahua informants, many of them from Tlatelolco, which had been a rival of Tenochtitlan within the island city. The text was written in alphabetic Nahuatl by Indigenous scholars trained at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco, including Antonio Valeriano, and was later accompanied by a Spanish version and illustrations. It was composed decades after the events it describes. The surviving Florentine manuscript was completed in 1577, and Sahagún prepared a separate revision of his conquest account in 1585.

Paraphrase of Florentine Codex, Book 12 (no direct quotation) In its account of the Tóxcatl festival, Book 12 describes celebrants assembled in the temple precinct to dance and sing in honor of their deity. The Spaniards, it reports, closed the entrances, attacked the dancers and drummers, and killed many of them, and then went through the precinct killing others. The account of the siege in the later chapters describes the hunger and thirst of the besieged, the dead lying in the streets, and the loss of leaders, while also recording the fate of Tlatelolco in the final days. The narrative is attentive to omens that purportedly foreshadowed the Spanish arrival, an element that historians read as a way of making sense of defeat after the fact.

Analysis: Book 12 is one of the most valuable surviving Indigenous accounts of the conquest, since it preserves Nahua experiences and values that Spanish sources omit, including detailed descriptions of the Tóxcatl attack and the suffering of the besieged. It also shows the complexity of Indigenous sources. The informants came largely from Tlatelolco and tend to emphasize its role. The text was shaped by the friars' project of studying Nahua culture in order to convert its people, and its authors wrote after decades of Christian teaching. The accounts of omens and the view of Motecuhzoma as passive are possible later constructions, and historians treat them as evidence about postconquest memory as much as about 1519. The source says relatively little about the Tlaxcalan and other allied leaders, whose perspective is better preserved in other documents.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the Tóxcatl attack and the experience of the siege?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purposes, given that it was prepared within a Franciscan project?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Nahua society and the memory of the conquest?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, and how might they use Spanish and Tlaxcalan sources alongside it?
Section 69.12

Conquest beyond Tenochtitlan: Mesoamerican Resistance and Continuity

The fall of Tenochtitlan did not end the conquest of Mesoamerica. It opened a prolonged and uneven series of campaigns. Cortés dispatched expeditions to the Gulf Coast, the Pacific, and the south, and Spanish commanders negotiated or fought with city states across central and southern Mexico. The Tarascan state of Michoacán, which had resisted the Mexica, submitted in 1522 following Spanish contact, partly through diplomacy and partly because of epidemic disease. Its ruler, the cazonci Tangaxuan II, was later tortured and executed in 1530 by Nuño de Guzmán, a brutal Spanish official whose campaigns in western Mexico became notorious. In Oaxaca, Zapotec and Mixtec communities, with long traditions of statecraft, negotiated different terms of submission, and many preserved local rulers.

The Maya zone illustrates the length and difficulty of conquest. In 1527 Francisco de Montejo began campaigns in Yucatán, and he and his son of the same name faced years of resistance from independent Maya polities. Spanish control was not firmly established in northern Yucatán until the 1540s, symbolized by the founding of Mérida in 1542. Large parts of the southern lowlands, including the Petén, remained independent of Spanish rule. The Itza Maya state of Nojpetén on Lake Petén Itzá held out until 1697, more than a century and a half after the fall of Tenochtitlan. Maya society was decentralized and rested on many small and flexible polities, which made it harder for Spanish forces to defeat a central authority. Maya communities also used flight, rebellion, and maintenance of local religious practice to resist.

Where Spanish rule took hold, Indigenous institutions persisted in modified form. In central Mexico, the altepetl, the local political community, survived as the basic unit of colonial administration. Indigenous rulers, whose title tlatoani continued in use for some time, became governors of Indigenous municipalities, and Indigenous town councils, or cabildos, administered local affairs, collected tribute, and organized labor drafts. Nahua nobles used Spanish law to defend land claims and status, and they produced a rich body of documents in Nahuatl, written in the Latin alphabet, including wills, land records, and petitions. Tlaxcala, in recognition of its alliance, received privileges and the title of city in 1535. Cortés himself received grants of Indigenous towns and the title of Marquis of the Valley of Oaxaca, but he was gradually displaced from power by royal officials and fell out of favor.

The Spanish administration in these years was in constant tension with the interests of conquerors, who regarded the labor and tribute of Indigenous communities as their reward. The Crown established the first audiencia of New Spain in 1528, under Guzmán, whose violent conduct provoked a second audiencia in 1530, and in 1535 the first viceroy, Antonio de Mendoza, arrived to coordinate authority. These developments, and the Indigenous continuities they accommodated, are examined further in Division V.

Division III · The Conquest of the Inca Empire and the Andes · Section 69.13

The Inca Empire before the Spanish Invasion

The Inca state called itself Tawantinsuyu, a Quechua term meaning "the four parts together," a reference to the four quarters into which its territory was divided around the capital, Cuzco. At its height in the early sixteenth century it stretched along the Andes from what is now southern Colombia and Ecuador through Peru and Bolivia to northern Chile and northwestern Argentina, an area of extraordinary environmental diversity that included coastal deserts, high mountain valleys, and tropical forest margins. Inca expansion was relatively recent. Tradition, recorded after the conquest, holds that the great expansion began under the ruler Pachacuti in the mid fifteenth century, and that his successors, Thupa Inka and Wayna Qhapaq, extended the empire further. Archaeology supports a rapid growth over roughly a century, though the details are debated.

The Inca ruled through a combination of conquest, diplomacy, and administrative organization. Local rulers, called kurakas, were often left in place, provided they accepted Inca authority and delivered labor and goods. The basic unit of Andean society was the ayllu, a kin based or community based group that held land and organized reciprocal labor. The state demanded a rotational labor obligation called the mit'a, which provided workers for agriculture on state lands, for public works, for construction, and for military service. Surplus goods were stored in warehouses, called qollqa, built along an extensive road system, the Qhapaq Ñan, which linked the empire with tens of thousands of kilometers of paths, bridges, and way stations. Runners carried messages, and officials recorded quantities and census information on khipu, systems of knotted cords whose precise interpretation remains incompletely understood. The Inca also relocated entire communities, called mitmaq, to secure frontiers or to settle valuable lands. There was no money economy or market system comparable to Mesoamerica, and exchange followed principles of reciprocity and redistribution administered by the state and its local intermediaries.

Inca authority was bound up with religion. The ruler, the Sapa Inca, was considered a descendant of the sun, and the bodies of dead rulers were preserved as mummies that continued to receive offerings, hold estates, and play roles in politics through their kin groups, the panacas. Shrines, called huacas, across the landscape were integrated into a ritual system centered on Cuzco. These features made the empire cohesive, but they also meant that its cohesion depended on the legitimacy of rulers and on the loyalty of subject peoples, many of whom had been incorporated within the lifetime of living elders. Groups such as the Cañari of Ecuador and the Chachapoya of northern Peru had fought hard against incorporation and retained memories of independence.

The empire was under political strain in the late 1520s. Wayna Qhapaq died around 1527, and some accounts attribute his death to an epidemic disease that may have reached the Andes ahead of the Spaniards, though the evidence for the identity and timing of the disease is uncertain. His death left a contested succession between his sons Huáscar, based in Cuzco, and Atahualpa, who commanded forces in the north around Quito. A civil war followed, with great loss of life and disruption. By 1532 Atahualpa's generals had defeated Huáscar's forces and captured Huáscar, and Atahualpa was traveling southward as the victor when the Spaniards arrived. The war had weakened the empire, divided its elites, and left many subject communities uncertain about who ruled.

Section 69.14

Francisco Pizarro and the Capture of Atahualpa, 1532

Francisco Pizarro had been a soldier in the Caribbean and Panama, where he took part in the expansion that followed Vasco Núñez de Balboa's crossing of the isthmus in 1513 and the founding of Panama City in 1519. He heard of a rich kingdom to the south and, with partners including Diego de Almagro and a priest named Hernando de Luque, organized expeditions down the Pacific coast in 1524 and 1526. These expeditions reached the northern Peruvian coast, where Spaniards encountered a city with textiles and metalwork at Tumbes, and obtained evidence of a large state. Pizarro traveled to Spain in 1528 and secured a royal agreement, signed in Toledo in 1529, which granted him authority to conquer and govern the region. In late 1530 and early 1531 he gathered a small force and sailed south, landing in 1532. His party numbered fewer than two hundred men, with several dozen horses, and he was joined by additional recruits.

Pizarro's expedition advanced into the highlands with Indigenous guides, interpreters, and allied groups. They learned of the civil war and of Atahualpa's presence near the town of Cajamarca. Pizarro's forces entered the town in November 1532 and arranged to meet Atahualpa, who came to Cajamarca on 16 November with a large following. The accounts of what happened are Spanish and emphasize the Spaniards' ambush. A friar, Vicente de Valverde, delivered a religious demand, a version of the Requerimiento discussed in section 69.24, and Atahualpa's rejection became a pretext for attack. Spanish soldiers fired arquebuses, charged with cavalry and steel weapons, and killed large numbers of Inca attendants, many of whom were unarmed or lightly armed, before seizing Atahualpa.

The attack was not a simple triumph of technology. The Spaniards had come prepared for surprise and used methods already tested in Panama and Central America, including seizure of a ruler. Their ability to do so depended on circumstances: the Inca ruler was exhausted by war and confident, the Spaniards had gained information about his movements, and his followers did not anticipate a treacherous attack on a ceremonial occasion. Atahualpa proposed a ransom, reportedly offering to fill a room once with gold and twice with silver. Over the following months, enormous quantities of precious metal arrived at Cajamarca from across the empire, much of it sacred objects and ornaments that were melted into ingots. The distribution of the treasure among the Spanish company was recorded and amounted to a very large quantity of gold and silver, tens of thousands of pounds by contemporary estimates, enriching the conquerors and funding further enterprises.

Atahualpa used his captivity to continue to govern. He ordered the killing of the captive Huáscar, possibly to remove a rival, and may have hoped that the Spaniards would restore him to power. In 1533, after the ransom was paid, Pizarro's company tried and executed him, probably in late July (commonly dated to 26 July), by garrote after he accepted baptism. His death was condemned by some Spaniards at the time, and it left Andean society without an obvious head. Indigenous groups reacted differently: some rallied around claimants, others saw an opportunity to escape Inca rule, and still others fought. The seizure of Atahualpa in 1532 was thus the beginning of a new phase of conflict and not the end of Inca power.

Section 69.15

The Conquest of Cuzco and the Restructuring of Andean Authority

After Atahualpa's execution, the Spaniards advanced toward Cuzco, accompanied by Indigenous allies including Cañari and other groups hostile to the Inca, and by supporters of Huáscar's faction who had suffered under Atahualpa's generals. Atahualpa's general Quizquiz and others resisted, and fighting took place on the road. Spanish forces entered Cuzco in November 1533 and installed Manco Inca, a half brother of Huáscar, as the new Sapa Inca. The decision reflects the Spaniards' strategy of governing through Inca institutions and legitimizing their presence by supporting a member of the royal lineage. Manco in turn accepted the arrangement because it offered him authority and a chance to defeat his rivals and, perhaps, to use the Spaniards for his own purposes.

The Spaniards established a municipal government in Cuzco in 1534 and distributed encomiendas, assigning groups of Andean people to Spanish colonists. Pizarro founded a Spanish city on the coast, Lima, in January 1535, a decision that located the colonial capital near the Pacific and the shipping route to Panama rather than in the highlands. The conquerors used the roads and storehouses of the Inca state, seized stores of food and textiles, and disrupted the labor systems that had supplied them. Ancestor mummies, central to Inca political authority, were seized or hidden, and sacred objects were looted. Spanish demands for gold and silver led to the looting of temples and the melting of artifacts, an irreparable loss of material culture.

The structure of Andean authority changed in complex ways. Inca nobles lost the empire that had given them status, though some retained prestige among Indigenous communities and continued to claim lineage and rights. Kurakas, who had been intermediaries in the Inca system, became intermediaries in the Spanish system, balancing the demands of encomenderos with the needs of their communities. Competing claims to legitimacy remained. The Spaniards claimed to hold their authority by royal grant and by right of conquest, while Inca claimants asserted a claim based on lineage. Many communities were uncertain which authority to obey, and some, particularly those that had been recently conquered by the Inca, saw the Spaniards as potential liberators.

The experience of the conquest in these years was marked by violence against civilians, looting, enslavement, and sexual exploitation. Spanish soldiers took Andean women, including noble women, and demanded porters and servants. Disease and disorder reduced the labor available to support communities. At the same time, the new situation created opportunities for some Indigenous groups and individuals, who learned Spanish, served as interpreters, or negotiated privileges. The tension between Spanish exploitation and Indigenous accommodation, evident in Cuzco in 1534 and 1535, produced the conditions for the great rebellion of 1536.

Section 69.16

Manco Inca, the Revolt of 1536, and Vilcabamba Resistance

Manco Inca's relationship with the Spaniards deteriorated under the pressure of their demands. He was imprisoned by Francisco Pizarro's brothers Hernando, Gonzalo, and Juan in the course of disputes over gold and treatment, and he was subjected to humiliations. In 1536 he escaped from Cuzco and raised an army of Indigenous warriors, which Spanish sources estimated at many tens of thousands, a figure that should be treated cautiously but that indicates a large mobilization. In the spring of 1536 his forces besieged Cuzco, burned parts of the city, and attacked the Spanish garrison, which sheltered in the buildings around the main plaza and in the nearby fortress complex of Sacsayhuamán. Indigenous allies of the Spanish, notably Cañari and others, fought alongside the Spaniards, and the Spaniards' horses and defensive positions allowed them to survive.

The siege lasted for much of a year. The fighting included the capture of Sacsayhuamán by Spanish forces in a battle in which Juan Pizarro was killed. The Inca army, which was supplied by communities contributing agricultural labor, eventually dissolved as the agricultural season required farmers to return home and as Spanish reinforcements arrived. Manco withdrew to the valley of Ollantaytambo, from where he moved deeper into the Vilcabamba region, a remote mountainous area northwest of Cuzco. There he established what historians call the Neo-Inca state, a court that maintained Inca ritual and political forms and conducted raids and diplomacy with the Spaniards for decades.

Manco was killed in 1544 by Spanish refugees from the civil wars who had been given shelter. His sons continued the state. Sayri Túpac negotiated with the colonial government in the 1550s and left Vilcabamba, accepting an estate and the title of a nobleman, but he died in 1560. Titu Cusi Yupanqui, who ruled from the 1560s, negotiated with Spanish officials, permitted Augustinian missionaries to enter Vilcabamba, and dictated an account of the conquest from an Inca point of view, a significant source. His brother Túpac Amaru succeeded him. In 1572 Viceroy Francisco de Toledo decided to eliminate the state, sent an army into Vilcabamba, captured Túpac Amaru, and had him executed in Cuzco. The event marked the end of organized Inca political resistance, more than thirty five years after the siege of Cuzco and almost forty after Atahualpa's seizure.

Resistance in the Andes did not end with Vilcabamba. In the 1560s a movement called Taki Onqoy, "dancing sickness," spread across the central Andes, preaching rejection of Christianity and the revival of the huacas, which were said to be returning to defeat the Spaniards and their God. It was suppressed by Spanish clergy, but it demonstrates that Andean religious and political resistance continued in many forms. Studying this period shows that the conquest of the Andes was a protracted process, in which defeat in a campaign or the death of a ruler did not end Indigenous opposition.

Section 69.17

Spanish Civil Wars and the Struggle for Royal Authority in Peru

While Inca and Spanish forces fought in the highlands, the conquerors were also fighting one another. The fundamental dispute concerned the distribution of the spoils: land, Indigenous labor, and political authority. Pizarro and Almagro quarreled over control of Cuzco, which each claimed lay within his jurisdiction, and in 1537 and 1538 their followers fought a civil war. Pizarro's forces defeated Almagro's at the Battle of Las Salinas in April 1538, and Almagro was executed. In June 1541, supporters of Almagro's son assassinated Pizarro in Lima. The Crown then sent officials to restore order, including Cristóbal Vaca de Castro, who defeated the younger Almagro in 1542.

The New Laws of 1542, which will be examined in section 69.26, intensified the conflict. They restricted the encomienda, which the conquerors regarded as their reward. When the Crown's first viceroy of Peru, Blasco Núñez Vela, arrived in 1544 to enforce them, encomenderos rose in rebellion under Gonzalo Pizarro, Francisco's brother. Núñez Vela was defeated and killed at the Battle of Iñaquito in January 1546, and Gonzalo Pizarro governed Peru as a virtual independent ruler. The Crown responded by sending Pedro de la Gasca, a cleric and jurist, who revoked the offending provisions, offered pardons, and raised an army. Gasca's forces defeated Pizarro at Xaquixaguana in April 1548, and Gonzalo Pizarro was executed. A further rebellion under Francisco Hernández Girón in 1553 and 1554 was also suppressed.

These wars show the fragility of royal authority at a distance of thousands of kilometers and months of travel. Conquerors regarded themselves as having earned rewards by risking their lives, and they resisted Crown efforts to limit their power. The Crown, for its part, wished to prevent the emergence of an independent aristocracy in the Indies and to protect Indigenous people as vassals whose tribute should enrich the Crown. In Peru, the Crown won by combining concessions with military force, by relying on officials and clergy, and by appealing to the loyalty of other Spanish colonists. The conflict also had significant effects on Indigenous populations, who were conscripted as porters and soldiers, whose communities were looted, and whose labor became the object of the dispute.

After 1548 the Crown strengthened colonial institutions. An audiencia was established at Lima in 1543, and royal officials expanded their reach. The most important consolidation occurred under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo, who governed from 1569 to 1581. Toledo undertook a general inspection of the viceroyalty, reorganized Indigenous communities into new settlements called reducciones, regulated tribute, reorganized the mita labor draft for the silver mines of Potosí, and ended the Neo-Inca state. His policies produced a more durable colonial order, and also placed heavy burdens on Andean communities.

Section 69.18

Andean Communities under Conquest: Adaptation and Survival

For most Andean people, the decisive experience of the sixteenth century was not the clash of armies but the transformation of daily obligations. Indigenous communities continued to be organized in ayllus, which held land and organized work, and kurakas continued to serve as intermediaries, collecting tribute, assigning labor, and defending community interests before Spanish authorities. Many kurakas used the Spanish legal system to protect land and resources, and some became wealthy. Others, under pressure from encomenderos and priests, exploited their own communities. The role of the kuraka thus became ambiguous, a position of both leadership and compromise.

The Andean ecological system, in which communities used lands at different elevations to produce a variety of crops, was disrupted by labor drafts, by population decline, and by Spanish resettlement. The scholar John Murra described this pattern as "vertical" control of ecological zones, and although the model has been debated and refined, it helps explain why Spanish policies that moved or concentrated populations had damaging effects. Toledo's reducciones relocated Indigenous people into compact villages, intended to facilitate tribute collection and religious instruction, but they also separated communities from fields and ancestral sites. Mortality from epidemic disease, in waves including smallpox, measles, and other infections, reduced populations in the coastal valleys particularly severely and left the highlands with a more mixed pattern.

Religion illustrates the combination of survival and adaptation. Missionaries and priests campaigned to eliminate Andean religion, and in the 1560s Spanish officials initiated campaigns to extirpate idolatry, destroying huacas and punishing religious specialists. Indigenous people adopted Christianity in diverse ways: some embraced it, others accepted its outward forms while continuing older practices, and still others merged elements of the two. Andean festivals, saints, and rituals took on new meanings. The Third Council of Lima in 1583 authorized catechisms in Quechua and Aymara, an acknowledgment that evangelization required using Indigenous languages, and it helped Quechua spread as a colonial lingua franca. Indigenous and mixed ancestry authors also began to write. Garcilaso de la Vega, known as El Inca, a son of a Spanish conquistador and an Inca noblewoman, wrote his Royal Commentaries of the Incas in Spain and published it in 1609, while Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala composed a long letter to the king.

Regional differences were considerable. Coastal valleys suffered earlier and more severe depopulation and became more rapidly integrated into Spanish agricultural and commercial systems. Highland communities, particularly those far from Spanish towns and mines, often retained greater autonomy. Communities near Potosí faced the greatest burdens from the mita, which compelled men to travel hundreds of kilometers to work in the mines. The sixteenth century thus produced a mosaic of experiences. Yet across the Andes, communities preserved crucial elements of social organization, language, agricultural knowledge, and cultural memory, which would shape the colonial period and the Andean societies that emerged from it.

Primary Source Window

Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno, c. 1615

Creator: Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, an Andean Christian writer and artist from the highlands of Huamanga who claimed noble Indigenous lineage · Date: completed around 1615, a retrospective source composed roughly eighty years after the conquest of Cuzco · Type: Illustrated letter and history in Spanish with Quechua passages, addressed to King Philip III

Context: Guaman Poma wrote a long manuscript of roughly a thousand pages with hundreds of pen drawings. It presents a history of Andean peoples before and under the Incas, an account of the Spanish conquest, and a detailed critique of colonial abuses. The manuscript was addressed to the king and seems not to have reached him. It remained unknown until it was identified in the Royal Library in Copenhagen in 1908.

Paraphrase of themes in the Nueva corónica (no direct quotation) Guaman Poma describes Andean society as having possessed order and ethical government before and under the Incas, and he criticizes Spanish priests, corregidores, encomenderos, and Indigenous authorities who cooperated with them for exploiting Andean people through labor, tribute, and sexual abuse. He proposes a reformed colonial order in which Andean lords would govern their own communities under the Spanish king and in which Spanish officials would be restrained. His drawings depict scenes of labor, punishment, and the hierarchy of Andean and Spanish society.

Analysis: The source is exceptional because it presents an Andean writer's analysis of colonial society, in a form that mixes Spanish and Andean traditions. Its limitations are equally important. Guaman Poma wrote more than eighty years after the events of 1532 and 1533, and his account of the conquest is retrospective, shaped by his purposes, his Christian outlook, and his claim to noble status. He was critical of the Incas as well as the Spaniards, and he sought a position for Andean elites like himself. His arguments reflect the interests of a provincial Indigenous lord and cannot be assumed to represent all Andean people, including women, commoners, and those outside his region.

Questions for students:

  1. What kinds of colonial abuse does Guaman Poma describe, and what reforms does he propose?
  2. Why does it matter that he wrote around 1615 rather than in the 1530s, and how might that affect his account of the conquest?
  3. What can historians learn from the combination of text and drawings?
  4. What perspectives are not represented, and what other sources would help historians evaluate his claims?
Division IV · Regional Conquests, Frontiers, and Portuguese Brazil · Section 69.19

Central America and the Extension of Spanish Conquest

The conquest of Central America proceeded through a series of expeditions that drew on Mexican and Caribbean experience. In 1523 Cortés sent Pedro de Alvarado, who had been prominent at Tenochtitlan, with a force of Spaniards and thousands of Indigenous allies from central Mexico, including Tlaxcalans and Mexica, into Guatemala. Spanish campaigns encountered Maya communities, including the K'iche', whose capital Q'umarkaj was captured in 1524 after battles in the highlands. Later tradition celebrates a K'iche' leader, Tecún Umán, as a hero who died in single combat with Alvarado, but the historical basis for the details of that story is limited, and it belongs partly to the later memory of the conquest.

The Kaqchikel, rivals of the K'iche', initially allied with the Spaniards, who established their first capital at Iximché, the Kaqchikel capital, in 1524. Spanish demands for tribute, gold, and labor soon provoked a revolt, and the Kaqchikel withdrew and fought for several years, with their war ending in the 1530s. Maya sources, such as the Annals of the Kaqchikels, a Maya language account written in the sixteenth century, describe the arrival of the Spaniards, the epidemic of the early 1520s, and the burdens imposed by Alvarado, offering a rare Indigenous account of these years. Epidemic disease, which may have reached Guatemala from Mexico by 1520 or 1521, preceded the Spanish military campaigns in some areas and weakened Maya communities.

Further south, Spanish expeditions reached Honduras and Nicaragua. The conquest of Nicaragua began with the expedition of Gil González Dávila in 1522 and was continued by Francisco Hernández de Córdoba, who founded Granada and León in 1524 under the authority of the Panama governor Pedrarias Dávila. Nicaragua's Indigenous population, which included Nahua speaking Nicarao and other communities, suffered particularly from enslavement. Thousands were exported as slaves to Panama and Peru to supply labor for the expeditions in the Andes, a drain that produced demographic collapse. In Honduras, a Lenca leader known as Lempira led a major rebellion in 1537, and was killed in 1538. Las Casas and other Dominicans experimented in the 1530s with a peaceful mission in the Verapaz region of Guatemala, which sought to convert communities without military conquest, an attempt that was later undermined by colonial pressures.

The outcomes in Central America varied. In the highlands of Guatemala, the Maya remained a majority of the population, preserved languages and community institutions, and negotiated colonial demands through their own authorities. In Nicaragua and parts of the Pacific coast, the Indigenous population was devastated by slaving, disease, and violence, and the colonial population became more mixed. Large areas of the Caribbean coast and the eastern lowlands remained outside effective Spanish control. The extension of Spanish rule across Central America thus did not produce a uniform colonial order but a variety of regional outcomes.

Section 69.20

New Granada and the Northern South American Interior

The conquest of the northern Andes and the Colombian interior began on the Caribbean coast. Spanish settlements at Santa Marta in 1525 and Cartagena in 1533 provided bases, though the coastal region was difficult, with tropical disease, hostile communities, and few valuable resources. The expedition of Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada, which set out in 1536 and traveled up the Magdalena River, took roughly a year of hardship through jungle and marsh and reduced his force from several hundred men to a fraction of its original size. In 1537 and 1538 the survivors entered the highland basin of the Muisca, a densely populated agricultural society.

The Muisca were organized into several confederations of communities, of which the best known were centered on Bacatá, ruled by a leader called the zipa, and Hunza, ruled by the zaque. They produced maize, potatoes, and other crops, wove cotton textiles, traded salt and emeralds, and worked gold into ritual objects. The two principal rulers were rivals, which assisted the Spanish. Quesada's force defeated the zipa's forces and plundered the region, and in 1538 he founded Santa Fe de Bogotá. Within months two other expeditions arrived on the high plateau: Sebastián de Belalcázar, who had come from Quito and the southern Andes, and Nikolaus Federmann, a German agent of the Welser banking company, which held a concession to colonize Venezuela from the Spanish Crown. The three leaders agreed to travel to Spain to settle their claims, an episode that illustrates how European financiers, as well as Spanish military entrepreneurs, were involved in American ventures.

Spanish colonization of New Granada followed the usual pattern of encomiendas, Indigenous tribute, and, in the 1540s, royal administration. The Muisca were reduced in number through violence, disease, and forced labor, though they survived as a community and retained elements of their language until it was replaced by Spanish in the following centuries. Their ritual practices, including the ceremony at Lake Guatavita in which a ruler was said to cover himself in gold dust and make offerings in the lake, helped inspire the myth of El Dorado, a tale of a golden king that Spanish expeditions pursued across the northern part of South America for decades. The legend grew in the retelling and was projected onto many different regions.

Conquest in much of the region was slower and less complete. Communities in the Caribbean lowlands, such as the Tairona of the Santa Marta mountains, and in the interior, such as the Pijao, resisted Spanish rule for decades. The terrain, with high mountain ranges, deep river valleys, and tropical forest, made travel difficult, fragmented political authority, and limited the effectiveness of cavalry. The challenges of inland conquest show that Spanish expansion depended on geography, supplies, Indigenous political structures, and the availability of manpower, and that many areas were taken only after decades of effort or not at all.

Section 69.21

Northern New Spain and the Limits of Colonial Expansion

North of the densely settled agricultural societies of central Mexico lay a very different environment: semi arid plateau, mountain ranges, and desert, inhabited by diverse communities, some farming, others hunting, gathering, and moving seasonally. Spanish expansion into the region began with raids for slaves and the pursuit of reported riches. Nuño de Guzmán's brutal campaign in western Mexico in 1529 to 1531 established the province of Nueva Galicia, killing and enslaving many people. Although the Crown later removed him, the violence he inflicted left resentments that contributed to rebellion.

In 1540 and 1541 the communities of Nueva Galicia, including the Caxcanes, rose in the Mixtón War. The rebels fortified hilltop positions and defeated Spanish forces on several occasions, killing Pedro de Alvarado, who died in 1541 after being crushed by a horse during the retreat from the stronghold at Nochistlán. The rising threatened the Spanish presence in the region and forced Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza to lead a major campaign with an army that included large numbers of Indigenous allies from central Mexico. The rebellion was suppressed in 1542 with heavy loss of life, and many captives were enslaved. The war showed that Spanish control could not be taken for granted and that Indigenous allies from central Mexico were essential for colonial armies even on distant frontiers.

The discovery of silver at Zacatecas in 1546 and in other districts brought rapid Spanish and Indigenous migration to the north. Mining towns developed, ranches multiplied, and roads connected the new settlements to Mexico City. The mines attracted the hostility of the groups the Spanish called Chichimecas, a term applied to many mobile peoples, including the Zacatecos and Guachichiles. From about 1550 to about 1590, in the long Chichimeca War, they raided mining communities, ranches, and the roads along which silver and supplies moved. Their mobility, intimate knowledge of the terrain, and decentralized political structure allowed them to avoid the pitched battles in which Spanish arms were most effective.

Spanish responses combined force and persuasion. The Crown built a chain of presidios, fortified garrisons that protected roads, and eventually pursued a policy sometimes called "peace by purchase," in which the colonial government provided food, clothing, tools, and land to Chichimeca groups that agreed to settle near missions and stop raiding. In the 1590s the government also relocated Tlaxcalan families to settle in the north, to provide models of sedentary agriculture and Christian life. At the same time, Spanish expeditions led by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado explored northward in 1540 to 1542 in a futile search for wealthy cities, and in 1598 Juan de Oñate began the colonization of New Mexico. The northern frontier was thus a region where Spanish power was limited, where Indigenous groups used mobility and negotiation, and where colonial institutions were shaped by compromise.

Section 69.22

Chile, the Southern Frontiers, and Indigenous Resistance

After the conquest of Peru, Spanish commanders looked south. Diego de Almagro's expedition into Chile in 1535 to 1537 returned in failure, having found neither wealth nor an easily conquered population. In 1540 Pedro de Valdivia set out with a small Spanish force and a larger group of Andean auxiliaries and founded Santiago in 1541, where the central valley offered fertile land, mild climate, and a population accustomed to Inca rule and to agriculture. The Spanish established encomiendas, opened gold washings, and founded further towns, extending south to Concepción and Valdivia in the 1550s.

South of the Biobío River, however, they encountered the Mapuche, a people who lived in dispersed farming communities without a centralized state, but who could unite under war leaders in times of conflict. They resisted Spanish demands for labor and land. In December 1553 a Mapuche force defeated and killed Valdivia at Tucapel. The Mapuche leader Lautaro, who had lived among the Spaniards as a servant and had learned Spanish tactics and weapons, became a significant commander and defeated Spanish forces before he was killed in 1557. The Spanish founded forts and cities, but the Mapuche adopted horses, learned to use cavalry, and kept up their resistance in a long conflict known as the Arauco War. In 1598 a Mapuche victory at Curalaba, in which the governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola was killed, led to the loss of most Spanish towns south of the Biobío.

The Arauco War continued, with changing intensity, well into the seventeenth century and beyond. The Spanish built a standing army, paid by the royal treasury through silver from Peru, which made Chile a peculiar case of a colonial territory sustained at a cost by transfers from richer districts. Eventually the Spanish and Mapuche negotiated agreements recognizing Mapuche territory south of the river, and the frontier remained a zone of trade, diplomacy, and conflict. The Mapuche preserved their autonomy for centuries, until their conquest by the Chilean state in the nineteenth century.

Other southern frontiers show comparable variety. In the Río de la Plata region, Spanish expeditions founded Buenos Aires in 1536, but the settlement was abandoned in 1541 under pressure from Indigenous hostility and hunger, and was refounded in 1580. Further inland, Asunción, founded in 1537 on the Paraguay River, developed differently. Spanish settlers allied with the Guaraní and took Guaraní women into their households through kinship arrangements, which produced a society that was strongly mixed in language and culture, with Guaraní widely spoken and an economy based on labor from Guaraní kin networks. These cases demonstrate that conquest could take the form of accommodation and dependency as well as military victory, and that the Spaniards on the frontier often needed Indigenous partners.

Section 69.23

Portuguese Brazil: Coastal Settlement, Indigenous Relations, and Colonial Beginnings

Brazil entered the Portuguese sphere under the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494, which placed the eastern part of South America to the east of the demarcation line, and in April 1500 the fleet of Pedro Álvares Cabral reached the coast on its way to India and claimed the land for Portugal. For three decades Portuguese activity consisted mainly of coastal trade in brazilwood, a tree whose wood produced a valuable red dye, which Portuguese and French ships obtained by bartering with Indigenous coastal communities, who cut and carried the timber in exchange for metal tools, cloth, and trinkets. The trade was run by private merchants, and settlements were limited to small trading posts.

The Portuguese Crown responded to French competition and the potential of the land by organizing a more permanent colonization. Beginning in 1534, King John III granted hereditary captaincies, stretches of coast, to noble and merchant grantees, who held rights to govern, grant land, and enslave Indigenous people under certain conditions. Only a few of the captaincies prospered, notably São Vicente and Pernambuco, where sugar plantations were established. Failures led the Crown to centralize. In 1549 Tomé de Sousa arrived as the first governor general and founded Salvador in Bahia as the capital. He was accompanied by Jesuit missionaries led by Manuel da Nóbrega, who began a program of mission villages, called aldeias, intended to concentrate Indigenous people for conversion and labor.

The Indigenous peoples of the coast were largely Tupi speaking, including the Tupinambá, Tupiniquim, and others, who lived in large villages, practiced shifting agriculture centered on manioc, and were organized in kin groups and alliances that often fought one another. Their warfare included the ritual execution and consumption of captives, which European observers recorded with fascination. The accounts of Hans Staden, a German who was captured by Tupinambá in the 1550s and returned to publish his story, and of the French Calvinist Jean de Léry, who stayed in 1557 and 1558, are crucial sources but were written for European audiences and shaped by their authors' religious and commercial purposes. Tupi leaders made alliances with Portuguese or French depending on their own interests. The Portuguese allied with the Tupiniquim and other groups against the Tupinambá, who were allied with the French, while a great Tupinambá confederation, the Confederation of the Tamoios, fought the Portuguese from 1554 to 1567. The Portuguese and their allies defeated the confederation, and Rio de Janeiro was founded in 1565 after the expulsion of the French.

Indigenous people faced epidemic disease, enslavement, and forced relocation. Portuguese colonists enslaved Indigenous people captured in "just wars" or purchased from other Indigenous groups, and plantation owners demanded labor. The Jesuits defended their mission villages against slave raiders but also pressed Indigenous people to adopt Christian practices, abandon customs such as ritual cannibalism, and accept labor discipline. Epidemics in the mission villages in the 1560s killed many people. As Indigenous labor declined and as sugar production expanded, planters turned increasingly to enslaved Africans, especially after the 1570s, a development explored in Chapter 70. Brazil thus combined conquest, mission, and plantation in a way that anticipated the Atlantic slavery system.

Historical Debate

Why Did Some European Conquest Campaigns Succeed?

The question: How can historians explain the success of Spanish campaigns against the Mexica and the Inca, and the failure or partial success of campaigns against Maya polities, the Mapuche, and Chichimeca groups? What weight should be given to Indigenous alliances, internal political conflict, disease, military technology, coercive tactics, and contingency?

Interpretation A: Technology and disease as decisive: One tradition, long influential in popular accounts and in some influential syntheses of world history, emphasizes European advantages: steel weapons, armor, firearms, horses, ships, and above all introduced diseases, to which Indigenous populations had no prior immunity. In this view, European biological and technological advantages made Spanish victories likely once contact occurred, and the epidemics of the 1520s and later explain why large and well organized states fell to small forces.
Interpretation B: Indigenous politics and alliances as decisive: Many recent specialists in colonial Latin American history stress that the Spanish operated within Indigenous political systems and depended on Indigenous allies, who provided the great majority of fighting forces, food, intelligence, and labor. Studies of Tlaxcala, Texcoco, and the Cañari show how local political interests shaped alliances and why those alliances persisted. This approach also questions the notion that Spanish success was inevitable, noting the narrow escapes of the Spanish in 1520, and it criticizes the "myth of superiority" developed in Spanish narratives written to claim rewards.

Evidence: Both interpretations use Spanish letters and chronicles, which emphasize Spanish heroism and technology, and Indigenous sources, which preserve information on alliances and politics. Demographic evidence supports claims about the effects of disease, while campaign records, tribute rolls, and petitions by Indigenous allies document their participation. Comparative evidence, from the Maya, Mapuche, and Chichimeca regions, where epidemics and technology were also present but conquest was slow or incomplete, complicates any single explanation. Many historians now combine the two views, stressing that disease and technology mattered but operated through political circumstances, and they add other factors such as Spanish willingness to use terror, the ability to reinforce from a Caribbean base, and chance.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors changes how they portray Indigenous peoples, either as victims of unavoidable forces or as political actors whose decisions shaped the outcome, and affects how they understand the contingency of colonial history and the origins of colonial societies.

The Legitimacy of Conquest: Law, Sovereignty, and Moral Debate

Spanish conquest was accompanied by sustained argument about whether, and on what terms, it was lawful. The Crown's claims rested first on the papal bulls of 1493, which granted Castile authority over the newly found lands on condition that its rulers promote the conversion of their inhabitants, and on the Treaty of Tordesillas, which allocated spheres between Castile and Portugal. These documents regulated relations among Christian monarchs and did not, in the view of most jurists, themselves strip Indigenous rulers of their authority. The question of how Spanish rule could be justified toward Indigenous peoples themselves therefore remained open, and theologians, jurists, and officials returned to it repeatedly.

The debate began in earnest on Hispaniola. In December 1511 the Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos preached a sermon in Santo Domingo condemning the colonists' treatment of Indigenous people and asking by what right they waged war on peaceful communities and exploited them. The sermon scandalized colonists and prompted the Crown to convene jurists, resulting in the Laws of Burgos of 1512 and 1513, which regulated the encomienda, set standards for housing, food, and treatment, limited the labor of women and children, and required religious instruction, but did not abolish the system. In 1513 the jurist Juan López de Palacios Rubios composed the Requerimiento, a text to be read to Indigenous communities before hostilities. It summarized Christian history, asserted papal and royal authority, and demanded that listeners acknowledge the Church and the Crown, warning that refusal would justify war and enslavement. It was commonly read in Spanish, often without interpreters, and sometimes at a distance or at night, and contemporaries ridiculed it. It functioned mainly as a legal ritual to protect the conscience of the conqueror and the legal position of the expedition.

Bartolomé de las Casas, who arrived in the Caribbean in 1502, held an encomienda, and participated in the conquest of Cuba, experienced a change of conviction around 1514, renounced his grant, and became the most prominent advocate for Indigenous rights. He joined the Dominican order in 1522, wrote extensively, and in 1544 became bishop of Chiapas. In his Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies, presented in 1542 and published in 1552, he catalogued Spanish atrocities in vivid and sometimes exaggerated language. His opponents included colonists, encomenderos, and scholars such as Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, a humanist and Aristotelian who argued, drawing on Aristotle's idea of natural slavery, that war against Indigenous peoples was justified by their alleged barbarism, idolatry, and violations of natural law, and that it was a legitimate means of establishing Christian rule.

The confrontation between Las Casas and Sepúlveda was staged before a panel of theologians and jurists at Valladolid in 1550 and 1551. It is often described, misleadingly, as a debate on whether Indigenous people were fully human. Neither party questioned their humanity, and the pope had declared in the bull Sublimis Deus of 1537 that they were rational beings capable of faith and entitled to liberty and property. The actual issues were narrower and in some ways more consequential: whether war could be waged against Indigenous communities before peaceful preaching, whether their religious practices justified conquest, what degree of political authority Spanish rulers could claim, and how Indigenous labor could be regulated. The Salamanca theologian Francisco de Vitoria had explored similar questions in lectures of 1539, arguing that Indigenous rulers possessed legitimate dominion and that the Spanish claims could not rest on papal donation alone, but could be justified only on limited grounds such as the right of free travel and preaching. The panel reached no formal verdict. The debates shaped the intellectual climate and gave jurists arguments that later affected colonial law, but conquest and exploitation continued.

His indignation did not prevent him from proposing, at an earlier stage, measures that relied on African labor, and he later expressed regret about that advice. Section 69.25

Colonial Administration: Viceroys, Councils, Audiencias, and Local Government

The American territories were possessions of the Crown of Castile, not of Spain as a unified state, and their government was built through a series of improvised institutions that gradually acquired a more systematic form. In Spain, the Casa de Contratación, founded in Seville in 1503, regulated shipping, trade, and the registration of passengers. The Council of the Indies, which took shape in the early 1520s and was formally established in 1524, advised the king on legislation, appointments, and justice for the Indies. The Crown's authority over the church in the Indies was reinforced by the patronato real, a series of papal concessions that gave the king rights of nomination and control over ecclesiastical revenues, and which made missionaries and bishops, in part, agents of the Crown.

In the Americas, the key institution was the audiencia, a high court that also exercised administrative functions. Santo Domingo received the first in 1511, followed by Mexico in 1527 and 1528, Panama in 1538, Lima in 1543, and Guatemala in 1543, with others later in the century. Audiencias heard appeals, supervised officials, and defended royal authority against the ambitions of governors and conquerors. The Crown also appointed viceroys, representatives of the king with broad powers: Antonio de Mendoza became the first viceroy of New Spain in 1535, and Peru received its first viceroy in 1544, though the post was effective only after Toledo. Beneath them, governors and provincial officials called corregidores administered districts, collected tribute, and settled disputes, and municipal councils, called cabildos, governed Spanish towns, often under the influence of local property owners.

The system depended on paper and distance. Royal officials communicated through letters and reports that took months to cross the ocean. Officials were inspected by visitas, and their conduct was reviewed in a residencia at the end of their term. These mechanisms made the bureaucracy accountable in theory, but they could not prevent corruption, collusion with local elites, or neglect of orders. A common formula, "I obey but do not comply," allowed officials to acknowledge a royal command while delaying its execution if it seemed unworkable or unjust, a practice that reconciled loyalty to the Crown with local conditions. Royal authority was therefore real but constantly negotiated, and conquerors, officials, clergy, and Indigenous communities all used the legal system to advance their interests.

Indigenous communities occupied a recognized legal category. The Crown treated Indigenous people as free vassals who owed tribute but were entitled to protection, and Spanish law gave them access to courts, including a legal protector, and Indigenous towns had their own councils. Spanish jurists described a "republic of Indians," separate from the "republic of Spaniards," a distinction that reflected both an intent to protect Indigenous people and a policy of administrative segregation. In practice, the boundary was porous. Indigenous litigants filed thousands of suits over land, labor, and tribute, and their success shaped colonial law. The overall picture is of a state that was ambitious, legalistic, and dependent on negotiation, whose capacity to control its distant territories was limited, and that served as the template for later phases of Spanish colonial governance examined in connection with the Habsburg monarchy in Chapter 75.

Section 69.26

Encomienda, Repartimiento, Mita, and the Organization of Colonial Labor

Spanish colonists could not sustain themselves or extract wealth without the labor of Indigenous people, and the colonial period saw the development of several systems to mobilize it. The encomienda, discussed in section 69.3, assigned Indigenous communities to a Spaniard who could demand tribute in goods, money, or labor. In central Mexico and Peru, encomiendas drew on existing tribute systems, so that Spanish demands were layered over earlier obligations. Encomenderos lived mostly in towns and relied on Indigenous leaders to collect tribute. Though the law forbade enslavement of those in encomienda, abuses, including excessive demands, physical punishment, and the forced relocation of workers to mines and construction sites, were widespread, and in Mexico and Peru mortality in some encomiendas was extremely high.

The Crown regarded powerful encomenderos as a threat to its authority and sought to limit their rights. In 1542, influenced by Las Casas and by reports of abuse, Charles V approved the New Laws of the Indies, which are examined in the source window below. They prohibited the enslavement of Indigenous people, forbade new encomiendas, and provided that existing grants would revert to the Crown at the death of the holder. In Peru, the rebellion of Gonzalo Pizarro followed. In Mexico, Viceroy Mendoza and Franciscan friars advised suspending enforcement. In 1545 the Crown revoked the clause ending inheritance, preserving the encomienda for a generation or two, though it gradually declined as a source of private power and was reduced to a fiscal grant of tribute.

As encomienda weakened and populations fell, other systems developed. In New Spain the repartimiento, known in Nahuatl as coatequitl, was a rotating draft in which Indigenous communities supplied a fixed proportion of adult men for periods of work, administered by officials and caciques, for agriculture, construction, and mining. In Peru, the Spanish adapted the Inca mit'a, a rotational labor service that communities were already accustomed to providing. Under Toledo, from 1573, a system of mining mit'a required Andean communities in sixteen provinces to send roughly one seventh of their adult men each year to work at Potosí, a conscription that disrupted families and villages, with harsh conditions in the mines and mortality from accidents and mercury poisoning. Enslaved Africans and free wage laborers were also employed, particularly in sugar and urban crafts, and in the later sixteenth century wage labor grew, in part because debt and need pushed Indigenous people to take employment.

Indigenous people negotiated within these systems. Communities used petitions and litigation to reduce tribute assessments, to challenge illegal labor demands, and to demand fair pay. Caciques and kurakas mediated between community and Spaniards, sometimes defending their people and sometimes profiting. Women performed labor in textile production, household service, and agriculture, and were often required to produce cloth as part of tribute. The legal reforms of the sixteenth century did not end coercion but did give Indigenous litigants arguments that they could deploy, and they ensured that forced labor was a subject of continuing public debate within the Spanish empire.

Indigenous communities used its principles in petitions, and its ban on Indigenous slavery had effects in many regions, though enslavement continued on frontiers and under other labels. Section 69.27

Christian Missions, Religious Conversion, and Indigenous Religious Life

Evangelization was the principal religious justification of conquest and a central concern of colonial institutions. Missionary orders came to the Americas in waves. In Hispaniola the Dominicans, Franciscans, and Hieronymites arrived in the early sixteenth century. In 1524 twelve Franciscans, whose leader Martín de Valencia led a symbolic group meant to recall the apostles, reached Mexico, followed by Dominicans in 1526 and Augustinians in 1533. The Jesuits, who had been founded in 1540 and are discussed in Chapter 65, reached Brazil in 1549, Peru in 1568, and Mexico in 1572. These religious orders, trained in European universities and influenced by humanism and reform currents, brought ideals of Christian renewal to the Americas.

In central Mexico, friars used mass baptisms, which in the 1520s and 1530s reportedly numbered in the thousands, destroyed temples and images, and built churches, often on sites of earlier religious significance. They founded schools for the sons of Indigenous nobles, including the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco in 1536, where students learned Latin, Spanish, and Christian doctrine and later collaborated with friars on projects such as the Florentine Codex. Franciscans such as Bernardino de Sahagún studied Nahuatl and Nahua religion with the aim of eliminating idolatry, and in doing so preserved a great deal of knowledge. At the same time, some friars used coercion. In Yucatán in 1562, Bishop Diego de Landa, a Franciscan, conducted an inquisitorial campaign against Maya religious practices in which many Maya were tortured and Maya books were burned, an act that caused irreplaceable loss, though Landa also wrote an influential description of the Maya.

Indigenous reactions varied. Some embraced Christianity, drawn by the friars' teachings, the ritual and artistic appeal of the church, or the hope of protection. Others resisted, hid images, or maintained older practices in private. Many created combinations. Nahua communities adopted saints as patrons of their altepetl, formed confraternities that organized festivals and funerals, and absorbed Christian symbols into their own cosmology. Indigenous artists and craftsmen worked on churches and painted sacred images. The devotion to the Virgin of Guadalupe at Tepeyac near Mexico City, which became central to Mexican religious identity, is documented from the mid sixteenth century, though the narrative of the apparition to Juan Diego in 1531 appears in writing only in the seventeenth century and its early history remains debated.

In the Andes, extirpation campaigns attempted to destroy huacas and religious specialists, and communities sometimes concealed ancestral mummies, as the Taki Onqoy movement of the 1560s shows. Spanish clergy debated whether Indigenous people were capable of full participation in the church. In 1571, after the establishment of the Inquisition in the Indies, Indigenous people were exempted from its jurisdiction, on the grounds that they were new to the faith and in need of instruction rather than trial. Conversion was not a single event but a long process that involved negotiation, misunderstanding, and creativity, and that produced the regional forms of Christianity, now often described by historians as syncretic, though the term itself is debated because it can imply a simple mixture rather than the active construction of new religious worlds.

Living under the New Colonial Order, 1520s to 1570s

From the perspective of an Indigenous noble ally, such as a Tlaxcalan lord: The alliance with the Spaniards had been a political decision to defeat a hated overlord. From the perspective of a Spanish conquistador and encomendero: Years of risk and hardship had earned a reward in land, tribute, and status, and the Crown's attempts to limit grants appeared as ingratitude. Encomenderos defended their rights in petitions that listed services rendered, even as they depended on Indigenous intermediaries and on a Crown that could confirm or revoke their holdings.
From the perspective of a missionary friar: The conversion of Indigenous peoples was both a religious duty and an opportunity to create a purer Christian society. Friars could be protectors of Indigenous communities against encomenderos, and also agents of cultural destruction when they burned images, demanded obedience, or punished religious practices.
From the perspective of a royal official: The task was to enforce the Crown's laws, collect revenues, and maintain order among competing interest groups, with limited information and slow communication. Officials negotiated between the demands of Madrid, the pressure of colonists, and the petitions of Indigenous communities.
From the perspective of an Indigenous commoner or enslaved worker: Daily life was shaped by tribute, labor drafts, disease, and the loss of family members. Commoners had few direct means to influence policy, but they used flight, work slowdowns, litigation through community leaders, and the preservation of customs to resist and to survive. Enslaved Africans, present in growing numbers, faced similar coercion without the legal protections that Indigenous vassals sometimes claimed.
Section 69.28

Colonial Cities, Families, Social Hierarchies, and Cultural Change

Colonial cities were instruments of conquest and centers of cultural exchange. Spanish founders laid out towns on a grid around a central plaza, with the church, town hall, and houses of leading settlers arranged around it, a pattern encouraged by royal ordinances. In Mexico City, built over the ruins of Tenochtitlan, Spanish buildings occupied the center, while Indigenous districts such as San Juan Tenochtitlan and Santiago Tlatelolco, governed by Indigenous officials, lay around it. Lima, founded in 1535, and Puebla de los Ángeles, founded in 1531 as a town intended for Spanish farmers, were created as Spanish centers. Indigenous people nevertheless lived and worked in all of them, as servants, craftsmen, laborers, and market traders, and cities became places of interaction, tension, and cultural blending.

The social order emphasized distinctions among Spaniards, Indigenous people, and Africans, but it was fluid. Spanish men outnumbered Spanish women among early settlers, though the proportion of women among emigrants rose over the century. Many Spanish men formed unions with Indigenous women, some through marriage, many through concubinage or coercion. Their children, called mestizos, held an ambiguous place. The most famous example is Martín Cortés, son of Cortés and Malintzin, but the group also included many children who were raised in Indigenous households, in Spanish households, or in neither. Indigenous noble women, such as daughters of Motecuhzoma, married Spaniards and passed on land and status. Africans, enslaved and free, worked as domestic servants, artisans, mine laborers, and soldiers. Juan Garrido, a free man of African origin, participated in expeditions in the Caribbean and Mexico, and Africans formed confraternities and communities in colonial cities.

Language and culture changed. Nahuatl became a lingua franca in much of Mesoamerica, used by missionaries and Spanish officials, and Quechua served similarly in the Andes. Spanish, introduced by the colonists and the Church, spread more slowly. Indigenous scribes learned alphabetic writing and produced documents in their own languages. Women of various backgrounds held significant responsibilities in households and in markets, and Castilian law gave women certain property rights, which some Indigenous women learned to use. Food, dress, and architecture mixed Spanish and Indigenous elements, and Indigenous artisans produced goods for Spanish tastes.

Historians caution against reading later categories back onto this period. The elaborate hierarchy of "castas," the legal and social classification of mixed ancestry, was elaborated in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the "caste paintings" that illustrate it date from the eighteenth century. In the sixteenth century, categories such as indio, español, and negro were used, but the boundaries were loosely defined, and a person's standing depended on wealth, occupation, reputation, and connections, a concept Spaniards later called calidad. In the first decades after conquest, social identities were being formed and negotiated rather than inherited from a fixed system.

Everyday Life and Social History

Two Communities: A Nahua Town and a New Spanish Settlement, c. 1550

A Nahua community in the countryside of central Mexico in the mid sixteenth century remained organized around the altepetl, with a governor and council drawn from the local nobility, and with families farming maize, beans, squash, and chiles on plots held by kin groups. Food remained largely Indigenous, with tortillas and maize gruel at the center of the diet, supplemented by foods such as turkey and fish. Women ground maize, cooked, wove cloth, and often raised chickens and pigs introduced by the Spanish. Tribute, however, now had to be paid in coin or goods demanded by Spanish officials, and men were subject to labor drafts that took them away to mines or construction for weeks. Epidemics, repeated in the 1540s and 1570s, killed many, and surviving families concentrated in fewer villages. The church dominated the center of the settlement, and confraternities organized festivals for patron saints, combining Christian ritual with community obligations. Indigenous officials wrote wills and petitions in Nahuatl, using Spanish legal forms to protect land.

A new Spanish settlement, such as Puebla or an emerging mining town, was a different world. Settlers, mostly men from Andalusia, Extremadura, and Castile, built houses of adobe and stone, planted wheat and vines, raised cattle and sheep, and operated workshops. Households included Spanish families, Indigenous servants, enslaved Africans, and mestizo children, with women often managing households and sometimes businesses. Diet blended Spanish bread, wine, and beef with American maize and beans. Disease and mortality were also severe, though less than among Indigenous communities. Religion centered on parish churches and convents. The settlers depended on Indigenous labor drafts and on Indigenous markets for food, and they lived amid a much larger Indigenous population that was both necessary and feared. Comparing the two communities shows both separation and constant contact, and it reveals how family, food, labor, and religion were reshaped on both sides.

Section 69.29

Tribute, Mining, and the Economic Foundations of Colonial Empire

Early Spanish colonial wealth came from plunder, tribute, and gold. After the first decades, the economy of the Spanish Indies increasingly depended on silver. In 1545 an Indigenous man, according to tradition, found silver at Potosí in the high Andes, in a mountain later known as the Cerro Rico, and in 1546 silver was discovered at Zacatecas in northern New Spain, followed by other discoveries. Potosí grew into one of the largest cities in the Americas, with a population estimated at well over a hundred thousand by about 1600, living in an altitude of more than four thousand meters, a mining boomtown of Spaniards, Indigenous workers, Africans, merchants, and hangers on.

Technological and organizational change increased output. In Mexico, a process for refining silver using mercury, the patio or amalgamation process, was developed in the 1550s, and it was adopted in Peru in the 1570s, with mercury from the mines at Huancavelica. The mines required large amounts of labor, wood, and food. Indigenous workers supplied by the mita at Potosí, and by repartimiento and wage labor in Mexico, extracted and processed ore, and mule trains carried silver to ports. Mining stimulated agriculture, textiles, and transport, and led to the growth of towns, ranches, and roads.

The Crown claimed a fifth of the silver produced, the quinto real, and collected other taxes, so mining became a major source of royal revenue. Silver was shipped to Seville in fleets organized from the 1560s into the convoy system called the flota, to protect against pirates and privateers. These shipments strengthened the finances of Charles V and Philip II, who used them to fund wars in Europe. The Crown borrowed heavily from bankers, including Genoese and German firms, against anticipated silver, and it suspended payments to creditors several times, in 1557, 1575, and 1596, in what historians call bankruptcies. Silver also moved to Asia via the Manila galleons from 1565 and by European merchants who reexported it, a theme examined in Chapter 71.

Tribute from Indigenous communities supported local colonial administration and Spanish elites. Authorities assessed tribute at set levels, but the amounts rose and fell with changes in population, and communities complained that assessments did not keep pace with deaths. Spaniards also developed commercial agriculture, textile workshops called obrajes, and trade. The full analysis of how American silver and colonial commerce affected European prices, trade, and state finance belongs to Chapter 73. For now, the important point is that by the 1570s the Spanish colonial economy rested on a combination of Indigenous tribute and labor, mining, and the Atlantic trade controlled from Seville, and that these elements linked the lives of Andean miners to the credit markets of Antwerp, Genoa, and Augsburg.

Division VI · The Columbian Exchange and the Remaking of Biological Worlds · Section 69.30

Disease and the First Great American Epidemics

Historical Vocabulary
The Columbian Exchange

The Columbian Exchange is the term that the historian Alfred Crosby introduced in 1972 for the transfer of plants, animals, pathogens, and people between the Americas and the Afro-Eurasian world that followed the voyages of Columbus. The term is shorthand for a diverse set of biological and ecological processes, which unfolded over centuries and involved Africa and Asia as well as Europe and the Americas. It does not imply that the exchange was balanced or voluntary, since the movement of diseases and enslaved people had devastating human consequences, and "exchange" can obscure unequal power. It also does not mean that introduction equaled adoption. A species might arrive in a new region decades or centuries before it became important to farming or diet. This chapter uses the term to describe the transformation of biological worlds, while keeping attention on human decisions and on the people affected.

The Americas had been isolated from Afro-Eurasia for many thousands of years, and the populations that lived there had not been exposed to several infectious diseases that had circulated for centuries in the Old World, including smallpox, measles, and influenza. When Europeans and Africans carried these pathogens across the Atlantic, they encountered populations who lacked prior exposure, a condition often described as a "virgin soil" epidemic. Historians use that phrase cautiously, since it can imply that biology alone explains mortality, whereas the severity of epidemics depended heavily on social conditions, including hunger, war, forced labor, displacement, and the collapse of care for the sick, factors that in Europe also magnified the effects of the Black Death, discussed in Chapter 50.

The earliest epidemics are poorly documented. An outbreak on Hispaniola in the 1490s, perhaps influenza, is mentioned in Spanish sources, though its identification is uncertain. In late 1518 and 1519, smallpox struck Hispaniola with great severity, killing many Indigenous people and spreading to nearby islands. In 1520 it reached Mexico, where it accompanied the Narváez expedition, as discussed in section 69.10, and spread through central Mexico and southward. The epidemic apparently reached Guatemala by 1520 or 1521, where Kaqchikel annals record its terrible effects, and may have moved through the Andes by about 1524 to 1527, before Pizarro's arrival, though this is much debated, since the evidence is indirect. Measles followed in the early 1530s, and further epidemics of typhus, influenza, mumps, and other diseases appeared in subsequent decades. A devastating epidemic that Nahuatl speakers called cocoliztli struck central Mexico in 1545 to 1548 and again in 1576 to 1581. Its cause remains debated. Some scholars have identified it with a form of hemorrhagic fever or with a bacterial infection, and an analysis of ancient DNA from burials has been interpreted as showing salmonella, an identification that is itself contested.

Disease did not spread evenly. Densely populated areas and trade routes carried infections quickly, while isolated communities were sometimes spared for years. Epidemics repeated in waves, and each wave killed people who had been born since the previous one or had escaped it. They disrupted agriculture and trade, killed rulers and elders, and interrupted religious and political practices. For conquest, epidemics had varied effects. In Mexico, smallpox weakened the Mexica leadership at a critical time. In the Andes, the disruption of imperial succession by epidemic disease may have contributed to civil war. In other regions, disease spread far in advance of Spanish troops, so that conquerors often encountered communities already reduced and in disarray. In regions where Indigenous societies were already resistant to colonial rule, as among the Mapuche, disease had a more limited effect on the course of war. Disease was thus a major cause of conquest, but only in combination with other factors.

The reverse movement of disease is more contentious. The origin of syphilis, which appeared in Europe in the 1490s with great virulence, has been debated for centuries. Some scholars argue that it was brought from the Americas by Columbus's crews, while others suggest that an existing treponemal disease evolved into a new form in Europe, and the evidence from skeletal remains has not settled the question. Later, in the seventeenth century, yellow fever and malaria, both of African or Old World origin, became major diseases in the American tropics, with significant consequences for colonization and for the plantation societies discussed in Chapter 70.

Section 69.31

Demographic Catastrophe: Mortality, Displacement, and Historical Estimates

That the Indigenous population of the Americas declined dramatically in the sixteenth century is not in dispute. How large it was before contact and how much of it was lost are matters on which estimates differ widely. Historians reconstruct population from several kinds of evidence: Spanish tribute registers and censuses, which are partial and often politically motivated; church records of baptisms and burials; descriptions by missionaries and officials; archaeological surveys of settlement size and density; and, increasingly, genetic and environmental data. Questions of method are central, because early counts were made for tribute and could undercount or overcount, and because estimates of the pre contact population usually require extrapolation from later figures and assumptions about rates of decline.

Disagreements are large. In the mid twentieth century, scholars such as Ángel Rosenblat proposed relatively low estimates, around thirteen million for the whole of the Americas in 1492. In the 1960s and 1970s, researchers at Berkeley, notably Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah, proposed much higher figures, suggesting for central Mexico alone populations of twenty five million and more and for Hispaniola several million, using back projections from tribute counts. These very high estimates have been criticized as resting on assumptions about steep rates of decline, and many scholars now favor intermediate figures, such as those proposed by William Denevan, who estimated roughly fifty to sixty million people in the Americas, though his figures have also been revised and debated. For central Mexico, estimates of the pre conquest population range from a few million to more than twenty million, with a population of about one to one and a half million by the early seventeenth century. For the Andes, estimates are similarly uncertain, ranging from several million to over ten million before the conquest. Hispaniola's Indigenous population, as noted, is estimated at anywhere from a few hundred thousand to well over a million.

Despite this uncertainty, scholars broadly agree on the pattern: very steep declines, in many regions on the order of eighty or ninety percent over a century or so, that varied significantly by place. The Caribbean islands lost most of their Indigenous population within a few decades, while many highland regions of Mexico and the Andes lost the majority in the course of the century and continued to decline into the seventeenth. Peripheral regions with low population density experienced later and sometimes lower losses, though some, such as Amazonian and Brazilian communities, suffered heavily from slave raiding and disease. A recent study that used estimates of land abandonment to link the reforestation of the Americas to a decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide and a cooling episode during the Little Ice Age has been influential and criticized in turn, and it exemplifies the way demographic reconstruction is entangled with other debates.

Explaining mortality requires attention to multiple interacting causes. Epidemic disease was a principal factor, but so were warfare, massacres, enslavement, forced labor in mines and on estates, forced relocation, famine caused by the disruption of agriculture and by war, and a fall in birth rates resulting from separation of families, stress, and death among women and children. Some historians emphasize the role of violent and exploitative colonial institutions and argue that terms such as genocide describe the Spanish treatment of Indigenous peoples in particular episodes, while others point to the role of disease and note that Spanish authorities depended on Indigenous labor and did not plan to exterminate it. This chapter does not attempt to settle those debates but stresses that the choice of terms rests on the definition used, and that the demographic collapse resulted from the interaction of biological and human factors, with responsibility distributed across different actors and events.

Cause and Effect

The Interacting Causes of Conquest and Demographic Catastrophe

Long-term causes

The long isolation of American populations from Old World pathogens; Iberian experience of frontier conquest and Atlantic colonization; Indigenous rivalries, tribute systems, and political strains within the Mexica and Inca empires; and European maritime and military capacity developed in the fifteenth century.

Short-term causes

Spanish demand for gold, land, and labor; the establishment of Caribbean bases; the decisions of Indigenous leaders to ally with, resist, or negotiate with newcomers; the arrival of smallpox in 1518 to 1520; and the succession crises in Tenochtitlan and Tawantinsuyu.

Event or transformation

Conquest campaigns in the Caribbean, Mexico, Central America, and the Andes, and the establishment of colonial institutions of tribute, forced labor, and mission.

Immediate effects

Destruction of capitals and ruling institutions; looting and enslavement; epidemic mortality; hunger from disrupted agriculture; flight and displacement; and the reorganization of communities under Spanish officials and clergy.

Long-term consequences

Population collapse that lasted for generations; the demand for new sources of labor, including enslaved Africans; the creation of colonial societies with mixed populations; and the opening of the silver economy that linked the Americas to Europe and Asia.

Historical complexity

Epidemic disease alone cannot explain either the conquests or the demographic collapse. Violence, forced labor, famine, and displacement interacted with disease, and their effects varied by region, by Indigenous political strategies, and by the policies of colonial authorities.

Historical complexity: Treating disease as the sole explanation for Indigenous defeat can obscure the roles of Indigenous decision making and of Spanish coercion, while ignoring disease would omit one of the largest mortality events in recorded history. Good historical explanation holds these factors together.

Section 69.32

Animals and Crops from Afro-Eurasia to the Americas

Spanish colonizers brought familiar animals and plants, which they considered essential to a civilized life and a reliable diet, and these species altered American landscapes. On Columbus's second voyage in 1493, ships carried horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, and chickens, along with wheat, vines, sugar cane, and other seeds and cuttings. These were multiplied in the Caribbean and then shipped to the mainland. Horses, in particular, became prized in warfare and travel and, by the later sixteenth century, were bred in New Spain and the Andes. Indigenous people also acquired horses, and by the late sixteenth century groups such as the Mapuche used them effectively in war. Further north, horse cultures on the Great Plains developed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, beyond the chronological scope of this chapter, but rooted in the spread of horses from Spanish settlements.

Livestock reshaped the economy. Cattle and sheep reproduced rapidly on open grasslands without natural predators, and herds multiplied in Hispaniola, Mexico, and Peru. Pigs became feral and spread through forests. Ranching became an important economic activity, producing hides, tallow, and meat for local consumption and for export, and mules and donkeys supplied transport for silver and goods. Many Indigenous communities adopted new animals, raising chickens and pigs in household economies, weaving wool from sheep, and using oxen for plowing, but livestock also damaged Indigenous fields, as wandering herds trampled crops, and ranchers claimed Indigenous lands for pasture.

Crops from the Old World also found places in the Americas. Wheat was cultivated in Mexico by the 1520s and became important in highland valleys and in Peru and Chile, supplying bread that Spanish colonists considered essential. Vineyards and olives were planted in Mexico, Peru, and Chile, though the Crown at times restricted their production to protect metropolitan producers. Sugar cane, introduced to Hispaniola in 1493 and cultivated in mills from the early sixteenth century, spread to Mexico and Brazil, where it would become the basis of plantation economies, linked to the enslaved labor examined in Chapter 70. Citrus fruits, bananas, rice, and melons also became widespread. Indigenous communities selectively adopted these crops, sometimes integrating them into traditional farming, and in some regions the new plants complemented rather than replaced staples such as maize, beans, squash, potatoes, and manioc.

The introduction of species reshaped food, transport, warfare, and work, but its effects were not uniform. Horses transformed warfare in some regions and had little role in the tropical forests. Livestock favored Spanish estates and pastoral communities and displaced Indigenous farmers, and the gradual shift from cropland to pasture, as Indigenous populations declined, changed land use patterns. In Indigenous communities, the use of new species depended on local ecology, labor, and choice, so that adoption often represented a creative adaptation and not simply an imposition.

Section 69.33

American Crops and Their Introduction into Europe

American plants began to arrive in Europe in the first decades after 1492, and over the following centuries they altered European diets, agriculture, and consumption. Their path, however, was uneven. Many plants were first grown as curiosities in botanical gardens or as medicines, and acceptance as food depended on taste, cultural preferences, agricultural conditions, and social status. This distinction between introduction and adoption is crucial for avoiding the assumption that the arrival of a crop in Europe immediately transformed European life.

Maize was among the earliest to arrive, probably brought by Columbus in 1493, and cultivated in southern Spain in the early sixteenth century. In Europe it was initially used as animal feed and as a garden crop, and its role as a human staple grew slowly, in regions such as northwestern Spain and Portugal, northern Italy, where polenta became common, and parts of southeastern Europe, where it spread under Ottoman rule. Potatoes, native to the Andes, were brought to Spain by the 1560s and 1570s, with records showing their purchase for a hospital in Seville in 1573. They were met with suspicion and were grown in gardens, used by the poor, and fed to animals, before slowly becoming important in northern Europe. Their role as a staple food, notably in Ireland, developed largely in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the nineteenth century dependence of Irish and other populations on the potato, with the catastrophic consequences of blight discussed in Part XV, was the result of later social and economic conditions rather than an immediate result of sixteenth century introduction. Chapter 85 examines the role of agricultural change in the demographic growth of the eighteenth century.

Tomatoes, from Mexico, were known in Spain and Italy by the mid sixteenth century, where an Italian botanist described them in 1544, but they were initially grown for ornament or medicine, and they became important to Mediterranean cuisines only gradually. Peppers, known as chiles in Mexico, spread rapidly because they provided a strong flavor that could substitute for imported spices and could be easily grown. Beans of the Phaseolus type were adopted widely and complemented existing legumes. Turkeys, domesticated in Mexico, were adopted as poultry in Europe with great speed in the sixteenth century. Cacao, which Mesoamericans consumed as a bitter beverage, was introduced to Spain in the sixteenth century and, sweetened with sugar and flavored with spices, became a fashionable drink among elites, with its use spreading to other courts in the seventeenth century. Tobacco, adopted in Europe from the mid sixteenth century, was promoted by figures such as the French ambassador Jean Nicot, who sent seeds to France in the 1560s, and was used medicinally before becoming a recreational commodity.

These plants altered European life, but their effects were mediated by markets, empire, and culture. Some, such as tobacco and cacao, became commodities in transatlantic trade, produced increasingly in plantations that depended on enslaved labor, a development examined in Chapters 70 and 73. Others, such as maize and the potato, contributed to agricultural intensification and population growth, though much later. The pace of adoption varied according to region, social class, and local conditions, which shows that the Columbian Exchange did not impose a single pattern of change on Europe.

Section 69.34

The Columbian Exchange beyond Europe and the Americas

The Columbian Exchange was global, not only transatlantic. Portuguese and Spanish ships carried American plants across the Atlantic to Africa and across the Indian and Pacific Oceans to Asia, and the commercial networks discussed in Chapter 68 and Chapter 71 provided the channels. The Portuguese, who held trading posts on the African coasts, in India, and in Southeast Asia, introduced crops to places they traded with, and local farmers, traders, and rulers adopted them according to their own needs.

In Africa, maize and cassava, also known as manioc, were among the most significant introductions. Maize reached West Africa and the Kongo region in the sixteenth century, and cassava, a root crop from Brazil that tolerates poor soils and drought, spread through the Kongo and Angola from the late sixteenth century and later through much of tropical Africa. Peanuts, sweet potatoes, and chili peppers were also adopted. These crops had important effects on diets and farming, though their influence took time to develop, and historians debate how far they affected population in Africa, including during the era of the slave trade.

In Asia, American crops spread through multiple routes. The Portuguese brought chili peppers to Goa and thence across India, and they became a central feature of South Asian cuisines. After the Spanish established Manila in 1571 and the galleon route across the Pacific from 1565, goods and plants moved between Mexico, the Philippines, and China. Sweet potatoes, maize, and peanuts were introduced to southern China in the sixteenth century, and in time they allowed cultivation of marginal and upland lands and contributed to later population growth, though the scale and chronology of that influence are still debated. Tobacco spread rapidly across Asia and the Ottoman world in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. In the opposite direction, American silver traveled across the Pacific, creating one of the first truly global commercial links.

Looking at the Columbian Exchange on this global scale shows its significance for world history. It created new networks of biological and commercial connection and changed the food supplies of every continent. It also illustrates how Spanish and Portuguese maritime power, discussed in the preceding chapter, produced consequences that went far beyond the empires that created it, and how African, Asian, and American farmers and merchants made their own decisions about what to grow and to eat. The exchange was therefore not just a European project but a global process shaped by many actors.

Section 69.35

Environmental Transformation and the New Colonial Landscapes

The conquest and the Columbian Exchange reshaped American environments. Chapter 2 stressed that European landscapes had been modified by human activity over millennia, and the same was true of the Americas, where Indigenous farmers had terraced slopes, built raised fields and irrigation works, managed forests with fire, and shaped the vegetation of many regions. The arrival of the Spanish and Portuguese did not introduce human influence into a pristine landscape but changed the kind and direction of that influence.

One major change was the spread of livestock. As Indigenous populations declined, fields and terraces were abandoned, and Spanish ranchers moved herds into former agricultural land. In central Mexico, for example, the Mezquital valley, home to Otomí farmers, was turned over to sheep in the sixteenth century, and its vegetation and soil changed. In some areas, erosion followed the abandonment of terraces and the grazing of animals on steep slopes. Elsewhere, fields returned to forest, an effect that some researchers link to large scale regrowth, though the extent and effects of this regrowth are debated.

Mining and plantation agriculture also transformed landscapes. Silver mining at Potosí and Zacatecas demanded wood for fuel and timbers, so that forests near mining towns were cut over wide areas, and refining with mercury poisoned workers and polluted rivers. Sugar plantations in Hispaniola and later Brazil required clearing of forest, and the harvesting of brazilwood reduced stands of that species along the Brazilian coast. The Atlantic Forest of Brazil was progressively cleared for plantations. Introduced plants and animals, including weeds and rats, spread through colonial landscapes, sometimes displacing local species.

Indigenous communities responded by adapting. Some preserved agricultural systems such as chinampas near Mexico City, which continued to supply food for the capital, while others adopted European tools and crops. But the new landscapes were shaped by Spanish priorities, such as the concentration of population in towns, the supply of mines, and the production of export commodities. Environmental change thus affected the distribution of power, the availability of resources, and the vulnerability of communities to famine and drought. It formed part of the larger story of colonial transformation, in which ecological and political changes were intertwined.

Section 69.36

Human Migration and the Formation of New Atlantic Societies

The conquest produced new movements of people. Spanish migration to the Americas, regulated by the Crown through licenses and recorded in Seville, amounted to a few hundred thousand people over the sixteenth century by common estimates, and most came from Andalusia, Extremadura, and Castile. The Crown formally restricted emigration to subjects of Castile and excluded Muslims, Jews, and recent converts, though some New Christians of Jewish descent traveled to the Indies, and the Inquisition, established in Mexico and Lima in 1571 and 1570, prosecuted people accused of secretly practicing Judaism. Women made up a small proportion of early emigrants, but their numbers grew, reaching perhaps a quarter of recorded passengers in later decades. Portuguese migrants settled in Brazil and also in Spanish America, where their commercial networks proved significant.

Indigenous people were also moved. Spanish expeditions took allies and porters over long distances, such as the Nahua and Tlaxcalan warriors who accompanied Alvarado to Guatemala and later settlers sent to the northern frontier. Slave raids carried Indigenous people from Nicaragua to Peru and from the Bahamas to Hispaniola. Resettlement policies such as the reducciones moved communities within their regions, and migration to towns and mines drew Indigenous workers far from their homes, changing the composition of mining districts and cities.

Africans arrived in increasing numbers, as slaves, and also as free people. By the late sixteenth century, thousands of enslaved Africans had been brought to Spanish America, including Mexico, Peru, and the Caribbean, and to Brazil, where the numbers rose sharply after the 1570s. Estimates of the total number carried across the Atlantic during the century are in the low hundreds of thousands, depending on how destinations and Atlantic islands are counted, and numbers grew dramatically in the seventeenth century. These people brought their languages, skills, and cultural traditions, and they formed communities, built confraternities, resisted through flight and rebellion, and established maroon settlements. Their story, and the commercial and financial structures that supported it, will be taken up in Chapter 70. Migration from Asia also began, with ships from Manila carrying merchants, sailors, and enslaved people to Mexico after 1565.

The emergence of new societies resulted from these movements. Cultural contact produced new languages, foods, religious practices, and family forms, and communities formed through marriage, kinship, and work. These societies were marked by inequality, violence, and the dominance of Spanish and Portuguese institutions, but they were also creative, and they included Indigenous, African, European, and mixed communities who negotiated their positions. The Atlantic world that resulted linked Europe, Africa, and the Americas through trade, migration, and shared and contested institutions, a world that Chapters 70 to 73 will explore.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Institutions, Religion, Family, Agriculture, and Authority before and after Conquest

What Changed

  • The imperial structures of the Triple Alliance and Tawantinsuyu were destroyed or replaced by Spanish institutions, and rulers lost sovereignty
  • Populations declined drastically, and many communities were relocated, merged, or abandoned
  • Tribute and labor were redirected to Spanish encomenderos, officials, and the Crown, and mining labor demands created new forms of coercion
  • Christianity became the public religion, and Indigenous temples and priesthoods were suppressed
  • New crops, animals, tools, and markets altered agriculture and diet, and Spanish became a language of law and administration

What Stayed the Same

  • Local communities such as altepetl and ayllus continued as basic units of organization, with Indigenous officials still governing many matters
  • Indigenous languages, including Nahuatl, Quechua, Aymara, and Maya languages, remained the everyday languages of most people
  • Core crops, such as maize, beans, squash, potatoes, and manioc, remained central to diets and agriculture
  • Family and kin structures, local religious practices, calendars, and memories of earlier rulers persisted, often in modified forms
  • Indigenous elites continued to claim status and sometimes retained wealth and leadership roles

Who experienced change most directly? Rulers and nobles of the former empires lost sovereignty, and people in mining districts, in cities, and in the Caribbean experienced the most abrupt transformation. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural communities in the highlands and in remote regions often retained much of their daily life, though still burdened by tribute and disease. Why the pattern matters: The mixture of destruction and persistence shows why colonial societies cannot be understood either as complete replacements of Indigenous worlds or as simple continuations of them.

Division VII · Historical Interpretation and Chapter Synthesis · Section 69.37

Conquest, Exchange, Indigenous Survival, and the Transformation of the Atlantic World

The conquest of the Americas combined military campaigns, political alliances, legal arguments, biological exchange, and the creation of new institutions. The campaigns varied by region. In the Caribbean, concentrated Spanish settlement, tribute, and forced labor met small scale chiefdoms and produced rapid collapse. In Mesoamerica, a coalition of Indigenous allies and Spaniards, aided by epidemic disease, overthrew the Triple Alliance, but Maya and other communities retained independence for generations. In the Andes, a civil war and an ambush at Cajamarca opened a conquest in which Inca resistance continued for decades. On frontiers such as northern Mexico and Chile, mobile and decentralized societies defeated or limited Spanish expansion. In Brazil, the Portuguese worked with and against Tupi communities to establish coastal settlements and a plantation economy.

Colonial rule was contested everywhere. The Crown struggled to control conquerors, who in turn struggled with Indigenous communities that litigated, negotiated, resisted, and adapted. The Church played a dual role as an agent of cultural change and as a protector of Indigenous people. Colonial institutions of tribute, forced labor, and mining mobilized enormous resources and connected the Americas to European finance and the global economy. At the same time, the Columbian Exchange altered diets, ecosystems, and disease environments on every continent that touched the Atlantic or the Pacific. Disease and demographic collapse reshaped Indigenous societies and increased demand for enslaved African labor, linking conquest to the plantation systems examined in the next chapter.

For Europe, these events increased the wealth and ambitions of the Spanish and Portuguese monarchies, supplied silver that financed Habsburg wars, introduced new foods and commodities, and prompted new debates on law, sovereignty, and human rights. They also set precedents for other European powers, as English, French, and Dutch adventurers and merchants studied and challenged the Iberian empires, as Chapter 72 will show. The conquest was not, however, a predetermined consequence of European superiority. It rested on contingent circumstances, Indigenous choices, and the interplay of many forces, and it did not end Indigenous societies, which survived and reshaped the colonial world.

Section 69.38

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

Students reviewing this chapter should organize their understanding around four comparisons. The first compares conquest patterns: Caribbean domination through tribute and labor, Mesoamerican conquest through alliances and siege, Andean conquest through the seizure of a ruler followed by prolonged resistance, and frontier conflicts in which mobility and decentralized politics limited Spanish success. The second compares institutions, from encomienda to repartimiento and mita, and from audiencias to viceroyalties, and asks how far each represented royal policy and how far local negotiation. The third compares ecological exchanges, distinguishing introduction from adoption and region of origin from later center of cultivation. The fourth concerns evidence: the Spanish letters, chronicles, and legal records, the Nahuatl and Maya texts, the Andean writings of Guaman Poma and Garcilaso, and the demographic and archaeological research that allow historians to test and revise older narratives.

Uncertainty remains at several points: the size of pre contact populations, the motives of Indigenous leaders, the precise circumstances of events such as the death of Motecuhzoma II, the origin of some diseases, and the extent to which Spanish legal restraints were enforced. The next chapter, Chapter 70, turns from conquest to the economic and human system that grew alongside it, examining how the transatlantic slave trade and plantation economies developed, how European merchants, financiers, and states participated in them, and how the coerced labor of Africans and their descendants became central to the Atlantic economy.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Atlantic encounters of the late fifteenth century became conquest, colonial rule, and biological transformation. Its central argument is that the conquest of the Americas was neither a single event nor an inevitable European victory. It was a series of regional campaigns and negotiations, shaped by Indigenous politics, European capabilities, introduced disease, coercive institutions, and contingency, and it produced colonial societies in which Indigenous peoples remained active historical participants.

In the Caribbean, Spanish settlement after 1493 turned a voyage of exploration into a regime of tribute, forced labor, and military domination. Taíno chiefdoms, politically divided and unprepared for the intensity of Spanish demands, were overwhelmed by violence, enslavement, hunger, and disease. Leaders such as Caonabó, Anacaona, and Enriquillo resisted in different decades and ways, and Indigenous identities and ancestry persisted despite the collapse. The Caribbean also provided the institutions, personnel, and logistical base for the mainland conquests, and it was the setting for the earliest African presence in Spanish America.

In Mexico, the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 resulted from the interaction of Spanish and Indigenous forces. Cortés exploited the grievances of Totonacs, Tlaxcalans, and other communities against Mexica overlordship, and the allied armies, which vastly outnumbered the Spaniards, were decisive. The Spaniards nearly lost everything in 1520, and the smallpox epidemic and the deaths of Mexica leaders changed the balance. After the fall of the capital, conquest of other regions continued unevenly, and Maya polities resisted for generations, the Itza of Nojpetén until 1697.

In the Andes, the Inca Empire was weakened by a civil war between Huáscar and Atahualpa when Pizarro's small expedition captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532. Spanish forces, aided by Indigenous groups hostile to the Incas, occupied Cuzco in 1533, but Manco Inca's rebellion in 1536 and the Neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba maintained organized resistance until 1572. Spanish civil wars over land and labor, culminating in the rebellion of Gonzalo Pizarro, reflected conflict between conquerors and the Crown, and the consolidation under Viceroy Toledo created a lasting colonial order.

Regional conquests showed the diversity of outcomes. In Guatemala, Alvarado's campaign with Mexican allies and Kaqchikel cooperation gave way to revolt; Nicaragua was devastated by slaving; in New Granada, Quesada's expedition defeated Muisca rulers before a German company's agent arrived to contest territorial claims; in northern New Spain, silver mining provoked a long war with mobile Chichimeca peoples; in Chile, the Mapuche defeated Spanish armies and preserved autonomy; and in Brazil, the Portuguese, working with and against Tupi communities, established captaincies and a governor general at Salvador in 1549, while moving toward plantations and African slavery.

Colonial institutions turned conquest into government. Debate over the legitimacy of conquest, from Montesinos and the Laws of Burgos to the Requerimiento, Las Casas, Sepúlveda, and the New Laws, shows that Spaniards argued intensely about the legal and moral foundations of empire, even as practice often diverged. The Council of the Indies, audiencias, viceroys, and cabildos administered distant territories through negotiation. The encomienda, repartimiento, and mita organized labor, and mining at Potosí and Zacatecas connected Indigenous labor to the finances of the Habsburg monarchy and to European and Asian markets. Missionary orders reshaped Indigenous religious life, while Indigenous communities produced syncretic and resilient forms of Christianity and preserved much of their knowledge.

The Columbian Exchange transformed the biology of the world. Introduced pathogens, among them smallpox and measles, spread through populations without prior exposure and, combined with warfare, forced labor, hunger, and displacement, produced demographic collapse whose scale remains debated. Horses, cattle, sheep, pigs, wheat, and sugar changed American economies and landscapes, while maize, potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, cacao, tobacco, and other American crops moved to Europe, Africa, and Asia at different speeds. Distinguishing introduction from adoption is essential: the potato's arrival in Spain in the sixteenth century did not immediately make it a European staple.

Historical interpretation of these events has changed. Older accounts emphasized Spanish heroism, technology, or biological advantage. More recent scholarship emphasizes Indigenous agency, alliances, and the contingency of events, and it attends to Indigenous sources such as the Florentine Codex, Maya annals, and Guaman Poma's letter. Debates over population estimates, the causes of mortality, and the language used to describe colonial violence show how historical knowledge is constructed from incomplete evidence and shaped by questions of terminology and moral judgment.

The chapter's themes connect forward. The shortage of Indigenous labor and the expansion of sugar prepared the ground for the Atlantic slave system, which Chapter 70 will examine. The Spanish and Portuguese empires became models and targets for Dutch, British, and French expansion discussed in Chapters 71 and 72. The silver, commodities, and knowledge that crossed the Atlantic affected European finance, consumption, and politics, themes of Chapter 73. For Europe, as for the Americas, Africa, and Asia, the sixteenth century opened a world in which societies were more tightly connected, more unequal, and more deeply interdependent than before.

A Crown grant of the right to tribute and labor from an Indigenous community to a Spanish colonist, in return for obligations of protection and instruction in Christianity.
Repartimiento
In the early Caribbean, the distribution of Indigenous people among colonists; later, a system of rotating draft labor, known in Mexico as coatequitl, supplied by Indigenous communities.
Mita
An Andean rotational labor obligation, taken over and adapted by the Spanish, notably after 1573 to supply workers for the Potosí silver mines.
Requerimiento
A text of 1513 to be read to Indigenous people demanding recognition of the Church and the Spanish Crown before the beginning of hostilities.
Audiencia
A royal high court in the Spanish colonies that also performed administrative functions and checked the power of governors and conquerors.
Viceroyalty
A large colonial jurisdiction governed by a viceroy as the king's representative, first in New Spain from 1535 and in Peru from 1544.
Council of the Indies
The royal council in Spain, formally established in 1524, that advised the king on legislation, appointments, and justice in the American territories.
Altepetl
The Nahua local political community, headed by a ruler and comprising land, people, and institutions, which remained the basic unit of colonial administration in central Mexico.
Tlatoani
The Nahuatl title of a ruler of an altepetl, such as the ruler of Tenochtitlan.
Triple Alliance
The coalition of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan that dominated central Mexico through tribute and conquest before 1521.
Malintzin
A Nahuatl speaking woman who served as interpreter and intermediary for Cortés and whose role has been interpreted in changing ways.
Tawantinsuyu
The Quechua name of the Inca state, meaning "the four parts together."
Ayllu
The basic Andean community or kin group holding land and organizing reciprocal labor.
Kuraka
An Andean community leader who mediated between communities and the Inca and, later, Spanish authorities.
Khipu
A system of knotted cords used in the Andes to record numerical and possibly other information.
Neo-Inca state
The remnant Inca polity at Vilcabamba, founded by Manco Inca and surviving until 1572.
Reducción
A policy of resettling Indigenous communities into compact towns, especially under Toledo in Peru, to ease tribute collection and religious instruction.
Cabildo
A municipal council, Spanish or Indigenous, governing a town and often defending local interests.
Patronato real
The royal patronage of the Church in the Indies, giving the Crown rights over appointments and revenues.
Quinto real
The royal fifth, the Crown's share of mined precious metals and other treasure.
New Laws of 1542
Royal legislation that abolished Indigenous slavery and sought to end the heritability of encomiendas, partly revoked in 1545 after rebellion.
Columbian Exchange
The transfer of plants, animals, pathogens, and people between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after 1492.
Virgin soil epidemic
An epidemic in a population lacking prior exposure to a pathogen, a concept used cautiously because social conditions strongly influenced mortality.
Mestizo
A person of mixed Spanish and Indigenous ancestry, a category whose meaning and status were fluid in the early colonial period.
Captaincy
A hereditary Portuguese grant of territory and governing rights in Brazil from 1534, later complemented by a royal governor general.
Syncretism
The combination of religious elements from different traditions, a term historians use cautiously to describe the creative formation of Indigenous Christianities.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Indigenous and Spanish Accounts of a Conquest Event

This project asks you to investigate one conquest episode by comparing sources produced by different participants, and to evaluate how historians reconstruct events from conflicting evidence.

  1. Choose an episode. Options include the Cholula massacre, the Tóxcatl attack, the siege of Tenochtitlan, the seizure of Atahualpa, the siege of Cuzco, the Mixtón War, or the death of Valdivia.
  2. Locate sources. Using the MGU Library, identify at least two accounts from different perspectives, such as a Spanish letter or chronicle and a Nahua, Tlaxcalan, Maya, or Andean account, and consult published scholarly editions or translations.
  3. Analyze each source. Identify author, date, audience, purpose, and relation to the events, and distinguish eyewitness testimony from later report.
  4. Compare and evaluate. Note points of agreement and disagreement, explain possible reasons for differences, and identify what each source omits.
  5. Write your findings. Prepare a 4 to 6 page essay or equivalent presentation that states a reasoned conclusion about what can be established, what is probable, and what remains uncertain.

Research path (categories only; titles should be verified through the MGU Library catalog): primary sources in reliable translation, including conquerors' letters and chronicles, the Florentine Codex, Maya annals, and Andean writings; major scholarly surveys of colonial Latin America; specialized studies of Nahua, Andean, and Maya communities; historical atlases of the Americas; documentary collections on the Laws of the Indies and the Valladolid debate; and archival finding aids for the Archivo General de Indias and similar repositories.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how European expansion produced consequences that cannot be captured by a story of discovery or of simple conquest. The Spanish and Portuguese entered a continent of many societies and became participants in conflicts, alliances, and negotiations that they only partly controlled. Understanding the conquest in this way corrects the idea that European power was destined to prevail, and restores Indigenous leaders and communities to the center of the story.

The chapter also reveals processes that linked Europe and the wider world. Silver from Potosí and Zacatecas financed the wars of the Habsburgs, shaped the credit markets of Genoa, Antwerp, and Augsburg, and flowed onward to Asia. American crops and commodities entered European kitchens and markets, though more slowly and unevenly than later memory suggests. Theological and legal debates over the rights of conquered peoples influenced European thought about sovereignty, and Las Casas's text, translated for Protestant audiences, became a weapon in the religious conflicts of Europe. Overseas empire, in short, was woven into European history, not external to it.

Many of the chapter's developments continued far beyond the sixteenth century. Indigenous communities preserved languages, political forms, and religious practices that remain visible today. The demographic collapse and the demand for labor drove the expansion of African slavery, while the ecological changes of the exchange altered diets and landscapes worldwide. Within the argument of Volume III, the chapter shows how dynastic states, religious institutions, and commercial networks joined in building empires whose consequences reshaped Europe and the world.

Chapter 70 now turns to Atlantic slavery and the European economy, examining how the transatlantic slave trade and plantation systems became central to the Atlantic world that conquest and exchange had created. Students should carry forward the habits of this chapter: attention to the perspectives of all participants, care in distinguishing evidence from later narrative, and the recognition that large historical outcomes emerged from the interaction of many causes rather than from a single force.
