The Roman Empire at Its Height

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

The Roman Empire was not held together by a vast central bureaucracy. It was held together by layered cooperation among an emperor, an army, a small corps of governors and officials, and thousands of local communities whose own elites did most of the daily work of ruling. This chapter asks how that arrangement functioned, and how differently it felt to those who lived inside it.

Around the year 100 CE, at the fort of Vindolanda in northern Britain, a woman named Claudia Severa sent a short letter on a thin leaf of wood to another woman, Sulpicia Lepidina, the wife of the commander of a unit of auxiliary soldiers stationed nearby. She invited her friend to a birthday celebration, added a few affectionate lines in her own hand at the end, and sent greetings to the commander from her husband. The letter is one of hundreds of ink writing tablets recovered from waterlogged soil at the site. It is remarkable not for any political importance but for what it shows: two women whose social world stretched from the Rhine delta, where the soldiers of Lepidina's husband had originally been recruited, to a wet hillside on the northern edge of the Roman provinces, writing to one another in Latin, exchanging invitations as people did in Italy, and doing so at a place where the empire's armed power met unconquered and semi controlled lands.

Nothing in that note announces the "Roman Empire at its height." Yet it points directly to the questions that this chapter examines. How did a political system centered on a single ruler, whose capital was more than two thousand kilometers from Vindolanda, command the allegiance of soldiers, officials, taxpayers, and neighbors from Britain to Syria and from the Danube to the edge of the Sahara? How did people whose ancestors had spoken Gaulish, Punic, Aramaic, Thracian, or Egyptian come to write letters, build baths, and seek legal status within a shared imperial framework, and how much of their own languages, gods, and customs did they keep? And what did "Roman peace" mean to a recently conquered farmer, an enslaved miner, a frontier soldier, or a city councilor eager for imperial favor?

Chapter 21 examined how Augustus constructed the Principate, a political order that preserved republican forms while concentrating decisive power in one household and in the army that served it. This chapter begins where that account leaves off, with Augustus's death in 14 CE and the successful accession of Tiberius, and follows the imperial system across more than two centuries, to the murder of the emperor Severus Alexander in 235 CE. Its subject is less the story of individual emperors than the way the imperial order worked once it existed: through government, cities, roads and sea lanes, commerce, armies and frontiers, provincial societies, and cultural interaction.

The central problem may be stated in a single question. How did Rome govern, connect, and sustain an enormous empire with a relatively small central administration, and how differently did Roman rule affect the peoples living within it? The answer developed here is that the empire depended less on direct administration of individuals than on governing communities. Rome required taxes, soldiers, public order, and loyalty, and it obtained them largely through city councils, provincial elites, army commanders, and existing local institutions, which in turn gained status, protection, and access to imperial favor. This arrangement was durable and adaptable, but it was also unequal, it rested on conquest and coercion, and it produced widely varying experiences of "Roman" life.

The chapter therefore proceeds thematically after an opening political survey. It examines the working of imperial government and its limits, the cities and local elites through which Rome ruled, the roads and sea lanes that connected the Mediterranean world, the economy of production, taxation, and trade, the great cities and their monuments, the army and the frontier zones, the violence that accompanied the so called Roman peace, the gradual expansion of Roman citizenship, the debates over cultural change often summarized as "Romanization," and the daily lives of people from very different backgrounds. It closes with the Antonine and Severan empires and with the strains visible by 235 CE, without narrating the crisis that follows, which belongs to Chapter 24.

The evidence for these subjects is unusually varied, and its variety is part of the chapter's argument. Historians read literary works by senators such as Tacitus and Cassius Dio, but also the letters of a provincial governor, inscriptions on stone and bronze, papyri from Egypt, wooden tablets from Britain, coins, milestones, shipwrecks, and pottery distributions, along with the archaeology of cities, forts, farms, and workshops. Each type of source privileges certain people, mostly male, urban, literate, and wealthy, and leaves others in the background. Reading the empire honestly means asking constantly whose voice is present and whose is absent.

This chapter belongs to Volume I, whose larger task is to show how ancient Mediterranean societies developed and were transformed, and it closes Part III, which traced Rome from a central Italian city to a Mediterranean power. It also sets up Part IV. Chapter 23 will examine religious change within this imperial world, including the rise of Christianity, and Chapter 24 will examine the political, military, and economic crisis that followed 235 CE. Understanding how the Principate worked at its height is the necessary foundation for understanding both its endurance and its later transformation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Principate survived the deaths of Augustus and later emperors, and why succession remained an unresolved problem.
  2. Analyze how a relatively small imperial administration governed tens of millions of people through cities, provincial elites, and local institutions.
  3. Describe the roles of governors, procurators, prefects, the imperial household, and municipal magistrates in imperial government.
  4. Trace the network of roads, ports, rivers, and sea lanes that connected the empire, and distinguish their administrative, military, and commercial functions.
  5. Assess the evidence for and the limits of economic integration in the Roman Mediterranean, avoiding both modern market analogies and claims of purely local subsistence.
  6. Compare how different frontiers, including the Rhine, Danube, Britain, the East, and North Africa, functioned as zones of interaction rather than sealed borders.
  7. Evaluate the phrase Pax Romana, distinguishing imperial order from the absence of violence.
  8. Trace the expansion of Roman citizenship from the early Principate to the Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE and explain its significance.
  9. Evaluate the strengths and limits of "Romanization" as a concept for understanding cultural change in the provinces.
  10. Contrast the experiences of soldiers, taxpayers, local elites, merchants, enslaved people, women, and recently conquered populations under Roman rule.
  11. Identify the kinds of evidence, literary, epigraphic, papyrological, archaeological, and numismatic, that historians use to reconstruct the imperial system.
  12. Connect the strengths and dependencies of the imperial system by 235 CE to the crisis examined in Chapter 24.

Historical Context

By the death of Augustus in 14 CE, Rome had already spent two centuries acquiring the territories that the Principate would govern. Chapters 16 and 17 traced the conquest of Italy, the wars with Carthage, and the absorption of Iberia, North Africa, Greece, and the eastern Mediterranean, along with the improvised systems of provincial administration, taxation, and tax collection that grew from them. Chapters 19 and 20 showed how the competition for command of the armies produced civil wars that destroyed the republican political settlement, and Chapter 21 showed how Augustus reorganized power. He retained the Senate, magistracies, and public rituals of the Republic but placed the armies, key provinces, finances, and patronage under his own control, and he settled the armed forces on a permanent, salaried footing, reducing the number of legions to about twenty five.

The empire that resulted was geographically vast and culturally heterogeneous. It surrounded the Mediterranean, which Romans called mare nostrum, "our sea," and extended northward to the Rhine and Danube and much of Britain, eastward into Syria and along the Euphrates, and southward into Egypt and the North African coast. Population estimates for the second century CE vary widely, but many modern reconstructions place the total between roughly fifty and seventy five million people, with the caution that these figures rest on inference from sparse census data, archaeology, and comparative demography rather than direct enumeration. Only a minority, perhaps ten to twenty percent by common estimates, lived in cities of any size. The overwhelming majority worked the land.

The regions Rome ruled had extremely different histories. In the Greek East, city states with centuries of institutions, literary traditions, and philosophical schools had been absorbed into Roman rule, and many kept Greek as the language of local government and elite culture. In Egypt, a Ptolemaic administrative system centered on the Nile valley became an imperial province governed under exceptional arrangements through an equestrian prefect directly accountable to the emperor. In Gaul, Iberia, Britain, and the Danubian lands, societies organized around tribal communities, hillforts, and regional aristocracies encountered Roman power more recently and in more disruptive ways. In North Africa, the legacy of Carthage and Numidian kingdoms shaped the cultural landscape. The discussion in Chapter 6 of Iron Age peoples and the survey of imperial expansion in Chapter 17 should be kept in mind: "the provinces" were never a single kind of place.

Roman government inherited from the Republic a strong preference for ruling through existing communities. Conquered cities and peoples were commonly left to manage their own affairs under Roman supervision, subject to taxation and to demands for soldiers and order. Augustus regularized this system rather than replacing it. The result was a political structure with a very thin layer of imperial officials on top of thousands of civic and tribal governments, and this thinness is the key to understanding both the flexibility and the limits of the imperial order examined in this chapter.

Finally, the sources for this period differ sharply from those for the Republic. Literary accounts by Tacitus, Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, all members of the senatorial or equestrian elite, dominate narrative history, and they were often preoccupied with emperors, senators, and the court, sometimes with strong hostility toward individual rulers. They must be supplemented by material that gives voice to other people: thousands of inscriptions recording dedications, tombstones, laws, and building projects; papyri from Egypt preserving tax receipts, petitions, contracts, and letters; military records and the Vindolanda tablets; coins minted in the imperial name; and the physical remains of cities, roads, farms, kilns, mines, harbors, and shipwrecks. This chapter draws on all of these, and emphasizes their limits as much as their usefulness.

Section 22.1

The Principate after Augustus, 14–96 CE

The first question a historian of the Principate must ask about the decades after Augustus is not what any particular emperor did, but why the system continued at all. The Augustan settlement contained no rule for succession. Augustus had arranged for Tiberius, his stepson, to receive powers that marked him as heir, and Tiberius took over in 14 CE without a contest. But this success rested on the loyalty of the armies, the acquiescence of the Senate, and the prestige of the Julian household, not on a legal mechanism. Each subsequent transfer of power would test the same three supports, and several failed.

The dynasty of Augustus, known as the Julio Claudian, ruled through Tiberius (14–37 CE), Gaius, better known as Caligula (37–41), Claudius (41–54), and Nero (54–68). Their reigns illustrate different aspects of the system. Tiberius's later years showed how mistrust between emperor and senators, together with the ambitions of the praetorian prefect Sejanus, could poison politics at the center. Caligula's assassination in 41 by officers of the Praetorian Guard, and the guard's decision to proclaim Claudius, showed that the soldiers closest to the emperor could make and unmake rulers. Claudius, who as a scholarly, physically disabled member of the dynasty had been overlooked, proved an active administrator. He relied on freedmen from his household to manage correspondence and finances, extended citizenship and Latin rights to communities in the provinces, and launched the conquest of Britain in 43. Nero's reign began with cooperation between the young emperor and senatorial advisers but ended in political isolation, revolts by provincial commanders in Gaul and Spain, and his suicide in 68.

What followed demonstrated what the Augustan order had concealed. In the civil wars of 68–69 CE, four men, Galba, Otho, Vitellius, and Vespasian, were proclaimed emperor in quick succession, mostly by armies in different regions. The senatorial historian Tacitus later observed that this crisis revealed a secret of the imperial system, that an emperor could be made somewhere other than Rome. The victor, Vespasian, had been commanding legions in Judaea, and he was proclaimed by eastern armies and supported by legions on the Danube. His accession did not restore the Julio Claudian household. Instead, the Flavian dynasty (Vespasian, 69–79; Titus, 79–81; Domitian, 81–96) built legitimacy through military success, administrative reconstruction, and public building, including the great amphitheater in Rome that opened in 80. A surviving bronze inscription records the powers conferred on Vespasian by the Senate and people, and modern historians read it as evidence of how constitutional language was used to frame the outcome of military force.

The Flavians also continued to open the imperial elite to provincials. Vespasian is reported to have granted Latin rights to communities in Spain, and members of families from Gaul, Spain, and elsewhere increasingly entered the Senate. Domitian's autocratic manner, however, alienated many senators, and when a palace conspiracy assassinated him in 96 CE, the senatorial authors who wrote the surviving histories portrayed him as a tyrant. Because these accounts come almost entirely from a hostile group, historians treat their moral judgments cautiously while accepting that Domitian's relationship with the Senate had broken down.

By 96, then, the Principate had survived assassination, civil war, and the extinction of two dynasties. It had done so because emperors, whatever their character, performed functions that armies, provincial elites, and much of the Senate needed: paying and commanding the soldiers, arbitrating disputes, distributing patronage, and guaranteeing an order in which local leaders could thrive. The system's persistence therefore says less about any single ruler than about how deeply its supporting relationships ran. Those relationships are the subject of the next sections.

Section 22.2

The Imperial Government at Work

Students should not imagine Roman government as a network of ministries staffed by thousands of professional civil servants. The imperial administration was small by any later standard. The emperor stood at its center as commander of the armies, supreme judge, chief patron, and the one person to whom any community with a grievance or request could ultimately appeal. Around him operated an informal circle of friends and advisers, a household staff of slaves and freedpeople who handled correspondence, accounts, and records, the Praetorian prefects who commanded the guard in Rome and increasingly acted as senior officials, and a small number of senators and equestrians holding formal offices. The historian Fergus Millar, in a study that changed the field, argued that much of the emperor's work consisted of responding to petitions and embassies rather than initiating policy, and that the empire's central government was, in this sense, reactive.

The provinces were divided into two broad categories. Provinces that had long been peaceful, such as Asia, Africa, and Baetica in Spain, were governed by senatorial proconsuls selected by the Senate for short terms, generally a year. Provinces containing legions or requiring closer supervision, such as Britain, Syria, and the Danubian provinces, were governed by senators of appropriate rank who served as the emperor's legates, appointed by him and usually serving longer. Egypt, for particular reasons of grain supply, wealth, and strategic sensitivity, was governed by an equestrian prefect, and senators were forbidden to enter it without imperial permission. Smaller or specialized provinces might be governed by equestrian procurators, who also managed imperial finances throughout the empire. There were roughly three dozen to forty provinces at various times, and the exact number changed as territories were added and subdivided.

A governor's own staff was tiny. He was accompanied by friends and relatives who acted as advisers, a small group of soldiers seconded from the army for clerical and police work, and slaves and freedpeople for domestic and administrative tasks. Judicial duties consumed much of his time. Governors toured districts to hear cases, held assizes in leading cities, resolved disputes between communities, and dealt with crimes that local courts could not handle or that concerned Roman citizens. Their financial duties were limited largely to supervising the collection of taxes by cities and by procurators, who reported separately to the emperor. Their principal responsibility was maintaining order and the empire's obedience.

Law was another mechanism of imperial government. Emperors issued edicts, letters, and decisions in response to petitions, known as rescripts, and jurists, whose writings increasingly shaped legal practice, interpreted this growing body of material. Provincial governors also applied local law and custom where these did not conflict with Roman interests, so that a single province might contain several legal traditions. The empire thus produced a body of imperial law that gradually overlaid, without replacing, the legal systems of subject communities.

Taxation was the other great task, and here too the empire relied heavily on local institutions. Provincial communities paid taxes on land and, in many provinces, on persons, assessed through periodic censuses and registers that Roman officials supervised but that cities and villages helped compile. Italy was largely exempt from direct provincial taxes in this period, though Roman citizens paid indirect taxes, including duties on inheritances and on manumission. Customs charges, known as portoria, were levied at provincial and regional boundaries, generally at modest percentage rates. By the imperial period, the old system in which private companies of tax farmers collected much of the revenue had been reduced in favor of collection through cities and imperial officials, though private contractors remained involved in some sectors.

This raises the question at the center of the chapter. How could so few officials control so many people? The answer is that the government did not, for the most part, administer individuals. It governed communities, and it demanded from them a defined set of things: taxes, recruits, obedience, and public order. Cities, tribal units, and villages handled almost everything else, from local courts and markets to festivals, roads, and water supply. Where local institutions failed, governors and, ultimately, the emperor might intervene. The empire's real administrative capacity was therefore located outside the formal imperial staff, in the elites who ran local communities and had strong reasons to keep the system functioning. The limits of this arrangement were also significant. Imperial officials could be corrupt, and there were prosecutions of governors for extortion, including cases in which Pliny the Younger participated. Communication between the provinces and the emperor was slow, and central authorities often knew little about local conditions. The system rewarded the cooperation of local elites but could offer little protection to ordinary people against abuse by powerful local landowners or officials.

Section 22.3

Provinces, Cities, and Local Elites

If governors were the visible face of Roman authority, cities were its working machinery. The Roman world was organized around urban centers, each governing a surrounding territory that might be very large, and the imperial government, in effect, ruled a mosaic of city territories and other communities. In the western provinces, the Romans encouraged the creation or reorganization of cities where tribal societies had previously prevailed, granting them formal status as coloniae, settlements of Roman citizens often including veterans, or as municipia, self governing towns with Roman or Latin rights. In the Greek East, cities with long traditions of civic life were generally recognized in their existing forms, with Roman authorities intervening chiefly to preserve order and finances.

City government followed a common pattern despite differences of language and tradition. A council, in Latin towns the ordo decurionum and in Greek cities the boule, made major decisions. Annual magistrates were chosen from among the wealthier citizens, and they administered justice in minor cases, managed public property, organized festivals, and, crucially, ensured that taxes were collected. Membership in the council and eligibility for magistracies usually required a property qualification. A bronze tablet from the town of Irni in Spain, discovered in 1981 and containing large parts of a Flavian municipal law, provides rare detailed evidence for how such a community was expected to operate, from elections to the rules for holding property and resolving disputes.

Local elites were more than administrators. They were expected to spend their own wealth on the community, a practice modern scholars call euergetism, from the Greek word for "doing good." Wealthy individuals paid for temples, baths, aqueducts, theaters, feasts, and games, or sponsored distributions of food and money. Officeholders were often required to make payments on taking up a post. In return they received public honors, inscribed statues, front seats at spectacles, and the deference of the community. This system supplied much of the civic monumental landscape of the empire without imperial funding, and it tied the loyalty of local notables to the city and, through the city, to the empire.

Historical Vocabulary
Euergetism

Euergetism is the modern term for the practice, widespread in Greek and Roman cities, by which wealthy individuals financed public buildings, festivals, grain distributions, and other communal benefits, often in connection with holding office or receiving public honor. The word derives from the Greek euergetes, "benefactor."

It mattered because it allowed the empire to provide urban amenities and maintain local order at little cost to the imperial treasury. It also linked social status to public display, since benefactors expected recognition, usually recorded in inscriptions. Modern readers should not mistake it for charity or for taxation. It was a system of reciprocal obligation in which the community and the elite each depended on the other, and it also imposed real financial burdens on those who held office. When those burdens became heavier, as they did in some regions by the third century, the willingness of local families to serve on councils could decline.

Provincial assemblies provided a further link. In many provinces, delegates from cities met annually in a provincial council, usually centered on ceremonies honoring the emperor. These assemblies gave provincial elites a regional forum, a channel for sending embassies to the emperor, and, on occasion, a means of complaining about a governor. The first such Gallic assembly was founded at Lugdunum (modern Lyon) in the reign of Augustus. The religious dimension of the imperial cult is developed in Chapter 23. Here the point is political: the empire gave leading provincials a role in a shared imperial order.

That order also opened routes of advancement. Local magistrates who became Roman citizens could hope for their sons to enter the equestrian order and, in time, the Senate. In 48 CE, the emperor Claudius argued before the Senate, in a speech that survives in part on a bronze tablet at Lyon and in a version by Tacitus, in favor of admitting leading Gauls, using precedent from Rome's own history of absorbing outsiders. By the second century, senators and emperors from Spain, southern Gaul, Africa, and the Greek East had become common. Trajan and Hadrian came from families settled at Italica in Spain, and Septimius Severus came from Leptis Magna in North Africa. The empire, in short, incorporated provincial elites into the governing class, and this incorporation was one of the most important reasons for its endurance. It should not be overstated, however. The people who benefited were a small minority. The majority of provincial inhabitants encountered Roman power through taxation, requisitions, and local landlords, not through advancement.

Section 22.4

Roads, Ports, Shipping, and Imperial Communications

The Roman Empire was as much a network of routes as a territory. Roman roads, built or improved by soldiers and local labor, radiated from Rome and crisscrossed the provinces. Estimates of the length of the major road network are often placed around eighty thousand kilometers, though the figure depends on what is counted and includes many minor routes of uncertain status. Roads were engineered with drainage, foundations, and, in many places, paved surfaces, and they were supplemented by bridges, such as the great bridge at Alcántara in Spain, completed under Trajan. Milestones bearing the emperor's name and distances to nearby cities mark the routes and served, among other purposes, as a reminder of imperial presence.

A common misconception holds that Romans built roads primarily for trade. The evidence indicates otherwise. Roads were built to move soldiers, officials, supplies, and dispatches efficiently and to make provincial control possible. The cursus publicus, an official system of relay stations, animals, and vehicles created under Augustus and later reorganized, carried imperial messengers and officials, and its costs were largely borne by local communities, which was a frequent source of grievance. Once roads existed, however, they were used by everyone: merchants, migrants, craftspeople, pilgrims, soldiers' families, and private couriers carrying letters. Local roads connecting villages, farms, and markets, most of them unpaved, carried much of ordinary traffic and left fewer traces.

For bulk goods, the sea and navigable rivers mattered far more than roads. Moving grain, wine, oil, stone, or timber by land was slow and expensive, and carriage by water was very much cheaper per unit of distance. The Mediterranean's role, emphasized in Chapter 2, was therefore central. Merchant ships carried enormous cargoes between ports, sailing in the open season, roughly spring through early autumn, while voyages in winter were more dangerous and less frequent. Rome's own supply depended on maritime routes to its harbor at Ostia and, from Claudius and Trajan onward, the artificial harbor complex at Portus. Alexandria, with its lighthouse and large harbor, connected Egypt to the Mediterranean, while Carthage, Puteoli, Ephesus, and many others were major ports. Rivers such as the Rhône, Rhine, Danube, Guadalquivir, and Nile served as arteries of movement into the interior.

Travel and information moved at varied speeds. A favorable sea passage from Rome to Alexandria might take a couple of weeks, but adverse winds could double or triple the time. Overland, official couriers could cover long distances in a few days along the best routes, but ordinary travelers moved much more slowly. Governors often waited weeks or months for replies from the emperor. This delay explains a great deal about the system. Provincial officials had to make many decisions on their own, and imperial authority rested on the ability of local agents to act without close supervision. Historians have created models that estimate travel times and costs across the Roman world, and while these are approximations, they show that transport costs shaped the economy in decisive ways.

The roads and sea lanes also carried people, ideas, and diseases. Soldiers were often stationed far from their birthplaces, merchants settled in distant ports, enslaved people were transported across the Mediterranean, and religious and philosophical movements spread along the same routes. The connectivity that made the empire cohesive also made it vulnerable, since an epidemic could travel as fast as an army. The Antonine plague, discussed in Section 22.12, exemplifies this.

Section 22.5

Production, Taxation, and Mediterranean Commerce

Any account of the Roman economy should begin by insisting that it was overwhelmingly agrarian. Most people farmed, and most of the empire's wealth derived from land, whether small holdings, tenant farms, or large estates worked partly by enslaved labor. Cereals, olives, and grapes formed the staple crops of the Mediterranean, and in the northwestern provinces mixed farming and stock raising predominated. Rents and taxes extracted from rural producers supported the cities, the army, and the elite. The state and the aristocracy were, in effect, the greatest consumers, and the movement of goods often followed the flow of taxes and rents rather than open market demand.

At the same time, the empire supported a great deal of trade. Rome itself, a city of perhaps a million people by common estimates, could not feed itself from Italy and drew large quantities of grain from Egypt, Africa, and Sicily. Estimates of the annual grain requirement are commonly placed in the hundreds of thousands of tons. Olive oil from Baetica in Spain reached Rome and the northern frontier in enormous quantities. Monte Testaccio, a mound near the Tiber composed of broken oil amphorae discarded over roughly two centuries, is estimated to contain the remains of tens of millions of vessels. Wine from Italy, Gaul, and the Aegean, fish sauce from Spain and the Black Sea, marble from Italy, Greece, and Asia Minor, metals from mines in Spain, Britain, and the Balkans, pottery from workshops in Gaul and North Africa, and textiles from the East moved along the sea lanes and rivers. The Red Sea ports connected the empire to the Indian Ocean, and Pliny the Elder complained that large amounts of Roman coin flowed eastward in exchange for luxury goods, a claim that exaggerates but reflects a real trade.

The army was itself an engine of commerce. Stationing tens of thousands of soldiers, who were paid in coin, on frontiers such as the Rhine, the Danube, and northern Britain created concentrated markets for food, clothing, tools, and building materials, and encouraged the development of local farming and industry. Government spending on the army, on building projects, and on the capital fed back into the provinces through payments and requisitions, and taxes drawn from the provinces financed the soldiers. In this sense the imperial fiscal system moved resources across the empire independently of private trade.

Historians who assess this evidence face a methodological challenge. There are no national accounts for the Roman Empire. Evidence includes the distribution of amphorae and fine tablewares, shipwrecks and their cargoes, inscriptions on trade goods, coin finds, harbor and warehouse installations, papyrus documents from Egypt recording loans, sales, and shipping arrangements, and ice cores and lake sediments that show pollution from metal production. This evidence shows extensive interregional trade and specialization on an exceptional scale for a preindustrial economy, but it does not show a modern, unified market. Local production still supplied most goods, transport costs limited what could be traded profitably over distance, and much long distance movement was driven by state needs and elite consumption. Scholars debate how far to characterize the Roman economy as integrated, with a range of views from those who emphasize market mechanisms and growth to those who stress the limits set by technology, low productivity, and status. A cautious summary is that the empire had a sophisticated, regionally specialized economy in which imperial taxation, military spending, private trade, and local production interacted.

Money supported these exchanges. The imperial coinage, with the silver denarius and the bronze sestertius as principal denominations, circulated across the empire, and local mints in the East continued to produce coins for local use. Coin carried the emperor's image and titles, making it an instrument of political communication as well as commerce. Over the long run, emperors from Nero onward reduced the silver content of the denarius, gradually at first, and this debasement accelerated under the Severans, a pressure point examined further in Chapter 24. Banking and credit existed alongside informal lending through family, patronage, and other private networks, with their reach varying by region and social setting.

Section 22.6

Rome and the Empire of Cities

Rome was the largest city in the ancient Mediterranean, with a population commonly estimated around one million at its peak, though estimates vary. It was also unusual: a capital sustained by tribute, taxes, and an elaborate supply system, and a place where emperors displayed power through buildings and spectacle. The Colosseum, dedicated in 80 CE, the Forum of Trajan with its column commemorating the Dacian wars, the Pantheon in its Hadrianic form, and the vast baths of Trajan and Caracalla were monuments of imperial patronage. The state distributed grain to a substantial number of citizens, reportedly in the range of 150,000 to 200,000 recipients, and provided games and entertainments that helped to maintain the emperor's popularity. But Rome's ordinary residents mostly lived in crowded apartment blocks, known as insulae, in conditions of fire risk, noise, and poor sanitation, and many depended on irregular labor. Augustus created a fire brigade, the vigiles, and the great fire of 64 CE nonetheless devastated large sections of the city.

What is more striking historically is the extent to which Rome's urban forms spread across the provinces. In city after city, from Britain to Syria, local elites and imperial officials built forums, basilicas, temples, theaters, amphitheaters, bathhouses, and aqueducts. Aqueducts such as the Pont du Gard in southern Gaul and the eleven great aqueducts serving Rome by the early third century demonstrate the engineering skill and organized labor involved. Timgad (Thamugadi) in Numidia, founded around 100 CE as a veteran colony, exhibits a planned grid of streets and public buildings, while Leptis Magna, the home city of Septimius Severus, was monumentally embellished during his reign. Ephesus in Asia Minor, Antioch in Syria, and Alexandria in Egypt were much larger and older cities with their own traditions, and Roman rule added to, rather than replaced, their urban character.

These public buildings had social functions. Baths were places of hygiene, exercise, and sociability where citizens of different status might meet. Theaters and amphitheaters hosted performances and gladiatorial games that expressed civic pride and imperial loyalty and that involved the deaths of condemned prisoners and enslaved and hired fighters. Temples, in all their diversity, formed centers for local religious life. Markets, warehouses, and workshops clustered in commercial districts. The physical form of such cities encouraged uniformity in appearance, yet the details of use, decoration, language, and religion varied enormously from place to place.

It is essential, however, not to let marble monuments stand for the empire as a whole. Most cities were small by modern standards, with populations of a few thousand, and only a minority of the empire's inhabitants lived in them. Cities depended on their rural territories for food, taxes, and rents, and the wealthy families who financed monuments typically drew their income from land. Behind the forums lay crowded neighborhoods of workers, small shops, and poor housing, and cities generally had high death rates, so their populations were sustained by continued immigration from the countryside. Excavations at Ostia, Pompeii, and elsewhere show contrasts between spacious houses of the rich and cramped apartments, single room shops, and rented rooms of the poor. The city and the countryside formed one economic system, and the "empire of cities" rested on the labor of rural people, tenants, and enslaved workers who rarely appear in monumental inscriptions.

Section 22.7

The Army and the Frontiers

The army was the most important institution of the empire after the emperor, and its stationing, recruitment, and pay shaped provincial societies. By the second century the force consisted of some twenty eight to thirty legions, later more than thirty under Septimius Severus, each of several thousand heavy infantry who were Roman citizens, together with a roughly equal or larger number of auxiliary troops recruited from provincial peoples, organized in infantry cohorts and cavalry units of hundreds of men. The total is often estimated at around three to four hundred thousand soldiers. Legionaries normally owed twenty years of active service, often followed by a reserve period that could bring total service to about twenty five years, while auxiliary service was commonly about twenty five years. Auxiliaries could receive Roman citizenship on honorable discharge, a benefit recorded on bronze documents called military diplomas that survive in large numbers. Soldiers were formally forbidden to marry during service, though many formed unions, and the ban was relaxed under Septimius Severus.

Frontier armies were stationed in permanent bases that became centers of settlement. Around a legionary fortress or auxiliary fort grew civilian settlements of traders, craftspeople, soldiers' partners and children, and veterans, and some of these became towns. The Vindolanda tablets, written on thin wooden leaves in the decades around 100 CE, reveal the life of one such community: requests for supplies, lists of food and equipment, a note accompanying socks and underwear sent to a soldier, complaints about local conditions, and the birthday invitation with which this chapter began. They show a frontier society sustained by literacy, supply networks, and a mix of peoples, including Batavians from the Rhine delta and others.

Historical Vocabulary
Limes

The Latin word limes originally meant a path or track and came to be used, especially in later usage, for a military frontier zone or road along it. Modern scholars use "limes" for the systems of forts, roads, watchtowers, and in some places walls or ditches along the empire's edges, but the term should not suggest a sharply drawn border line like a modern national boundary.

Roman frontiers were zones of control and interaction. They regulated movement and could facilitate customs collection at some crossings, and they provided bases for operations beyond the provinces, and connected the Roman world to neighbors through diplomacy, recruitment, exchange, and conflict. Different frontiers functioned differently, and the notion of a uniform defensive wall around the empire misrepresents the evidence.

The frontiers varied substantially. Along the Rhine and Danube, rivers served as lines of communication and demarcation, backed by legionary bases such as Mogontiacum (Mainz), Carnuntum, and Vindobona (Vienna), auxiliary forts, roads, and watchtowers. Domitian and later emperors organized the territory between the upper Rhine and the Danube, and forts, palisades, and ditches were built. Roman traders crossed these frontiers to deal with peoples such as the Marcomanni, and Roman authorities cultivated alliances with leaders beyond the provinces, subsidizing some and fighting others. In Britain, the province's northern edge was never stable. Conquest in the north under the governor Agricola in the 80s was not consolidated, and Hadrian's Wall, begun around 122, with its forts, turrets, and ditch, marked a frontier line stretching about 117 kilometers (73 miles) across the island. Roman forces advanced north again to build the Antonine Wall in the 140s, then withdrew to Hadrian's line. The wall did not seal a border but controlled movement and may have facilitated customs collection at some crossings, and communities on both sides interacted.

In the East, the frontier lay among a mosaic of cities, kingdoms, and desert routes. Rome and the Parthian empire competed for influence over Armenia and Mesopotamia, using diplomacy, client rulers, and periodic warfare. The empire kept legions in Syria and Cappadocia and along the Euphrates, but there was no continuous wall. Roman power depended on cities such as Antioch, roads, and mobile armies, and caravan cities such as Palmyra, which combined Roman and local traditions, played a significant role in commerce. In North Africa, the legion stationed at Lambaesis and a network of forts and roads managed relations with pastoral and semi nomadic peoples, while the spread of olive and grain agriculture in Africa transformed the landscape. In each case, the frontier was a space of recruitment, exchange, movement, and violence.

The terms Romans used for peoples outside the provinces, especially "barbarians," should be handled carefully. The Greek and Latin words expressed an outsider's perspective and often carried condescension. The peoples beyond the frontiers, whether Germanic speaking, Sarmatian, Dacian, Caledonian, Parthian, or Saharan, were organized in a great variety of ways, some as small groups, some as confederations under leaders, some as kingdoms. This chapter uses the terms of the sources with caution and, wherever possible, names the specific people involved.

Section 22.8

Expansion, Rebellion, and the Limits of the Pax Romana

Historical Vocabulary
Pax Romana

Pax Romana, "Roman peace," is the modern label for the extended period of relative internal stability under the Principate, traditionally dated from Augustus to the later second century. The Latin word pax in Roman political language referred less to an absence of conflict than to a condition secured through victory and submission. To Romans, peace was what followed the defeat of enemies and the establishment of order.

The phrase is useful because for long periods much of the Mediterranean world was spared the wars among states and civil conflicts that had characterized earlier centuries, and because travel and trade occurred within one political system. It is misleading if it implies universal absence of violence or equal benefit. The peace was enforced by armies, and campaigns, revolts, and repression continued throughout.

The centuries of the "Roman peace" were full of war. Expansion continued after Augustus, though at an irregular pace. Claudius's invasion of Britain in 43 CE established a new province that took decades to consolidate. Rebellion within the provinces was a recurring feature of the period. Under Tiberius, Tacfarinas led a prolonged revolt in Africa, and Gallic leaders rebelled in 21 CE. The Batavian revolt of 69–70, led by Julius Civilis, a Batavian who had served in the auxiliary forces, exploited the civil war and drew in Rhineland peoples and units of the Roman army itself. In Judaea, the Jewish revolt of 66–70 ended with the capture of Jerusalem and the destruction of the Temple, and the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135 led to further devastation and the renaming of the province. These episodes are important to Jewish history and receive further treatment in Chapter 23. Here they serve chiefly as evidence that Roman rule was maintained by force and that some communities resisted it in arms.

The revolt of Boudicca in Britain in about 60 or 61 CE provides a well documented example of how provincial policy could provoke rebellion. According to Tacitus, when the client king of the Iceni, Prasutagus, died, Roman officials seized property and mistreated his family. Boudicca, his widow, led the Iceni and their neighbors in a revolt that destroyed the new colony at Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium, and Verulamium before Roman forces under Suetonius Paulinus defeated them. The archaeology of burnt layers in these towns corroborates the destruction. The account, however, comes from a Roman senator, and the motives and organization of the rebels are known only through Roman eyes. Tacitus also composed, in his biography of his father in law Agricola, a speech for a Caledonian chieftain that condemned Roman conquest as making a desert and calling it peace. The words are Tacitus's own literary invention, not a transcript, but they show that at least some Roman writers understood how conquest looked from the other side.

Under Trajan, expansion resumed dramatically. His conquest of Dacia in two wars (101–102 and 105–106), commemorated on the column in his forum, brought the gold and silver mines of the Carpathian region under Roman control and added a new province north of the Danube. In the East, after annexing the Nabataean kingdom as the province of Arabia in 106, he undertook a campaign against the Parthians beginning in 113, took territory in Armenia and Mesopotamia, and reached the Persian Gulf. This brought the empire to its greatest extent around 117. Almost immediately, however, revolts broke out in the newly conquered East and among Jewish communities in Egypt, Cyrenaica, Cyprus, and Mesopotamia, and Trajan died in 117. His successor Hadrian gave up the most exposed eastern acquisitions, and this is often interpreted as a strategic recognition of the limits of what the empire could hold. The evidence for Hadrian's motives is limited, and historians disagree about how far he pursued a coherent policy of consolidation, but his building of frontier works and his extensive tours of the provinces are consistent with that interpretation.

Taken together, these events suggest a more accurate way to use the phrase. The Pax Romana was imperial order, not a universal absence of violence. It gave many regions a long period without invasions or civil wars of the kind that had wracked earlier centuries, and it allowed cities, commerce, and travel to flourish. But it was achieved by conquest, sustained by garrisons, and accompanied by taxation, extraction of resources, enslavement of captives, and violent responses to resistance. To ask "peace for whom?" is not to deny that the empire brought stability to many but to recognize that the experience of order varied with one's position in it.

Section 22.9

Provincial Society and the Expansion of Citizenship

Roman law divided the free population into categories with different rights. Chapter 18 introduced Roman citizenship in the Republican context. Under the empire, the categories were as follows. Roman citizens held the fullest legal protections, including access to Roman civil law, the right of appeal in certain circumstances, and eligibility for legions and public office. Communities and individuals holding Latin status possessed intermediate rights, including a path to Roman citizenship for local magistrates in some places. Peregrines, free inhabitants without citizenship, lived under their own local law and custom, subject to Roman authority, and made up the majority of provincial residents in the early empire. Below them were enslaved people, who had no independent legal personality, and freedpeople, formerly enslaved people who normally became citizens if freed by a citizen, though with social and legal disabilities.

Paths from one category to another multiplied. Soldiers in the auxilia gained citizenship on discharge. Emperors granted citizenship or Latin rights to individuals and to entire communities, as in the extension of Latin rights to Spain attributed to Vespasian. Local magistrates in Latin communities became Roman citizens through office. Enslaved people freed by citizens acquired citizenship. Service to the emperor, in the army or the household, brought advancement. Citizenship in these ways became both a reward and a tool by which Rome incorporated local leaders and bound them to the empire.

The largest transformation came in 212 CE, when the emperor Caracalla issued a measure known as the Constitutio Antoniniana, granting Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. The evidence for the text is limited. A damaged papyrus now in Giessen preserves part of the edict and refers to giving thanks to the gods, and it seems to exclude a group called dediticii, whose exact meaning is disputed. The senatorial historian Cassius Dio, who disliked Caracalla, claimed that the emperor's purpose was to increase tax revenue, since citizens paid certain taxes, such as on inheritances and manumissions. Historians consider the fiscal motive plausible but debate whether it was primary, and other explanations include a desire to honor the gods after escaping conspiracy and a wish to unify the population. The consequences are also debated. Local laws did not disappear overnight, and documents from Egypt and elsewhere show continued use of local custom. Legal distinctions of wealth and rank were already becoming more significant than the distinction between citizen and noncitizen. During the second century, and increasingly by Caracalla's reign, the distinction between honestiores, the wealthy and privileged, and humiliores, the poor, became important in determining criminal penalties.

The transformation in the meaning of "Roman" was profound. In the early Republic, citizenship had belonged to the members of a single city community, and later it spread through Italy. By 212, it belonged to free people throughout an empire that stretched from Britain to Syria. A citizen could speak Greek, Aramaic, Punic, or Gaulish as a first language, worship local gods, and live under different customs from a resident of Italy. Roman citizenship had become a legal and political status that did not require a single cultural identity. This is a crucial point for understanding both the cohesion and the diversity of the empire, and it prepares for the later Roman world examined in Part IV, in which Roman identity would continue to attach to political and legal membership, and in the eastern empire, discussed in Part VI, to a Greek speaking Christian state whose inhabitants called themselves Romans.

Section 22.10

Romanization, Cultural Change, and Local Identity

How did living within the Roman Empire change the peoples who did so? The traditional answer, "Romanization," was developed by scholars in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many of them working within imperial societies that found in Rome a model for their own rule. Its classic English statement, by Francis Haverfield in 1905 concerning Britain, portrayed conquest as followed by the gradual adoption of Latin, Roman law, urban life, architecture, dress, and customs by provincial populations, who thereby became Roman. Later scholars have criticized this account for treating culture as a package delivered from a superior center to passive recipients.

The evidence supports a more complex picture. Provincial societies chose which Roman practices to adopt, and they adapted them to local circumstances. In Britain and Gaul, elite families built villas, used Latin in inscriptions, and adopted Roman styles of dining and bathing, while rural communities often continued older patterns of settlement and household life. In Egypt, temples were built and decorated in Pharaonic style with Roman emperors depicted as pharaohs, while Greek was the language of administration and Egyptian survived in daily and religious use. Funerary portraits from the Fayum combine Greek and Roman painting techniques with Egyptian burial practice. In Africa, Punic remained in use for centuries, and cults of local deities were expressed in Roman forms. Syria's Palmyra provides bilingual inscriptions in Greek and Palmyrene Aramaic, such as the tax tariff of 137 CE. In Britain, the god Sulis and the Roman Minerva were combined at the sanctuary of Bath. Such patterns can be described as adoption, adaptation, and hybridization, and the results were not simply "Roman."

The Greek East presented a special case. Greek remained the language of culture and much administration, and Latin was principally the language of the army, of Roman law, and of official communications with the emperor. Greek elites in the second century, in a movement often called the Second Sophistic, cultivated an ideal of classical Greek language and learning, and they participated in the empire while emphasizing their Greek identity. The emperor Hadrian, an admirer of Greek culture, founded an association of Greek cities, the Panhellenion. In the mid second century, the Greek orator Aelius Aristides delivered a speech praising Rome's order and citizenship, and his oration reveals how elite provincials could embrace Roman rule while remaining Greek. Marcus Aurelius wrote his private philosophical reflections in Greek. Latin never simply displaced Greek.

Language itself is an example of regional variety. Latin became the dominant written language in the western provinces, and inscriptions in Latin multiplied there, especially in the second century, a phenomenon scholars call the "epigraphic habit," which then declined in the third. But this does not mean that everyone spoke Latin at home. Gaulish, Punic, Thracian, Illyrian, and many other languages persisted in speech, though many left few written traces. Uncovering them requires care, since the surviving evidence, mostly inscriptions and literary texts, overrepresents the language of elites and of official life.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: What Does "Romanization" Explain, and What Does It Hide?

The question: Should historians continue to use "Romanization" to describe cultural change in the provinces, and if so, in what sense? Or does the term impose a misleading model of one directional cultural transmission?

Interpretation A — Romanization as a useful, if simplified, description of a real process: Proponents, drawing on the older tradition, point to unmistakable and widespread changes after conquest: the spread of Latin inscriptions, Roman style towns, baths and amphitheaters, standardized ceramics, coinage, and Roman legal and administrative concepts. They argue that many provincials, especially elites, deliberately adopted Roman ways as a route to status and advantage, and that the empire's institutions encouraged this. More recent scholars such as Greg Woolf, in a study of Gaul, have kept the idea of "becoming Roman" but presented it as an active process in which provincials participated and which changed what "Roman" meant.
Interpretation B — Romanization as a misleading model that obscures agency, diversity, and power: Critics argue that the concept treats culture as unified and superior, casts provincials as passive, and implies a common endpoint. They emphasize local agency, resistance, selective adoption, and hybridization, drawing on concepts such as creolization, discrepant experience, and globalization. David Mattingly, for example, has stressed that the empire was a system of domination and that its impacts, including taxation, extraction, and coercion, varied sharply by region and social position. Jane Webster and others have shown how religious and material practices combined local and Roman elements in new ways.

Evidence: Both sides draw on the same broad evidence: inscriptions, urban archaeology, ceramics, burial practices, religious dedications, rural settlement patterns, and literary sources. They differ in how they interpret it. A Latin inscription might indicate identification with Roman culture, or a local family's strategy to display status, or both. The absence of change in rural settlement might indicate resistance, poverty, lack of interest, or simply a different local logic.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes whether the empire is understood primarily as a civilizing process, a system of domination, or a shared, contested space in which many identities coexisted. It also affects how later European histories draw on Rome. Modern nations and empires have often claimed a Roman inheritance, and the term "Romanization" carried the political assumptions of some of them. Many historians now retain the word only with careful qualification, or use alternatives.

Student task: Choose one province discussed in this chapter and list evidence that would support each interpretation. Then explain what other evidence you would want in order to decide how far local people shaped, adopted, or resisted Roman practices.

What emerges is not a single "Roman culture" that spread uniformly, but layers of identity. A resident of a provincial city might identify with the city, the region, a language community, a family and its ancestors, a legal status, and, increasingly, the empire. A person could be a Roman citizen without being culturally identical to an Italian. This flexibility helps explain the empire's staying power: it did not demand that people abandon their gods and languages, but that they accept the framework of Roman rule. It also cautions students against equating Roman citizenship, the Latin language, and cultural identity. Chapter 23 will examine the specific case of religion, in which local, imperial, and new traditions interacted.

Section 22.11

Life across the Empire

There was no single Roman daily life. A subsistence farmer in the Nile valley, a tenant on a large estate in Africa, a shopkeeper in a Gallic town, an enslaved miner in Spain, a legionary on the Danube, and a senator in Rome lived within the same empire but in very different worlds. The evidence is unevenly distributed, and much of what is known about ordinary life comes from a few unusually well documented places: Egypt through its papyri, Pompeii and Ostia through preserved buildings, and frontier forts through tablets and rubbish deposits.

Most people were farmers. Rural households produced grain, olives, grapes, vegetables, and livestock in ways that varied enormously by region. Some owned small plots, many were tenants who paid rent in cash or produce, and others worked as laborers on large estates owned by senators, equestrians, wealthy local families, and the emperor. In Egypt, papyri show villagers dealing with tax collectors, landlords, and neighbors, and petitioning officials about debts and violence. In Britain, many rural people continued to live in round houses of local design alongside a small number of villas. Diet in most of the empire centered on cereals, prepared as bread or porridge, supplemented by legumes, olive oil, wine, vegetables, and, where available, fish and meat, which many ate rarely. Harvest failures and food shortages brought hardship, and the state had limited ability to respond.

Slavery pervaded the empire. Estimates of the enslaved share of the population vary, with common figures ranging from about ten to twenty percent overall and higher in Italy, but the numbers are uncertain. Enslaved people worked in agriculture, mines, households, workshops, urban services, and the imperial household, and some held positions of trust as estate managers, clerks, and skilled craftsmen. Their conditions differed sharply. Domestic and administrative slaves sometimes had prospects of manumission, while those in mines and on large estates faced brutal labor. Enslaved people were sold, punished, sexually exploited, and separated from families, and the law offered limited protection, though emperors from the second century imposed some restrictions on the worst abuses. Chapter 18 discussed the institution in its earlier form. Its persistence throughout the Principate is a reminder that Roman prosperity depended on coerced labor.

Women's lives depended on status, region, and wealth. Under Roman law, women could own property and, in many circumstances, manage it. Although adult women formally remained subject to guardianship in certain legal transactions, by the Principate the guardian's role was often limited in practice. Women of the elite exercised influence through family networks and patronage, and some funded public buildings, like Eumachia at Pompeii. Women of lower status worked as weavers, shopkeepers, midwives, bakers, entertainers, and agricultural laborers. Egyptian papyri show women litigating, buying and selling land, and running households. The Vindolanda invitation shows that some women of the military elite were literate. But women had no formal political rights, and marriage arrangements, especially for the wealthy, were shaped by family strategy. Girls could marry very young by modern standards.

Children faced high mortality. Modern demographic estimates suggest that perhaps a third of infants died in their first year, and that life expectancy at birth was in the range of twenty to thirty years, though those who survived childhood often lived considerably longer. Disease, poor sanitation, and periodic epidemics affected all groups, though the poor suffered most. Literacy was limited, but impossible to quantify securely. Estimates sometimes place it in the range of roughly 5 to 15 percent in parts of the empire, with enormous variation by region, gender, occupation, status, and by what historians count as literacy. Education, including the study of rhetoric, was for those who could pay, and most people learned trades through apprenticeship or family work.

Soldiers formed a distinct group. Their pay, rations, and prospects of veteran land or a discharge bonus gave them a more secure life than many civilians, but the work was dangerous, discipline harsh, and service long. Around forts, families lived in the settlements outside the walls. Veterans often settled near their bases, becoming local notables. The army thereby affected demographics and social structure in frontier regions. Migration was a general feature of imperial life: soldiers, merchants, artisans, and enslaved people moved in large numbers, and cities such as Rome, Alexandria, and Carthage had very diverse populations. Inscriptions record people from Syria in Britain, Africans on the Rhine, and Thracians in Gaul.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in a Provincial Roman City

Note: this is a composite reconstruction of a medium sized city in a western province in the second century CE, assembled from patterns documented at sites such as Pompeii, Ostia, and Roman towns in Gaul, Spain, and Britain. It does not describe any single named place or real individual.

At dawn, water carriers and bakers are already at work. Public fountains, fed by an aqueduct, supply households without private water, and the street is loud with carts delivering goods. An enslaved domestic worker in a modest house of an artisan family begins the day tending the hearth and fetching water, work that continues until night and for which she receives no wage. A member of the town council prepares for a morning of public business: he will attend a meeting about the maintenance of the aqueduct, and he expects to be asked to contribute to it from his own pocket, a duty attached to his office and a mark of his standing.

In the forum, market sellers set out produce, cloth, and pottery, and a shopkeeper opens the front of a small shop that is also his family's living quarters. Language varies with the encounter. Official notices and formal inscriptions are in Latin, but many people also speak a local language at home and in the marketplace, and a migrant merchant from the eastern Mediterranean arranges a deal in a mixture of Greek and Latin. A veteran of the auxiliary forces, who received citizenship on discharge, visits the forum to register a document. A woman who manages her late husband's workshop discusses a contract with a supplier, relying on the help of a male guardian required in some circumstances by law.

Midday brings a lull. Wealthier residents head to the baths, where the elite mix, briefly, with artisans and shopkeepers, though slaves and the poor may attend under different conditions. Others stay at work in the workshops of potters, weavers, and leatherworkers. In the afternoon there may be entertainments at the amphitheater or theater for a festival, and the city's leading families, who have funded them, expect recognition. In the evening the streets empty and the poorer households, some living in rooms above shops or in tenements, eat a simple meal of bread, oil, legumes, and wine.

A different city would look and sound different. A town in Roman Britain might have fewer marble buildings and more timber, and an eastern city such as Ephesus would use Greek in its assembly and cultivate a long civic history. An Egyptian town would place a temple of Egyptian gods at its heart, and a frontier settlement would be dominated by the garrison. What they shared was a framework of municipal government, taxation, and imperial law within which each local society worked out its own way of life.

Section 22.12

The Antonine and Severan Empires, 96–235 CE

The century after Domitian's murder is often called the high point of the empire. The emperors from Nerva (96–98) through Trajan (98–117), Hadrian (117–138), Antoninus Pius (138–161), and Marcus Aurelius (161–180) succeeded one another largely by adoption, and later writers, most influentially Edward Gibbon, described this as an age of good government. Students should treat this tradition with care. Adoption was largely a consequence of the absence of surviving sons, not a constitutional principle, and the peaceful transfers depended on the emperors' ability to secure army and senatorial support. The rulers differed considerably in policy and in how they were judged, and the label "Five Good Emperors" conceals both their conflicts and the fact that the Senate and army, not any principle of merit, largely determined success.

Within this period, the empire reached its territorial peak under Trajan and then consolidated under Hadrian, who traveled widely to inspect armies, cities, and frontier works, often for years at a time. Antoninus Pius reigned for over two decades in relative calm and, according to tradition, never left Italy, an illustration of how administrative continuity could survive an emperor's absence from the provinces. The regime gained the goodwill of provincial elites, who took part in its institutions, and there was extensive building in cities.

Under Marcus Aurelius, the pressures increased. War with Parthia in the early 160s, conducted under Marcus's colleague Lucius Verus, was followed by the outbreak of a serious epidemic, known as the Antonine plague, which historians associate with troops returning from the East and which is described by the physician Galen. Its effects are debated. Some scholars have argued for very high mortality, on the order of ten percent or more of the population and possibly considerably higher, while others, pointing to limited evidence for economic and demographic collapse, propose lower figures and regional variation. Papyri from Egypt, inscriptions, and later literary accounts are all difficult to interpret. At the same time, Marcus fought long wars along the Danube against the Marcomanni, Quadi, and other peoples, the so called Marcomannic wars, which required prolonged campaigning and the transfer of resources. His private reflections, the Meditations, composed in Greek, reveal an emperor troubled by these burdens.

Marcus's son Commodus (180–192) was assassinated at the end of 192, and the resulting civil war of 193–197 pitted several claimants, including the governor of the Danubian provinces, Septimius Severus, against rivals commanding armies in Britain and Syria. Severus emerged victorious and founded a dynasty. His origins in Leptis Magna in North Africa and his marriage to Julia Domna, from a priestly family in Syria, illustrate the increasingly provincial character of the imperial house. Severus emphasized the army as the basis of his power. He increased soldiers' pay, created new legions, and, according to the historian Cassius Dio, advised his sons on his deathbed to keep harmony, pay the soldiers, and disregard everyone else. Whether or not the words are accurate, they express a widely recognized reality of the dynasty. He campaigned in Mesopotamia and Britain, where he died at Eboracum (York) in 211.

His son Caracalla (211–217), who murdered his brother and co-ruler Geta, issued the citizenship edict of 212 and built the great baths in Rome that bear his name. He was killed by soldiers in 217, and Macrinus, the first emperor drawn from the equestrian order rather than the Senate, ruled briefly before the Severan house was restored in the persons of Elagabalus (218–222) and Severus Alexander (222–235), both put forward by the influence of the family's women. The 220s brought a major change abroad. Around 224–226, Ardashir overthrew the Parthian dynasty and founded the Sasanian empire, which posed a new and increasingly assertive challenge to Rome's eastern provinces. Severus Alexander campaigned against it in 231–233 with mixed results and then had to turn to the northern frontier. In 235, soldiers near Mogontiacum, angered by his policy and the perception of weakness, killed him.

By that year the imperial system was still formidable. It controlled a vast territory, with mature networks of cities, roads, and sea lanes, an experienced provincial elite, a large professional army, and citizenship extended to nearly all free inhabitants. Yet its strengths had created dependencies. The army was expensive, and emperors increasingly needed to satisfy it, which strained finances and encouraged debasement of the coinage. Succession remained unresolved, and armies had shown repeatedly that they could make or unmake emperors. The frontiers were long and faced rivals who were sometimes better organized. The empire depended on the willingness of local elites to serve and to pay, and on long distance communication that was vulnerable to shocks such as plague. Chapter 24 will show how these pressures combined after 235 into a period of severe crisis. Chapter 23, which comes first, turns from politics to religion, examining a transformation already under way within this same imperial world.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Pliny the Younger and Trajan on a Fire Brigade at Nicomedia, c. 110 CE

Creator: Pliny the Younger, senator and governor of Bithynia and Pontus, and the emperor Trajan · Date: about 109 to 112 CE, with the exact dating of the governorship debated · Type: Official correspondence between a provincial governor and the emperor, preserved as Book 10 of Pliny's collected letters, in Latin, and read here in paraphrase

Context: Pliny was sent by Trajan to Bithynia and Pontus, a province in northern Asia Minor, to address financial and administrative problems in its cities. Book 10 of his letters preserves his questions and the emperor's replies, apparently published after Pliny's death, so that the collection may have been selected and edited. The exchanges concern building projects, city finances, legal cases, and the status of individuals.

Paraphrase of Pliny, Letters 10.33–34 Pliny reports that a serious fire had destroyed many private houses and two public buildings in the city of Nicomedia, and that it spread because of the lack of equipment and of organized firefighting. He asks Trajan whether he may authorize a company of firefighters, proposing a limited group of roughly one hundred and fifty men, and promises to ensure that they are used only for that purpose. Trajan refuses. He notes that associations of this kind in the region had been sources of political disturbance and prefers that the city provide equipment and rely on property owners and the public to fight fires.

Analysis: The exchange shows several features of imperial rule. A governor of considerable rank turns to the emperor over a practical municipal problem, suggesting that decisions were referred upward rather than resolved by a large bureaucracy with detailed rules. The reply reflects imperial concern about voluntary associations as potential political threats, and shows an emperor prepared to accept a greater risk of fire rather than authorize a group that might organize. It shows that cities had their own resources, institutions, and responsibility, and that imperial authority lay chiefly in approving or refusing. The correspondence is also a reminder of the time and effort that communication required. Its limitations are significant. Pliny and Trajan were members of the elite writing to one another, the letters may have been edited for publication, and the perspective of ordinary residents of Nicomedia is absent. The source also cannot show how typical Bithynia was of other provinces.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: What Did Roman Rule Mean?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action.

A senator in Rome: Imperial service brought prestige, offices, and wealth, but the senator's independent military power was limited and his position depended on the emperor's favor. Some senators regretted the loss of republican freedom of action while others valued the stability.
A municipal elite family in Gaul or Spain: Office in the town, Roman citizenship, and the possibility of advancement for sons enhanced local prestige. The costs of office and civic generosity were real, but Roman rule protected their position.
A Greek civic notable in the East: Roman rule coexisted with a strong sense of Greek cultural superiority, and Roman power provided security and access while allowing the cultivation of classical traditions.
A frontier soldier: The army offered pay, status, and possibilities of citizenship and land, but also long service, danger, and separation from home.
A merchant: Roads, ports, coinage, and legal structures within a large political space reduced risks and costs, though piracy, storms, and tolls remained.
A rural taxpayer: Rome was mainly experienced through tax collectors, requisitions for transport, and local landlords, and the benefits of imperial monuments in distant cities were less visible.
An enslaved worker: "Roman peace" meant continued dependence, exploitation, and vulnerability to sale, and for those in mines or large estates, harsh conditions. Peace did not mean personal freedom.
A recently conquered population: Imperial order could mean occupation, loss of land and leaders, enslavement of captives, and forced adaptation, as the Iceni experience under Boudicca's leadership indicates, though some communities later found ways to prosper within the system.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: The Empire of 14 CE and of 235 CE

What Changed

  • Provincials entered the Senate and became emperors, so that the imperial elite became increasingly provincial in origin
  • Citizenship expanded from a minority to nearly all free inhabitants by 212 CE
  • Frontier systems of forts, roads, and works matured, and the army became more deeply rooted in frontier regions
  • Cities across the provinces acquired monuments and institutions on a scale unimaginable in 14 CE
  • The imperial administration grew somewhat, with more equestrian officials, though it remained small

What Stayed the Same

  • The emperor remained the central figure, his position dependent on the army
  • Local elites continued to govern communities and collect taxes
  • Slavery, agrarian labor, and deep inequality persisted
  • Linguistic and cultural diversity persisted, with Greek and many local languages alongside Latin
  • The central administration remained thin relative to the size of the empire

Who experienced change most directly? Provincial elites and soldiers, who gained access to citizenship, office, and status, experienced the most direct change. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural farmers, tenants, and enslaved workers experienced the empire as continuity in labor and obligation, with taxation and rents as the main link to imperial power. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the empire changed most where local elites and armies interacted with imperial power, while the structural conditions of ordinary life changed slowly.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Did the Roman Imperial System Endure?

Long-term causes

The geography of the Mediterranean as a connective sea, the results of two centuries of conquest, the existing network of cities and local political traditions, and republican experience of ruling through communities.

Short-term causes

Augustus's settlement of the army and finances, the survival of the dynasty through Tiberius, and the reconstruction of legitimacy after the civil wars of 68–69 under the Flavians.

Event or transformation

The consolidation of a Mediterranean wide system in which emperor, army, governors, municipal elites, and local institutions cooperated to secure taxes, order, and loyalty, reaching maturity in the second century.

Immediate effects

Extended periods of relative internal stability, the growth of cities and trade, provincial participation in imperial government, the spread of citizenship, and considerable cultural interaction.

Long-term consequences

An empire whose institutions and legal culture shaped later states, but whose dependence on armies, local elites, and fiscal extraction created vulnerabilities that appear in the third century.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains endurance. The army, law, citizenship, taxation, commerce, and local elite cooperation reinforced one another, and the system also faced persistent limits: unresolved succession, frontier pressure, epidemic disease, fiscal burdens, and reliance on military support.

Historical complexity: Endurance should not be mistaken for inevitability. Different choices by emperors, armies, or provincial elites could have produced different outcomes, as the civil wars of 69 and 193 demonstrated.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the imperial system created by Augustus operated between 14 and 235 CE. Its central argument is that the Roman Empire was sustained less by a large central bureaucracy than by cooperation among the emperor, the army, a small corps of governors and officials, and the elites of thousands of local communities. Rome required taxes, soldiers, order, and loyalty, and it obtained them by governing communities rather than individuals.

Politically, the Principate survived the deaths of Augustus and several of his successors, the extinction of two dynasties, and civil wars in 68–69 and 193–197, because emperors performed functions that armies, provincial elites, and much of the Senate needed. Succession, however, remained unresolved, and the army repeatedly demonstrated that it could make emperors. The system's endurance was real, but it was never guaranteed.

Government at work meant governors with tiny staffs, equestrian procurators and prefects, an imperial household, and a responsive rather than initiating central authority. Cities, through councils, magistrates, and benefactors, carried out most administration, and provincial assemblies and opportunities for citizenship and advancement bound local elites to the empire. The empire's network of roads, ports, rivers, and sea lanes served military and administrative purposes first, but also facilitated commerce, migration, and the spread of ideas and diseases.

The economy was agrarian but sophisticated, marked by regional specialization and large scale trade in grain, oil, wine, metals, and manufactured goods, sustained by imperial taxation, military spending, and elite consumption. It was not a modern integrated market, and evidence from amphorae, shipwrecks, papyri, and inscriptions supports a middle position between claims of full integration and of local subsistence. Rome and provincial cities displayed the monumental results of imperial patronage and local euergetism, but they rested on rural labor and coexisted with poverty and inequality.

The army and frontiers were central. Legions and auxiliaries were stationed in permanent bases that shaped local economies and societies, and frontiers, whether on the Rhine and Danube, in Britain, in the East, or in North Africa, functioned as zones of control, exchange, and violence rather than sealed borders. The Pax Romana was imperial order, not the absence of conflict. It coexisted with conquest, rebellion, and coercion, and its benefits fell unevenly.

Social and cultural history shows both integration and diversity. Citizenship expanded through service, grants, and local office, culminating in the Constitutio Antoniniana of 212, which turned Roman status into a legal identity shared by free people of many cultures. The older model of Romanization has been challenged by scholars who emphasize local agency, adaptation, hybridization, and the coercive aspects of empire, though the concept of becoming Roman remains a subject of debate. Greek, Latin, Punic, Aramaic, Egyptian, Celtic, and other languages and traditions persisted and interacted.

Daily life differed enormously. Most people were farmers, tenants, or laborers, and slavery was pervasive. Women, children, soldiers, migrants, and enslaved people experienced the empire differently from elites, and demographic conditions were harsh. Evidence from Egypt, Vindolanda, Pompeii, and elsewhere allows some of these lives to be reconstructed, though imperfectly.

By 235 CE, the empire remained formidable, with mature institutions, extensive infrastructure, a professional army, and a citizenry extending across the Mediterranean world. Yet its strengths generated dependencies: expensive armies, contested succession, long frontiers, fiscal pressures, reliance on local elites, and vulnerability to epidemic disease and to stronger rivals. These would intensify after the murder of Severus Alexander, and Chapter 24 examines the resulting crisis. Chapter 23 first turns to the religious transformation under way within the same imperial world.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Roman Empire at its height was less an efficient machine than a negotiated system. Its central government was small, its officials few, and its communications slow, yet it endured for centuries because it aligned the interests of an emperor, an army, and thousands of local elites who found in imperial rule protection, status, and opportunity. That alignment produced a Mediterranean world of unusual connectivity, with roads, ports, cities, and law shared across many cultures. It also depended on conquest, taxation, and slavery, which meant that its benefits were unequal and that its stability was never universal.

The chapter also complicates common images of Roman civilization. The empire did not simply spread a uniform culture. It created a framework in which Greek, Latin, Punic, Aramaic, Egyptian, Celtic, and many other traditions coexisted, and in which "Roman" came to mean a legal and political status rather than a single way of life. That flexibility is one of the empire's most important legacies, and it is also a reminder to resist reading later national identities backward into the ancient Mediterranean, a caution introduced in Chapter 1.

In the larger argument of Volume I, this chapter marks the point at which one political system unified much of the Mediterranean and large parts of Europe on a scale unmatched in the region for centuries. Later chapters will show what happened when that system was strained. Chapter 23 examines the religious transformation that developed within it, Chapter 24 the crisis that followed 235 CE, and the chapters that follow trace how the empire was reorganized and how its western territories were transformed. The eastern half of the empire, which will remain Roman in its own understanding for centuries, is examined in Part VI.

Students should carry forward two habits of mind. The first is to ask how a political system actually works, through which institutions, intermediaries, and incentives, rather than accepting its self image. The second is to ask consistently whose experience the evidence represents. The same empire that built the aqueducts also relied on enslaved miners, and the same roads that carried imperial couriers carried migrants and merchants. Understanding both is essential to understanding the Roman world honestly.
