Humanism and the Recovery of Classical Learning

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In June 1345, Francesco Petrarca, whom English readers call Petrarch, sat down in northern Italy and wrote a letter to Marcus Tullius Cicero, a man who had been dead for nearly fourteen centuries. The occasion was a manuscript. Petrarch had recently found, in the library of the cathedral chapter of Verona, a collection of Cicero's private letters, and what he read there disappointed him. The letter he wrote back across the centuries is a reproach, and it is also a declaration that antiquity could be read as the work of real, fallible human beings.

The scene is worth pausing over because it contains most of what this chapter examines. A manuscript had survived, copied and recopied by hands unknown to us, and had come to rest in a church library. A reader with trained Latin and strong opinions about Roman moral character found it, read it closely, and treated its author as a person with a historical situation rather than as a timeless authority. He then did what educated men of his time did with what they valued: he copied it, circulated news of it through correspondence, and wrote about it in polished Latin. In one episode we see the search for texts, the cultivation of classical language, the use of letters as a medium of scholarship, and a new curiosity about the historical distance between ancient Rome and the present.

This chapter studies the movement that historians call Renaissance humanism. Humanism in this sense was not a single philosophy, and it was certainly not the modern secular outlook that shares its name. It was a program of study and a professional culture, centered on grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, and devoted to recovering, editing, teaching, and imitating the literature of Greece and Rome. Its participants were teachers, secretaries, chancellors, translators, librarians, and court scholars. Many were devout Christians. Some were bitter rivals. What united them was a conviction that careful study of classical language and literature could improve both expression and conduct, and that educated persons should be formed by it.

The chapter's second purpose is to resist a tempting simplification. It is easy to narrate humanism as a rediscovery, in which a medieval darkness gave way to the light of antiquity, and many humanists themselves told the story in precisely that way. Modern historians read such statements as part of the self-presentation of a cultural movement rather than as neutral description. Monasteries, cathedral schools, and universities had preserved and studied classical texts for centuries, as Chapter 31 and Chapter 48 showed. Humanists changed which texts they valued, how they read them, what they thought a good Latin sentence looked like, and what they believed education was for. That is a substantial transformation, but it was built on medieval foundations and carried out largely by Christians within Christian institutions.

The evidence for this history is unusually direct. We have the manuscripts that humanists collected and annotated, the letters they exchanged, the school treatises they wrote, the translations they produced, the orations they delivered, and the catalogues of the libraries they assembled. We also have their quarrels, because humanists wrote invective as readily as they wrote praise. Each of these sources must be read with its purpose in mind. A letter published by its author was shaped for posterity. A treatise on education described an ideal, not necessarily a classroom. A claim to have rescued a text from neglect might be accurate, or might be rhetoric.

Within Part IX, this chapter follows Chapter 56, which examined the Italian city-states and the political, commercial, and urban conditions in which the Renaissance arose. Here the emphasis falls on what scholars learned, taught, wrote, and believed about the classical inheritance. The artistic transformations associated with the same cultural environment belong to Chapter 58. The diffusion of humanist methods beyond the Alps belongs to Chapter 59, and the political, diplomatic, and print-cultural consequences are reserved for Chapter 60. At the start of Volume III, Chapter 57 therefore supplies the intellectual foundation on which much of the Volume's later argument about religious reform, state formation, and the changing European mind will rest.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define Renaissance humanism historically and distinguish it from modern secular humanism and from the whole of Renaissance philosophy.
  2. Explain how medieval monasteries, cathedral schools, universities, notaries, and translators prepared the ground for humanist scholarship.
  3. Trace the contributions of Petrarch, Boccaccio, Salutati, Bruni, Poggio Bracciolini, and Lorenzo Valla to the recovery and study of classical texts.
  4. Describe the methods of manuscript recovery, copying, collation, and philological criticism, and assess their significance for historical reasoning.
  5. Analyze the gradual revival of Greek learning in Italy, including the roles of Manuel Chrysoloras, Byzantine teachers, and the Council of Ferrara-Florence.
  6. Compare humanist and scholastic methods and explain why the two traditions interacted as much as they conflicted.
  7. Explain the studia humanitatis and the educational programs of humanist teachers such as Guarino da Verona and Vittorino da Feltre.
  8. Evaluate the concept of civic humanism and the debate over its relationship to Florentine political practice.
  9. Examine how humanists served republics, princely courts, and the papacy, and what dependence on patrons meant for their work.
  10. Interpret the experiences of women humanists and explain the social limits on Renaissance learning.
  11. Distinguish among humanist ideas about human dignity, moral formation, and religion without reducing them to a single doctrine.
  12. Connect the chapter's developments to the interpretive debate over whether humanism represented a rupture with medieval culture.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What distinguished Renaissance humanists from earlier readers of classical texts, and what did they share with them?
  2. How did humanists learn to treat ancient and medieval texts as evidence located in particular historical contexts?
  3. Why did the recovery of Greek take place gradually and through Byzantine teachers, and why should it not be attributed only to 1453?
  4. In what ways did schools, correspondence, libraries, and patrons make humanist learning a social enterprise as well as an intellectual one?
  5. How should historians evaluate claims that humanists revived republican liberty when Florentine political institutions remained narrow and oligarchic?

  7. Who was excluded from humanist education, and what do the careers of women scholars reveal about the movement's limits?

Historical Context

Chapter 56 explained why northern and central Italy became the setting for the early Renaissance. Its cities were densely urban, commercially wealthy, politically competitive, and governed by communal, oligarchic, signorial, or princely regimes that needed literate administrators, ambassadors, and legal specialists. Those conditions created a demand for people trained in Latin composition, law, and persuasive writing, and they created patrons able to pay for books and for the scholars who copied and interpreted them. This chapter assumes that background and asks what those educated people actually did with classical learning.

The learning they inherited was not small. Latin remained the language of the church, the universities, law, diplomacy, and much of medicine and philosophy. Roman authors such as Virgil, Cicero, Seneca, and Ovid had been read in schools throughout the Middle Ages, and the late antique and medieval Christian authors who followed them, Augustine and Jerome especially, were themselves steeped in classical literary culture. Universities, examined in Chapter 48, had organized advanced study around law, medicine, theology, and an arts curriculum dominated by logic and the works of Aristotle. In Italy, education was also supplied outside universities. A Florentine chronicler, Giovanni Villani, reported in the 1330s that thousands of boys were learning to read, that more than a thousand were studying practical arithmetic in abacus schools, and that several hundred were learning Latin grammar and logic. His figures are contemporary estimates and should be treated as indications of scale, not exact counts, but they show a society in which basic literacy among boys of some means was widespread and in which commercial and Latin schooling followed different tracks.

Two further developments shaped the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. The first was the instability of the Latin church, examined in Chapter 53, whose papal residence at Avignon and subsequent Great Schism drew clerics, secretaries, and scholars into courts and councils. These gatherings, especially the Council of Constance (1414 to 1418), brought Italian scholars into contact with monastic libraries north of the Alps. The second was the shrinking position of the Byzantine Empire, discussed in Chapters 40 and 41. As Ottoman power advanced, Byzantine emperors and churchmen traveled west seeking assistance, and some of these envoys were also scholars of Greek who found themselves in demand. The Greek learning that reached Italy therefore arrived through diplomacy, church politics, and trade, long before the fall of Constantinople in 1453.

The material basis of learning also mattered. Books before the spread of printing, a development reserved for Chapters 55 and 60, were copied by hand onto parchment or increasingly onto paper, and each copy introduced the possibility of error. A text might survive in a single damaged manuscript, or in several divergent copies. Scholars who wished to read an ancient work accurately had first to locate it, then to compare witnesses, and finally to judge which readings were authentic. The development of those habits, and of the institutions that supported them, is the subject of much of this chapter.

The historian's sources reflect this world. Manuscripts survive with the marginal notes of their readers. Letters, often collected and revised by their authors, record friendships, rivalries, and requests for books. School treatises set out programs of study. Orations, chancery letters, translations, dedicatory prefaces, and library inventories show humanists at work in public and private roles. Because the surviving record privileges those who wrote in Latin, held office, or attracted patronage, it says much more about male scholars with elite connections than about students, copyists, bookshop workers, and the women who studied at home. This chapter accordingly pays attention to what the evidence leaves out.

Section 57.1

What Was Renaissance Humanism?

The words "humanist" and "humanism" invite misunderstanding because their modern meanings differ from their historical ones. In the fifteenth century, the Italian word umanista appeared in university and school usage for a teacher or student of the humanities, in the same way that a "jurist" was a student of law. The abstract noun "humanism" is much later. It was coined in the early nineteenth century, by a German educator, to describe an education centered on the Greek and Latin classics, and it was soon adopted by historians. The Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt, writing in 1860, helped make humanism a central concept in the story of the Renaissance, but he also associated it with a new individualism and a turn away from religion that more recent scholarship has substantially qualified.

The most influential twentieth-century corrective came from Paul Oskar Kristeller, who argued that Renaissance humanism should be understood as a cultural and educational program rather than as a philosophical doctrine. Humanists, in his account, were professional teachers and writers of grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, who might hold any of a wide range of philosophical and religious views. This definition has been refined and challenged, but it remains a useful starting point. It explains why humanists could be Platonists, Aristotelians, Stoics, skeptics, or simply uninterested in systematic philosophy, and why most were practicing Christians. Humanism in this sense differs sharply from modern secular humanism, which is a worldview that excludes appeal to the supernatural.

Humanism emerged gradually, over generations, in Italian cities from about the late thirteenth century, and it did not spread at the same speed everywhere. Padua, Verona, Florence, the papal court at Avignon, and later Venice, Rome, Milan, Ferrara, Mantua, and Naples each contributed. Its principal language was Latin, though many humanists also wrote in the vernacular, and some, such as Dante before them and Boccaccio and Leon Battista Alberti after them, defended or elevated Tuscan prose and poetry. The central intellectual problems the movement addressed were practical as well as theoretical. How should one read an ancient text, and what did it mean to imitate it? What should education aim at, and for whom? How might a Christian use pagan literature? And what, if anything, did antiquity teach about politics and the good life? The following sections take these questions in turn, beginning with the medieval inheritance that made them possible.

Section 57.2

Medieval Learning and the Classical Inheritance

Medieval Europe did not lose antiquity and then find it again. Monasteries preserved and copied Latin texts from the Carolingian period onward, as Chapter 31 explained, and Carolingian scholars such as Alcuin and, later, Lupus of Ferrières, a ninth-century abbot who collected manuscripts and compared copies, took pains to correct and circulate classical works. Cathedral schools of the eleventh and twelfth centuries taught grammar through classical poets and prose writers, and Latin literary models such as Virgil, Horace, Ovid, and Cicero were studied as "authors" worthy of commentary. Christian writers drew freely on them. Augustine, Jerome, and Boethius had built Christian thought partly out of pagan philosophy and rhetoric, a process traced in Chapter 26.

Medieval scholars also reached beyond the Latin inheritance. In Spain, particularly at Toledo, and in southern Italy and Sicily, scholars translated Greek and Arabic works into Latin during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Gerard of Cremona, in the twelfth century, translated a large body of Arabic scientific and philosophical writing, and in the thirteenth century William of Moerbeke translated Aristotle and other Greek authors directly from Greek, often with a very literal method. These efforts supplied the universities with the Aristotelian corpus that dominated scholastic learning, and they remind us that the medieval West was not culturally closed.

In Italy, a distinctive lay literate culture developed alongside the clerical one. Notaries, judges, and city officials needed training in Latin composition, and a tradition of ars dictaminis, the formal art of letter writing taught especially at Bologna, supplied models for official correspondence. Around Padua at the end of the thirteenth century, jurists and notaries such as Lovato Lovati and, a generation later, Albertino Mussato wrote Latin verse and drama that deliberately imitated Roman poets and studied the Roman tragedian Seneca. Historians such as Ronald Witt have argued that this lay, legal, and notarial milieu was an important source of the later humanist movement, a thesis that places the movement's roots before Petrarch and in professions rather than in a single brilliant individual.

What, then, was new? The answer lies less in access to ancient texts than in attitudes toward them. Humanists increasingly regarded the classical Latin of the first century BCE as the standard of excellence and medieval Latin as corrupt or "barbarous." They sought a wider range of ancient writers, especially historians, orators, and moral writers, and they treated them less as sources of quotations to be fitted into a scholastic argument than as whole works from another time that required historical understanding. They also came to prize the ability to write in the classical manner. These shifts, rather than a sudden discovery of antiquity, are the basis for describing humanism as a transformation of learning. As Section 57.19 will explore, historians continue to debate how sharp a break this represents.

Section 57.3

Petrarch and the New Engagement with Antiquity

Francesco Petrarca was born in Arezzo in 1304 to a Florentine notary exiled from his native city. He grew up largely in Provence, near the papal court at Avignon, studied law at Montpellier and Bologna, and then took minor ecclesiastical orders that gave him benefices and the leisure to write. Over a long life he divided his time among Avignon, Vaucluse, various Italian cities, and eventually Arquà near Padua, where he died in 1374. He wrote Latin epic poetry, historical and moral treatises, and many letters, as well as the Italian lyrics later assembled as the Canzoniere, which brought him wide fame. In April 1341 he was crowned with the laurel on the Capitoline Hill in Rome, a ceremony that deliberately evoked ancient practice and that Petrarch regarded as recognizing the restoration of poetry to its ancient honors.

His scholarship was equally important. Petrarch collected manuscripts of Livy, Virgil, Cicero, and other classical authors, annotated them heavily, and tried to reconstruct Livy's history from scattered copies of its surviving parts. His discovery of Cicero's letters to Atticus at Verona in 1345 is among the best-known episodes in the history of classical scholarship. The letters revealed a Cicero who vacillated, complained, and compromised, and Petrarch's reproachful letter to him is simultaneously an expression of disappointment and a recognition that an ancient writer was a person with a biography. In another letter he described good imitation as the resemblance of a son to a father, not of a portrait to its subject, which means that he did not want writers to copy the ancients mechanically but to absorb their qualities and express them in their own voices.

Petrarch also contributed a way of thinking about history. He regarded Roman antiquity as a high point of civilization and the centuries following as comparatively dark, a view that later writers expanded into the familiar tripartite scheme of ancient, "middle," and modern ages, a periodization that modern historians treat as an analytical convention rather than a natural division of time. His judgment was literary and moral rather than a modern account of medieval society, and it reflected his own preferences about language and learning. He did not reject Christianity. He admired Augustine intensely, wrote devotional works, and in his later treatise on his own ignorance sharply criticized contemporary dialecticians and Aristotelians who, in his view, valued logical subtleties above moral wisdom. His Christian humanism, if the term is permitted, is a reminder that classical enthusiasm and religious devotion were compatible.

Petrarch's limits should also be recorded. He knew almost no Greek. A Calabrian monk, Barlaam, gave him some instruction in the 1340s, and in 1354 a Byzantine ambassador gave him a manuscript of Homer in the original, which he treasured but could not read. He is a pivotal figure, a model for later humanists, and an effective publicist of his own importance, but he emerged from existing literary and scholarly traditions and had immediate contemporaries and predecessors. He should be described as an influential early humanist, and not as the first or sole founder.

Section 57.4

Boccaccio and the Formation of Humanist Intellectual Networks

Giovanni Boccaccio, born in 1313 and raised partly in Florence and partly in Naples, where his father worked for Florentine banking interests, is best known for the Decameron, the collection of one hundred tales in Tuscan prose composed around 1349 to 1353, whose frame narrative is set during the plague of 1348 examined in Chapter 50. Yet Boccaccio was also a learned Latin writer. His Genealogia deorum gentilium, "Genealogy of the Gentile Gods," was a reference work on classical mythology which defended poetry as compatible with Christian truth, and his De mulieribus claris, "On Famous Women," was a collection of biographies of notable women of antiquity and later periods. Both works became widely used reference books. He also wrote a life of Dante and, in 1373 and 1374, gave public readings on Dante's Commedia in Florence, which helped establish the poem as a subject of civic and scholarly attention.

Boccaccio's friendship with Petrarch exemplifies the personal networks through which humanism spread. The two met in Florence in 1350, corresponded for the rest of Petrarch's life, and exchanged books, advice, and criticism. Boccaccio copied manuscripts himself, with his own hand, and assembled a substantial library which he left, at his death, to an Augustinian friar at the church of Santo Spirito in Florence, so that his books became available to a religious community and, through it, to later scholars. A later anecdote reports that he found the library at Monte Cassino neglected and ruined, but the source is a later report and should be treated with caution.

Boccaccio's most consequential sponsorship concerned Greek. Around 1360, he supported the presence in Florence of Leontius Pilatus, a scholar from Calabria who knew Greek and who taught and translated Homer into Latin. Pilatus was an irascible and difficult man, and his Latin renderings were crude, but his residence demonstrated that Greek could be taught in Florence and produced a Latin Homer that Petrarch and Boccaccio could use. It also showed how much depended on the patronage of a few well-placed individuals, since the effort lapsed after Pilatus left.

Taken together, Petrarch and Boccaccio established a pattern: scholars connected through letters and visits, exchanged manuscripts, supported one another's projects, and cultivated an audience of younger readers. That audience included Coluccio Salutati, who regarded both as masters, and through him a rising generation of Florentine scholars and officials. Humanism was thus from an early date a social network as much as a literary program, one that relied on friendship, patronage, and the shared conviction that good letters were worth the trouble of acquiring.

Section 57.5

Cicero, Classical Latin, and the Ideal of Eloquence

No ancient author was more prominent in humanist culture than Marcus Tullius Cicero, the Roman orator, politician, and philosopher whose public career, examined in Chapters 19 and 20, ended with his execution in 43 BCE. Cicero offered humanists several things at once. His speeches were models of persuasive composition. His philosophical dialogues presented Greek moral thought in elegant Latin. His letters, rediscovered in the fourteenth century, provided a model of personal and political correspondence. And his own term humanitas, which he used for the cultivated character formed by liberal study, gave the movement its name through the phrase studia humanitatis. The ideal of the orator as a virtuous and learned person who uses eloquence for the common good became a founding aspiration of humanist education.

The study of Cicero was joined by the study of the Roman teacher of rhetoric Quintilian, whose Institutio oratoria described the complete education of an orator from childhood. Only parts of it had circulated in the Middle Ages. Poggio Bracciolini's discovery of a complete manuscript at St. Gall in 1416, discussed in Section 57.6, gave humanist educators a full account of ancient pedagogy. Other Roman writers, including Sallust, Livy, Virgil, Horace, and Seneca, supplied models of historical narrative, poetry, and moral reflection.

Imitation was the method. Students and writers read the ancients closely, absorbed their vocabulary and syntax, and composed their own speeches, letters, and poems in the same style. Humanists debated how closely to follow a model. Some, following Petrarch, argued for assimilation rather than copying, while others pursued stricter fidelity. A later and more intense quarrel over strict Ciceronianism belongs to the sixteenth century and is touched on in Chapter 59. At this stage the main point is that classical Latin became a cultural standard against which medieval Latin, with its distinctive vocabulary and constructions, was judged inferior. Lorenzo Valla's Elegantiae linguae Latinae, a study of correct Latin usage that circulated from the 1440s, was among the most influential works in this campaign.

The prestige of eloquence had social consequences. A mastery of classical Latin signaled education and status, qualified a man for chancery or diplomatic service, and gave him a claim to moral authority. It also created a barrier, because Latin education was costly, available chiefly to boys of means, and did not reach most of the population. Humanists were criticized from several directions. Some clerics feared that admiration for pagan writers undermined Christian piety, and the Florentine Dominican Giovanni Dominici argued against classical literature in the early fifteenth century, while Salutati replied in defense of the study of pagan poets. Vernacular writers, and humanists who wrote in Tuscan, also showed that eloquence was not restricted to Latin.

Section 57.6

Manuscript Hunting, Libraries, and the Recovery of Texts

The recovery of classical texts was a purposeful activity. Humanists wrote to friends and patrons asking them to look through monastic libraries, to buy codices, to arrange for copies, and to report on what they found. Such work depended on travel and on institutions that made travel possible. The Council of Constance, which met from 1414 to 1418 to end the Great Schism, gathered thousands of clergy, officials, and scholars in a German-speaking city near monastic libraries. Poggio Bracciolini, a papal secretary and gifted calligrapher, used breaks in the council's business to visit nearby monasteries. In 1416 he found at St. Gall a complete text of Quintilian's Institutio oratoria, and in the following year he located and arranged for a copy of Lucretius's De rerum natura, a Latin philosophical poem expounding Epicurean ideas that had been rare in the medieval West. The monastery that held the Lucretius manuscript is not certain, though the abbey of Fulda is frequently suggested. Poggio also found works by Cicero, Vitruvius, Statius, and other authors. In 1421 another scholar, Gerardo Landriani, discovered at Lodi a manuscript of Cicero's major rhetorical works, including a complete De oratore.

Accounts of these discoveries require care. Humanists often presented them as rescues of texts from neglect and ignorance, and a famous letter of Poggio describes St. Gall in terms of near-imprisonment for Quintilian. Such statements served the writer's reputation and the narrative of recovery. Monks usually knew their books and often cooperated willingly in the production of copies, sometimes in exchange for payment or favors. Many of the "discovered" texts were already known to scholars in some form, and some had circulated in fragments. The achievement was real, because complete texts reached a wider educated audience and were copied rapidly, but the heroic language has to be read as part of humanist self-presentation.

Copying changed as well as spread texts. Humanists in Florence, including Salutati, Poggio, and the wealthy collector Niccolò Niccoli, developed a clearer, more regular handwriting that modeled itself on the Carolingian minuscule of early medieval manuscripts, which they mistakenly believed to be the script of ancient Rome. This "humanist script" and the slanting cursive derived from it, italic, became standard in Italian manuscripts and later in printing. The error is instructive: humanists admired antiquity so intensely that they sometimes mistook a ninth-century hand for a Roman one.

Libraries gave recovered texts a home. Niccoli assembled a large collection. After his death in 1437, his executors, including Cosimo de' Medici, arranged for it to serve the public benefit, and with Cosimo's support the books became the core of the library established at the convent of San Marco in Florence, which opened in 1444 as one of the earliest libraries accessible to scholars. The Visconti rulers of Milan formed a notable library at Pavia that included Petrarch's books. Pope Nicholas V sought manuscripts for what became the Vatican Library, which was formally organized under Sixtus IV in 1475. Malatesta Novello established the library at Cesena that still stands, and the bookseller Vespasiano da Bisticci supervised copyists who produced elegant manuscripts for Federico da Montefeltro of Urbino and others. Mass printing, a development of the second half of the fifteenth century treated in Chapters 55 and 60, later multiplied copies, but before it the production of a single book required months of skilled labor and substantial expense.

Section 57.7

Philology, Textual Criticism, and Historical Evidence

Historical Vocabulary
Philology

Philology is the study of language and texts in their historical contexts, including the comparison of manuscripts, the identification of copying errors, the study of grammar and vocabulary, and the evaluation of authorship and date. In the Renaissance, philology was not yet a separate discipline. It was practiced by grammarians, rhetoricians, and teachers who were also poets, secretaries, or historians.

Philology matters here because it gave humanists a method for treating texts as products of particular times. It should not be confused with modern scientific textual editing, which relies on later techniques for classifying manuscripts into families, although the humanists' work prepared the way. Its relationship to theology and law made it politically and religiously consequential.

Recovered manuscripts were rarely perfect. Scribes introduced errors, omitted lines, or "corrected" what they did not understand, and copies of copies compounded the problem. Humanists therefore began to compare manuscripts of the same work, to decide among variants, and to conjecture emendations where all witnesses seemed corrupt. Petrarch and Salutati collated manuscripts, Poggio and his contemporaries judged copies by their scripts and sources, and by the later fifteenth century Angelo Poliziano, a Florentine scholar, was examining the relationship among manuscripts with considerable sophistication. These procedures were uneven and sometimes arbitrary by modern standards, but the underlying premise, that a text had an authentic form distinct from its accumulated corruptions, was a decisive intellectual step.

Humanist philology also developed tools for historical judgment. Because humanists knew classical Latin well, they could detect anachronism, that is, language or ideas out of place in the supposed period of a document. Petrarch, in 1355, examined for the emperor Charles IV some privileges that purported to come from Julius Caesar and Nero and concluded, on grounds of style and content, that they were forgeries. The German scholar Nicholas of Cusa, in 1433, argued that the Donation of Constantine was spurious. The most celebrated critique, however, was Lorenzo Valla's.

The Donation of Constantine was a text, produced probably in the eighth century in or near papal circles, that claimed the first Christian emperor had granted the pope and his successors authority over Rome, Italy, and the western provinces when he moved his capital to Constantinople. Medieval popes and canon lawyers cited it at various times, and parts of it entered the canon law collections of the twelfth century, though its use varied and many medieval authorities did not rely on it. Valla, a Roman-born scholar then in the service of King Alfonso V of Aragon, wrote his declamation De falso credita et ementita Constantini donatione around 1440. Alfonso was at that time in conflict with Pope Eugenius IV, and Valla's polemic had political utility. Its force, however, came from arguments of language and historical probability. Valla's work illustrates how humanist skills could be turned against an institution that claimed support from antiquity.

Section 57.8

The Revival of Greek Learning in Italy

Greek had never entirely disappeared from the Latin West. Southern Italy and Sicily contained Greek-speaking communities and monasteries, Venetian and Genoese merchants traded in Byzantine ports, and a few Latin scholars, such as William of Moerbeke, had learned the language. Yet before the late fourteenth century few Italians could read classical Greek literature, and Petrarch, as noted, could not read his own Homer. The decisive change began when Coluccio Salutati and others invited the Byzantine scholar Manuel Chrysoloras to teach in Florence. Chrysoloras arrived in 1397 and taught until 1400, with a public salary at the Florentine Studio. He had come west partly as an envoy of the Byzantine emperor Manuel II, who sought military and diplomatic support against the Ottomans, a context described in Chapter 41.

Chrysoloras was an effective teacher. He composed a Greek grammar in question-and-answer form, the Erotemata, which became a standard beginner's text, and he trained students who became leading scholars, among them Leonardo Bruni, Guarino da Verona, and several members of the Florentine elite. Bruni later recalled the excitement with which he abandoned some of his legal studies to learn Greek. Chrysoloras also taught at Pavia, Milan, Venice, and Rome, and he died in 1415 while attending the Council of Constance as a representative of the Byzantine emperor. With Uberto Decembrio he produced a Latin translation of Plato's Republic, an early example of the Latin rendering of Plato that would continue through the century.

Subsequent contacts widened the stream. Guarino spent several years in Constantinople early in the fifteenth century, and Francesco Filelfo studied and married in the Byzantine capital in the 1420s. Giovanni Aurispa traveled in the East and returned to Venice in 1423 with 238 Greek manuscripts, including important texts of Greek poets and dramatists. The Council of Ferrara-Florence of 1438 and 1439, convened to negotiate the union of the Latin and Greek churches discussed in Chapter 40, brought a large Byzantine delegation to Italy. Among its members were Bessarion, a churchman and scholar who later became a cardinal in the Latin church, and Gemistos Plethon, a philosopher whose lectures on the differences between Plato and Aristotle stirred Florentine interest in Plato. In the decades after the council, Theodore Gaza taught Greek at Ferrara, John Argyropoulos lectured on Aristotle and Greek literature at Florence from 1456, and Bessarion built a great collection of Greek manuscripts, which he donated to Venice in 1468.

The fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453 hastened the movement of scholars and books westward but did not initiate it. Some of the best-known Greek teachers had arrived before 1453, others came for varied reasons including patronage, church careers, and the instability of the Byzantine world, and the flow of manuscripts continued into the 1490s, when Janus Lascaris traveled east to collect for Lorenzo de' Medici. Greek learning also remained a minority accomplishment. Many humanists read little or no Greek, and many students encountered Greek authors in Latin translation. The revival of Greek was gradual, depended on Byzantine teachers, and benefited from the cooperation of Italian patrons who valued what they brought.

Section 57.9

Translation and Intellectual Exchange across Cultural Boundaries

Translation was the main means by which Greek writings reached Latin readers. Humanist translators differed from medieval ones in method. Medieval translators such as Moerbeke often rendered Greek word for word, which preserved the original's structure but produced awkward Latin. Leonardo Bruni, in a treatise on correct translation written in the 1420s, argued that a translator should convey the sense and style of the original in elegant Latin, a method often described by the contrast between ad verbum (word for word) and ad sensum (according to sense). Bruni's own versions of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and Politics, produced between about 1416 and 1438, provoked controversy because he replaced the established medieval translation, and critics such as the Castilian bishop Alonso de Cartagena objected that he sacrificed precision and the established technical vocabulary of scholastic philosophy. Humanists also translated Plato, Plutarch, Xenophon, Lucian, and Greek historians. Valla translated Herodotus and Thucydides for Nicholas V, and a Latin version of Xenophon's Cyropaedia circulated in the same milieu, with manuscript catalogues attributing it to Poggio in some cases and to Francesco Filelfo in others. In religious literature, Ambrogio Traversari, a Camaldolese monk and prior general of his order, translated Greek patristic writings and the Lives of the Philosophers of Diogenes Laertius, demonstrating that monastic scholars were also deeply involved.

Humanist relations with Arabic and Hebrew learning were more complicated. Much of the Latin Aristotelian tradition, as well as medical and scientific literature, had reached Europe through Arabic intermediaries, and universities in the fifteenth century continued to teach Avicenna in medicine and Averroes's commentaries in philosophy. Some humanists scorned this inheritance as a distortion of Greek originals and advocated going directly to Greek sources, a position visible in medicine, where scholars such as Niccolò Leoniceno of Ferrara criticized errors in inherited authorities and promoted Greek medical texts. Other scholars sought to broaden their linguistic equipment. Giannozzo Manetti learned Hebrew and Greek and translated parts of the Bible, and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola studied Hebrew and Arabic with the help of several Jewish teachers and converts. Elia del Medigo, a Jewish philosopher from Crete working in Italy, was important chiefly for his interpretation of Jewish Aristotelian and Averroist philosophy, Yohanan Alemanno taught Pico Hebrew and Kabbalistic literature, and Flavius Mithridates produced Latin translations of Hebrew, Aramaic, and Arabic works for him. Jewish communities in Italy were thus both a source of linguistic expertise and a minority subject to restriction, a duality that the humanist narrative sometimes obscures.

These exchanges show that humanism was embedded in Mediterranean networks of knowledge. Byzantine Greeks provided teachers and books, Italian merchants and diplomats provided transport and funds, Jewish and Muslim intellectual traditions supplied sources and sometimes teachers, and the Latin church framed many of the institutions in which scholars worked. The "recovery" of classical learning was therefore a process of translation across languages, religions, and political boundaries, and the participants included many who are less well remembered than the Italians who organized the results.

Section 57.10

Humanist Correspondence, Scholarly Communities, and Libraries

Humanist scholarship moved by letter. Writers exchanged requests for books, reports of discoveries, advice, gossip, and praise, and they often prepared collections of their letters for circulation or publication. Petrarch edited his own correspondence with great care, and later humanists, among them Salutati, Poggio, Bruni, and Guarino, left large bodies of letters that are the main sources for their relationships and projects. Such collections are not spontaneous records. The authors selected, revised, and arranged them to display learning and to shape reputation, so historians must consider why a letter was preserved and for whom it was written.

Letters were also instruments of collaboration. Scholars lent manuscripts for copying, arranged for the exchange of books, vouched for one another with patrons, and recommended students and clients. Small groups formed around libraries and teachers, as in the circle of Niccolò Niccoli in Florence, the household of Cosimo de' Medici, the humanist gatherings in Rome associated with Pomponio Leto, whose academy came under papal suspicion in 1468, and the friends and students who surrounded teachers such as Guarino. These communities were informal, and the term "academy" in the fifteenth century described a loose association of scholars rather than a chartered institution.

Competition was fierce. Humanists wrote invective, a genre of abusive rhetoric modeled on Roman examples, and quarrels between Poggio and Valla, Poggio and Filelfo, and others were extensively aired in treatises and letters. Invective served as a form of public performance and a means of claiming standing, and much of its abuse is conventional, though real animosities lay behind it. Patronage determined careers. A scholar needed a salary, a position in a chancery, a court, a university, or a church, or the generosity of a wealthy sponsor, and the loss of a patron could be calamitous. Humanist writing is full of dedications, requests, and expressions of gratitude, reflecting this dependence.

The practical work of scholarship was labor-intensive. Copyists, binders, booksellers, and parchment makers formed part of the world that produced humanist books, though they often leave little record. A book was a costly object, and a library represented a substantial investment. These material conditions explain why wealthy individuals and institutions, such as the Medici, the Este, the Gonzaga, the Montefeltro, the Visconti and Sforza, and the papacy, held so much influence over humanist culture. They also explain why communities of scholars valued libraries that opened their collections to others.

Section 57.11

The Studia Humanitatis and the Humanist Curriculum

Historical Vocabulary
Studia Humanitatis

The studia humanitatis, "studies of humanity," named the cluster of subjects taught by humanist masters: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, all pursued through the reading of classical authors. The phrase echoes Cicero and the Roman writer Aulus Gellius, who used humanitas for the education and character appropriate to a cultivated person, and it was in common use by the early fifteenth century.

The term matters because it identifies what humanists professionally did. It did not include law, medicine, theology, or natural philosophy, which had their own faculties and traditions, and it should not be mistaken for a complete system of Renaissance philosophy. The modern word "humanities" descends from it, but the Renaissance meaning was narrower and more specifically tied to classical texts.

The five subjects of the studia humanitatis formed a connected program. Grammar taught correct Latin and, increasingly, Greek, through close reading of poets and prose writers. Rhetoric taught how to compose speeches, letters, and arguments, drawing on Cicero and Quintilian. Poetry, understood as a branch of literary culture rather than mere entertainment, taught style, mythology, and moral reflection. History, studied through authors such as Livy, Sallust, and Caesar, supplied examples of virtue and vice and an understanding of political life. Moral philosophy, including Cicero's ethical writings, Seneca, and increasingly Aristotle's Ethics, addressed the conduct of life. The aim was both eloquence and virtue. An educated person should speak well, but should also use that ability for the common good.

Humanist educators expressed this program in treatises. Pier Paolo Vergerio, writing around 1402 or 1403, addressed the education of free-born youth, emphasizing moral formation, the liberal arts, physical exercise, and the careful choice of studies suited to talent and station. Leonardo Bruni's De studiis et litteris, composed in the early fifteenth century for Battista di Montefeltro, a noblewoman of the Malatesta family (scholars disagree about its precise date), recommended the study of Greek and Latin authors, history, poetry, and moral works and warned against subtle logical exercises. Later, in 1450, Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini, the future Pope Pius II, wrote a treatise on the education of a king's son. These works describe ideals, and the extent to which actual schools followed them varied.

The curriculum also had boundaries. It concentrated on Latin literary culture and, for some, Greek, at the expense of the vernacular, technical training, and most natural philosophy. It tended to assume male pupils of means. Advanced training in law, medicine, or theology required further study, usually in universities, and humanist subjects often formed a preliminary or complementary stage. Understanding these limits is necessary for an accurate sense of what humanists claimed and what they actually controlled.

Section 57.12

Humanist Schools, Teachers, and Educational Reform

Two schoolmasters became especially associated with humanist pedagogy. Guarino da Verona, born in 1374, studied Greek under Chrysoloras's circle, taught in Verona, Venice, and Florence, and in 1429 settled at Ferrara as tutor to Leonello d'Este, the heir to the Este marquisate. He taught at Ferrara, including at the university after its reorganization, until his death in 1460, attracting students from many parts of Italy and beyond. Vittorino da Feltre, who studied and taught at Padua, was invited in 1423 by Gianfrancesco Gonzaga, the ruler of Mantua, to educate his children. His school, known as the Casa Giocosa or "Joyful House," combined classical reading with moral instruction, religious observance, and physical exercise. Vittorino admitted, alongside the Gonzaga children, other sons of elite families and some talented boys whose fees were covered by patronage, and the Gonzaga daughters, including Cecilia Gonzaga, also received a humanist education. Such schools remained rare, and the available evidence for them comes largely from admiring biographies and letters.

The new schools sought to replace the grammar manuals of the medieval Latin tradition with direct reading of classical authors, and to teach style by imitation. Instruction relied on reading aloud, memorization, grammatical analysis, and the composition of letters, speeches, and verse. Greek was introduced where a teacher was competent. Humanist teachers also criticized harsh punishment, though corporal discipline remained common and criticism was uneven. Education aimed at preparing young men for service as secretaries, ambassadors, officials, and rulers, and its moral claims, that eloquence should be joined to virtue, reflected the careers its graduates would pursue.

Students from artisan and merchant families often received a different education. Commercial cities such as Florence and Venice maintained abacus schools in which boys learned the practical arithmetic needed for trade, and many merchants' sons learned only the vernacular and basic reckoning. Humanist education therefore did not replace the existing educational landscape. It occupied a prestigious niche within it, often drawing pupils from the political and mercantile elite who wished to qualify for office or to display culture.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside a Humanist School: A Composite Reconstruction

No single document describes a typical day in a humanist classroom. Historians reconstruct the experience from educational treatises, student notebooks, teachers' letters, and biographies, all of which describe practices and ideals with different degrees of reliability. The following account is therefore a composite and should not be taken as a record of any one school.

A schoolmaster such as Guarino was likely to begin with Latin grammar and reading, having pupils recite rules, parse sentences, and read aloud from a classical text, often with the master dictating or explaining difficult words. Because books were expensive, pupils frequently copied the text and the master's commentary into their own notebooks, which meant that writing practice and learning were joined. Afternoons might be given to composition exercises, such as rewriting a letter of Cicero in different words or composing a short speech on a moral theme, and to memorization of verse. Older students read historians and moral writers, and, where the teacher could teach it, began Greek.

Vittorino's school offered a notable addition in physical training and games, motivated by the Roman and Greek ideal of balance between body and mind. Religious observance, including attendance at mass, formed part of daily life, and both teachers and patrons expected moral supervision. The evidence for girls and for poor pupils is thinner. Where a school admitted them, as at Mantua, it did so by the grace of a patron, not through any institutional entitlement. The classroom is therefore best understood as an elite and highly personal space, dependent on the skill and reputation of a single master.

Section 57.13

Humanism, Universities, and Scholastic Traditions

Humanists often presented themselves in opposition to the scholastic masters of the universities, whom they accused of writing in barbarous Latin, of wasting time on logical subtleties, and of neglecting moral and historical learning. Petrarch's attack on contemporary Aristotelians, Bruni's gibes at the schoolmen, and Valla's polemics against medieval logicians and jurists are notable examples. Scholastic thinkers, for their part, regarded humanists as superficial. Some preachers and theologians worried that devotion to pagan authors threatened piety, as Giovanni Dominici's treatise against secular learning and Salutati's reply illustrated.

This portrait of conflict is real but incomplete. The universities of Italy, such as Bologna, Padua, Pavia, and the Florentine Studio, taught arts, law, medicine, and theology, and the arts curriculum emphasized logic and natural philosophy, largely through Aristotle. Humanists in the universities typically held chairs in rhetoric, poetry, or Greek, which were established alongside the existing faculties rather than in place of them. Chrysoloras taught at the Florentine Studio, and Filelfo, Argyropoulos, and others also lectured at universities. Padua remained a center of Aristotelian natural philosophy and medicine throughout the Renaissance, and many scholars moved comfortably between literary and philosophical pursuits. Humanists also studied Aristotle, but they wished to read him in better translations and, increasingly, in Greek. Scholastic philosophers, in turn, absorbed some humanist methods and vocabulary.

Section 57.14

Salutati, Bruni, and Humanism in Florentine Public Life

Coluccio Salutati, born in 1331 and trained as a notary, became chancellor of Florence in 1375, an office he held until his death in 1406. The Florentine chancery drafted official correspondence with rulers, cities, and allies, and Salutati's letters became famous for their learning and persuasiveness. During conflicts with the papacy in the 1370s and then with the Visconti of Milan, his writing served as political argument, and tradition reports that the Milanese ruler Giangaleazzo Visconti said one of his letters was worth a troop of cavalry. The remark is traditional and cannot be verified, but it reflects a contemporary perception that rhetoric had political weight. Salutati defended the study of poetry against its Christian critics, wrote on tyranny and on the relative worth of law and medicine, corresponded widely with scholars, collected manuscripts, and promoted the invitation of Chrysoloras to Florence.

Leonardo Bruni, born at Arezzo around 1370, studied under Salutati and Chrysoloras, served as a papal secretary in the early fifteenth century, and became chancellor of Florence in 1427, holding the post until his death in 1444. His writings shaped the way later generations understood Florence. In his Laudatio Florentinae urbis, composed around 1403 or 1404, he praised the city in a style modeled on ancient panegyric, celebrating its liberty and its cultural and political achievements. In the Dialogi ad Petrum Histrum, written about 1401 and later revised, he engaged earlier Florentine writers and the question of the city's intellectual standing. His largest work, the History of the Florentine People, begun around 1415 and extended over decades, applied a Livian narrative model to the city's history, drew on documentary sources, and argued that Florence had been founded during the Roman Republic by veterans of Sulla, not in the later Empire, which tied the city's identity to republican liberty. The claim served a political purpose and also reflected careful use of sources. The chancellor also composed a funeral oration for Nanni Strozzi in 1428, praising a citizen-soldier in terms drawn from Greek models.

Both men were more than scholars. They were officials in a government where a few families dominated. Salutati served an oligarchic regime after 1382, and Bruni held office under the oligarchy of the early fifteenth century and into the years after 1434, when the Medici rose to dominance. Humanist service in the chancery supplied the city with a polished public voice, provided scholars with income and status, and raised important questions about how classical language could be applied to contemporary governance. These questions are the basis of the debate over "civic humanism."

Section 57.15

Civic Humanism, Republican Liberty, and Historical Debate

Historical Vocabulary
Civic Humanism

Civic humanism is a modern term, popularized by the historian Hans Baron in the 1950s, for the strand of early Italian humanism that celebrated active participation in the political life of the republic, the cultivation of civic virtue, and the idea that liberty depended on citizens' commitment to the common good. Humanists drew on Roman and Greek writers, including Cicero and Aristotle, to articulate these ideals.

The term matters because it names a real body of writing, particularly in Florence, but it can mislead. Humanist praise of liberty did not imply broad political participation, and the term should not be confused with modern democracy. Some historians question whether civic humanism was a distinct movement or one of several political languages available to humanists, and others emphasize its relationship to princely and monarchical settings.

Hans Baron argued in The Crisis of the Early Italian Renaissance (1955) that Florence's resistance to the expansionist designs of Giangaleazzo Visconti around 1400 changed Florentine humanism. Facing a powerful rival, Baron suggested, Florentine writers such as Bruni and Salutati reevaluated the ideal of the contemplative life and turned toward active citizenship, republican liberty, and the Roman Republic as a model. In his account, this "civic humanism" was an important source of modern political thought.

Subsequent scholarship has qualified Baron's thesis in several ways. Critics have argued that Salutati's and Bruni's praise of liberty had earlier antecedents, that humanists wrote with equal enthusiasm for princes, and that the contrast between the contemplative and active lives was less sharp than Baron believed. Some, following Kristeller, have emphasized that humanists were primarily rhetoricians who adapted their skills to whatever regime employed them. Others, such as James Hankins, have argued that humanist political thought was often concerned with virtue and the moral formation of rulers, whether republican or princely, more than with constitutional forms. Quentin Skinner and others, by contrast, have stressed the importance of republican ideas of liberty in Italian political language. These discussions concern interpretation more than evidence, since all agree that texts such as Bruni's Laudatio and History exist and praise liberty. The disagreement concerns what they meant and how representative they were.

The social context supplies a necessary corrective. Florentine "liberty" was the liberty of the commune from outside domination, and participation in office was limited to male members of the guilds who passed periodic scrutinies, which in practice restricted high office to a relatively small group of families. Most residents of the city and the territories it controlled, including wage workers, women, and subject communities, took no part. Humanist celebration of republican freedom coexisted with oligarchic control and, after 1434, with the rise of the Medici. Civic humanism therefore tells us about the ideals and self-image of an educated elite and about the political uses of classical learning. It does not prove that Florence was broadly democratic. The fuller development of Renaissance political thought, including Machiavelli, belongs to Chapter 60.

Section 57.16

Humanism in Princely Courts and the Papal World

Humanists were not found only in republics. Princes and lords valued classical learning for the prestige it conferred and for the practical skills of its practitioners, who composed official letters, orations, histories, and dedications. At Milan, the Visconti and later Sforza rulers employed scholars such as Filelfo and maintained the Pavia library. At Ferrara, the Este family patronized Guarino and his students. At Mantua, the Gonzaga supported Vittorino da Feltre. At Urbino, Federico da Montefeltro assembled a large library through Vespasiano da Bisticci. At Naples, Alfonso V of Aragon, who took the city in 1442, gathered a circle that included Valla, Antonio Beccadelli (called Panormita), and Bartolomeo Facio. Beyond Italy, rulers such as Matthias Corvinus of Hungary began collecting books and employing Italian scholars later in the century, a diffusion treated in Chapters 54 and 59.

The papal court in Rome was one of the largest employers of humanists. Popes needed secretaries who could write elegant Latin, and humanists such as Poggio, Bruni, and Valla served as papal secretaries at different times. Nicholas V (r. 1447 to 1455), himself a scholar, commissioned Latin translations of Greek works, collected manuscripts, and helped lay the foundations of the Vatican Library. Pius II (r. 1458 to 1464), formerly the humanist Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini, wrote prolifically and brought humanist tastes to the papacy. The scholar Flavio Biondo produced antiquarian studies of Roman topography and Italy in the 1440s. In Rome, however, humanist culture also generated suspicion. In 1468 Pope Paul II had members of Pomponio Leto's circle arrested on charges that included conspiracy and, according to some accounts, paganism; they were released, and the group later resumed activity under another pope. The episode shows that papal patronage was real but contingent.

Courtly humanism differed from civic humanism in its institutional context. A court scholar owed his position to a patron's favor and might be rewarded with offices, estates, or pensions, but he could also be dismissed, ignored, or forced to move. The writing that resulted, panegyrics, genealogies, and histories celebrating a dynasty, sometimes strained against scholarly independence. Yet courts also supported genuine scholarly work, as in the translations Nicholas V commissioned or the philological projects at Naples. Humanism was therefore compatible with republics, principalities, and the papacy alike, which helps explain its wide diffusion.

Section 57.17

Women, Education, and the Limits of Humanist Learning

Women were excluded from universities, from civic office, and from the clerical careers through which many male scholars rose. A small number of elite women nonetheless acquired a humanist education, typically in their fathers' households or in princely courts. Battista di Montefeltro, the addressee of Bruni's De studiis et litteris, delivered a Latin oration before the emperor Sigismund in 1433. Cecilia Gonzaga studied Greek and Latin under Vittorino, and later entered a convent. Bruni's treatise, however, advised that women should study religion, morals, history, and literature, but should avoid rhetorical and other training oriented toward public speaking, which he considered unsuitable for them. The educational ideal was thus gendered from the start.

Isotta Nogarola, born in 1418 in Verona, studied with her sister Ginevra under a humanist tutor and corresponded with leading scholars, including Guarino. She became the object of anonymous slander that accused her of immorality, apparently provoked by her public learning, and she withdrew for a time to a life of study and seclusion at home. Around 1451 she wrote a dialogue with the Venetian governor Ludovico Foscarini on whether Adam or Eve bore greater responsibility for the first sin, arguing for Eve's lesser guilt. Cassandra Fedele, born in Venice in 1465, gained a reputation for learning in Latin and Greek, delivered a Latin oration at the University of Padua in 1487, and corresponded with rulers and scholars. In later life she lived modestly, and Venice honored her ceremonially in old age. Laura Cereta, born in Brescia in 1469, wrote Latin letters defending women's capacity for learning and criticizing women who neglected education, and she was widowed young and died in 1499.

These women reveal both opportunity and constraint. Their achievements show that humanist education could be adapted to female pupils, and their letters constitute some of the earliest sustained arguments for women's intellectual capacity in Latin. But they remained exceptions. Few had access to formal institutions, public careers, or secure patronage, and their reputations depended on family support, marriage, or religious life. The existence of distinguished women humanists does not establish widespread educational equality, and the evidence suggests that women who sought scholarly careers frequently faced suspicion, withdrawal, and limited recognition.

Section 57.18

Human Dignity, Moral Philosophy, and Religious Learning

Humanists wrote extensively about ethics, and their moral writings drew on Cicero, Seneca, Plutarch, Aristotle, and Christian authors. Valla's dialogue De voluptate, later retitled De vero bono, staged a debate among Stoic, Epicurean, and Christian speakers about pleasure and the highest good, and scholars still disagree about how far it expressed Valla's own views, with some seeing a defense of Christian happiness and others a more provocative praise of pleasure. Giannozzo Manetti, a Florentine statesman who learned Greek and Hebrew, wrote De dignitate et excellentia hominis around 1452 in answer to a twelfth-century treatise on human wretchedness by Lothar of Segni, the later Pope Innocent III, and emphasized the capacities of the human body and mind. The theme of human dignity was not new to the Renaissance, since patristic and medieval authors had praised the human being as made in the image of God, and Kristeller cautioned against overstating its centrality to humanism, but it received distinctive and eloquent treatment.

Platonic philosophy offered another route. Gemistos Plethon's presence at the Council of Ferrara-Florence stimulated Florentine interest in Plato, and in the 1430s and 1440s a controversy over the relative merits of Plato and Aristotle engaged Byzantine and Italian writers, including George of Trebizond, who attacked Plato, and Bessarion, who defended him in a work published in 1469. Marsilio Ficino, a Florentine priest and physician supported by Cosimo de' Medici, translated Plato's works into Latin, completing them around 1468 or 1469 and publishing them in 1484. He also translated the Corpus Hermeticum, a collection of Greek texts then believed to be extremely ancient, and works of Plotinus, and he wrote his own Platonic Theology, which argued for the immortality of the soul and the compatibility of Platonic philosophy and Christian faith. Later tradition speaks of a "Platonic Academy" at Florence, but modern historians describe it as an informal circle around Ficino rather than a formal institution.

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, a young nobleman educated at several Italian universities, studied Hebrew and Arabic texts, Kabbalah, and scholastic philosophy, and in 1486 composed 900 theses for a public disputation in Rome. A papal commission condemned thirteen of them, and the disputation did not take place. The introductory speech Pico wrote for the occasion, later known as the Oration on the Dignity of Man, a title given to it by later editors, portrays the human being as a creature able to shape its own nature and aspire upward. It has often been read as a manifesto of modern individualism. Contemporary scholars stress its dependence on Platonic, Hermetic, Kabbalistic, and Christian traditions and its character as a rhetorical introduction rather than a systematic treatise.

Humanism also affected Christian learning. Ambrogio Traversari translated Greek Fathers, Manetti produced Latin versions of the Bible, and Valla compared Latin and Greek New Testament texts. These efforts anticipated the biblical scholarship of Erasmus, treated in Chapter 59, and the religious controversies of the sixteenth century, examined from Chapter 61 onward. The overall pattern defies simple summary. Some humanists emphasized piety, others practical statecraft, others skeptical or pagan themes, and many combined elements. Humanism was neither inherently secular nor inherently religious. It supplied tools and texts that Christians and others used for varied purposes.

Section 57.19

The Transformation and Limits of Renaissance Humanism

By the late fifteenth century, humanism had transformed several aspects of European intellectual life. Educated Italians had at their disposal a larger corpus of Latin and Greek literature than their predecessors, studied through more critical methods and in a more elegant register. They had developed institutions, such as humanist schools, chanceries staffed by classical scholars, and libraries open to learned visitors. Teachers, secretaries, and advisers were using classical rhetoric, history, and moral thought to address practical problems. And they had begun to see antiquity as a distinct historical world with its own language, institutions, and values, a perspective that made both imitation and criticism possible.

The movement's limits were equally evident. It was primarily male, elite, Latinate, and urban, and it reached only a small share of the population. It relied on patronage and could be absorbed by the powers it served. It never produced a unified philosophy. Humanists disagreed about politics, religion, and the relative merits of Latin and the vernacular. Their enthusiasm for antiquity sometimes led to errors and idealization, including the mistaken belief that Carolingian script was Roman. And some of their criticism, such as Valla's attack on the Donation, was entangled with political interests.

The consequences extended well beyond the fifteenth century. The philological habits of the humanists provided tools later used by reformers examining scripture, by scholars analyzing law and history, and by scientists reevaluating inherited authority, as later chapters will show, including Chapter 62 and Chapter 81. Humanist educational ideals influenced Protestant and Catholic schooling, including the Jesuit institutions treated in Chapter 65. The movement's networks prepared its spread north of the Alps, examined in Chapter 59, and the printing press, discussed in Chapters 55 and 60, multiplied its texts. The connection between humanism and the later Enlightenment, explored in Chapter 82, is real but indirect and should not be treated as inevitable. Humanist learning prepared the intellectual setting for the artistic developments of Chapter 58, though artists and humanists were not identical groups and their relationship is considered there.

Historical Debate

Was Renaissance Humanism a Rupture with Medieval Intellectual Culture?

The question: Did humanists break decisively with medieval traditions of learning, or did they continue and transform them?

Interpretation A: Humanism marked a significant departure. Following Burckhardt and later scholars such as Eugenio Garin, some historians emphasize humanists' new sense of historical distance, their critique of medieval Latin and scholastic method, their search for texts, and their civic and moral ideals. In this view, the movement created a recognizably new relation to the past and a new cultural program.
Interpretation B: Humanism developed within and from medieval traditions. Other historians, including Kristeller and Ronald Witt in different ways, stress the continuity of grammar, rhetoric, and letter writing from the Middle Ages, the importance of notaries and cathedral schools, and the coexistence of humanists with scholastic masters. They view humanism as a shift in emphasis and style that grew from existing practices.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same sources: manuscripts, letters, school treatises, and the careers of individual scholars. They differ in how they weigh humanists' own claims of novelty against evidence of earlier practice, and in how they define "rupture." Many scholars now combine the views, describing a significant transformation that nevertheless grew from medieval roots.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians periodize European history, how they understand the Renaissance's relation to the Middle Ages, and whether they treat humanists' narrative of "rebirth" as description or rhetoric.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Preserving, Interpreting, and Teaching Classical Texts

What Changed

  • Classical Latin of the first century BCE became the preferred model of style, and medieval Latin was criticized
  • Scholars compared manuscripts, identified anachronisms, and treated texts as historical products
  • Greek reading returned to Latin Italy through Byzantine teachers
  • Humanist schools, chanceries, and libraries became recognizable institutions

What Stayed the Same

  • Latin remained the language of the church, universities, and learned exchange
  • Manuscripts, monastic libraries, and patron networks remained essential to preservation
  • Christianity framed most intellectual life, and many humanists were clerics or devout laymen
  • Education remained largely male and elite, and universities retained their faculties

Who experienced change most directly? Scholars, secretaries, teachers, and their elite students experienced the sharpest change, while peasants, most urban workers, and most women saw little direct difference. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a major cultural transformation can occur within a narrow social band and within inherited institutions.

Cause and Effect

Why Humanism Grew: Manuscripts, Schools, Institutions, and Networks

Long-term causes

Medieval preservation of classical texts, an urban lay literate culture, the demands of communal and princely governments for skilled writers, and Mediterranean links to Byzantium.

Short-term causes

Petrarch's example, church councils that opened northern libraries, Byzantine diplomatic missions, and wealthy patrons willing to fund teachers and books.

Immediate effects

Recovery of Latin works, Greek teaching, new translations, humanist schools, and the employment of scholars in chanceries and courts.

Long-term consequences

Philological methods used in law, scripture, and history, humanist educational models, and networks that prepared the spread of humanism beyond Italy.

Historical complexity: The factors reinforced one another. More manuscripts increased demand for editing, schools created demand for texts, patrons rewarded visible learning, and networks circulated results. No single cause, individual, or event explains the movement, and each factor depended on the others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Renaissance humanism was a program of study, teaching, and writing, centered on the studia humanitatis, that transformed how educated Europeans read, edited, translated, and applied the literature of Greece and Rome. It was not a single philosophy and not the modern secular outlook that bears a similar name. Its participants were teachers, secretaries, chancellors, translators, librarians, and court scholars, most of them Christians, working within the institutions of a changing Italian society.

Humanism grew from medieval foundations. Carolingian monasteries, cathedral schools, universities, translation movements in Spain and Sicily, and a lay notarial culture around Padua had all preserved and studied classical material. What changed was the choice of authors, the preference for classical Latin over medieval Latin, a deepening sense of historical distance from antiquity, and the conviction that eloquence and moral formation could be achieved through imitation of ancient models.

Petrarch and Boccaccio were pivotal early figures, but neither was alone. Petrarch collected and annotated manuscripts, found Cicero's letters at Verona in 1345, and articulated a vision of cultural renewal that also reflected his Christian piety. Boccaccio, besides writing the Decameron, compiled Latin reference works and sponsored the first sustained Greek teaching in Florence. Their friendship set a pattern of scholarship conducted through personal networks.

The recovery of Latin texts depended on travel, correspondence, patrons, and institutions such as the Council of Constance. Poggio Bracciolini and others located works by Quintilian, Lucretius, and Cicero in northern libraries, copied them in a new humanist script, and housed them in growing libraries. Their heroic accounts of rescue must be read as self-presentation, since monks often cooperated and many works were already partly known.

Philology gave humanists a method for treating texts as historical objects. They compared manuscripts, noticed errors, and detected anachronism. Valla's critique of the Donation of Constantine around 1440 demonstrated the method's power, though it was entangled with the politics of Alfonso of Aragon and the papacy, and its wider influence came mainly in the sixteenth century. The revival of Greek began with Chrysoloras in Florence from 1397, continued through travelers such as Guarino and Aurispa, and deepened through the Council of Ferrara-Florence and the later careers of Bessarion, Gaza, and Argyropoulos. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 accelerated, but did not begin, this process.

Humanist education, defined by grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, was developed in treatises and in schools such as those of Guarino at Ferrara and Vittorino at Mantua. It existed beside abacus schools and universities and was never universal. Its relationship with scholasticism combined polemic with practical coexistence, and humanists held chairs in rhetoric and poetry within existing universities.

Humanism had public applications. Salutati and Bruni used classical rhetoric and historical writing in the service of Florence, producing a language of liberty that historians have debated since Baron's civic humanism thesis. Humanists also served princes at Milan, Ferrara, Mantua, Urbino, and Naples and popes at Rome, so that the movement flourished in republics, principalities, and the church alike. Dependence on patronage was constant, and invective and rivalry were common.

The movement's limits were substantial. Women such as Isotta Nogarola, Cassandra Fedele, and Laura Cereta displayed learning in Latin but faced suspicion and lacked institutional access. Most people had no part in humanist culture. Humanists also contributed to moral and religious thought, from Valla's De voluptate and Manetti's treatise on human dignity to Ficino's Platonism and Pico's Conclusions, but these works resist reduction to a single doctrine.

The historical debate over whether humanism represented a rupture or a transformation within medieval culture remains open. The most defensible position recognizes both significant change in method, taste, and institutions and strong continuity in language, religion, and social setting. The chapter's larger lesson is that the "recovery" of antiquity was a long, cumulative, and collaborative process shaped by Byzantine, Italian, and other contributors, and that it prepared the ground for the artistic developments of Chapter 58, the northern diffusion of Chapter 59, and the political and print-cultural consequences of Chapter 60.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 57 matters because it shows how a cultural movement can alter the habits of a society without overturning its institutions. Humanists did not abolish universities, the church, or the courts. They changed what many educated people read, how they judged texts, how they wrote, and how they imagined the relationship between past and present. Those changes were slow, collaborative, and uneven, and they depended on schools, libraries, patrons, and the labor of copyists and translators as much as on famous scholars.

The chapter also reveals how connected the Mediterranean and European worlds were. Byzantine teachers, Greek manuscripts, Arabic and Hebrew learning, northern monastic libraries, and Italian merchants and princes all contributed. The "recovery of antiquity" was therefore not an Italian invention alone but a process of translation and exchange across languages, religions, and political boundaries, and it was shaped as much by the pressures on Byzantium examined in Chapter 41 as by Italian prosperity.

Humanism's legacies were wide and ambiguous. Its philological methods enabled later critical study of scripture, law, and history. Its educational ideals shaped schools for centuries. Its celebration of eloquence and civic virtue supplied vocabulary for political argument. Yet it also reinforced social hierarchies by tying prestige to Latin learning and by excluding most women and most commoners. Students should keep both aspects in view.

Within Volume III, this chapter supplies the intellectual foundation for what follows. Chapter 58 turns to the artists, architects, and patrons who shared the same cultural world. Chapter 59 follows humanist methods north of the Alps and into religious reform, and Chapter 60 examines their political, diplomatic, and print-cultural consequences. Together these chapters prepare the Volume's later questions about religious division, state formation, and the changing European mind, none of which was an inevitable result of the recovery of classical learning.
