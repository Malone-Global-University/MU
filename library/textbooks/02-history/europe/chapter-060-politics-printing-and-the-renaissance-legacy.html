Politics, Printing, and the Renaissance Legacy

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In December 1513 a dismissed Florentine official wrote to a friend in Rome that he spent his evenings in conversation with the ancient authors, and that he had composed a short work on principalities. The letter, the treatise, the diplomatic dispatches that trained its author, and the presses that would eventually carry his ideas across Europe together show how the Renaissance changed the ways Europeans thought about power and how ideas about power travelled.

Niccolò Machiavelli had been a servant of the Florentine Republic for fourteen years. In the autumn of 1512 the republic collapsed and the Medici returned. By early 1513 he had been dismissed, implicated in a conspiracy against the new regime, and tortured, before an amnesty released him. He withdrew to a small estate at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, south of Florence, and in a surviving letter to Francesco Vettori, the Florentine ambassador in Rome, dated 10 December 1513, he described a daily routine of ordinary rural labor, tavern company, and evening reading. He also reported that he had written a small treatise on how principalities are acquired and held. That treatise is now known as The Prince. It was not printed until 1532, five years after its author died. This chapter begins with a man at his desk because the history of Renaissance politics is inseparable from the experience of people who had been inside governments, had watched them fail, and wrote about what they had seen.

The chapter has a broader task than one famous book. It asks how Renaissance political experience and the expansion of printed communication changed European ideas about power, government, learning, and public life, and what limited those changes. Its principal period is roughly 1490 to 1550, with necessary foundations in the mid fifteenth century. It brings together three clusters of historical development. The first is political: the shock of foreign invasion in Italy after 1494, the instability of republican Florence, and the writings of Machiavelli and Francesco Guicciardini. The second concerns the cultures of political service, including resident diplomacy, the court and the ideal of the courtier described by Baldassare Castiglione, and the real but constrained political roles of elite women. The third is the history of the printed book as a commercial, educational, and political system.

These clusters are connected. Ambassadors, secretaries, and courtiers were frequently humanist-trained, and they wrote letters, treatises, and histories whose circulation depended on scribes first and printers later. Printers needed patrons, learned editors, and political protection, and rulers and churches learned to use presses for their own purposes while also trying to restrict them. Readers encountered Livy, Cicero, Tacitus, and Plutarch in printed editions that made classical political examples more widely available than they had been, even as access to books remained sharply unequal. The Renaissance legacy examined here is therefore not a list of achievements. It is a set of changing practices of analysis, representation, negotiation, and communication, and those practices spread across Europe unevenly.

The chapter also resists three simplifications. Machiavelli was not merely an advocate of tyranny: he wrote both The Prince, a work about acquiring and holding power, and the Discourses on Livy, a work that praised republican liberty and civic conflict, and historians still debate how the two fit together. Printing did not instantly create a literate Europe or an independent public opinion: manuscripts remained important, most people read little or nothing, and governments and churches supervised and used the press. The Renaissance did not make Europe modern in a single stroke: religious institutions, aristocratic privilege, dynastic politics, and inherited legal traditions remained powerful, and some of the period's own innovations reinforced hierarchy as well as challenging it.

Historians reconstruct this subject from diverse evidence. Diplomatic dispatches, chancery registers, and private letters reveal how political information was gathered and interpreted. Treatises, histories, plays, and dialogues show how contemporaries argued about power and conduct. Printed books themselves are evidence: title pages, colophons, dedications, prefaces, marginal annotations, and surviving print runs reveal markets and readerships, while privilege grants, licensing decrees, and ecclesiastical legislation reveal authorities' efforts to regulate them. Each kind of source has limits. Ambassadors wrote for their employers, treatises were often directed at a patron, and the survival of books favors those that libraries chose to keep. Throughout, the chapter asks who created a source, for whom, and who is missing from it.

Chapter 60 closes Part IX, Renaissance Europe. It builds on the Italian city-states of Chapter 56, the humanist education and philology of Chapter 57, the artistic patronage of Chapter 58, and the northern adaptations of Chapter 59, and it does not repeat them. It also stands at a major Part boundary within Volume III, preparing the transition from Renaissance Europe to the Reformation and its religious conflicts. The technological introduction to printing belongs to Chapter 55, and the religious controversies that printing helped to amplify belong to Part X, beginning with Chapter 61. The final section of this chapter explains how the Renaissance habits of political analysis, scholarly criticism, and printed communication helped create the conditions for religious division without making that division inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the French invasion of Italy in 1494 and the later Italian Wars exposed weaknesses in Italian political systems and shaped contemporary political writing.
  2. Trace the political circumstances of Florence between 1494 and 1512 and the career of Niccolò Machiavelli within them.
  3. Analyze the main arguments of The Prince and the Discourses on Livy, including virtù, fortuna, necessity, and republican liberty, and explain why historians debate their relationship.
  4. Compare Machiavelli, Guicciardini, and Castiglione as responses to Renaissance political and courtly life.
  5. Describe the development of resident diplomacy and explain how ambassadors' dispatches produced political knowledge.
  6. Assess the political functions of court culture and the extent and limits of elite women's political influence.
  7. Explain how printing operated as an economic and organizational system rather than a single invention, distinguishing printers, publishers, booksellers, and patrons.
  8. Evaluate evidence for literacy, readership, and access to books, and distinguish reading ability from writing ability and Latin from vernacular readerships.
  9. Distinguish early print controversy and regulation from modern mass media, copyright, and public opinion.
  10. Interpret competing historical arguments about whether the Renaissance inaugurated modern European political thought, and identify continuities with medieval traditions.
  11. Contextualize the uneven European reception of Renaissance political, courtly, and printing practices, including in regions beyond Italy and among Jewish, Orthodox, and Muslim communities.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why can The Prince not be understood fully without the Discourses on Livy, and what interpretive problems remain even when both are read together?
  3. How did diplomats, secretaries, courtiers, and rulers create and communicate political knowledge, and whose perspectives did their records exclude?
  4. In what ways was court culture a political institution rather than merely a social refinement?
  5. Why did printing transform some forms of communication quickly and others slowly, and why did manuscripts continue to matter?
  6. What does evidence for schools, signatures, book ownership, and print runs allow historians to say about literacy, and what does it not allow them to say?
  7. Why did governments and churches both promote and restrict printing, and how did privileges differ from modern copyright?
  8. How far was the Renaissance a break with medieval Europe, and how should historians describe its uneven influence across regions and social groups?

Historical Context

By the 1490s the Italian peninsula was divided among several kinds of political community. The Republic of Venice ruled a maritime empire and a mainland territory. The Duchy of Milan was ruled by the Sforza family, the Kingdom of Naples by a branch of the Aragonese dynasty, and the Papal States by the bishop of Rome as a territorial monarch elected by cardinals. Florence was a republic in form, though from 1434 the Medici family had dominated it through patronage, marriage alliances, and control of key offices. Smaller courts such as Ferrara, Mantua, and Urbino were ruled by families who often served as military captains for larger powers. After the Peace of Lodi of 1454 and the Italic League of 1455, a rough balance among the largest states had reduced, though not eliminated, large-scale warfare. Chapter 56 examined these states; here they matter as the setting in which political practice and political writing developed.

North of the Alps, the monarchies of France, England, and the Iberian kingdoms had been strengthening their fiscal and military systems, while the Holy Roman Empire remained a complex federation of princes, cities, and ecclesiastical territories under a Habsburg emperor, Maximilian I (r. 1493 to 1519). The Ottoman Empire, which had taken Constantinople in 1453 (Chapter 41), was a major presence in Balkan, Adriatic, and Mediterranean politics, and its relations with Venice, Hungary, and other powers were central to diplomacy. Italian wealth made the peninsula a prize. The commercial and banking cities of Italy offered revenues, strategic ports, and dynastic claims that foreign monarchs could invoke.

Intellectually, the humanist movement examined in Chapter 57 had trained a generation of secretaries, tutors, and diplomats in classical Latin, rhetoric, and history. Humanist educators argued that the study of ancient authors such as Cicero, Livy, Sallust, and Tacitus prepared men for public life. Late medieval and Renaissance writers also produced "mirrors for princes," advice literature on rulers' virtues, and Italian humanists such as Giovanni Pontano and Bartolomeo Platina wrote treatises on rulers in the later fifteenth century. Roman and canon law, Aristotle's Politics, and scholastic arguments about justice and law remained available frameworks. Renaissance political writers did not invent political thought from nothing. They worked within, against, and beyond these traditions.

Communication around 1490 depended on handwriting as well as print. Letters, chancery records, ambassadorial dispatches, sermons, and many scholarly and literary texts moved by hand and by courier. Movable-type printing, developed in Mainz in the mid fifteenth century and introduced to Italy in the 1460s, was already established when this chapter begins; Chapter 55 covered its invention and early spread. This chapter asks what printing meant once it had become an organized trade, who bought and read its products, how it changed the transmission of classical and political texts, and how authorities responded. By 1500, presses operated in hundreds of European towns, but the capital, the markets, and the readers were concentrated in particular cities, above all Venice, and the largest share of early printing was in Latin.

The chapter's evidence differs from that of political narratives. Historians use the archives of chanceries and embassies, notably Florentine, Venetian, and Milanese records, the correspondence of princes and secretaries, and the books themselves. Studies of printing rely on surviving copies, publishers' prefaces, privilege registers, inventories, and legal disputes. Because estimates of print runs, readers, and literacy are uncertain, the chapter distinguishes documented facts from scholarly inference throughout.

Section 60.1

The Renaissance and the Problem of Political Power

The central problem of this chapter is how people who exercised, served, observed, or sought to influence power in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries made sense of what they saw, and how they communicated it. Renaissance political writing emerged from circumstances in which governments rose and fell quickly, armies crossed the Alps, republics rewrote their constitutions, and dynasties sought legitimacy through learning, ceremony, and display. Writers rarely began with abstract questions about the ideal state. More often they began with practical problems: how to hold a city, how to command loyalty, how to negotiate with a stronger neighbor, how to advise a ruler, or how to explain a defeat.

Students should be cautious about the phrase "the birth of the modern state." Scholars use the Italian word stato to follow a change in vocabulary in this period, from words denoting a ruler's standing or condition toward a word for a political community's governing order, and the change is real. But the polities of 1500 were not modern states. Rulers depended on personal relationships, privileged corporations, hereditary offices, and religious institutions, and the sovereignty claimed by monarchs, republics, and the pope overlapped in complex ways. The Holy Roman Empire, the Venetian Republic, the kingdom of France, and the Ottoman Empire were different kinds of political order, and no single model describes them. Chapter 74 will examine the early modern state more broadly.

The chapter's guiding claim is that the Renaissance transformed political practice and communication in several overlapping ways while leaving other structures intact. Experience of crisis encouraged new analytical writing. Diplomacy became more continuous and more documented. Courts developed codes of conduct that were also strategies for political survival. Printing multiplied copies, widened some audiences, and gave rulers, churches, scholars, and polemicists new tools, while also creating new risks and new efforts at control. These changes were substantial but uneven, and they reached some regions, ranks, and genders far more fully than others.

Section 60.2

The Italian Wars and the Crisis of Renaissance Politics

In 1494 Charles VIII of France crossed the Alps with a large army to assert an Angevin claim to the Kingdom of Naples. Ludovico Sforza, who held power in Milan, had encouraged the expedition in the hope of using French strength against his rivals. The invasion exposed how vulnerable Italian states were to a large, well-supplied army with mobile siege artillery, and city after city negotiated or surrendered. The French took Naples in February 1495. An alliance including Venice, Milan, the pope, Ferdinand of Aragon, and Emperor Maximilian then formed against Charles, and the king returned north, fighting an indecisive battle at Fornovo in July 1495. The French withdrawal did not end foreign intervention. Louis XII conquered Milan in 1499. Ferdinand of Aragon and his Spanish forces had secured Naples by 1504. In 1508 the League of Cambrai united the pope, the emperor, France, and Spain against Venice, and the Venetian defeat at Agnadello in May 1509 shook the confidence of the most stable Italian republic.

Later years brought further reversals. The French victory at Marignano in 1515 under Francis I was followed by the imperial victory at Pavia in 1525, where Francis himself was captured. The Sack of Rome by the unpaid troops of Emperor Charles V in May 1527 shocked contemporaries because it struck the papal city and the pope's own court. These events belong to the wider contest between Habsburg and Valois monarchies examined in Chapter 75, and the military narrative is not repeated here. What matters for this chapter is how the wars changed political experience. Italian states discovered that wealth did not guarantee security, that commanders hired on contract could prove unreliable, that alliances shifted rapidly, and that taxation to pay for war strained governments and subjects alike.

These experiences shaped political writing. Contemporary observers described the invasions as the beginning of a period of disaster for Italy, and Guicciardini opened his later History of Italy with the 1490s as the threshold of that calamity. Machiavelli drew conclusions about military organization, mercenary armies, and the dependence of rulers on their own subjects. The wars also changed diplomacy, because states needed accurate information about distant courts and armies, and they changed the fortunes of cities and families, because every defeat could bring a new regime. Political thought in this period is therefore inseparable from the sequence of crises that framed it.

Section 60.3

Florence, Republican Government, and Political Instability

In November 1494 Piero de' Medici, who headed the family that had dominated Florence for sixty years, gave Charles VIII control of several Florentine fortresses and ports. The concession angered the city, and the Medici were expelled. Florence then reorganized its government. A Great Council (Consiglio Maggiore), modelled in part on the Venetian system and open to roughly three thousand eligible citizens, became the central body, and a gonfaloniere and Signoria carried out executive functions. The new system widened participation among Florence's established citizen class while leaving out large numbers of artisans, laborers, women, and the inhabitants of Florence's subject territories. Florence's wider dominion also faced rebellion: Pisa broke away from Florentine rule in 1494, and the effort to recover it occupied the republic until 1509.

The Dominican preacher Girolamo Savonarola became the dominant figure of the republic's first years. From the pulpit of the cathedral and from the convent of San Marco he preached repentance, prophesied divine judgment, and supported the Great Council as a regime pleasing to God. His followers campaigned against luxury, gambling, and what they judged to be immorality, and in the carnival seasons of 1497 and 1498 they organized public burnings of objects condemned as vanities. Savonarola's influence depended on popular preaching, the printing of his sermons and tracts, factional alliances, and the weakness of other leaders. He also faced enemies, including Medici sympathizers, rival factions that opposed his political influence, and Pope Alexander VI, who excommunicated him in 1497. When political support collapsed in the spring of 1498, he was tried, condemned, and executed on 23 May. Machiavelli later wrote in The Prince that armed prophets conquered and unarmed ones failed, a judgment widely understood to refer to Savonarola.

Instability continued. In 1502 the republic created a gonfaloniere for life, Piero Soderini, in an attempt to provide continuity. Soderini sought to strengthen the republic with a citizen militia and diplomacy, but the strategy depended on the larger European political situation. When the Holy League of 1511 aligned the pope and Spain against France, Florence's alliance with France proved costly. In August 1512 Spanish troops sacked Prato, Soderini fled, and in September the Medici returned, supported by Spain and the papacy. In 1513 a Medici, Giovanni, was elected pope as Leo X. Florence's republican period thus lasted eighteen years and reflected the interaction of internal faction and external force. It is the setting in which Machiavelli worked, and it also reminds students that republics in this period were fragile, restricted to select citizens, and vulnerable to the power of larger states.

Section 60.4

Machiavelli: Secretary, Diplomat, and Political Observer

Niccolò Machiavelli was born in Florence on 3 May 1469 into a family of modest means and some distinction, and his father Bernardo was a lawyer with a library. He received a humanist education adequate to a career in public administration. In June 1498, weeks after Savonarola's execution, he was chosen as head of the Second Chancery, and shortly afterward he was also made secretary to the Ten of War, the magistracy handling diplomacy and military affairs. He was not a nobleman, a banker, or a member of the leading factions; he was a professional secretary, serving a city governed by committees and temporary officeholders who relied on him for correspondence, record-keeping, and missions.

Those missions brought him into contact with powerful figures. In 1499 he visited Caterina Sforza, the countess of Forlì, who would soon lose her lordship to Cesare Borgia. He traveled to the French court in 1500, 1504, and 1510 to 1511, and the experience led him to write about French government and military organization. In 1502 and 1503 he accompanied Cesare Borgia in the Romagna, observed his methods, and was present as Borgia's fortunes collapsed following the death of his father Pope Alexander VI. He was in Rome during the conclave of 1503, observed Julius II in 1506, and visited the court of Emperor Maximilian in 1507 and 1508. His dispatches, preserved in Florentine archives, show a reporter attentive to power, geography, and personality. They also show a representative with limited authority, for Florentine instructions were often cautious, and he could not negotiate beyond them.

Machiavelli's interest in military organization led him to design a citizen militia for Florence, approved in 1506, as an alternative to dependence on mercenary companies. The militia contributed to the recovery of Pisa in 1509, but it failed in 1512 when Spanish troops overwhelmed it at Prato. That failure, and the fall of the regime he had served, ended his public service. He was dismissed in November 1512 and in February 1513 was arrested on suspicion of involvement in an anti-Medici plot. He was tortured with the strappado, and he was released in March, apparently under an amnesty after Leo X's election. Without a post, he retired to his farm and began to write.

In the decade that followed he composed The Prince, the Discourses, the Art of War (printed in 1521, the only major political or military work to appear in print in his lifetime), the comedy Mandragola, and the Florentine Histories, which he began at the request of Cardinal Giulio de' Medici, the future Clement VII, in 1520. In 1526 he worked on Florence's fortifications, and in 1527, after the Sack of Rome and the expulsion of the Medici, he hoped for a return to office. The restored republic did not employ him, and he died on 21 June 1527. Students should therefore keep in mind that Machiavelli wrote as a former official, usually in exile from power, seeking either employment or understanding.

Primary Source Window

Machiavelli to Francesco Vettori, 10 December 1513

Creator: Niccolò Machiavelli, former Florentine secretary · Recipient: Francesco Vettori, Florentine ambassador at Rome · Date: 10 December 1513 · Place: Sant'Andrea in Percussina · Type: Private letter in Italian, part of an ongoing correspondence between friends

Context: Machiavelli wrote nine months after his torture and release, while Vettori served the Medici regime in Rome. Machiavelli wanted to remain involved in political affairs, to obtain employment from the Medici, and to maintain a relationship with an influential friend. The letter survives and is widely quoted, but readers should recall that it is a private communication shaped by friendship, irony, and the writer's need for work.

Paraphrase of the letter (not a translation) Machiavelli describes rising with the sun, supervising woodcutters, and reading poets such as Dante, Petrarch, Tibullus, and Ovid before passing time at an inn with local men, where card games and quarrels over trifles leave his mind, he says, clogged with mold. In the evening he returns home, changes out of his dirty clothes, and enters what he calls the courts of ancient men, where he is welcomed and where he asks them about the reasons for their actions. For several hours he feels no boredom and forgets his troubles. He reports that he has noted what he learned from them and composed a little work on principalities, which discusses what a principality is, what kinds exist, how they are acquired, held, and lost. He also says he is considering offering the work to a Medici in the hope of being given employment, even if he is first put to some modest task.

Analysis: The letter is the earliest firm evidence for the composition of The Prince and shows its author combining humanist reading with practical experience. It also shows the purpose of the book as a demonstration of competence to a patron, a point that complicates readings of it as pure theory. The tone combines self-mockery with serious ambition. The description of the courts of ancient men is a literary conceit, not a literal report, but it illustrates the humanist habit of treating ancient authors as interlocutors. Historians can infer that the work was composed in 1513, though they cannot determine from the letter how much was later revised. The letter does not show that the book reflected Machiavelli's whole political outlook, that the Medici read it carefully, or that he expected it to become famous. It also tells us little about the thousands of Florentines who lived through the same political crisis without the leisure to write about it.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about Machiavelli's daily life and intellectual habits after 1512?
  2. Who created the letter, to whom, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the composition and intended use of The Prince?
  4. What can historians not establish from this letter alone, and what other evidence would they need?
Section 60.5

The Prince: Power, Necessity, and Political Survival

The Prince is a short treatise of twenty-six chapters, written mainly in 1513 with possible later revisions. It circulated in manuscript during Machiavelli's lifetime, was dedicated in its final form to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici (the nephew of Leo X, who had become duke of Urbino in 1516), and was printed in Rome and Florence in 1532. It belongs to the tradition of advice to rulers but departs from it. Earlier mirrors for princes, including those written by Italian humanists, usually stressed the ruler's moral virtues such as justice, generosity, and clemency, drawing on authors like Cicero and Seneca. Machiavelli declared that he wished to follow the "effectual truth" of political life, rather than imagined republics and principalities that no one had seen, and that a ruler who insisted on perfect goodness among people who were not good would be ruined.

The book begins with a classification of principalities, distinguishing hereditary rulers, who can often rely on custom, from new rulers, who must establish their authority. It discusses states conquered with one's own arms and ability, as with the founders Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, and Theseus, and states obtained by fortune or the arms of others, with Cesare Borgia as the principal example. It discusses a ruler who rises through crime, as Agathocles of Syracuse did; ecclesiastical principalities; civil principalities, in which a citizen becomes ruler with the favor of the people or the great; and the problems of fortresses, advisors, flatterers, and alliances. Several chapters concern military organization, where Machiavelli argues that rulers who rely on mercenary or auxiliary forces place themselves at the mercy of others, and that a state needs its own armed subjects.

Some of the most discussed passages concern conduct. Machiavelli argues that rulers must sometimes use cruelty or break promises, that they should avoid being hated, and that a prudent ruler should be both fox and lion, combining cunning with force. He observes that it is safer for a ruler to be feared than loved if he cannot be both, but he adds that fear must not become hatred, and that the most reliable defense against conspiracy and rebellion is the goodwill of the people. He notes that the people desire not to be oppressed while the great desire to dominate, and he treats this difference as a central fact of politics. The book repeatedly insists that political judgment must respond to necessity, the pressure of circumstances that may require actions an ordinary private person would condemn.

The ending is often overlooked. In the final chapter, Machiavelli urges the Medici to unite and liberate Italy from the "barbarians," meaning foreign armies. Earlier, in chapters on why the princes of Italy lost their states, he blames reliance on mercenaries and a failure to prepare for adversity. He argues that fortune governs perhaps half of human affairs, but that a prudent person can prepare for her changes, much as people build embankments against floods. The book is therefore a work of advice, a reflection on the Italian crisis, and a bid for patronage at once. It does not recommend cruelty as an end, and Machiavelli did not use the formula that the ends justify the means, though later critics and admirers often summarized him that way. Students should treat that slogan as part of the later memory of Machiavelli rather than as a reliable summary of the text.

Historical Vocabulary
Virtù, Fortuna, and Necessity

Virtù is Machiavelli's term for the qualities that enable a political actor to act effectively: energy, skill, courage, foresight, and the ability to adapt. It is related to the Latin virtus and to the ordinary Italian word for virtue, but it is not equivalent to Christian moral virtue and should not be translated simply as "virtue." Fortuna means fortune or chance, a force imagined in classical and Christian traditions as a goddess, a wheel, or a river, representing events beyond a person's control. Necessity (necessità) denotes the pressure of circumstances that compels action, sometimes forcing choices between bad options. The three terms matter because Machiavelli's argument concerns how skilled actors respond to forces they cannot control. Modern equivalents can mislead: "virtue" suggests moral goodness, "luck" suggests randomness, and "necessity" suggests justification for any action, whereas Machiavelli used the terms analytically and as part of a particular humanist vocabulary.

Section 60.6

The Discourses on Livy: Republican Liberty and Civic Conflict

The Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy was composed over several years, approximately 1514 to 1519, and was dedicated to two friends, Zanobi Buondelmonti and Cosimo Rucellai, who belonged to a circle that discussed politics and literature in the Rucellai gardens in Florence. It was first printed in Rome in 1531 by Antonio Blado, with a privilege granted by Pope Clement VII. It is much longer than The Prince, with 142 chapters organized in three books: the first on internal political life, the second on expansion and warfare, and the third on individual leaders, conspiracies, and the changes that threaten and restore states. Its method is to comment on the Roman historian Livy's account of early Rome, comparing it with contemporary events.

Machiavelli praises the Roman Republic for its capacity to channel conflict. He argues that the struggles between the Roman patricians and the plebeians, which many writers had condemned as disorder, produced laws favorable to liberty, since the people's desire not to be dominated checked the ambitions of the great, and institutions such as the tribunes gave that conflict a legal form. He treats a mixed constitution, combining monarchical, aristocratic, and popular elements, as more durable than a single form. He also argues that a free people is, in some respects, wiser and more constant than a prince, that citizen armies strengthen republics, and that religion can serve public order when used to support laws and oaths. The work also criticizes the Church, claiming that the papacy had helped keep Italy divided, and suggests that a religion that glorifies humility can weaken civic courage. The latter argument was one reason the work later drew ecclesiastical condemnation.

The Discourses complicates any view of Machiavelli as a theorist of tyranny. It values law, participation, and liberty, and it treats corruption as a condition in which citizens place private interest above the common good. Yet it also contains passages about the need for exceptional founders, the use of force in establishing or reforming a state, and cases of ruthless decisiveness. Readers have therefore debated the relationship between the two works. Some argue that The Prince and the Discourses address different circumstances, one a new ruler in a corrupted Italy, the other the conditions for free government. Others stress shared concepts such as virtù, fortune, the importance of arms, and the need to understand actual human behavior. A third view treats The Prince as, in part, an indirect lesson about how rulers behave, written for citizens as much as for princes. The debate is considered in Section 60.18 and is not settled.

Section 60.7

Guicciardini, Historical Judgment, and the Limits of Political Rules

Francesco Guicciardini, born in Florence in 1483, came from a wealthy family closely connected to the city's governing elite and received legal training at Florence, Ferrara, and Padua. He married into the Salviati family, which was allied with the Medici, and served the Florentine Republic as ambassador to Ferdinand of Aragon between 1512 and 1514. After the Medici's return, he entered papal service under Leo X and Clement VII, governing Modena, Reggio, and Romagna, and in 1526 he served as lieutenant general of the papal forces in the war of the League of Cognac. His career therefore combined Florentine politics with direct experience of princely government and military command, and the failures of 1527 touched him personally. After 1530 he participated in the Medici regime's consolidation and later retired to write.

Guicciardini's most distinctive writings are the Ricordi, a collection of maxims revised over many years, and the History of Italy, composed in the last years of his life, and first printed in 1561. He also wrote reflections on Machiavelli's Discourses, probably around 1528. His work stresses the difficulty of predicting events, the importance of particular circumstances, and the role of judgment, which he often described as discretion. He doubted that general rules drawn from the Roman past could apply directly to the present, because circumstances differ in ways that cannot be reduced to formulas, and he regarded Machiavelli as too inclined to absolute conclusions. He accepted that self-interest dominates politics and that cunning is sometimes necessary, but he thought that success depended on a combination of ability, experience, and fortune that no treatise could capture.

The contrast with Machiavelli should not be exaggerated. They were friends and collaborators, and Machiavelli worked with Guicciardini in the Romagna in 1525 and 1526. Both were Florentine political observers and officials who drew on humanist learning and practical experience, although Machiavelli served as a chancery secretary while Guicciardini pursued a career as a lawyer, ambassador, papal governor, and senior political administrator. Both responded to the Italian crisis, and both stressed the primacy of power politics over moral idealism. The difference lies in method and temperament. Machiavelli looked for patterns visible across Roman and modern examples and sought to teach through them. Guicciardini was more skeptical about generalization and emphasized the particular case. Taken together they show that Renaissance political thinking was not a single doctrine, and that a pessimistic, empirical, and experience-based mode of analysis could take several forms.

Section 60.8

Diplomacy, Resident Ambassadors, and the European State System

Rulers had long sent envoys on temporary missions, but in the fifteenth century Italian states increasingly maintained ambassadors who stayed at a foreign court for extended periods. Historians have often placed the beginnings of this practice in the Italian peninsula, especially in the mid fifteenth century when Milan, Venice, Florence, Naples, and the papacy needed continuous information in a system of shifting alliances. The practice then spread. Spain under Ferdinand and Isabella, and later England, France, and the Habsburgs, established resident embassies at major courts, and by the early sixteenth century resident ambassadors were becoming a recognized feature of European diplomacy. The change was gradual. Many missions remained temporary, and the papal court, which housed ambassadors from many powers, remained a major diplomatic center.

An ambassador's tasks included representation, negotiation, and intelligence. He attended ceremonies, delivered messages, bargained over marriage alliances, trade privileges, military aid, and territorial claims, and wrote reports. Dispatches in cipher conveyed gossip, troop movements, finances, and the personalities of rulers, and they were typically read, copied, and filed by government secretaries. Venetian ambassadors, at the end of a mission, delivered a formal relazione to the Senate summarizing the political, military, and economic condition of the court where they had served, and these reports became a distinctive model of political description. Marino Sanudo's voluminous diaries of Venetian affairs show how much of diplomatic information circulated within the city's governing class. Postal systems, such as the one organized for the Habsburgs by the Taxis family from the 1490s, improved the speed and reliability of correspondence, though winter, war, and interception could delay or compromise messages.

Diplomacy was also a space of ceremony and status. Ambassadors disputed precedence at religious ceremonies and public occasions because rank signified political standing, and masters of ceremonies such as Johann Burchard at the papal court recorded elaborate protocols. Ambassadors were often scholars. Humanist-trained writers like Ermolao Barbaro of Venice, who wrote on the duties of an envoy around 1490, argued that diplomacy required learning, discretion, and skill in persuasion. Thomas More's mission to Flanders in 1515 formed the setting in which he began Utopia. Machiavelli and Guicciardini wrote their own reports and drew political conclusions from them. Diplomacy was thus a source of political knowledge as well as a form of negotiation.

The system was never confined to Latin Christendom. Venice maintained a resident bailo at Constantinople, and Ragusa (Dubrovnik) sent envoys to the sultan as part of its tributary relationship with the Ottomans. Hungary, Poland, Muscovy, and other states exchanged embassies with Ottoman and European courts. The Ottoman court received foreign envoys but did not establish permanent embassies in European capitals until the late eighteenth century, relying on temporary missions, and this difference reflects a different diplomatic practice, not an absence of diplomacy. Treating resident ambassadors as the beginning of the European state system risks overstating the Italian origins and understating the plurality of diplomatic forms.

Historical Vocabulary
Resident Ambassador and Diplomatic Dispatch

A resident ambassador was an envoy who remained at a foreign court for an extended period, as distinct from a temporary envoy sent for a particular negotiation. A dispatch (dispaccio) was a report sent by an ambassador to his government, often partly in cipher. Together they formed a continuous channel of political information. The terms matter because they show how states increased their capacity to monitor rivals. They should not be equated with modern ambassadors: ambassadors in this period were personal representatives of a ruler or republic, enjoyed uncertain protections, and often served without a professional career structure or a settled diplomatic law.

Section 60.9

Court Culture, Castiglione, and the Ideal of the Courtier

Courts were the households, administrations, and ceremonial centers of rulers, and they were places where careers were made through access, patronage, and favor. A court included nobles, officials, secretaries, tutors, physicians, soldiers, musicians, artists, servants, and visitors. Rulers used ceremonies, entertainments, buildings, and commissions to communicate status, and courtiers competed for position. Italian courts such as Urbino, Mantua, Ferrara, and Milan, the papal court in Rome, the court of Burgundy and its Habsburg successors, and the courts of France, England, and Spain all developed their own forms. Court culture was therefore a political institution, because favor determined who could advise a ruler, receive offices, and influence decisions. The sociologist Norbert Elias famously linked court society to changing patterns of self-restraint; historians continue to debate his claims, but his work stimulated study of etiquette as a means of power.

Baldassare Castiglione, born near Mantua in 1478, served the Gonzaga of Mantua and then the dukes of Urbino from 1504, represented them as an envoy, and later entered papal service, becoming papal nuncio to Charles V in Spain from 1524. He died in Toledo in 1529. His Book of the Courtier, drafted from about 1508 and revised over many years, was printed in Venice in 1528. It is set in 1507 at the court of Urbino, where Duchess Elisabetta Gonzaga presides over four evenings of conversation among real figures, including Pietro Bembo, Giuliano de' Medici, Emilia Pia, and others. The characters debate the qualities of the ideal courtier, the role of the court lady, and the courtier's relationship to the prince. Castiglione explained in a dedicatory letter that he printed the work because unauthorized manuscript copies had begun to circulate, an example of the interaction between manuscript and print discussed in Section 60.11.

The ideal courtier is a nobleman skilled in arms, horsemanship, languages, music, drawing, letters, and conversation, and above all marked by sprezzatura, a studied nonchalance that conceals effort and makes accomplishments appear natural. The discussion also addresses language, with the participants arguing over how Italian should be written, and Castiglione advocated a courtly language not narrowly bound to Florentine models. The third book discusses the court lady, defended by Giuliano de' Medici against more hostile interlocutors, and the fourth book argues that the courtier's highest purpose is to guide his prince toward virtue by giving honest counsel. Bembo's closing speech on love draws on Neoplatonic ideas, treating earthly beauty as a path toward the divine.

The book is not simply a manual of manners. It addresses the problem of how an educated nobleman could preserve integrity while serving a ruler, and it presents courtly skill as an instrument of counsel. Castiglione's world was also one of dependence, and some readers have understood sprezzatura as a technique for concealing effort and managing impressions in a competitive environment. The book was highly successful, and translations appeared in Spanish in 1534 and in English in 1561, among other languages. Its influence beyond Italy is considered in Section 60.17. Compared with Machiavelli, Castiglione was less concerned with the techniques of acquiring power and more with the formation of character and the culture of service, but both were concerned with how people should behave in political environments where success depended on perception and judgment.

Section 60.10

Women, Courtly Power, and the Social Limits of Renaissance Politics

Renaissance political culture was male-dominated in its offices and institutions. Women could not sit in the Florentine Great Council, serve as Venetian magistrates, hold university chairs, or command most armies, and rules of inheritance such as the French Salic law barred them from the royal succession. Even so, elite women exercised influence through dynastic marriage, regency, patronage, correspondence, and household management, and some governed in their own right or on behalf of absent male relatives. Their authority depended on rank, family connections, and circumstance, and the exceptional cases should not be read as evidence of general access to political power.

Isabella d'Este (1474 to 1539), daughter of the duke of Ferrara and wife of Francesco Gonzaga, marquis of Mantua, is among the best documented. She governed Mantua during her husband's absences, notably in 1509 and 1510 when he was held prisoner by Venice during the wars that followed the League of Cambrai. She maintained an extensive correspondence with rulers and ambassadors, negotiated on behalf of her family, and used patronage of art, music, and books to enhance Mantua's prestige. She also purchased Aldine editions, and her correspondence with artists shows a patron actively shaping her collections. Caterina Sforza, countess of Forlì, defended her fortress of Ravaldino against Cesare Borgia in 1499 and 1500 before being captured, and Machiavelli had earlier visited her court. Margaret of Austria governed the Habsburg Netherlands from 1507 to 1530, and Louise of Savoy acted as regent of France when her son Francis I campaigned in Italy and while he was a captive after Pavia. In 1529 these two women negotiated the Peace of Cambrai, often called the Ladies' Peace, between France and the emperor. In Castile, Isabella ruled as queen in her own right from 1474 to 1504, and Anne of Brittany held a duchy in her own name while serving as queen of France.

Women also participated in learned and literary culture. Vittoria Colonna's poems were printed from 1538 and read widely, and Veronica Gambara and others maintained literary circles. In Castiglione's dialogue, the debate about the court lady shows that educated men contested women's capacities, and the discussion belongs to a long literary quarrel about women. The ideal of the court lady described there was still shaped by male expectations, emphasizing grace, modesty, and conversational skill. Women of other ranks were largely excluded from these spaces, though they participated in commerce, household production, and, as examined in Section 60.12, in the book trade. A history of Renaissance politics that notes only exceptional princesses can therefore mislead, but a history that omits them also misses a real and documented political resource.

Section 60.11

From Manuscript to Print: Technologies and Continuities

Chapter 55 described the development of movable-type printing in Mainz in the mid fifteenth century, and this section recalls only what is necessary to explain its later consequences. The printer cut a steel punch for each letter, struck it into a copper matrix, and cast many identical pieces of type in a mold from a lead-based alloy. Compositors assembled type letter by letter into pages, which were locked into a frame, inked, and pressed onto paper in a hand-operated press. The same type could then be reused. Paper, which had become cheaper than parchment, made large print runs economically practical, though paper remained a major cost. Printers sometimes issued deluxe copies on vellum for wealthy buyers.

The new technology did not abruptly replace the old. Early printed books imitated manuscripts in layout and script, and Italian printers adopted forms of letters from humanist handwriting that developed into roman and italic types. Manuscripts continued to be produced for many purposes: presentation copies for patrons, private correspondence, internal government records, confidential diplomatic dispatches, scribal copies of texts the owner could not buy, and prestige volumes. Vespasiano da Bisticci's memoir of Federico da Montefeltro, the duke of Urbino who died in 1482, claims that the duke's celebrated library contained no printed books, which illustrates how manuscript prestige persisted among elite collectors even when the claim itself is open to doubt. Machiavelli's Prince circulated in manuscript for nearly two decades before being printed, and Castiglione's decision to print the Courtier was prompted by unauthorized manuscript copying. Handwritten newsletters (avvisi) from Rome and Venice remained a major means of circulating political news among elites long after printing was established.

Historians disagree about how best to describe the change. Elizabeth Eisenstein argued in the 1970s that printing created a "typographical fixity" that supported standardization, accumulation of knowledge, and comparison of texts. Later scholars, among them Adrian Johns and Anthony Grafton, have emphasized that printed books were variable, error-prone, and subject to piracy, and that readers' trust in a text depended on social conventions and personal credit, not the technology alone. Both views agree that printing did change the speed and scale of reproduction, but they differ on how automatic its effects were. The evidence in this chapter supports a cautious position: print created new possibilities, but outcomes depended on markets, institutions, readers, and political authorities.

Section 60.12

Printers, Publishers, Booksellers, and the Renaissance Book Trade

Printing was an economic enterprise requiring capital, labor, raw materials, and markets. A printer needed type, presses, paper, ink, premises, skilled workers, and money to cover costs until copies sold. Often printers, financiers, and booksellers were separate: merchants invested in editions, printers produced them, and booksellers distributed them through fairs and shops. Some firms integrated these roles. Publishing involved choosing texts, finding editors, obtaining privileges, setting the print run, and arranging distribution. Print runs in the fifteenth century are often estimated at a few hundred copies, though larger or smaller figures occurred, and estimates remain uncertain. The financial risks were real. In 1472 the Rome printers Sweynheym and Pannartz appealed to Pope Sixtus IV, citing the large unsold stock of their editions, and many early firms failed.

Some printers became central figures in intellectual life. Aldus Manutius (c. 1450 to 1515), a humanist teacher before he became a printer, established his press in Venice in the mid 1490s. He printed Greek authors, including the works of Aristotle, in editions that made Greek texts more available to scholars, and in 1501 he introduced small octavo editions in italic type, including Virgil and, later, Petrarch and Dante, which were portable and relatively affordable. His device of a dolphin and anchor became a mark of quality, and counterfeit editions soon appeared in Lyon, forcing him to publicize warnings. Aldus's firm continued under his relatives after his death in 1515, and in 1528 it printed Castiglione's Courtier. In Basel, Johann Froben built a firm in close association with scholars, printing patristic texts and works of Erasmus, including the 1516 Greek New Testament edition, and employed artists such as Hans Holbein for illustrations. In Nuremberg, Anton Koberger ran one of the largest early operations, printing illustrated and scholarly books for a wide market.

The book trade was also diverse in language and script. Jewish printers produced Hebrew books in Italy from the 1470s, and in 1517 Daniel Bomberg, a Christian printer from Antwerp working in Venice with Jewish editors, issued a major Rabbinic Bible. Expelled Sephardic Jews established a press in Constantinople in 1493. The first Croatian-language printed book, a Glagolitic missal completed in 1483, survives, although its printing location remains disputed, with Kosinj and Venice among the proposed sites. Schweipolt Fiol printed Cyrillic liturgical books in Kraków in 1491, and a press at Cetinje in Montenegro issued Cyrillic books in the 1490s. An Arabic prayer book, printed at Fano in 1514 for Eastern Christian readers, was among the earliest books in Arabic type. Printing in Arabic script by Muslim communities in the Ottoman Empire did not become established until the 1720s, a delay historians attribute to a combination of religious, economic, and institutional factors that remain debated. The expansion of print was therefore uneven across languages and communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Incunabulum, Edition, and Printing Privilege

An incunabulum (plural incunabula) is a book printed before 1501, from a Latin word for "cradle." An edition is a set of copies produced from a single setting of type, and can differ from other editions in text, format, and quality. A printing privilege was a grant from a ruler or authority giving a printer exclusive rights to print a text, a type, or a category of work for a limited period, usually in a specified jurisdiction. Privileges protected investment, but they were not equivalent to modern copyright. They granted a privilege to a printer or sometimes an author, were enforceable only where the issuing authority had power, and could be used to control as well as encourage publication.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside an Early Sixteenth-Century Printing House

A printing house was a workshop organized by skill and hierarchy. A master printer owned or rented the premises and equipment, supervised work, and managed relations with scholars, patrons, and merchants. Compositors selected individual pieces of type from cases and arranged them line by line in a composing stick, assembling pages that were then locked into forms. At the press, two workers usually operated together, one inking the type with leather-covered balls and the other pulling the lever that pressed the paper against it. Sheets were printed on one side, dried, and then printed on the other. Correctors read proofs, though proofreading was often limited, and corrections were sometimes made while printing was in progress, so that copies from the same edition could differ. Apprentices learned the trade under contract, and journeymen could be hired for specific jobs.

Many books were sold as unbound sheets, and buyers had them bound according to their means and tastes. Women participated in the trade, especially as widows who inherited and managed businesses. Charlotte Guillard in Paris, for example, ran a prominent press for decades in the first half of the sixteenth century. The work was physically demanding, and guild regulations, wages, and disputes with employers appear in the records of towns where printers gathered. The workforce was also a literate community of its own, and printing houses served as meeting places for scholars, correctors, and authors. Evidence for printshop life comes from contracts, litigation, workshop inventories, and a few contemporary descriptions, which are often written from the master's perspective.

Section 60.13

Printed Scholarship, Editions, and the Circulation of Knowledge

Printing changed how scholars obtained texts, compared versions, and established reputations. The first printed edition of a classical work, known as the editio princeps, could become the basis for later editions, and the choice of manuscript used had lasting consequences. Livy's History of Rome was printed in Rome in 1469, and Cicero's De officiis, a treatise on duty and expediency central to Renaissance moral and political thought, was among the earliest classical works printed in Mainz in 1465. The first six books of Tacitus's Annals, discovered in a manuscript from the monastery of Corvey, were printed in Rome in 1515, adding a more skeptical account of imperial politics to the available classical models. Aldus and his editors made many Greek texts available, and the Latin and Greek editions of Plutarch and Aristotle supplied materials for political discussion.

Printing also created tools for study. Indexes, tables of contents, title pages, page numbers, and marginal notes made it easier to find passages and compare authors, and reference works such as Ambrogio Calepino's Latin dictionary of 1502 found wide use. Scholars could now consult several editions side by side, notice discrepancies, and publish corrections, and the humanist habit of textual criticism, explained in Chapter 57, gained a wider audience. Printed maps, including editions of Ptolemy's Geography printed in Bologna in 1477 and Rome in 1478 and Martin Waldseemüller's world map of 1507, reorganized geographic knowledge. Printed accounts of Columbus's first voyage appeared in 1493 and were reprinted and translated quickly, an early example of printed news about the wider world, a subject developed in Chapter 69.

These changes had limits. Errors in printed texts could be multiplied as easily as correct readings, and the same technology allowed a flawed or unreliable text to circulate widely. Authors often lost control over their works, as Castiglione's case shows, and some adopted privileges and dedications to protect them. Dedications also served authors' search for patrons, linking scholarship to political patronage. Medical and natural-history authorities such as Pliny, long considered unquestionable, could be criticized when scholars compared texts and observations, as Niccolò Leoniceno did in 1492. Printing helped scholars find and challenge each other's work, but it worked within a system of patronage, institutions, and personal reputations that shaped which books were printed, who read them, and what authority they carried.

Section 60.14

Literacy, Education, Readers, and Access to Books

Literacy in this period is difficult to measure. The ability to read and the ability to write were different skills, taught separately, and many people could do one without the other. Historians use indirect evidence, such as signatures on legal documents, wills, school records, book inventories, tax and census records, and the contents of libraries, and these sources favor urban, male, propertied, and Christian populations. The Florentine chronicler Giovanni Villani estimated in the 1330s that thousands of children in Florence were learning to read, with smaller numbers studying arithmetic and Latin, but his figures cannot be verified and describe a period before printing. Later estimates for particular regions are often derived from signatures and vary widely, and any figure for the whole of Europe is a rough approximation. What is reasonably clear is that literacy was higher in cities than in the countryside, higher among men than women, higher among merchants, clergy, officials, and nobles than among laborers, and uneven between regions.

Schooling took many forms: elementary schools for reading and basic arithmetic, grammar schools teaching Latin, abacus schools for commercial training, church and monastic schools, household tutors, universities, and, for girls, convents and private instruction. Humanist educators promoted a curriculum of classical literature and rhetoric, and their influence on elite education was strong, as Chapter 57 showed. Readers were divided between those who read Latin and those who read only their vernacular language, and printers responded with editions in both. Vernacular printing grew steadily. Pietro Bembo's Prose of the Vernacular Language (1525) argued that Italian writers should take Petrarch and Boccaccio as models, and Ludovico Ariosto's Orlando Furioso appeared in successive editions in 1516, 1521, and 1532. Latin remained the language of scholarship, law, and the Church, and it kept its international reach.

Books were bought, borrowed, copied, read aloud in households or workshops, and inherited. A reader might own one prayer book or a library of hundreds, and many encountered texts through listening and sermons rather than through personal reading. Libraries such as the Vatican Library, refounded under Sixtus IV in 1475, and the collection Bessarion bequeathed to Venice supported scholarship, while private libraries displayed status. Cost mattered: a printed book cost far less than a comparable manuscript, but remained expensive for most families. Women read devotional, literary, and courtesy works, but fewer received formal training in Latin. Rural populations had limited access, and minority communities developed their own networks. Printing expanded the number of people who could obtain books, but it did not create universal literacy, and a text's circulation was shaped by price, language, schooling, and social position.

Section 60.15

Printing, Political Communication, and Public Controversy

Printed matter included books but also proclamations, statutes, indulgence certificates, almanacs, news reports, broadsheets, pamphlets, satires, and illustrated sheets. Governments printed laws and ordinances, and by the 1480s royal statutes were being printed in England and France. The Church used printing for devotional works, liturgical books, and printed indulgence letters, which appeared as early as the 1450s. Rulers used it for propaganda: Emperor Maximilian I sponsored woodcuts, such as the Triumphal Arch, and illustrated works such as Theuerdank (1517) that promoted his image. Printed news of the French invasion of Italy, the discoveries overseas, and the wars of the 1510s and 1520s circulated in letters, poems, and short tracts.

Controversy also found its way into print. The Savonarola controversy produced many printed sermons and tracts. In Rome, the tradition of the Pasquino statue, to which anonymous satirical verses were attached from the early sixteenth century, produced both handwritten and printed satires of the papal court, and the writer Pietro Aretino became notorious for satirical writing and, in the 1530s, for publishing his letters. The Reuchlin affair in the German lands, beginning in 1510, involved the convert Johannes Pfefferkorn's campaign to confiscate Jewish books and the humanist Johann Reuchlin's argument against destroying them. The dispute drew in the emperor, universities, and the papacy, and the satirical Letters of Obscure Men (1515 to 1517) mocked Reuchlin's opponents. It showed how a scholarly quarrel, partly about the status of Jewish texts, could become a public controversy with printed pamphlets and polemics, and it anticipated later religious disputes.

Historians must be careful not to equate this activity with modern public opinion. Most of the population could not read these works, political decisions remained concentrated in courts, councils, and assemblies, and print was only one of many ways of spreading information, alongside sermons, rumor, proclamations read aloud, images, ceremonies, and handwritten newsletters. Printed polemic did reach beyond court circles and created an environment in which arguments could spread quickly across jurisdictions. But the audiences were limited, the controversies often elite or clerical, and authorities could and did intervene. The press expanded the possibilities for political communication without turning Europe into a society of mass media or democratic debate.

Section 60.16

Authorities, Privileges, and the Regulation of Print

Rulers and churches responded to printing with a mixture of encouragement and control. Privileges, granted from the late fifteenth century, allowed a printer exclusive rights for a period. Venice granted early privileges, including one to Johannes de Spira in 1469 for printing in the city, and later issued privileges for particular texts, types, and editions, and popes and emperors issued their own. Aldus held privileges from Venice and later the pope for his italic type, though counterfeiters in Lyon and elsewhere ignored them. These privileges protected investment and also gave rulers a way to supervise who printed what. They differed from modern copyright, which recognizes authors' rights across a legal system, because privileges were discretionary grants, geographically limited, and often attached to printers rather than authors.

Church authorities developed procedures for supervising books. In 1479 Pope Sixtus IV authorized the University of Cologne to take measures against heretical printed works, and in 1486 Archbishop Berthold of Mainz issued an edict requiring prior approval of certain books in his archdiocese. Alexander VI's bull of 1501 extended supervision to German ecclesiastical provinces, and in May 1515 the Fifth Lateran Council's decree Inter sollicitudines, issued under Leo X, required prior examination of books by church authorities, with penalties for violation. Secular governments also acted. The ruling councils of Venice supervised the press through several magistracies, and other governments suppressed works they considered seditious or blasphemous. Enforcement was uneven because jurisdictions were fragmented, printers could move or publish elsewhere, and officials were often inconsistent. The papacy, moreover, itself used presses and issued privileges: Clement VII granted a privilege to Blado's edition of Machiavelli's Discourses in 1531, a reminder that early attitudes toward Machiavelli's works were not uniformly hostile. His works were condemned in the Roman Index of 1559, a development beyond this chapter's principal period.

Section 60.17

The Renaissance beyond Its Italian Origins: European Influence and Adaptation

Renaissance practices spread across Europe through travel, patronage, diplomacy, trade, education, and print, but not as a single package. The French court under Francis I (r. 1515 to 1547) invited Italian artists, including Leonardo da Vinci, who died in France in 1519, supported humanist scholars, and in 1530 established royal lecturers in Greek, Hebrew, and mathematics, an institution later known as the Collège de France. In England, Thomas More's Utopia (1516) and Thomas Elyot's Book Named the Governor (1531) combined humanist education with advice for officials, and Thomas Hoby's English translation of Castiglione appeared in 1561. Spain saw Juan Boscán's Spanish translation of the Courtier in 1534, Antonio de Nebrija's grammar of Castilian in 1492, and Cardinal Cisneros's Complutensian Polyglot Bible, printed at Alcalá between 1514 and 1517. Erasmus's Education of a Christian Prince (1516) offered a northern humanist counterpart to Italian advice literature, as examined in Chapter 59.

In central and eastern Europe, adoption took local forms. Kraków, with its university and royal court, became a center of humanist learning and printing, and Queen Bona Sforza, who arrived in Poland from Milan and Bari in 1518, supported Italian architects and artists at Wawel. King Matthias Corvinus of Hungary, who died in 1490, assembled a large library at Buda before the Ottoman conquest and the dynastic crisis that followed. In Muscovy, Ivan III invited Italian architects in the 1470s, including Aristotele Fioravanti, who worked on the Dormition Cathedral in the Kremlin. In the Ottoman Empire, Sultan Mehmed II patronized Italian artists and scholars, and Venetian and Ottoman exchange continued through diplomacy, commerce, and war. Venice and other Italian ports, with their Greek, Albanian, Slavic, Jewish, and Muslim residents and visitors, also carried knowledge in several directions. Leo Africanus, a Muslim scholar born in Granada and raised in Fez, who was captured, baptized in Rome in 1520, and wrote a description of Africa, is one example of how Renaissance Italy absorbed and transmitted knowledge from beyond Latin Christendom.

Machiavelli's reception offers a case study. Printed in 1531 and 1532, his works circulated across Europe in Italian, Latin, and translation, and later in the sixteenth century they provoked sharp reaction. The French Protestant writer Innocent Gentillet attacked Machiavelli in 1576, and the English stage popularized the figure of the scheming "Machiavel," which often bore little relation to his texts. Others read him differently, drawing on his analysis of military organization or republican arguments. Such afterlives belong to later chapters and to the history of memory, and students should distinguish the historical Machiavelli from the legend. More broadly, the Renaissance influenced European education, courtly manners, political writing, and publishing, but the reception differed by region and rank. In many places it reinforced hierarchy by providing nobles with a vocabulary of refinement, and it had little immediate effect on the daily lives of peasants and laborers.

Renaissance Transformation, Historical Debate, and the Road to Religious Reform

Chapter 57 traced humanist education and textual scholarship, which altered how learned people read, taught, and argued. Chapter 58 examined new practices of artistic representation and the social relations of patronage and workshops, and Chapter 59 showed how northern communities adapted and transformed humanist ideas, especially in religious scholarship. This chapter has added political experience and printed communication. Taken together, they suggest that the Renaissance was a set of connected transformations in scholarship, representation, government, and communication, not a single moment of rebirth, and that its effects reached different groups and regions unequally.

Historical Debate

Did the Renaissance Inaugurate Modern European Political Thought?

The question: Historians ask whether the political writings of Renaissance Italy, especially Machiavelli's, represented a break with medieval political thought and the beginning of a distinctively modern way of analyzing power, or whether they should be understood as developments within older traditions.

Interpretation A: A decisive innovation. Jacob Burckhardt's nineteenth-century account saw Renaissance Italian politics as the creation of the state as a calculated work of art. Later scholars, such as Hans Baron, emphasized a "civic humanism" responding to Florentine republican crises, and Quentin Skinner argued that Renaissance writers contributed to the vocabulary of the modern state. Machiavelli, in this view, treated politics as a field with its own rules, apart from theological moral frameworks, and his analysis of power, arms, and conflict was innovative.
Interpretation B: Continuity and adaptation. Other scholars emphasize that Renaissance writers drew on Roman law, scholastic thought, civic traditions in Italian communes, and the mirror-for-princes genre, and that terms such as "modern" and "state" risk anachronism. J. G. A. Pocock, while emphasizing republican language, stressed the Florentine problem of how republics could endure in time, a concern tied to older Aristotelian and Christian ideas. In this view, Renaissance thinkers rearranged inherited materials in response to new circumstances, with considerable originality, but within a continuous tradition.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on close reading of texts, comparison with medieval and classical sources, and analysis of political vocabulary, including shifts in the meaning of stato. Historians also weigh evidence about institutions, such as councils and chanceries, and about readership: how many people read these works, when, and with what purposes. The evidence for reception is complicated by the long delay between composition and publication, and by later political uses of Machiavelli.

Why it matters: If the Renaissance began modern political thought, then it provides an origin story for later ideas about states, sovereignty, and secular politics. If it reorganized older traditions, then modern thought appears as a longer process shaped by medieval law, theology, and communal politics. The question also affects how one reads Machiavelli: as an inventor or as a participant in a conversation. In Chapter 83, students will encounter later political theories that built on and responded to these debates.

Student task: Identify one passage or argument from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and propose additional evidence, such as readership records or comparison with specific medieval texts, that could help assess how innovative Renaissance political writing was.

Another debate concerns Machiavelli's own work. Jean-Jacques Rousseau suggested in the eighteenth century that The Prince was a satire that taught peoples about rulers' methods, a reading few modern scholars accept as a straightforward account of the author's intention but which shows how the book could be read in diverse ways. Others, including Isaiah Berlin, argued that Machiavelli recognized two incompatible moral worlds, Christian and civic-pagan, and chose the latter. Skinner and Pocock stressed the republican commitments of the Discourses and the humanist context of both works. These disagreements concern interpretation, not the basic facts of composition and publication, and a careful student distinguishes documented chronology from arguments over motive.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Counsel, Statecraft, and Political Institutions from the Later Middle Ages to c. 1550

What Changed

  • Resident embassies and written dispatches made diplomacy more continuous and better documented, especially among Italian and Iberian courts.
  • Political writers used recent experience and ancient history, in print as well as manuscript, to analyze power, arms, and conflict in terms less dependent on moral exhortation.
  • Printed books made classical and contemporary texts, including legal and political works, available in more places and at lower unit cost than manuscripts.
  • Governments and churches developed privileges and licensing procedures to supervise printed works.

What Stayed the Same

  • Dynastic marriage, family networks, and patronage remained central to politics.
  • The Church, aristocratic privilege, and legal corporations retained substantial authority.
  • Manuscripts continued to carry confidential and elite communications.
  • Most people did not read the political treatises and had little formal voice in government.

Who experienced change most directly? Secretaries, ambassadors, courtiers, scholars, printers, and urban elites encountered the new practices most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Peasants, laborers, and many women experienced fewer changes in political participation, though war, taxation, and prices affected them heavily. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against treating changes in elite practice as changes in the whole society.

Cause and Effect

The Expansion of Political and Printed Communication, c. 1490 to 1550

Long-term causes

Urban wealth, humanist education, competition among Italian states, the growth of chanceries, older advice literature, and a trade in books and paper provided institutions, readers, and skills.

Short-term causes

The invasion of 1494, the instability of Florence, the financial and diplomatic needs of states at war, and the commercial opportunities of the printing trade prompted new writing and publication.

Event or transformation

Political analysis, diplomatic reporting, courtly literature, and printed books became part of a connected system of communication across Italy and beyond.

Immediate effects

Works such as the Courtier and the Discourses reached printed audiences, rulers and churches adopted privileges and licensing, and satirical and controversial print appeared.

Long-term consequences

Renaissance models influenced European education, courts, and political vocabulary, set conditions for religious controversy discussed in Part X, and shaped debates later examined in Chapters 82 and 83.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the changes. Political crisis, learning, markets, technology, patronage, and regulation interacted, and outcomes varied by region, language, rank, and gender.

The ending of Part IX should not suggest that the Renaissance finished or that medieval society vanished. Europe remained shaped by religious institutions, aristocratic privilege, dynastic politics, unequal access to education, and long-standing legal and intellectual traditions. The same Europe that developed new practices of political analysis, humanist scholarship, artistic representation, and printed communication was approaching a period of profound religious division. Humanist criticism of ecclesiastical practice, the scholarly editing of sacred texts, the circulation of printed polemic, and the experience of controversy in the Reuchlin affair helped shape conditions in which religious conflict unfolded. They did not predetermine its outcome. Chapter 61 turns to the late medieval Church and the demand for reform.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Renaissance political thought grew from concrete experience. The French invasion of Italy in 1494 and the wars that followed revealed the vulnerability of wealthy Italian states, produced rapid changes of regime, and stimulated sustained reflection on arms, diplomacy, faction, and political survival. Florence's republic of 1494 to 1512, with its Great Council, its charismatic preacher Savonarola, and its eventual defeat, provided the setting in which Niccolò Machiavelli worked as a secretary and envoy.

Machiavelli's career gave him observations of Cesare Borgia, the French court, the papacy, and the emperor, and the fall of the republic gave him the leisure and motive to write. The Prince, composed in 1513 and printed in 1532, analyzed how rulers acquire and keep power, using the concepts of virtù, fortuna, and necessity, and he ended it with an appeal to liberate Italy. The Discourses on Livy, composed in the later 1510s and printed in 1531, defended the value of law, participation, and civic conflict in a republic. Their relationship remains debated, but both depend on empirical observation of political behavior and on classical models.

Francesco Guicciardini offered a contrasting form of analysis, emphasizing circumstance, judgment, and the limits of general rules, while Baldassare Castiglione's Courtier, printed in 1528, described a culture of conduct, learning, and counsel for service to rulers. Comparing these authors shows that Renaissance political writing was diverse, and it also shows the social setting of political service: courts, chanceries, and the networks of patronage on which careers depended.

Diplomacy developed alongside this writing. Resident ambassadors, dispatches, ciphers, and final reports gave states regular information about each other, and humanist-trained envoys often wrote political analysis. The practice spread from Italy to other courts but did not reach all regions in the same form, as the contrasting Ottoman practice of temporary embassies shows. Court culture was a political institution, and elite women such as Isabella d'Este, Margaret of Austria, and Louise of Savoy exercised influence as regents, patrons, and negotiators, though exclusion from most offices and councils limited their formal power.

The second half of the chapter treated printing as a system rather than an invention. Printers, publishers, investors, booksellers, correctors, and patrons formed networks through which texts moved, and printers such as Aldus Manutius and Johann Froben combined scholarship with commerce. Printing changed the circulation of classical editions, reference works, political treatises, maps, and news, but manuscripts continued to matter, errors multiplied as well as corrections, and different linguistic and religious communities adopted print at different times.

Literacy and readership remained limited and unequal. The ability to read differed from the ability to write, Latin and vernacular readerships overlapped without coinciding, and urban, male, wealthy, and clerical groups had greater access to books. Print also carried proclamations, satire, and controversy, as the Pasquino tradition and the Reuchlin affair show, but it did not produce modern mass media or democratic public opinion. Authorities used presses to communicate and legitimize themselves, and they developed privileges and licensing to supervise printing, with uneven results.

The Renaissance had substantial influence across Europe, but that influence was selective. Courts in France, England, Spain, Poland, Hungary, and Muscovy adapted Italian models in different ways, and Jewish, Orthodox, and Muslim communities engaged with print on different schedules and terms. Machiavelli's later reputation, shaped by sixteenth-century attacks and caricatures, illustrates the difference between historical analysis and memory.

As the conclusion of Part IX, the chapter has shown how urban institutions and wealth, humanist scholarship, new representational practices, northern adaptations, political thought, and print communication reinforced one another. It has also stressed that medieval institutions and inequalities persisted, and that the Renaissance was neither a complete break with the past nor the single moment of Europe's modernity. The scholarly criticism, printed controversy, and institutional tensions of the period helped create the conditions for religious division, a subject that Part X begins to examine in Chapter 61, without making that division inevitable.

Key Terms

Stato
Italian word that in this period increasingly denoted a political community's governing order, not only a ruler's standing or possessions.
Virtù
In Machiavelli, the energy, skill, and foresight that enable effective political action, distinct from conventional Christian virtue.
Fortuna
Fortune or chance, imagined as a force beyond human control that skilled actors try to anticipate and withstand.
Necessity
The pressure of circumstances that compels action and may require choices that private morality would condemn.

A tradition of political thought and practice valuing government by citizens through shared institutions, law, and avoidance of domination by one ruler.

A term, associated especially with the historian Hans Baron, for humanist writing linking classical learning to active participation in republican politics.

A genre of advice literature instructing rulers on proper conduct and virtues.

A military captain hired by contract to lead mercenary forces, common in Italian warfare.

The government office responsible for correspondence, records, and the drafting of official documents.

A diplomatic mission or the office of an envoy sent to another ruler or government.

An envoy stationed at a foreign court for an extended period to represent his government and report on affairs.
Dispatch
A report from an envoy to his government, often partly in cipher, containing news and analysis.
Relazione
A formal final report presented by a Venetian ambassador to the Senate on the state of the court where he had served.

A system of coded writing used to protect diplomatic correspondence.
Courtier
A person who served at a prince's court, whether as noble companion, official, adviser, or entertainer.

Castiglione's term for studied ease that conceals effort in the display of accomplishments.

The system by which wealthy or powerful individuals supported artists, scholars, and officials in exchange for loyalty, service, or prestige.

A book printed before 1501.
Edition
All copies produced from one setting of type, which can differ in text and quality from other editions.
Colophon
A statement, often at the end of a book, identifying the printer, place, and date of production.

A limited, jurisdiction-bound grant of exclusive printing rights by an authority, distinct from modern copyright.
Broadside
A single printed sheet carrying news, proclamations, verse, or images for public display or sale.
Pasquinade
An anonymous satirical verse, associated with the Pasquino statue in Rome, directed at public figures.
Vernacular
The spoken and written language of a region, as opposed to Latin as the language of scholarship and the Church.
Censorship
Official supervision or suppression of texts, carried out in this period by churches and governments through licensing, prior examination, and punishment.
Avvisi
Handwritten newsletters containing political and commercial news, which continued to circulate alongside printed material.
Editio princeps
The first printed edition of a text previously available only in manuscript.
Great Council
The broad assembly of eligible citizens at the center of Florence's republic from 1494 to 1512.
Italian Wars
A series of conflicts beginning in 1494 in which French, Spanish, imperial, papal, and Italian forces contended for control of the peninsula.
Inter sollicitudines
The 1515 decree of the Fifth Lateran Council requiring prior ecclesiastical examination of printed books.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 60 matters because it shows how a cultural movement acquired political and communicative consequences. The scholars, artists, and educators of earlier chapters worked within institutions of wealth, patronage, and power, and this chapter has shown those institutions under strain, analyzed by people who had served them, and extended by new means of reproducing texts. The result was a changed repertoire of political analysis, diplomatic practice, courtly conduct, and printed communication, rather than a single new ideology.

The chapter also matters for what it shows about method. The same developments can be described as innovation or as reorganization of inherited traditions, and the evidence supports both claims in different respects. Machiavelli's works were both original and part of a long conversation, and printing was both revolutionary in its capacity and limited in its immediate effects. Students who learn to hold such tensions together are better prepared for the historical questions of later chapters, from the religious controversies of Part X to the Enlightenment debates of Volume IV.

The chapter connects regions as well as ideas. Venetian printers, Florentine secretaries, Mantuan regents, Basel scholars, Kraków courtiers, Jewish and Orthodox printers, and Ottoman and Habsburg diplomats were all part of overlapping networks, and the Renaissance legacy took different forms in each. Its inequalities were also real: access to education, books, and political voice depended on rank, gender, language, religion, and place.

Within Volume III, the chapter completes the account of Renaissance Europe and prepares for the confessional conflicts, overseas expansion, and state formation that follow. The same capacities for criticism, communication, and organization that supported Renaissance culture would be employed in controversies over the Church, and in the reconstruction of political authority amid religious division. That story begins in Chapter 61, with the late medieval Church and the demand for reform.
