Reformed and Radical Protestantism

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In Zürich, in the first months of 1525, one reforming community celebrated its new Lord's Supper at the city's churches under the authority of the city council, while a small group of former allies gathered privately to baptize one another as adults. Both claimed to be restoring the practice of the New Testament. Within two years the second group's members were being drowned in the river that ran through the city. The Reformation did not produce a single alternative to the Catholic Church. It produced arguments about what the alternative should be.

According to the later chronicle tradition of the movement that came to be called Anabaptist, on 21 January 1525 a group met in a house in Zürich, and Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock, who then baptized others present. A few days earlier the city council had ordered that infants be baptized within eight days of birth and had forbidden the group's meetings. About three months later, at Easter, the council's own reformed ministers, led by Huldrych Zwingli, replaced the Mass with a Lord's Supper of bread and wine in the city's churches. Both events were acts of reform, both appealed to scripture, and both rejected much of the late medieval church. Yet the first was soon a crime punishable by death and the second the new public religion of the city. Understanding why requires an account of how reformers who agreed on so much came to disagree on baptism, the Eucharist, the church, and the proper relation between the church and secular power.

The central historical question of this chapter is therefore: Why did the Reformation produce multiple competing Protestant traditions rather than one unified alternative to the Catholic Church? Chapter 62 traced Martin Luther's challenge to indulgences and papal authority and the spread of Lutheran reform in the German lands. Chapter 63 follows the movements that built upon, borrowed from, and quarreled with that challenge. It develops three interconnected histories. The first is the Swiss Reformation, which began in Zürich under Zwingli and spread through the cities of the Swiss Confederation and the upper Rhine. The second is the development of Reformed Protestantism, shaped above all by John Calvin's Geneva, by the Zürich pastor Heinrich Bullinger, and by an international network of ministers, printers, teachers, and refugees. The third is the diverse family of movements grouped under the label Radical Reformation, including the Swiss Brethren, the revolutionary regime at Münster, Mennonites and Hutterites, and spiritualist and anti-Trinitarian thinkers who rejected the established churches altogether.

Historians distinguish two broad responses to the problem of religious authority. The so-called magisterial reformers, including Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, and Calvin, relied on city councils, princes, and other magistrates to introduce and protect reform, and they retained infant baptism and a church that included the whole community. Radical reformers, by contrast, generally insisted that the true church was a voluntary community of believers and that religious faith could not be directed by magistrates. This division matters, but it must be handled carefully. The terms are modern scholarly conventions, not categories used by the participants, and the movements under each heading differed widely among themselves. A reformer such as Calvin could clash with the magistrates of Geneva, and an Anabaptist such as Balthasar Hubmaier could defend the use of the sword by Christian rulers.

The evidence for these movements is unusually varied and unevenly distributed. Council minutes, ordinances, and disputation records survive for Zürich and Geneva, along with thousands of letters, sermons, confessions, catechisms, and treatises printed in the new print culture examined in Chapter 55. The Genevan Consistory kept registers recording the questioning of ordinary men and women, while Anabaptist history is known in large part through the records of their prosecutors, through hymns and letters written in prison, and through martyrologies and chronicles compiled by later believers. Each kind of source carries its own bias, and this chapter asks whose voices are recorded, whose are missing, and who preserved the evidence and why.

These histories were also social and spatial histories. Women appear as printers' wives, pastors' wives, religious writers, household teachers, martyrs, and refugees, and household prayer, schooling, marriage law, and poor relief were among the most important arenas in which reformed religion was practiced. The movements studied here followed rivers, trade routes, and the roads of exile from Zürich to Strasbourg, from Geneva to Frankfurt, Emden, and the Palatinate, and from the Alps and Danube valley into Moravia and the Low Countries. The geography of cities, cantons, and borderlands described in Chapter 2 helps explain why a confederation of semi-independent cities and valleys could shelter several competing reforms in a way a centralized kingdom could not.

Within Part X, this chapter follows the German Reformation of Chapter 62 and prepares the political and confessional conflicts examined in later chapters, including the English Reformation in Chapter 64, Catholic reform in Chapter 65, and the religious wars of France and the Netherlands in Chapter 66. It also advances the argument of Volume III that religious change and the formation of early modern states developed together. It does so without assuming that Protestant division was a step toward modern religious liberty. Most sixteenth-century Protestants, like most Catholics, believed that religious truth was a matter of public order, and the question of who could be tolerated remained bitterly contested.

Historical Context

The Swiss Reformation grew from a political landscape unlike that of the German principalities or the western kingdoms. By the early sixteenth century the Swiss Confederation (the Eidgenossenschaft) consisted of thirteen full member cantons, a mixture of urban republics such as Zürich, Bern, and Basel and rural, mountain, and forest communities such as Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden, together with allied territories, shared dependent lordships, and associated towns such as St. Gallen and the Three Leagues of Graubünden. It was nominally part of the Holy Roman Empire but had in practice become largely independent after its wars with the Habsburgs and its military victories of the late fifteenth century. The Confederation had no central executive. Its Diet (the Tagsatzung) brought together delegates of the cantons, each of which governed its own church affairs. Swiss soldiers, hired out as mercenaries to the French king, the pope, and others, formed an economic and political interest that city governments and their critics both had reason to care about. Defeat at Marignano in 1515 against the army of Francis I, in which Swiss troops suffered heavy losses, helped turn many Swiss thinkers, including Zwingli, against the mercenary system.

The religious setting combined older and newer pressures. Chapter 61 examined the late medieval church, its wealth, its pastoral practice, and the criticisms made by humanists and reformers, and Chapter 59 examined Christian humanism and the program of Erasmus, whose Greek New Testament of 1516 gave a generation of scholars a new textual base for argument. Zwingli, Oecolampadius, and many other early Swiss reformers were humanist scholars before they were reformers. Their approach to reform stressed preaching the text of scripture, returning to what they understood as the practice of the early church, and removing from worship whatever they regarded as unscriptural. Luther's challenge, begun in 1517 and examined in Chapter 62, supplied a model and a language, but the Swiss and upper German reformers did not simply follow Wittenberg. They developed their reforms independently, in dialogue and in conflict with Luther.

Two practices of the medieval church became disputed so quickly that they define this chapter. The first was the Eucharist. The Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation held that the bread and wine became in substance the body and blood of Christ at the priest's consecration. Luther rejected transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the Mass but insisted on Christ's real bodily presence "in, with, and under" the elements. Zwingli and, in a different way, Calvin rejected both. The second was baptism. Since late antiquity, infant baptism had been the near-universal practice in Latin Christendom, and it joined religious membership with membership in the local community, so that the parish was in effect the village or the town. Dissenting groups before the sixteenth century had sometimes challenged this link, but they were small, and the legal tradition of the empire treated rebaptism as heresy punishable by death. Any reformer who questioned infant baptism challenged social order as well as theology.

Finally, the political situation of the empire shaped the survival of reform. Emperor Charles V, whose wide dynastic commitments are examined in Chapter 75, was frequently occupied with wars against France and the Ottomans, and imperial diets repeatedly postponed decisions about enforcing the Edict of Worms of 1521 against Luther. This allowed reform to take root in cities and territories before a unified response could be organized. At the same time, the legal settlement that eventually emerged in the empire, the Peace of Augsburg of 1555, recognized only Catholics and Lutherans. Reformed and Anabaptist communities in the empire lacked legal protection, a difference with large consequences that this chapter notes and that Chapter 67 follows to the Peace of Westphalia in 1648.

Section 63.1

The Reformation Beyond Wittenberg

The Reformation did not spread outward from Wittenberg like ripples from a stone. It emerged in many places at once, in communities with different institutions, and the Swiss lands show why. Authority within the Confederation was dispersed among urban councils, rural assemblies, guilds, and local corporate bodies. A town such as Zürich was governed by a Small Council and a Large Council of two hundred drawn from the guilds, headed by a mayor, and its magistrates already held considerable control over the clergy, the church courts, and the property of monasteries. When religious reform arrived, these magistrates did not stand outside the church. They regarded the regulation of religious life as part of governing a Christian commonwealth, a conviction that long predated the Reformation and that explains much of the character of Swiss reform.

The Confederation's political structure meant that no authority could impose a single religious settlement. The Diet could debate but not command. Some cantons, notably Zürich, Bern, Basel, and Schaffhausen, adopted reform in the 1520s. Others, including Lucerne, Uri, Schwyz, Unterwalden, and Zug, the so-called inner or forest cantons, resisted it vigorously and remained Catholic. Shared dependent territories, known as common lordships, were governed jointly by cantons of both persuasions and became zones of negotiation and conflict. The result was a patchwork of confessions within one confederation that could lead to war but that also produced a tradition of negotiated coexistence unusual in sixteenth-century Europe.

The Swiss setting was also part of a wider upper Rhine and Danube world of cities, universities, and printing houses that included Basel, Strasbourg, Constance, Augsburg, and Nuremberg. Basel was a major center of humanist printing associated with Erasmus. Strasbourg sheltered numerous reformers and exiles. Printers carried pamphlets quickly along the Rhine, so that reform debates in Zürich were known in Strasbourg within weeks. Early radicals were also active outside Switzerland. Andreas Karlstadt, an early Wittenberg colleague of Luther who pressed for faster change in worship and for the removal of images, later sought refuge in the upper Rhine. Thomas Müntzer, an apocalyptic preacher, was executed in 1525 after the defeat of insurgent peasants at Frankenhausen, in the context of the German Peasants' War, which is treated here only where it affects radical religion. These figures are reminders that the Reformation produced radical alternatives from the beginning, not only after the formation of established churches.

For these reasons the chapter treats the Swiss Reformation not as an echo of Luther but as a distinct set of reforms developed in conversation with him. The shared principles, including scripture as the supreme authority, justification by faith, and the criticism of papal and sacramental claims, were real. So were the differences, which concerned the Eucharist, the place of images and music in worship, the role of civil magistrates, and the nature of the visible church itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Magisterial Reformation and Radical Reformation

Magisterial Reformation is a modern scholarly term for reform movements that worked through the authority of the magistrate, meaning city councils, princes, and kings, and that kept a church embracing an entire political community. Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, and Calvin belong here. Radical Reformation is the label, popularized by the historian George Huntston Williams in the twentieth century, for movements that rejected the alliance of church and magistrate, often favoring voluntary membership, believers' baptism, spiritual inspiration, or communal property. Neither term was used by participants. "Radical" does not mean violent or extreme, and some radicals were the most thoroughly nonviolent groups of the century. The labels matter because they describe differences about authority, but they hide the diversity within each group.

Section 63.2

Huldrych Zwingli and the Beginnings of Reform in Zürich

Huldrych Zwingli was born in 1484 in the Toggenburg valley and educated in Basel, Bern, and Vienna, where he absorbed humanist learning. He served as parish priest at Glarus and as a chaplain accompanying Swiss troops, including at Marignano in 1515, an experience that deepened his opposition to the mercenary service and the pensions paid to Swiss leaders by foreign powers. While at Einsiedeln he read widely in Erasmus and the church fathers and studied the Greek New Testament. In December 1518 he was elected people's priest (Leutpriester) at the Grossmünster in Zürich, taking up his duties on 1 January 1519. In the autumn of that year he survived a plague epidemic, an experience that later writings link to his sense of vocation.

Zwingli's early preaching at the Grossmünster departed from convention in a simple but striking way. Instead of following the cycle of readings assigned by the church calendar, he preached continuously through books of the Bible, beginning with the Gospel of Matthew. His sermons attacked practices he regarded as unscriptural, including the invocation of saints, the sale of indulgences, fasting rules, clerical privilege, and the mercenary system. He later described his development as independent of Luther, and historians generally accept that he reached his positions through humanist scholarship and his own reading of scripture, while acknowledging that Luther's writings were circulating in Zürich and shaped the climate of debate. The question of how far Zwingli's thought was "Lutheran" was already a political issue for contemporaries and has remained a historiographical one since.

In Lent of 1522 a group of men, including the printer Christoph Froschauer, ate sausages in defiance of the church's fasting rules. Zwingli defended them in print, arguing that scripture did not forbid the eating of meat. The episode, known as the affair of the sausages, brought the question of religious authority directly before the city council and the bishop of Constance, whose diocese included Zürich. Zwingli also addressed the problem of clerical celibacy, and in 1522 he joined other priests in petitioning the bishop to permit marriage. He himself married Anna Reinhart, a widow, privately in 1522 and publicly in 1524. Clerical marriage became a defining practice of Protestant ministry, and the pastor's household became a model of religious life, a development with large consequences for women's roles examined later in this chapter.

The key institutional fact is that Zwingli did not act alone. He worked with the council, with other pastors such as Leo Jud, and with leading citizens who shared his aims. His authority rested on his preaching and on the willingness of Zürich's magistrates to defend him against the bishop and the Swiss Diet. This partnership shaped his reform program and set the pattern for the Swiss Reformed tradition.

Section 63.3

Zürich's Civic Reformation

The council's role was made explicit in the first Zürich disputation of 29 January 1523. The council invited representatives of the bishop to discuss with Zwingli a set of Sixty-Seven Articles he had drafted, and it declared itself competent to decide the question on the basis of scripture. The decision that Zwingli could continue to preach, and that the preaching should be consistent with scripture, effectively transferred authority over doctrine from the bishop to the city magistrates. A second disputation in October 1523 addressed images and the Mass and exposed disagreement among the reformers themselves. Some radicals, including Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, wanted rapid abolition of both. Zwingli and the council preferred to move at a pace the community would accept.

Reform followed in stages. In 1524 the council ordered the removal of images from the city's churches, and the work was carried out in an orderly way by officials and craftsmen, an example of iconoclasm directed by magistrates. Religious houses were dissolved, and monastic property was redirected to schools and poor relief. At Easter 1525 the Mass was replaced by a Lord's Supper in which the congregation received bread and wine, served on wooden platters and cups at a table in the nave, with no sacrificial offering and with the sermon at the center of worship. Churches were whitewashed, organs were later removed, and congregational singing was restricted for a long period, a reflection of Zwingli's concern that worship focus on hearing the word. His own musical training contrasts with this severity, and historians explain the policy by his theology of worship, not any dislike of music.

Zürich built new institutions to support its reform. In June 1525 the Prophezei, a daily exercise in scriptural exegesis in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, was established for pastors and students, the seed of later theological education and ultimately of the Zürich Bible, completed in 1531. A marriage court (the Ehegericht), created in 1525 with pastors and council members, took over matrimonial jurisdiction previously held by church courts. Poor relief was reorganized under civic administration, funded in part by former church property. These institutions made the Reformation a civic project and extended the power of the council over religious life, women's legal status in marriage, and charity.

The costs and conflicts of this civic reform were real. Zürich's rural subjects, many of whom hoped that reform would end tithes and serfdom, were disappointed when Zwingli and the council held that tithes remained legitimate civil obligations. Zwingli's loyalty to the council and his belief that reform required orderly change led radicals to accuse him of betraying scripture in favor of magistrates. The tension between those wanting reform led by the council and those wanting reform led by committed believers produced the break described in Division III of this chapter.

Section 63.4

The Reformation Across the Swiss Confederation

Zürich's example influenced other cantons, but each followed its own path. Bern, the largest city-state in the Confederation, held a disputation in January 1528 and adopted reform soon afterward. Bern was strategically crucial. Its territory stretched from the Alps to the shores of Lake Geneva, and its decision made reform possible in the French-speaking lands to the west, including Geneva, as examined in Section 63.8. Basel adopted reform in 1529 after a political struggle between reformers and Catholic councilors that included iconoclastic riots. Schaffhausen, St. Gallen, and parts of Graubünden also moved toward reform, while the central Swiss cantons held to Catholicism and organized resistance.

Religious division became military and diplomatic confrontation. The Diet had already sided against Zwingli at a disputation at Baden in 1526, where the Catholic theologian Johann Eck debated Oecolampadius. Zürich responded by creating a Christian Civic Alliance (Christliches Burgrecht) with Constance and later Bern and other cities. The Catholic cantons, in turn, formed a Christian Union and in 1529 sought support from Ferdinand of Austria, Charles V's brother, raising the prospect of Habsburg intervention. The first Kappel war of 1529 ended without battle in a negotiated settlement. In 1531, after Zürich attempted an economic blockade of the Catholic cantons over grain and salt, the Catholic forces attacked. At the battle of Kappel on 11 October 1531, a Zürich army, outnumbered and poorly prepared, was defeated, and Zwingli, who had accompanied it as chaplain, was killed. The Second Peace of Kappel of November 1531 left each canton free to decide its religion and protected the Catholic position in the common lordships.

Kappel stopped the expansion of Zürich's Reformation by force and established a durable confessional boundary within Switzerland. It also exposed the risk inherent in Zwingli's view that reform should be defended politically and, if necessary, militarily. Later Reformed leaders, including Bullinger and Calvin, drew different lessons about the relationship between ministers and political power. The Confederation survived the divide, which many historians see as evidence that decentralization could contain confessional conflict, although it did so at the price of settling religion canton by canton, a principle with later parallels in the German Empire.

Section 63.5

Luther, Zwingli, and the Lord's Supper

The dispute over the Lord's Supper was the most consequential disagreement among early Protestant leaders. All rejected transubstantiation and the Mass as a sacrifice. They disagreed about what Christ's words at the Last Supper, "This is my body," meant. Luther held that the words were to be taken literally, so that Christ's body and blood were truly present with the bread and wine and received by all communicants, believers and unbelievers alike. Zwingli, influenced by humanist textual criticism and a conviction that Christ's body, now in heaven, could not be physically present in many places, read the words figuratively, so that the bread signified the body. For Zwingli the Supper was a sign and pledge of the believer's faith and a public act of communal commitment, a commemoration in which the community confessed its allegiance to Christ.

The terms of this conflict have often been simplified. Luther's critics called Zwingli's followers sacramentarians, suggesting that they reduced the Supper to a mere symbol, and many textbooks have repeated the label. Zwingli's writings in fact emphasized spiritual presence and the communion of the faithful, and later Reformed theologians such as Bullinger and Calvin insisted that believers truly received Christ spiritually. The differences concerned how Christ was present and what the unworthy received, not whether the Supper mattered. Each side also understood the stakes in political terms. For Luther, a symbolic reading threatened the objective character of God's promises. For Zwingli, a physical presence seemed to preserve the magical thinking of the Mass.

The conflict mattered because the Protestant princes and cities needed unity. In October 1529 Landgrave Philip of Hesse brought Luther, Melanchthon, and their allies together with Zwingli, Oecolampadius, and Bucer at Marburg. The theologians agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles, including the Trinity, justification by faith, and scripture, but not on the Supper. The Marburg Colloquy therefore became a symbol of failure, though it also fixed a common doctrinal core. Luther refused fellowship with the Swiss, and the failure contributed to the political isolation of Zwingli and to the later divisions between Lutheran and Reformed churches. The Schmalkaldic League formed by Lutheran princes and cities in 1531 did not include the Swiss, a fact with serious consequences for Zürich at Kappel.

Historians continue to debate whether a political compromise might have been reached at Marburg or whether the differences were too deep. Evidence in the form of the Marburg Articles survives, as do later accounts by participants, but these accounts are partisan and some of the best-known stories about the colloquy, such as Luther's writing of the Latin words on the table, derive from later reports and should be treated with caution.

Section 63.6

Basel, Strasbourg, and Competing Paths to Reform

Johannes Oecolampadius (1482–1531) was a humanist scholar who had assisted Erasmus on the Greek New Testament before becoming the leading reformer of Basel. He agreed with Zwingli on the Supper, advocated church discipline exercised by the congregation and not the magistrate alone, and was a persuasive public debater. In Basel the council adopted the Reformation in 1529 in response to popular pressure and iconoclastic disturbances, and Oecolampadius helped shape a church order that was more attentive to discipline than Zürich's. He died in November 1531, a few weeks after Zwingli, and his successor Oswald Myconius continued the reformed settlement.

Strasbourg, a free imperial city of considerable size and wealth, followed another route. Under the leadership of Martin Bucer, Wolfgang Capito, Matthias Zell, and Katharina Schütz Zell, the city developed a reform that drew on Luther, Zwingli, and its own pastoral concerns. Bucer sought to mediate between Wittenberg and the Swiss on the Supper, formulating subtle compromise language, and was among the authors of the Tetrapolitan Confession of 1530 submitted by Strasbourg, Constance, Memmingen, and Lindau to the Diet of Augsburg. His efforts led to the Wittenberg Concord of 1536 with Luther, which many southern German cities accepted but the Swiss did not. Bucer also emphasized church discipline, congregational life, and the education of the young, and his theory of the church as a community of discipline influenced Calvin directly, as Section 63.10 shows.

Strasbourg was also a refuge. Dissenters of many kinds found shelter there in the 1520s and 1530s, including Anabaptists such as Melchior Hoffman, spiritualists such as Sebastian Franck, and exiles from France and the Netherlands. The city's tolerance was always partial and shifting, but it created conditions in which differing ideas could be compared and debated, and some of its leading figures shifted their own positions in response. Katharina Schütz Zell, a pastor's wife who wrote theological and pastoral works and defended her husband and her own right to speak publicly, stands as an example of lay and female participation in the reform movement. Her writings are significant evidence of how women could claim religious authority within the broad framework of Protestant reform.

These cities show that the Reformation in the upper Rhine and Swiss lands was not a single line from Zwingli to Calvin. It was a conversation among urban churches with shared humanist training and differing political circumstances, and the Reformed tradition drew on that conversation as much as on any single figure.

Section 63.7

Heinrich Bullinger and the Consolidation of the Swiss Reformation

Zwingli's death in 1531 might have ended the Zürich Reformation. Instead Heinrich Bullinger (1504–1575), a pastor from Bremgarten who had studied at Cologne and was strongly influenced by humanism and by Zwingli, was elected to succeed him in December 1531, at the age of twenty-seven. Bullinger served until his death and shaped the church more than any other individual. He negotiated with a council chastened by defeat, defended the city's church against Catholic and Lutheran criticism, and avoided the political adventurism that had caused Kappel. In place of Zwingli's expansion, Bullinger emphasized consolidation, preaching, pastoral care, and a clear understanding of the proper spheres of church and magistrate.

Bullinger's theology continued Zwingli's emphasis on scripture and the covenant. He developed the concept of the covenant of God with humanity as a framework tying together the Old and New Testaments and used it to justify infant baptism as a sign of inclusion in the covenant community, a response to the Anabaptist challenge. His covenant theology became a lasting feature of Reformed thought. He also drafted the First Helvetic Confession of 1536 with colleagues from other Swiss cities, a statement that marked a gradual Swiss Reformed identity separate from both Lutherans and Catholics, and wrote what became the Second Helvetic Confession, completed privately in 1561 and published in 1566, which was adopted by Reformed churches in Switzerland, the Palatinate, Scotland, France, Hungary, and Poland.

Bullinger's greatest historical significance may lie in his correspondence. Thousands of his letters survive, exchanged with reformers, rulers, and scholars across Europe, including the English reformers who sought refuge in Zürich during the reign of Mary I, to be examined in Chapter 64, and Reformed leaders in Poland, Hungary, France, and the German lands. Zürich became a node in a network of communication that spread information, counsel, and theological advice. Bullinger also played a decisive role in reconciling the Zürich and Genevan traditions in the Consensus Tigurinus of 1549, as Section 63.13 explains, and in guiding the development of an international Reformed consensus that was never reducible to Calvin alone.

Section 63.8

Geneva Before Calvin

Geneva was a small trading city of roughly ten thousand inhabitants, located at the point where the Rhône leaves Lake Geneva and at a crossroads of routes among Italy, France, Germany, and the Alps. Its fairs had once been important to European commerce, though by the sixteenth century they had been overshadowed by Lyon. Three authorities contested control. The bishop of Geneva claimed lordship over the city. The Dukes of Savoy, who surrounded Geneva and had long sought to bring it under their rule, claimed influence through their relatives in the episcopal office. And the city's own councils, including a Small Council, a Council of Sixty, a Council of Two Hundred, and a General Council of citizens, aimed at autonomy, guided by four annually elected syndics.

The struggle for autonomy provided the context for religious reform. In the 1520s Geneva's leaders, including the party sometimes called the Eidguenots, who favored alliance with the Swiss, concluded a treaty of citizenship with Bern and Fribourg in 1526 that helped protect the city against Savoy. Bern's own reform in 1528 made it a patron of Protestant preaching in French-speaking lands. When the bishop left Geneva in the early 1530s, the political and religious questions became inseparable. Guillaume Farel, a French evangelical preacher from the Dauphiné who had worked with Bernese support in the region, arrived in Geneva in 1532 and began to preach. After a public disputation in 1535, the city council suppressed the Mass and removed images, and on 21 May 1536 the General Council of citizens voted to live by the gospel and the word of God.

Geneva's adoption of reform was therefore the outcome of a civic and political movement, not the work of Calvin, who was then not present. It involved the defense of independence against Savoy, the support of Bern, the preaching of Farel, the activity of printers and small groups of evangelical citizens, and the choices of the city's governing bodies. Historians who treat Geneva as Calvin's creation overlook the fact that a church order and a civic reform had begun before his arrival and that Genevan magistrates were not passive spectators when he did arrive. They had reasons of their own to value reform and to resist any claim that ministers could control it.

Section 63.9

John Calvin's Formation and the Institutes of the Christian Religion

John Calvin was born in 1509 in Noyon, in Picardy, the son of a legal official in the service of the cathedral chapter. He studied arts at Paris, where the colleges were centers of scholastic theology and of humanism, and then law at Orléans and Bourges, in an environment shaped by the humanist legal scholarship of the period. In 1532 he published a commentary on Seneca's On Clemency, a humanist work that shows his training in classical rhetoric. He later described a "sudden conversion" to a teachable heart, but he did not date it, and historians remain divided over when and how his turn to evangelical ideas occurred, placing it variously in the early 1530s. His circle in Paris included reform-minded friends, and in November 1533 an address by his friend Nicolas Cop, rector of the university, was denounced for evangelical content, with Calvin sometimes suspected of having had a hand in it.

The Affair of the Placards in October 1534, in which broadsheets attacking the Mass were posted in Paris and even near the king's bedchamber, brought a harsh crackdown by Francis I. Evangelicals fled and Calvin, like many others, left France. In Basel in March 1536 he published the first edition of the Institutes of the Christian Religion, a short Latin work in six chapters modeled in part on Luther's catechisms, covering the law, the creed, the Lord's Prayer, the sacraments, the five rites he considered false sacraments, and Christian liberty. It included a preface addressed to Francis I that defended the persecuted French evangelicals as loyal subjects and presented their beliefs as scriptural. The preface shows that from the start the work was a defense of a persecuted minority, and not only a theological treatise.

Calvin revised and expanded the Institutes repeatedly. A French translation appeared in 1541, and the definitive Latin edition, in four books, appeared in 1559, with a French edition in 1560. The final structure examined God the Creator, God the Redeemer in Christ, the way believers receive Christ's grace through the Holy Spirit, and the external means by which God draws people to Christ, including the church, sacraments, and civil government. The development of the work over twenty-three years is itself historical evidence of Calvin's changing priorities in response to controversy, experience, and the needs of the churches he served.

The Institutes was not the sole source of Calvin's influence. He also produced biblical commentaries on most books of the Bible, catechisms, sermons, treatises, and thousands of letters. His importance rested on this combination of teaching, writing, and direct pastoral work, and later Reformed churches used his writings along with those of Bullinger, Bucer, Vermigli, and others.

Section 63.10

Calvin's First Geneva Ministry, Exile, and Return

In July 1536, while traveling to Strasbourg, Calvin stopped for a night in Geneva, and Farel persuaded him to remain and help consolidate the new church. Calvin was first employed as a lecturer in scripture and then as a pastor. In 1537 he and Farel presented the council with articles on church organization, a confession of faith, and proposals on frequent communion and discipline, including the power to exclude unrepentant sinners from the Supper. The articles reflected Bucer's ideas about church discipline and a conviction that the church's holiness required both preaching and the community's moral oversight.

The proposals created conflict. Geneva's magistrates distrusted ministers who sought independent authority over excommunication, which they saw as belonging to the civil government. They were also under pressure from Bern, which expected Geneva to follow Bernese ritual practices, such as using unleavened bread in communion. In early 1538, after elections brought to office syndics hostile to the ministers, Calvin and Farel refused to celebrate communion at Easter under conditions they considered improper. The council expelled them in April 1538.

Calvin went to Strasbourg, where he served from 1538 to 1541 as pastor to a congregation of French refugees. There he learned from Bucer's church organization, observed worship in a city with developed discipline and a school system, taught and wrote, and married Idelette de Bure, the widow of an Anabaptist convert, in 1540. He also attended imperial religious colloquies and met Melanchthon. The years in Strasbourg shaped his understanding of the church and gave him practical experience he later applied in Geneva. They also placed him among refugees, a condition he would himself return to cultivate.

Geneva's leaders meanwhile found that they needed an effective church organization and a recognized leader, partly because of anxiety about Catholic efforts to recover the city and partly because of political divisions. They invited Calvin to return, and after hesitation he did so in September 1541. His return, like his first arrival, was the result of a negotiation. He accepted a position in a city whose councils retained ultimate civil authority, and the compromise established in 1541 gave the ministers a defined role in church affairs while leaving the final decisions about enforcement in the hands of magistrates.

Section 63.11

The Genevan Church: Government, Discipline, and the Consistory

The Ecclesiastical Ordinances of November 1541 established the framework of the Genevan church. They identified four offices drawn from Calvin's reading of the New Testament: pastors, who preached and administered the sacraments; doctors or teachers, who taught scripture and theology and oversaw education; elders, lay officials chosen from the city's councils who supervised the moral life of the community; and deacons, who managed poor relief and the city hospital. Pastors met weekly in the Company of Pastors to discuss doctrine, examine candidates, and coordinate preaching. The Ordinances also regulated worship, including the preaching schedule, catechism instruction for children, the celebration of the Supper several times a year, and the singing of psalms in the vernacular.

The Consistory, which first met in 1542, brought together the city's pastors and twelve elders and met weekly. It summoned residents accused of religious or moral offenses, including blasphemy, quarrels, gambling, dancing, absence from sermons, adultery, domestic violence, and the retention of Catholic devotional practices, and it also mediated disputes between spouses, family members, and neighbors. Its principal sanctions were admonition and temporary exclusion from the Supper. It had no power to inflict civil punishments, which remained with the Small Council, though the lines blurred in practice. The Consistory's registers, which survive in large numbers and have been studied intensively by historians such as Robert Kingdon, provide an unusual glimpse into the daily lives, speech, and beliefs of ordinary Genevans, including women, who appear in large numbers both as accused and as witnesses.

Debate about the Consistory long centered on whether the system was an instrument of social control or a form of pastoral care. Evidence supports both readings. The Consistory could be intrusive and, for those summoned for words or behavior regarded as impious, humiliating. But it also protected some women and servants from abuse, mediated marital quarrels, and treated reconciliation as its goal. Historians now generally avoid treating it as simply repressive or simply benevolent and emphasize its dependence on community participation, since many cases began with complaints from neighbors and family members.

The question of authority was the key conflict. Calvin insisted that the church, through the Consistory, must have the power of excommunication independent of the magistrates. The councils resisted. Only after 1555, when a faction opposed to Calvin's influence, led by Ami Perrin, lost power following disturbances in the city, did the ministers secure their position. Even then Geneva remained a city governed by civic councils, and the label sometimes applied to it, "theocracy," misleads. Ministers could not hold civil office, and the magistrates exercised authority over the church's finances and appointments. A more accurate description is a civic church in which ministers and magistrates worked in partnership and competition.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household, School, Marriage, and Poor Relief in Reformed Cities

The Reformation entered ordinary life through the household as much as through the church. Reformed pastors promoted daily prayer, scripture reading, and the catechism in families, and the pastor's household was presented as a model of Christian domestic life. Catechism classes for children, held on Sundays in Geneva, taught basic doctrine and, in many places, basic literacy. In Zürich and Geneva, schooling expanded, with new schools and academies training ministers and administrators. Girls had fewer opportunities for formal education than boys, though the emphasis on reading scripture encouraged literacy among women in Reformed communities.

Marriage law changed because reformers rejected the sacramental character of marriage and the jurisdiction of church courts. In Zürich and Geneva, civil or mixed courts of pastors and magistrates regulated engagements, divorce, and adultery. Protestant law allowed divorce on narrow grounds, including adultery and desertion, which in some cases gave women a path out of failed marriages that Catholic law, which permitted only separation, did not. At the same time, discipline over sexual behavior and household order was intense, and women were disproportionately punished for sexual offenses. Reformed charity was reorganized under civic oversight. Zürich's 1525 poor relief and Geneva's General Hospital, run by deacons, combined care for the sick and elderly with attempts to distinguish the "deserving" poor from vagrants. Women such as Marie Dentière, a former abbess who defended women's right to speak about scripture in a 1539 letter to Marguerite of Navarre, and Katharina Schütz Zell contested the limits placed on female participation, though most women's religious activity remained within the household and congregation.

Section 63.12

Calvin's Theology of Salvation and Christian Life

Calvin's theology begins with the knowledge of God and the human condition. Humans have an innate sense of the divine, but sin has corrupted human understanding, so that God must be known through scripture, which the Holy Spirit confirms in the hearts of believers. Salvation comes through Christ, and believers receive it by faith, joined to Christ by the Holy Spirit. Calvin called this union with Christ the source of the "double grace," justification, in which believers are declared righteous before God through Christ's righteousness, and sanctification, the lifelong process of growth in holiness. He agreed with Luther that justification is by faith alone, but he gave greater emphasis to sanctification and to the believer's obligation to live in obedience to God's law.

Predestination is the doctrine most associated with Calvin, but treating it as the center of his thought distorts both him and later Reformed teaching. The belief that God has chosen some for salvation before creation was not original with Calvin. It descended from Augustine and had been taught by Luther, and Zwingli had emphasized divine providence. Calvin's contribution was to present election as a source of comfort and assurance for believers, grounded in Christ, and to teach that God also decreed the damnation of others, a doctrine he himself called a "dreadful decree." In the 1559 Institutes predestination appears within the treatment of how believers receive grace, after faith and the Christian life, and not as the starting point of the system. Later Reformed theologians, including Beza, developed the doctrine into a more systematic framework, and subsequent controversies within Reformed churches over its meaning, notably in the Dutch Republic in the early seventeenth century, were as much about later interpretation as about Calvin.

Calvin also taught that the law has three uses: to show humans their sin, to restrain wrongdoing in society, and to guide believers in the Christian life. He affirmed that all legitimate work is a calling from God, a view that gave dignity to ordinary labor, and he taught that believers should live in the world with discipline but without contempt for creation. Historians have long debated the relation between Reformed Protestantism and the rise of capitalism, often framed as a response to the sociologist Max Weber's early twentieth-century thesis. The historical evidence shows no simple connection. Reformed merchants appear in many commercial centers, but so do Catholics, Lutherans, and Jews, and the question is better treated as one of how religious communities shaped attitudes to work, thrift, and credit than as a claim that Calvinism produced modern capitalism.

On politics, Calvin taught that civil government is ordained by God and that subjects owe obedience even to unjust rulers, though he allowed that lesser magistrates might resist tyranny, a qualification he placed at the end of the Institutes. Later Reformed writers, writing in the context of persecution in France and the Netherlands, developed theories of resistance that Chapter 66 examines.

Historical Vocabulary
Reformed and Calvinist

Reformed is the term used by members of the churches that descended from the Swiss and Genevan reforms and that regarded themselves as reformed according to the word of God. Calvinist began largely as a label used by opponents, especially Lutherans, and was applied to those who followed Calvin's teaching, and many Reformed people disliked it because it suggested a human founder. Modern scholars often prefer "Reformed" because the tradition drew on Zwingli, Bullinger, Bucer, Vermigli, and many others, and not on Calvin alone. The word "Presbyterian" refers to Reformed churches governed by assemblies of elders (presbyters), notably in Scotland.

Section 63.13

Reformed Worship, Sacraments, and Doctrinal Reconciliation

Reformed worship centered on the sermon, which was preached in the vernacular and was often an hour or more long, and on the singing of psalms. Calvin favored congregational singing of metrical psalms, unlike Zürich's initial restrictions, and in Geneva a psalter in French, begun with the poet Clément Marot and completed by Beza, appeared in 1562 and was soon translated and sung across Europe. Churches were stripped of images, altars were replaced by tables, and liturgical vestments were abandoned. The Supper was celebrated a limited number of times each year, though Calvin would have preferred more frequent communion. Sunday was kept as a day of worship and rest, and the number of holy days was reduced.

Calvin recognized only two sacraments, baptism and the Lord's Supper, which he understood as signs and seals of God's promises. On the Supper, he rejected both transubstantiation and Luther's teaching that Christ's body is present in, with, and under the elements. He also rejected the idea that the Supper is a mere memorial. In his view believers truly participate in Christ's body and blood, but this occurs spiritually, through the power of the Holy Spirit, who lifts believers to Christ in heaven. Unbelievers who receive the bread and wine do not receive Christ. This position, sometimes called spiritual presence, occupied a middle ground between Luther and Zwingli and was shaped by the Strasbourg mediation of Bucer.

In 1549 Calvin and Bullinger concluded the Consensus Tigurinus, or Zürich Consensus, a joint statement of twenty-six articles that harmonized the Genevan and Zürich teachings on the sacraments. It brought the Swiss Reformed churches together, though it was a negotiated agreement that required each side to adjust its language. It did not heal the breach with Lutherans. In the 1550s Lutheran theologians such as Joachim Westphal attacked the Reformed teaching, and Calvin responded in print, producing what is sometimes called the second sacramentarian controversy. The result hardened the boundary between Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism, which continued to matter in the German Empire, where the Peace of Augsburg protected only Lutheran and Catholic practice.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Worship and Church Order from Late Medieval Parish to Reformed Congregation

What Changed

  • The Mass, understood as a sacrifice offered by a priest, was replaced by a communion service centered on preaching
  • Images, side altars, relics, and most processions disappeared, and the cult of the saints and of Mary was rejected
  • Monasteries and convents were dissolved, and clergy were allowed to marry
  • Church government by bishops gave way to pastors, elders, and assemblies, with moral oversight shifting to consistories

What Stayed the Same

  • Infant baptism and the identification of the parish with the political community
  • Creeds inherited from the early church, including the doctrine of the Trinity
  • Sunday worship, church attendance as a public obligation, and the church's role in marking birth, marriage, and death
  • Parish structures, civic magistrates' oversight of religion, and the church's role in education and charity

Who experienced change most directly? Former priests, monks, and nuns, who lost their position or had to adapt; women whose devotional life had centered on saints, Marian devotion, and confraternities; and urban congregations in which the visible environment of worship was transformed. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural parishioners in many areas, where the calendar of work, local customs, and attachment to familiar ritual often persisted. Why the pattern matters: The Reformed Reformation was radical in worship but conservative in its attachment to a church that embraced the whole community, and this is exactly the point on which Anabaptists challenged it.

Section 63.14

Authority, Dissent, and Religious Coercion in Geneva

Calvin's authority in Geneva was never uncontested. Established Genevan families resented the growing influence of French refugees, whom some regarded as foreign interlopers, and resisted the Consistory's discipline over customs such as dancing, baptismal names, and drinking. In the 1540s and early 1550s the opposition, including the Perrin family and others whom Calvin called "Libertines," used civic institutions to challenge the ministers. In 1547 a Genevan, Jacques Gruet, was tortured and executed for blasphemy and political conspiracy after a threatening note was found in Calvin's pulpit. Other disputes involved predestination, as when Jérôme Bolsec attacked Calvin's teaching in 1551 and was banished. The magistrates were not uniformly supportive of Calvin, and many Reformed churches, including Bern and Basel, doubted some of his positions. By 1555 his position was secure, but only after years of conflict.

The most notorious case was that of Michael Servetus, a Spanish physician and theologian who had published attacks on the doctrine of the Trinity in 1531 and 1532 and then, in 1553, the Restoration of Christianity, which rejected both the Trinity and infant baptism. The book was published secretly at Vienne in France, where Servetus lived under an assumed name. After the Catholic Inquisition identified him, he escaped from prison, and in August 1553 he was recognized and arrested in Geneva. The Small Council, not the Consistory, conducted the trial, and consulted the other Swiss Reformed churches of Zürich, Bern, Basel, and Schaffhausen, which supported severe action. He was condemned for heresy and burned at the stake on 27 October 1553. Calvin had argued that he should be executed by the sword, not the fire, a request the council rejected.

The execution shaped the history of toleration. Most sixteenth-century Catholics and Protestants regarded heresy as a crime against God and society, and the death penalty for anti-Trinitarians had imperial and church legal precedent. But the case also provoked an early and influential protest. Sebastian Castellio, a former colleague of Calvin, argued in Concerning Heretics (1554) that killing people for their beliefs contradicted the gospel, and Beza replied in defense of the magistrate's duty. Historians must avoid two simplifications. It is anachronistic to judge Geneva by modern standards of religious liberty, as if most European states in 1553 allowed dissent, but it is equally wrong to treat the execution as inevitable, since contemporaries such as Castellio saw the moral issue clearly. Servetus's case also belongs to the history of anti-Trinitarian thought treated in Section 63.23.

Section 63.15

Education, Printing, Refugees, and Reformed Religious Networks

Geneva became the center of a Reformed international network because of its role as a school, a press, and a refuge. In 1559 the city opened the Genevan Academy under Theodore Beza as its first rector, comprising a lower school (the Collège) for boys and an upper school offering instruction in theology, Hebrew, Greek, and other subjects. Students from France, the Low Countries, the German lands, England, Scotland, Italy, Hungary, and Poland studied there and returned as pastors, teachers, and advisers. The Company of Pastors also trained and sent ministers into France, and records of these missions show that Geneva acted as a supply base for the underground French churches, though the number of ministers sent and the degree of control exercised from Geneva remain debated.

Printing multiplied Geneva's influence. Printers including Jean Crespin and the humanist Robert Estienne, who moved to Geneva in 1550, produced Bibles, psalters, catechisms, commentaries, and polemic in Latin and French, much of it exported across Europe through merchant networks. The Geneva Bible of 1560, a vernacular English translation prepared by exiles with extensive marginal notes, became one of the most widely read English Bibles. The press worked within, and was supervised by, a city that regulated what could be printed, and its output reflected the interests of Calvin and his colleagues, but it also served the commercial interests of printers and booksellers.

Refugees formed the human basis of the network. From the 1540s, French evangelicals fled persecution in large numbers, and Italian and English Protestants followed. Geneva's population, estimated at around ten thousand in the 1530s and 1540s, grew substantially by about 1560, mostly through immigration, though the exact figures are uncertain. The newcomers brought skills in printing, watchmaking, textile trades, and law, and their presence changed Geneva's economy and politics, creating tensions with older inhabitants. Refugee churches also developed in Strasbourg, Frankfurt, Emden, London, and elsewhere, forming links through which letters, books, and ministers circulated. The experience of exile gave Reformed Protestantism a transnational character, and refugees often saw themselves as members of a church persecuted but not defeated, a self-understanding that outlasted the sixteenth century.

Section 63.16

The Reformed Churches Across Europe

Reformed Protestantism spread through a variety of local circumstances, and its institutions and confessions took different forms. In France, Reformed congregations, called Huguenots by their opponents, organized themselves under a structure of local consistories, regional colloquies, provincial synods, and national synods. The first national synod met secretly in Paris in 1559 and adopted a confession of faith and church discipline, with Calvin's influence in its drafting. The structure of mutually accountable assemblies became a model for Presbyterian systems elsewhere. The wars that followed are treated in Chapter 66.

In the Low Countries, Reformed communities grew under Habsburg persecution and adopted the Belgic Confession of 1561, written by Guido de Brès. Exile congregations in East Frisia, especially at Emden, where the Polish nobleman Jan Łaski (John a Lasco) had served as superintendent, sent ministers and organized a synod in 1571 that helped shape Dutch church order. In Scotland, John Knox, who had spent years in exile in Geneva, helped lead the parliament of 1560 to adopt a Reformed confession and abolish papal authority. The Scottish church, governed by assemblies, developed in ways different from England's, a contrast followed in Chapter 64. Chapter 66 and Chapter 77 return to the Dutch case.

In the German lands, the Reformed tradition entered a constitutional gray area. The Palatinate under Elector Frederick III turned Reformed in the late 1550s and early 1560s, and the Heidelberg Catechism of 1563, written by Zacharias Ursinus and Caspar Olevianus, became one of the most widely used Reformed confessions. Other territories, including Hesse-Kassel, Anhalt, Bremen, and later Brandenburg, also took a Reformed direction, often through what historians call a "second Reformation" led by princes. But because the Peace of Augsburg did not recognize Reformed churches, these territories existed in legal ambiguity until 1648, as Chapter 67 explains, and they had strong incentives to ally with other Reformed powers.

Eastern central Europe offers further examples. In Poland-Lithuania, where the nobility had wide political power and the crown did not enforce religious uniformity, Reformed, Lutheran, and Bohemian Brethren churches reached a doctrinal agreement at Sandomierz in 1570, and the Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed peace among confessions. In Hungary, the Reformed church established itself widely in the sixteenth century, including in towns such as Debrecen, whose synod in 1567 adopted the Second Helvetic Confession. The Ottoman conquest of central Hungary in the 1540s and the creation of the principality of Transylvania produced a divided country in which Habsburg and Ottoman rulers and Transylvanian princes all had different interests, and in which Reformed Protestants were able to organize in the borderlands between rival imperial systems. Italian reformers such as Peter Martyr Vermigli, who fled to Strasbourg, Oxford, and Zürich, and the exiles from Locarno who settled in Zürich in 1555, show that the Reformed network extended beyond the places where it became established churches. Chapter 140 returns to the long-term significance of religious pluralism.

Section 63.17

The Origins of Anabaptism in Zürich, 1525

The earliest Anabaptists emerged from Zwingli's own circle. Conrad Grebel, the son of a Zürich councilor, and Felix Manz, an illegitimate son of a Grossmünster canon, were young humanist scholars and early supporters of Zwingli who came to believe that he was compromising with the council. They objected to the retention of tithes, the slow pace of change in the Mass, and the continuing practice of infant baptism, which they argued had no basis in the New Testament. Baptism, in their view, should follow repentance and faith, and the true church should consist of those who had made a conscious commitment to follow Christ and who were willing to live under discipline. George Blaurock, a former priest from Graubünden, joined them. In a letter of September 1524 to Thomas Müntzer, Grebel and his friends rejected violence and the use of force in religion, a position that distinguished them from Müntzer.

Zwingli at first engaged with them, and the dispute was debated in several public discussions. In January 1525, the council ruled that infant baptism should continue, that infants should be baptized within eight days of birth, and that Grebel, Manz, and their associates should stop holding meetings. On 21 January 1525, the group met in the house of Manz's mother and, according to the Hutterite chronicle and other later accounts, Grebel baptized Blaurock, who then baptized the others. The detail comes from sources written decades later and cannot be verified independently, but the date is widely accepted, and what happened was clear enough to the authorities. Zürich's government responded with imprisonment, fines, and banishment, and in March 1526 it made rebaptism punishable by drowning. Manz was drowned in the Limmat on 5 January 1527, the first Anabaptist executed by Protestant authorities. Grebel died of plague in 1526.

The terms of the conflict matter. The council saw the Anabaptists as a threat to public order because baptism marked membership of the civic community, and rejecting it meant rejecting the connection between church and town. The Anabaptists saw the council as exercising a power over religion that did not belong to it. Zwingli, caught between his conviction that scripture should govern and his commitment to a reform led by magistrates, defended infant baptism through the argument that it replaced circumcision as a sign of the covenant, and the council enforced the policy. The name "Anabaptist," meaning rebaptizer, was applied by opponents, who used it to link the movement to ancient heresies and to the legal penalties of Roman law. The movement's members normally said they were not rebaptizing, since they regarded infant baptism as no baptism at all, and referred to themselves as Brethren, Christians, or members of the covenant.

Historical Vocabulary
Anabaptist and Believers' Baptism

Anabaptist (from Greek for "baptize again") was a hostile label, translated into German as Wiedertäufer and into Dutch as wederdoper, applied to those who baptized adults who had been baptized as infants. Their own preferred term was often Täufer (baptizers). Believers' baptism is the practice of baptizing only persons who profess faith, which Anabaptists considered the only baptism taught in the New Testament. The issue was never only liturgical. Because infant baptism enrolled everyone in the parish and the town, rejecting it implied a church of voluntary adult members and a separation between church and political community, which governments of all confessions considered dangerous. The legal basis for execution was an old Roman law against rebaptism, revived by imperial mandate in 1529.

Section 63.18

The Swiss Brethren and the Schleitheim Articles

After the Zürich repression, the movement spread through the Swiss lands and the upper Rhine, carried by exiles and itinerant preachers into Basel, Bern, St. Gallen, Appenzell, Strasbourg, and southern Germany. As the first congregations formed, divisions appeared over how far to separate from the surrounding society and how to respond to political authority. In February 1527 a meeting of Swiss Brethren at Schleitheim, near Schaffhausen, adopted a statement of seven articles intended to unite the congregations around shared principles. The text is commonly associated with Michael Sattler, a former Benedictine prior from the Black Forest who had left his monastery, married, and joined the Anabaptists, though the extent of his authorship is debated and the statement should be understood as a collective product.

The seven articles dealt with baptism, the ban, the breaking of bread, separation from evil, pastors, the sword, and the oath. They defined a church of baptized believers who disciplined one another and shared the Lord's Supper only among themselves. They called for withdrawal from what they regarded as the corruption of the world, including attendance at Catholic or Protestant services, taverns, and the carrying of weapons. They taught that Christians should not use the sword of the magistrate or serve as magistrates, and that they should not swear oaths. The result was a form of Christianity that rejected the entire structure of church and state on which the magisterial reformers relied. Its emphasis on nonresistance, separation, and voluntary community defined the Swiss Brethren and the later Mennonite tradition.

Sattler was arrested shortly afterward, tried at Rottenburg am Neckar under Habsburg jurisdiction, tortured, and executed in May 1527. His wife, Margaretha, was drowned a few days later. The trial record, and Sattler's own letters, form an important body of evidence. They also show that civil authorities understood the political implications of the articles, especially the rejection of oaths and magistracy, and that the Swiss Brethren posed a challenge to the basis of social order as understood in the sixteenth century.

Paraphrase of the seven articles (translation and quotation avoided here) The first article holds that baptism is for those who have been taught repentance and amendment of life and who believe that Christ has taken away their sins. The second regulates the ban, a procedure of private and then public admonition of members who fall into sin, to be applied before the common celebration of the Supper. The third restricts the breaking of bread to those baptized into the community. The fourth calls for separation from evil, including Catholic and Protestant worship and the use of arms. The fifth describes the pastor of a congregation, who should have a good reputation, teach, discipline, and be supported by the community. The sixth declares that the sword is ordained by God outside the perfection of Christ, so that Christians should not wield it or serve as magistrates. The seventh forbids the swearing of oaths.

Analysis: The text shows how the Swiss Brethren connected baptism, discipline, and political withdrawal into one program. It also reveals what the authors wanted to rule out, since the articles on separation, the sword, and the oath respond to the practices of both Catholic and Protestant societies and to Anabaptists such as Hubmaier who accepted the Christian magistrate. Its limitations are significant. It is a normative document that says how members should live, not how they did live, and it was written by a small group of male leaders in a time of persecution. It says little about women, who were members and martyrs of the movement, and the voices of Catholic and Protestant opponents, who read the articles as a threat to social order, are absent. Historians must read it alongside interrogation records, letters, and later chronicles.

Section 63.19

The Diversity of Early Anabaptist Communities

Anabaptism did not spread from Zürich along a single line. In South Germany and Austria, preachers such as Hans Hut, a former follower of Müntzer who baptized in Franconia, Augsburg, and Austria, combined believers' baptism with apocalyptic expectation, predicting the imminent return of Christ in 1528. Hut died in prison in Augsburg in December 1527, after a gathering often called the Martyrs' Synod of August 1527 had tried to coordinate the movement. Balthasar Hubmaier, a university-trained theologian and pastor at Waldshut, accepted believers' baptism in 1525, later moved to Nikolsburg in Moravia, and defended the Christian magistrate's right to use the sword, a position that the Swiss Brethren rejected. He was arrested by Habsburg authorities and burned in Vienna on 10 March 1528, and his wife, Elisabeth, was drowned in the Danube.

In Moravia, nobles who needed laborers and who held authority over their estates offered refuge to Anabaptists fleeing Habsburg persecution. Communities there organized communal households, where property was held in common in imitation of the church described in the Acts of the Apostles. A group under Jakob Hutter, a Tyrolean who arrived in the early 1530s, consolidated these communities into a disciplined network. Hutter was arrested in Tyrol and burned at Innsbruck in February 1536, but his name remained attached to the movement. Further north, Melchior Hoffman, a furrier and lay preacher from Schwäbisch Hall, spread Anabaptist ideas in Strasbourg and northern Germany and the Netherlands in the late 1520s and early 1530s, combining them with intense apocalypticism and predictions that Strasbourg would become the New Jerusalem.

These groups differed on nearly every important question. Some, like the Swiss Brethren, practiced nonresistance and separation. Hubmaier accepted the sword. Hut expected a violent end-time judgment. Hoffman's followers in the Netherlands developed, in the 1530s, both pacifist and violent wings. The communities of Moravia practiced communal property that the Swiss Brethren did not. Their social composition also varied, including artisans, weavers, miners, peasants, former priests, and a few nobles, and women were prominent members, sometimes baptizing and prophesying in the first generation, though patterns of leadership narrowed as communities organized themselves. Recognizing this diversity is central to understanding why the single label "Anabaptist" is misleading.

Historical Debate

One Radical Reformation or Many? The Origins of Anabaptism

The question: Did Anabaptism have a single origin in Zürich in 1525, from which it spread, or did it emerge independently in several places with different characters?

Interpretation A: A single Swiss origin (monogenesis). Influential twentieth-century accounts, many written by historians from Mennonite communities, traced the movement to the Zürich Brethren and presented its authentic character as biblicist, pacifist, and committed to a church of voluntary believers. In this view, apocalyptic and violent movements such as Münster were deviations from the original model, not expressions of it.
Interpretation B: Several independent origins (polygenesis). Beginning in the 1970s, historians including James Stayer, Werner Packull, and Klaus Deppermann argued that Anabaptism arose in at least three regions, Switzerland, South Germany and Austria, and the Low Countries and northern Germany, with different intellectual roots, ranging from Zwinglian humanism to Müntzer's spiritualism and Hoffman's apocalypticism. In this view, the movement's early diversity was real and not a deviation from a pure origin.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same sources, including interrogation records, letters, hymns, chronicles, and printed tracts. They differ in how they weigh the links between early leaders such as Hut and Müntzer, the chronologies of baptisms outside Zürich, and later chronicles written by communities with their own reasons for stressing a unified origin. Most current scholarship accepts that there were multiple centers and significant cross-influences, so that the dispute has shifted from a choice between two models toward an understanding of how separate currents interacted.

Why it matters: If Anabaptism was a single movement with a peaceful origin, Münster looks like a distortion. If it was many movements, Münster must be treated as one, though not the only, product of the early radical Reformation. The question also bears on how modern religious communities understand their own history and on how historians distinguish memory from reconstruction.

Section 63.20

Persecution, Martyrdom, Migration, and Religious Survival

Anabaptists faced persecution from authorities of every confession. The Second Diet of Speyer in 1529 reaffirmed the application of the Roman law penalty of death for rebaptism throughout the empire, and Catholic and Lutheran and Reformed governments alike used it, with Catholic Habsburg territories and the Low Countries the scene of the most frequent executions. Methods included drowning, burning, beheading, and, for those who recanted, banishment or imprisonment. Scholarly estimates of the total number executed across Europe in the sixteenth century are uncertain and generally range from about two thousand to several thousand, since records are incomplete, but they show that Anabaptists suffered more executions than any other Protestant group.

Persecution shaped the movement's character and its sources. Interrogation records survive in territorial archives and document the beliefs and social backgrounds of those questioned, but they were produced by authorities seeking to establish heresy and sometimes to extract names under torture. Anabaptists themselves preserved memory through letters from prison, hymns collected in the Ausbund, a hymnal first printed in 1564 and containing songs written by prisoners at Passau, and martyrologies and chronicles compiled decades later, such as the Hutterite chronicle and the large Dutch collection assembled in the seventeenth century. These sources emphasize steadfastness and sometimes idealize their subjects, but they also record the experience of women and men, many of them artisans and farmers, who accepted death rather than recant. Women appear among the martyrs in considerable numbers, including Anneken Jans, executed at Rotterdam in 1539, whose letter to her infant son became a famous text.

Survival required movement and concealment. Congregations met in woods, barns, and private houses, shifted their leaders, and sent missionaries along trade and river routes. Communities migrated, often repeatedly, from Switzerland to Alsace and the Palatinate, from the Tyrol and Bavaria to Moravia, from the Low Countries to north Germany and the Baltic coast, where Mennonites later settled in the Vistula delta and contributed to the drainage and cultivation of lowlands, a connection to the land reclamation described in Chapter 2. Some states offered tolerance for practical reasons, as nobles in Moravia and, later, the city governments of the Dutch Republic found Anabaptist farmers and craftsmen economically useful. These localized forms of toleration were fragile and conditional, and were achieved through negotiation and payment, not through any principle of religious freedom.

Section 63.21

Münster and Revolutionary Anabaptism, 1534–1535

Münster, a prosperous episcopal city in Westphalia, became the site of the most famous and most misunderstood episode of the Radical Reformation. The city had gone through a Reformation in the early 1530s, led by Bernhard Rothmann, a preacher who moved from Lutheran to Anabaptist positions by 1533 under the influence of Melchiorite preachers from the Netherlands. Hoffman had predicted that Christ would return in 1533 and establish a New Jerusalem, and after his arrest in Strasbourg, followers led by Jan Matthijs, a Haarlem baker, reinterpreted the prophecy and decided that Münster, not Strasbourg, was the chosen city. In early 1534 Anabaptists won a majority in the city council, and Matthijs and his followers took control.

The regime that followed combined apocalyptic mission, communal property, and coercion. Those who refused rebaptism were ordered to leave, in winter, though some were allowed to remain after repentance. Property was pooled, books other than the Bible were burned, and the city prepared for siege as the prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck, assembled forces with help from neighboring Catholic and Protestant princes. Matthijs was killed in a sortie at Easter 1534, and the Dutch tailor Jan van Leiden took power. In summer 1534 polygamy was introduced, partly justified by an excess of women in the city and by Old Testament precedent, and was resisted by some residents. In September Jan was proclaimed king of a New Israel. Evidence for the details comes mainly from hostile accounts and from the testimony of survivors under interrogation, and historians treat claims of excess with care while recognizing that the regime used terror against its own people.

The city fell on 24 to 25 June 1535 after months of siege and hunger, aided by deserters who revealed weaknesses in its defenses. The leaders were tortured and executed in January 1536, and their bodies were displayed in iron cages hung from the tower of St. Lambert's church, where replacement cages still hang as a memorial of the event. The suppression was followed by a campaign of persecution against Anabaptists across northern Europe, and the Münster story became the standard image of the movement in Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed polemic.

Münster must not stand for all Anabaptists. The Swiss Brethren condemned violence, and later nonviolent leaders such as Menno Simons denounced the regime. But Münster was not an accident unrelated to the movement's history. It arose from Melchiorite apocalypticism within Anabaptism, from the severe persecution of the early 1530s, and from the unusual political circumstances of a city whose government had been captured by a radical faction. Violent remnants, such as the followers of Jan van Batenburg, continued for some years. It is also important to see the response. A city that had adopted a regime considered disastrous by neighbors was crushed by an alliance of Catholic and Protestant rulers united in their conviction that the radicals threatened all authority.

Section 63.22

Menno Simons, Mennonites, and Hutterite Communities

After Münster, the survival of Anabaptism in the Low Countries and northern Germany depended on leaders who reorganized scattered believers around nonviolent principles. Menno Simons, a Catholic priest from Friesland, had begun to doubt the Mass and infant baptism by the early 1530s and left the Catholic Church in January 1536. He was rebaptized, was ordained, and spent the next quarter-century as an itinerant preacher and writer, moving constantly to avoid arrest. His works, including a statement of faith commonly known as the Foundation of Christian Doctrine, rejected the Münsterites, taught believers' baptism and the ban, and insisted on a pure church that refused the sword. Menno's influence meant that nonviolent Anabaptists in the north came to be known as Mennonites, though he did not found a church and was only one of several leaders, including Dirk Philips and Obbe Philips.

Mennonite congregations organized themselves through elders and deacons, and enforced discipline through the ban, which excluded members for sin and sometimes involved social avoidance. Disputes over how strictly to apply the ban divided the Mennonites into several groups in the later sixteenth century. Their congregations survived under persecution in the Habsburg Netherlands, and, with the establishment of the Dutch Republic, found limited toleration in Holland, where Mennonite merchants, shipbuilders, and artisans formed a prosperous community, a point to which Chapter 77 returns.

The Hutterites developed a different form of survival, based on communal property. After Jakob Hutter's death, leaders such as Peter Riedemann, who wrote an influential statement of faith in the early 1540s, organized Moravian communities as large households called Bruderhöfe, where members worked together, shared meals, raised children communally, and held no private property. They produced goods for sale, including pottery, textiles, cutlery, and medicine, and some communities became economically prominent. Despite periods of expulsion, notably after pressure from Ferdinand I in the 1530s and later, Moravian Hutterite communities experienced a period of relative prosperity in the later sixteenth century before renewed persecution during the Thirty Years' War, examined in Chapter 67. Their chronicle, begun in the sixteenth century, remains a major source for their history, with the caution that it records the community's own view.

The consolidation of these traditions shows that Anabaptism after Münster was neither destroyed nor unified. It survived by building disciplined, mostly rural or small-town communities that were separate from the world and sustained by family, mutual aid, and religious commitment. Women's roles changed, with fewer women prophesying publicly and more taking part through household, family, and congregational discipline, though they continued to appear among the martyrs.

Section 63.23

Spiritualists, Anti-Trinitarians, and the Limits of Protestant Unity

The Radical Reformation included thinkers who did not fit the established Protestant churches or the Anabaptist congregations. Sebastian Franck, an author and printer who moved among Strasbourg, Ulm, and Basel, wrote histories and collections of proverbs, including a world chronicle published in 1531 that criticized all churches' claims to speak for God. He believed true Christianity was inward and invisible and that no external church or ceremony could capture it. Caspar Schwenckfeld, a Silesian nobleman, supported Luther at first but broke with him over the Lord's Supper and the relation between faith and moral renewal. In 1526 he proposed a "standstill" in the celebration of the Supper until the churches could agree on its meaning, and he advocated inward spiritual communion with Christ. Schwenckfeld's followers survived in small groups for centuries, as did Anabaptists and Mennonites. These spiritualists differed from Anabaptists because they did not necessarily seek separate congregations and because they thought that the Spirit, not external forms, mattered most.

Another group of thinkers questioned doctrines that the major confessions treated as common ground. Servetus attacked the doctrine of the Trinity, as Section 63.14 explained, and Italian humanists such as Lelio Sozzini and his nephew Fausto, who developed ideas that later gave rise to the label Socinian, argued for a rational reading of scripture that denied Christ's divinity. In Poland, the Minor Reformed Church of the Polish Brethren separated from the Reformed church in the 1560s and later published the Racovian Catechism. In Transylvania, the preacher Ferenc Dávid led a Unitarian church, and the Diet's decree at Torda in 1568, issued under Prince John Sigismund, guaranteed freedom of preaching according to each community's understanding. This edict was an exceptional piece of toleration that reflected the unusual conditions of a principality between Habsburg and Ottoman power, but it did not last, and Dávid died in prison in 1579.

These figures show the limits of Protestant unity. The principle that individual believers could read scripture and judge doctrine for themselves, which the magisterial reformers invoked against the pope, could also be turned against them. Reformed and Lutheran leaders, like Catholics, answered by defining orthodoxy through confessions, catechisms, and discipline, and treated radical dissent as a threat to the church, to order, and to the truth. The history of Protestantism was therefore a history of both the proliferation of positions and the tightening of boundaries.

Cause and Effect

Why the Reformation Produced Many Protestant Traditions

Long-term causes

Late medieval criticism of the church, humanist return to scripture, the decentralized political structure of the empire and the Swiss Confederation, urban corporate government, and a print culture that carried ideas faster than authorities could control them.

Short-term causes

The appeal to scripture alone without a final interpreter; disputes over the Eucharist and baptism; the alliance of reform with city councils and princes who had different interests; the Peasants' War and other social conflicts; and the persecution of dissenters.

Immediate effects

Division among Lutherans, Swiss Reformed, and radicals at Marburg and Zürich; competing church orders in Zürich, Geneva, and Strasbourg; executions of Anabaptists by Catholic and Protestant authorities; the Münster catastrophe.

Long-term consequences

Distinct confessional churches with their own confessions, schools, and networks; an international Reformed tradition; durable Mennonite and Hutterite communities; the growth of arguments about toleration; and the confessional politics examined in Chapters 66 and 67.

Historical complexity: The division was not the inevitable result of a single principle. Political circumstances, the personalities of reformers, local institutions, and contingent events such as Kappel and Münster each shaped the outcome. Reformers with similar theology could end up in different churches, while those with different theology could cooperate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter asked why the Reformation produced multiple competing Protestant traditions rather than a single alternative to the Catholic Church. The answer lay in the combination of shared principles and unresolved questions. Reformers agreed that scripture should be supreme and that papal authority and the sacrificial Mass should be rejected, but scripture did not by itself settle the meaning of the Lord's Supper, the proper subjects of baptism, the structure of the church, or the relation between faith and political power. These questions were answered in different ways by communities with different political institutions, and the answers hardened as they became the basis of church orders, confessions, and persecution.

The Swiss Reformation began in Zürich, where Huldrych Zwingli, a humanist priest, preached through scripture and worked with a city council that took responsibility for religion. Through the disputations of 1523, the removal of images, the replacement of the Mass by a Lord's Supper in 1525, and the creation of institutions such as the Prophezei, the marriage court, and civic poor relief, Zürich built a reformed church governed by magistrates. Reform spread to Bern, Basel, Schaffhausen, and other cities, while the central cantons remained Catholic. The death of Zwingli at Kappel in 1531 ended the hope of extending reform through military force and fixed a confessional boundary within the Confederation, which survived because the Confederation's decentralized structure allowed each canton to decide its religion.

The disagreement over the Lord's Supper divided the Protestant movement. Luther's insistence on Christ's real bodily presence clashed with Zwingli's reading of the words of institution as figurative, and the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 produced agreement on most doctrines but not this one. Bucer and others sought compromises, and in 1536 the Wittenberg Concord reconciled Lutherans with several south German cities, but the Swiss did not join. Under Bullinger, who succeeded Zwingli, Zürich consolidated its church, produced confessions and a vast correspondence, and entered the 1549 Consensus Tigurinus with Calvin, which created a common Swiss Reformed doctrine of the sacraments.

John Calvin, a French humanist trained in law, joined Geneva in 1536, was expelled in 1538, and returned in 1541 to build a church order that gave a defined role to pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons. The Consistory supervised moral and religious life through admonition and exclusion from the Supper, and its registers provide unusual evidence of ordinary life. Calvin's theology, set out in the Institutes, centered on God's sovereignty, union with Christ, justification and sanctification, and election, and his teaching on the Supper stressed spiritual participation. The execution of Michael Servetus in 1553 showed the limits of toleration, though it also provoked protest from contemporaries such as Castellio.

Reformed Protestantism became international through the Genevan Academy, printing, and the movement of refugees. Churches in France, the Low Countries, the German Empire, Scotland, Poland-Lithuania, and Hungary adopted confessions such as the Gallic, Belgic, Scots, and Heidelberg confessions and the Second Helvetic Confession, and developed forms of church government based on assemblies. These churches were shaped by local politics, from persecution in France to princely sponsorship in the Palatinate, and from noble government in Poland to the borderlands between Habsburg and Ottoman power. The Peace of Augsburg did not recognize Reformed communities in the empire, a legal gap that had consequences examined in later chapters.

The Radical Reformation began in Zürich, where Grebel, Manz, and Blaurock introduced believers' baptism in January 1525 after breaking with Zwingli. The Swiss Brethren articulated their principles at Schleitheim in 1527, with separation from the world, the ban, nonresistance, and rejection of oaths. Other Anabaptist currents developed in South Germany, Austria, Moravia, and the Netherlands, with differences over the sword, apocalyptic expectation, and property. Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed governments persecuted them under imperial law, and the movement's survival depended on migration, concealment, and the protection offered by some nobles and cities.

Münster's regime of 1534 to 1535 grew from Melchiorite apocalypticism and a radical takeover of a city council and ended in siege, defeat, and execution. It became the standard image of Anabaptism for opponents, though it did not represent the Swiss Brethren or later Mennonites. Menno Simons and others reorganized nonviolent communities in the north, and Jakob Hutter and Peter Riedemann helped create communal Hutterite households in Moravia. Spiritualists such as Franck and Schwenckfeld and anti-Trinitarians such as Servetus, the Sozzini, and Dávid pressed arguments that challenged both established churches and Anabaptist congregations, and Transylvania briefly offered exceptional toleration.

The chapter's main historical lesson is that Protestant diversity was neither accidental nor the inevitable path to modern pluralism. It arose from shared principles with open implications, from competing institutions, and from political choices. Women, refugees, printers, teachers, and ordinary parishioners were active participants in each of the three histories, and their experience appears in council registers, consistory records, interrogation transcripts, letters, and hymns. The traditions that emerged, Reformed churches and the Mennonite and Hutterite communities, outlived the sixteenth century and shaped the politics of religion in the chapters that follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Magisterial Reformation
Reform movements that worked through city councils, princes, or kings and kept a church embracing the whole community.
Radical Reformation
A scholarly label for movements rejecting the alliance of church and magistrate, including Anabaptists, spiritualists, and anti-Trinitarians.
Reformed
The Protestant tradition that descended from the Swiss and Genevan reforms and developed through Zwingli, Bullinger, Calvin, and many others.
Calvinism
A term, often used by opponents, for the teaching associated with Calvin; modern scholars distinguish Calvin's own thought from later Reformed orthodoxy.
Anabaptist
A hostile label for adherents of believers' baptism, who regarded infant baptism as invalid.
Believers' baptism
Baptism restricted to those who confess faith, in contrast to infant baptism.
Transubstantiation
The Catholic doctrine that the bread and wine become the substance of Christ's body and blood while keeping their outward appearance.
Real presence
The teaching, associated with Luther, that Christ's body and blood are truly present with the bread and wine.
Spiritual presence
Calvin's teaching that believers truly participate in Christ through the Holy Spirit while Christ's body remains in heaven.
Sacramentarian
A polemical label for those who denied the real presence, often applied to Zwingli's followers.
Disputation
A formal public debate, used in Zürich, Bern, Geneva, and elsewhere to decide religious questions, often under the oversight of magistrates.
Iconoclasm
The removal or destruction of religious images, carried out in some cities by order of magistrates and in others by popular action.
Prophezei
A daily exercise in biblical interpretation established in Zürich in 1525 for training pastors and students.
Consistory
The Genevan church court of pastors and lay elders that supervised religious and moral conduct.
Ecclesiastical Ordinances
The 1541 Genevan church order that defined the offices of pastor, teacher, elder, and deacon.
Presbyterian polity
Church government by assemblies of ministers and elders, developed in Reformed churches and especially in Scotland.
Predestination
The teaching that God has chosen some for salvation; in Calvin's thought it is tied to assurance in Christ and not the starting point of the system.
Justification
The declaration that believers are righteous before God through Christ, received by faith alone.
Sanctification
The lifelong process of growth in holiness that follows union with Christ.
Covenant
The relationship of promise between God and humanity, used by Bullinger and others to justify infant baptism and to organize biblical history.
The ban
The Anabaptist practice of excluding unrepentant members from the community after admonition.
Nonresistance
The refusal to use violence or the sword, characteristic of the Swiss Brethren and Mennonites.
Spiritualist
A reformer who emphasized inward spiritual experience over outward churches and sacraments.
Anti-Trinitarian
A thinker who rejected the traditional doctrine of the Trinity, as did Servetus and the Polish Brethren.
Confession of faith
A formal statement of doctrine adopted by a church or city, such as the Gallic Confession or the Second Helvetic Confession.
Catechism
A manual of religious instruction in question and answer form, such as the Heidelberg Catechism of 1563.
Martyrology
A collection of accounts of those executed for their faith, a major source for Anabaptist history, to be read with attention to its purposes.
Bruderhof
A Hutterite communal household in which members shared work, meals, and property.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 63 matters because it shows that the Reformation was a family of movements, not one revolution. By examining Zürich, Geneva, and the Anabaptist communities together, students can see how shared appeals to scripture produced different churches when they met different political institutions, social conditions, and personal choices. The chapter also demonstrates that questions of doctrine, such as the Supper and baptism, were inseparable from questions of authority, community, and power.

The traditions examined here shaped much of what followed. Reformed churches supplied the confessions, church orders, and international networks that appear in the French and Dutch conflicts of Chapter 66 and the confessional politics of Chapter 67. Their ideas about covenant, discipline, and the relationship of church and state influenced the political cultures of Scotland, the Dutch Republic, and other societies discussed in later volumes. The Mennonite and Hutterite communities, by contrast, preserved a model of the church as a voluntary community separate from the state, and the persecution they suffered, together with the arguments of Castellio and others, became part of the long history of debates about toleration traced further in Chapter 140.

The chapter also connects regions. Swiss valleys, Rhine cities, the Danube corridor, the Low Countries, Poland-Lithuania, and the Habsburg and Ottoman borderlands appear as parts of a single religious geography linked by printing, trade, schooling, and exile. That geography reflects the political fragmentation of central Europe, whose consequences Volume III follows as religious change and state formation developed together. At the same time, the chapter has avoided treating Protestant pluralism as a progressive path, since persecution and coercion were practiced by most of the churches discussed.

Chapter 64 turns to the English Reformation, where royal supremacy gave reform a different political form, and Chapter 65 to the Catholic response. Students should carry forward the habit this chapter has tried to develop: asking in each case who held authority, what the sources are, whose voices are missing, and how an outcome that later came to seem natural was in fact contested and contingent.
