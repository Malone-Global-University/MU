France, the Netherlands, and the Wars of Religion

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 1562 and 1609, religious division turned two of the wealthiest and most politically sophisticated regions of western Europe into theaters of civil war. In France the crown survived, changed, and restored itself under a new dynasty. In the Netherlands a rebellion against a distant Habsburg king produced a republic that no European monarch had planned and few had expected.

On Sunday, 1 March 1562, a Reformed congregation gathered for worship in a barn at Wassy, a small town in Champagne. Armed followers of François, duke of Guise, a great Catholic nobleman who happened to be passing through, confronted the worshippers. In the violence that followed, dozens of Protestants were killed and many more wounded. Contemporaries argued bitterly then, as historians have since, about who provoked whom, and whether the duke intended a massacre. What is not in doubt is the effect. Within weeks Protestant and Catholic noblemen were raising armies, towns were seizing their own churches and arsenals, and a kingdom that had been trying to manage religious difference by edict was sliding into civil war.

Four years later, in August 1566, crowds in the southwestern provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands began to enter churches and strip them of images, altars, and liturgical objects. The movement began in the countryside near Steenvoorde on 10 August and spread within days to Antwerp, Ghent, and dozens of other towns. It was largely directed at objects rather than persons, and it unfolded in a political setting already charged by a noble petition against royal heresy laws, high grain prices, and a trade slump. King Philip II, who ruled the Netherlands from Spain, concluded that the authority of the Crown itself was at stake and sent an army under the duke of Alba. The rebellion that followed lasted, with interruptions, for eighty years, and its first four decades are the subject of the second half of this chapter.

This chapter explains how religious division became prolonged civil war in France and rebellion in the Netherlands, and why the two conflicts produced such different political settlements. In France, after the Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres of 1572, a Catholic League that challenged the legitimacy of the Valois monarchy, and the extinction of the Valois line in 1589, a Protestant prince converted to Catholicism, won recognition as Henry IV, and in 1598 issued the Edict of Nantes, a royal settlement that protected the Huguenot minority without establishing equality. In the Netherlands, a rebellion that began as a defense of provincial privileges and of persecuted Protestants hardened into the formal deposition of Philip II in 1581, the creation of a federation of provinces without a prince, and in 1609 a twelve year truce that was in practice recognition of independence.

The governing historical question is therefore a comparative one. Why did religious conflict in France ultimately produce a renewed Catholic monarchy with limited Protestant protections, while rebellion in the Netherlands contributed to the emergence of an independent republic? Answering it requires more than a catalogue of battles and massacres. Students must understand how religion interacted with dynastic instability, noble rivalry, provincial privileges, taxation, urban political institutions, popular mobilization, foreign intervention, and changing ideas about legitimate government. Neither conflict can be explained by religion alone, and neither can be explained without it.

Historians reconstruct these events from a rich and often partisan body of evidence: royal edicts and the registers of the parlements that accepted or resisted them, the records of Reformed synods and consistories, municipal deliberations, notarial and judicial records, the dispatches of ambassadors, private letters and memoirs, printed pamphlets and martyrologies, engravings and commemorative medals, and, in the Netherlands, the records of the Council of Troubles, the correspondence of Habsburg officials, and the resolutions of provincial estates. Nearly all of this evidence was produced by people who were participants in the conflicts, and much of it was written to persuade. Reading it critically is one of the chapter's central skills.

The chapter belongs to Part X, which traces the destruction of western Christian institutional unity and the construction of competing confessional systems. Chapter 62 examined Martin Luther and the German Reformation, Chapter 63 the Reformed and radical Protestant movements whose churches are the protagonists of this chapter, Chapter 64 the English Reformation, and Chapter 65 the Catholic renewal that shaped the Catholic side of these struggles. Chapter 66 shows what happened when those competing confessions met inside two large, wealthy, and politically complex societies. It also prepares the ground for Chapter 67, which will examine the Thirty Years' War and the settlement of 1648 that finally recognized the Dutch Republic. Within Volume III, it shows how religious conflict reshaped the relationship between rulers, nobles, cities, and subjects that Chapters 74 through 80 will examine as the early modern state.

A caution applies throughout. The France that emerged in 1598 and the Dutch Republic that emerged by 1609 were not the inevitable products of these wars. The Valois monarchy might have fallen permanently; the Netherlands might have been reconquered, or divided along different lines, or settled by compromise in the 1570s. The modern borders of Belgium and the Netherlands are not the borders of the revolt, and the seventeen provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands were not a nation waiting to be freed. The chapter therefore treats outcomes as contingent results of choices, resources, and accidents, including the assassinations of three leaders who might otherwise have shaped events very differently.

Learning Objectives

  3. Analyze the Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres, including the decisions made at court, the role of urban violence, and the historiographical debate over responsibility and death tolls.
  4. Assess the conversion of Henry IV, the restoration of royal authority, and the provisions and limits of the Edict of Nantes.
  5. Describe the political structure of the Habsburg Netherlands and explain why religious persecution, taxation, and the defense of provincial privileges became intertwined grievances.
  6. Explain the Iconoclastic Fury of 1566, the policies of the duke of Alba, and the beginning of armed rebellion in Holland and Zeeland in 1572.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did religious difference lead to sustained civil war in France when earlier edicts had tried to manage it, and what changed between the 1560s and the 1580s in the nature of the conflict?
  2. How did dynastic weakness, noble faction, and religious allegiance reinforce one another after the death of Henry II in 1559?
  3. What does the evidence suggest about who decided to kill the Huguenot leaders in August 1572, and why did the killing spread beyond them?
  4. Why did the Dutch Revolt begin as resistance to a policy rather than as a demand for independence, and how did that aim change?
  5. How did the political structures of the Netherlands, including provincial estates, privileged cities, and noble stadholders, shape both the possibilities and the limits of rebellion?
  6. What role did money, mutiny, geography, and foreign intervention play in determining whether Habsburg or royal authority could be restored?
  7. How did participants in these conflicts justify resistance to a ruler, and how did the Catholic League and the Huguenots use similar arguments for opposite purposes?
  8. In what ways did the French and Dutch settlements establish limited coexistence without establishing religious equality, and what does the difference between them reveal about the relationship between religion and sovereignty?

Historical Context

By 1559 the western Church had been divided for four decades. Luther's challenge, examined in Chapter 62, had produced Lutheran territorial churches in much of the Holy Roman Empire and Scandinavia. The Reformed movement associated with Zwingli and above all John Calvin, examined in Chapter 63, had by the 1550s built a distinctive model in Geneva: congregations governed by pastors and lay elders, a rigorous discipline, an emphasis on preaching and the Psalms, and a missionary program that trained ministers and sent them, along with printed books, into France and the Low Countries. Chapter 65 examined the Catholic response, culminating in the Council of Trent, which clarified doctrine and discipline but did not by itself resolve the political problem of how Catholic rulers should treat subjects who adhered to the new churches. England, as Chapter 64 explained, had by 1559 settled on a Protestant established church under Elizabeth I, creating a Protestant power across the Channel that would repeatedly become involved in French and Dutch affairs.

The French and Dutch conflicts also unfolded within a broader dynastic context. The Habsburg and Valois dynasties had fought one another over Italy and the Burgundian inheritance for most of the previous sixty years, and the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis of April 1559 ended that struggle. Peace had two immediate consequences. It left both the French and Spanish crowns heavily in debt, with Philip II declaring bankruptcy on repeated occasions, and it demobilized soldiers and noble clients who had depended on war for income and patronage. At the same moment, in July 1559, Henry II of France died of a wound received in a tournament held to celebrate the peace, leaving a widow, Catherine de' Medici, and a succession of young sons. In the same year Philip II, who had succeeded his father Charles V in the Netherlands in 1555, returned permanently to Spain. Both realms thus entered the 1560s with dynastic authority weakened or at a distance, and with elites who had reasons to compete for influence.

The kingdom of France was one of the largest and most populous states in Europe, with a population commonly estimated in the range of sixteen to twenty million, though no precise count exists. It was governed not as a uniform state but as a composite of provinces with distinct customary law, provincial estates in some regions, sovereign law courts called parlements, and a corps of venal officeholders who owned their positions and therefore enjoyed considerable independence. The crown had acquired important control over the French church through the Concordat of Bologna of 1516, which gave the king the power to nominate bishops and abbots. That arrangement strengthened royal patronage and reduced the monarchy's incentive to break with Rome, but it also meant that religious policy was inseparable from royal prestige.

The Netherlands were a very different political space. Chapter 54 examined the Burgundian state from which the Habsburg Netherlands descended. By the 1540s Charles V had gathered roughly seventeen provinces, including duchies, counties, a margraviate, and lordships, under a single ruler and, through the Pragmatic Sanction of 1549, under a single line of succession. Each province retained its own institutions and privileges, its own estates, and its own relationship to the prince. The region was among the most urbanized in Europe, with a population commonly estimated at about three million, and it contained commercial and financial centers of European importance, above all Antwerp. Its people spoke varieties of Dutch, French, and German, and it contained no common identity that could simply be mobilized as a nation. The Crown valued the Netherlands highly, and the burden of taxation and defense that Philip II placed upon it after 1559 was a major source of grievance.

Students should also note the variety of evidence on which this chapter rests and its limits. Royal edicts, parlementary registers, and the decisions of provincial estates show what authorities decided but not how decisions were received. Reformed synod and consistory records reveal the structure and discipline of Protestant communities but seldom the views of the many Catholics who lived beside them. Narrative chronicles, memoirs, and martyrologies are rich in detail and often written years afterward by partisans. Dutch histories written in the seventeenth century, in particular, were composed by authors who knew the outcome and shaped their accounts accordingly. Numbers of dead, of Protestants, and of refugees nearly always come from interested sources and must be treated as estimates. Where this chapter gives figures it will identify them as such.

Section 66.1

The French Monarchy and the Religious Crisis

The Valois monarchy that confronted the Reformation was at once powerful and constrained. Francis I (r. 1515–1547) and Henry II (r. 1547–1559) had expanded royal revenues, fought costly wars in Italy, and cultivated an image of sacral kingship, in which the king was anointed at Reims and credited with healing powers. But the monarchy governed through intermediaries. Provincial governors, usually great noblemen, commanded troops and carried the king's authority into regions they also dominated through clientage. The parlements, of which the Parlement of Paris was the most important, registered royal legislation and could delay it by remonstrance. Venal officeholders, who had purchased their positions, were difficult to remove. In the south customary law gave way to Roman law, and several provinces, including Languedoc and Brittany, retained estates that bargained over taxes. Later historians would label the seventeenth century monarchy "absolutist," a contested term examined in Chapter 76, but sixteenth century French kings ruled by negotiation as much as command.

Religion was woven into this system. The Concordat of Bologna of 1516 allowed the king to nominate bishops and abbots, giving him an enormous reservoir of patronage and a strong interest in remaining in communion with Rome. French clerical and legal tradition, often called Gallicanism, asserted the liberties of the French church against papal claims while remaining Catholic. Heresy was treated as a crime against both God and the king. After the Affair of the Placards in October 1534, when anti-Catholic posters appeared in Paris and even near the royal bedchamber, royal policy hardened. Henry II established a special chamber of the Parlement of Paris to try heresy cases in 1547, and the Edict of Châteaubriant (1551) and the Edict of Écouen (1559) prescribed severe penalties. Yet enforcement was uneven, and the number of those who adhered to the new churches grew despite persecution. When the Wars of Religion began, the crown had a strong tradition of persecuting heresy but no established method for living with a significant dissenting minority.

Section 66.2

The Growth of French Protestantism and the Huguenots

Evangelical ideas had circulated in France since the 1520s, encouraged for a time by humanist reformers and by the king's sister Marguerite of Navarre. But the movement that became decisive was Calvinist. From the 1540s, Geneva became a training center: pastors educated there were sent into France, often at great personal risk, and Genevan presses produced Bibles, psalters, catechisms, and polemic in French in large quantities. Congregations that had met in secret began, in the later 1550s, to organize openly as "dressed" churches with ministers and elders. In May 1559, delegates from congregations met in Paris at the first national synod, adopted a confession of faith and a church discipline, and established the structure that would characterize French Reformed Protestantism: local consistories, regional colloquies and synods, and a national synod. This representative structure, as later sections will show, was to be adapted for political and military purposes.

The social base of French Protestantism was broader than the stereotype of urban artisans suggests. It included printers, merchants, lawyers, physicians, and artisans in towns, and, in the early 1560s, a significant proportion of the nobility, among them women who often led the way within their households and who protected congregations on their estates. The conversion of Jeanne d'Albret, queen of Navarre, and the adherence of the Bourbon prince Louis of Condé and the Châtillon brothers, including Admiral Coligny, gave the movement high political visibility. Geographically, Protestants were concentrated in the west and southwest, in Normandy, Poitou, Saintonge, and Guyenne, in Languedoc and the Rhône valley, and in Dauphiné, and were comparatively few in Brittany and in much of the north and east. Paris contained a significant Protestant minority but was overwhelmingly Catholic. Contemporary Reformed leaders claimed thousands of churches and very large numbers of adherents in the early 1560s; modern historians treat such claims as probably exaggerated, though they agree that Protestants may have formed something like a tenth of the population at their peak, with great regional variation. The term "Huguenot," of uncertain origin, was used first by opponents.

Historical Vocabulary
Huguenot

Huguenot was the name given to French Reformed Protestants, especially from about 1560. Its etymology is disputed, and it began as a hostile label that Protestants themselves generally avoided, preferring to call themselves "those of the religion," or "the Reformed." Historians use it as a convenient shorthand, but it conceals variety: Huguenots included nobles, townspeople, and rural communities, with different degrees of commitment and different attitudes toward armed resistance. It also conceals movement, since many people drifted into and out of Protestant allegiance, particularly after 1572. It matters to this chapter because the Huguenots were not a separate people or a foreign body but French subjects, many of them loyal to the monarchy, whose existence raised a new problem: whether a kingdom could contain two churches.

Section 66.3

Catherine de' Medici, the Guise, the Bourbons, and the Crisis of Royal Authority

Henry II's death in July 1559 brought to the throne his fifteen year old son Francis II, whose wife, Mary Stuart, was niece to the Guise brothers: Francis, duke of Guise, a celebrated soldier, and Charles, cardinal of Lorraine, a powerful churchman. The Guise dominated the court, to the resentment of the princes of the blood, notably Antoine of Bourbon, king of Navarre by marriage, and his younger brother Louis, prince of Condé, who considered themselves entitled to a larger role, and of the Montmorency family, whose head, Anne de Montmorency, was constable of France. The Châtillon brothers, nephews of Montmorency, were Gaspard de Coligny, admiral, and François d'Andelot. A conspiracy by minor noblemen in March 1560, known as the Conspiracy of Amboise, aimed to seize the king and remove the Guise from power. Its leaders were executed, and Condé was implicated, but the incident showed how quickly court rivalry and religious allegiance could merge.

Francis II died in December 1560, and the throne passed to his ten year old brother Charles IX. His mother, Catherine de' Medici, secured the regency, sidelining Antoine of Bourbon with the title of lieutenant general. Catherine, the daughter of a Florentine banking and ruling family, had been underestimated at court and attacked by opponents as a foreigner; in the following years she pursued a policy of balance, trying to prevent any faction from monopolizing the king. With her chancellor, Michel de L'Hôpital, she sought a route to civil peace through limited toleration, on the premise that a king's subjects could be united in obedience even if they were divided in worship. This position, later labeled that of the politiques, was shared by relatively few at first. The Estates General that met at Orléans and Pontoise in 1560 and 1561 revealed a wide range of views on religion, finance, and royal authority, and the crown's financial position, with debts left by the Italian wars, limited its choices. The regency made royal authority a prize, and in that struggle religious allegiance became a marker of political loyalty, even though many Protestants and Catholics did not fit neatly into either camp.

Section 66.4

Failed Accommodation and the Massacre of Wassy, 1562

In September and October 1561, at Poissy, Catherine convened a colloquy between Catholic bishops and Reformed ministers, among them Calvin's lieutenant Theodore Beza. The hope that theologians might find a basis for a shared national church was unrealistic, and the meeting broke down above all over the nature of Christ's presence in the Eucharist. The crown then turned to law. In January 1562, by an edict sometimes called the Edict of Saint-Germain, Catherine and L'Hôpital permitted Protestants to worship publicly outside walled towns and in private within them, on condition that they gave up churches they had seized and did not bear arms. This was the first official recognition of Protestant worship in France. The Parlement of Paris accepted it only after resistance, and militant Catholics were appalled. In the spring of 1561 the duke of Guise, the constable Montmorency, and the marshal Saint-André had already formed an informal alliance, later called the Triumvirate, that stood for Catholic firmness.

On 1 March 1562, Guise passed through Wassy with armed retainers, and a clash with a Reformed congregation worshipping in a barn within the town walls, contrary to the edict's terms, left dozens of Protestants dead and many wounded. Whether Guise planned the attack or whether it arose from an altercation is disputed, but its meaning was clear to contemporaries. Protestant leaders published accounts of a massacre; Condé left the court and in April seized Orléans as a Protestant base, declaring that the king was a prisoner of the Guise. Both sides claimed to be defending the crown. Weeks after Wassy, Protestants seized dozens of towns in the south and west, and in many places Catholics responded with violence of their own. By the spring, the kingdom was in a state of armed confrontation that the January edict could no longer contain.

Section 66.5

The First French War of Religion, 1562–1563

The armies of the first war were largely assembled by great noblemen from their clients and by hiring foreign troops: Swiss pikemen for the Catholic and royal side, German cavalry known as reiters and landsknecht infantry for both. Huguenot leaders obtained funds and troops from German Protestant princes and, through the Treaty of Hampton Court of September 1562, an English garrison in Le Havre, which Elizabeth I hoped to exchange for Calais, lost to France in 1558. Protestants seized and held a number of towns, including Orléans, Lyon, and Rouen, and in some of them Protestant crowds stripped churches of images. Catholics responded in kind. In Toulouse in May 1562, street fighting and organized killing left several thousand Protestants dead according to contemporary reports, though the numbers are uncertain. The war was thus fought as both a campaign between armies and a series of local struggles for control of towns.

The war's major engagements were inconclusive and costly. Royal forces besieged and took Rouen in October 1562; Antoine of Bourbon was fatally wounded in the siege. At Dreux on 19 December, the royal army and Huguenots fought a pitched battle in which both Condé and the constable Montmorency were captured and the marshal Saint-André killed. In February 1563, as the duke of Guise besieged Orléans, he was shot by a Protestant nobleman, Jean de Poltrot de Méré, and died. The killing began a blood feud between the Guise and Coligny, whom the Guise held responsible, that would shape later events. Catherine used the deaths of the leading commanders to impose a peace. The Edict of Amboise of March 1563 allowed Protestant worship in the households of great noblemen with rights of high justice and in one town in each judicial district, restricted elsewhere, and was weaker than the January edict. It satisfied few people. The royal army, joined by Huguenot forces, retook Le Havre from the English in July 1563, and in August 1563 Charles IX was declared of age. The settlement had demonstrated that the crown could stop the fighting but could not yet persuade either party that it had been protected.

Division II: France, Massacre, Catholic League, and the Struggle for Settlement, 1563–1598
Section 66.6

Renewed Civil Wars and the Failure of Religious Peace, 1567–1570

Between 1563 and 1567 the crown tried to rebuild unity. Charles IX and Catherine toured the kingdom from 1564 to 1566, and in 1565 Catherine and the king's sister Elisabeth, queen of Spain, met the duke of Alba at Bayonne. The details of those talks are poorly documented, but Protestants suspected a Catholic conspiracy, a suspicion reinforced in 1567 when Spanish troops marched toward the Netherlands along the eastern French frontier and the French crown hired Swiss troops. In September 1567 Huguenot leaders attempted the Surprise of Meaux, a failed attempt to seize the king, and the second civil war began. After an indecisive battle near Saint-Denis in November, in which Montmorency was killed, the Peace of Longjumeau in March 1568 restored the settlement of Amboise. It was soon broken.

The third war, from 1568 to 1570, was longer and more bitter. Coligny and Condé fled to La Rochelle, which became the principal Huguenot base and a haven for Protestant ships; Jeanne d'Albret joined them with her fifteen year old son, Henry of Navarre. Catholic forces under the king's brother Henry, duke of Anjou, defeated the Huguenots at Jarnac in March 1569, where Condé was killed, and at Moncontour in October. Yet Coligny's army marched across southern France and, in June 1570, fought a successful engagement at Arnay-le-Duc, forcing a negotiated settlement. The Peace of Saint-Germain-en-Laye of August 1570 was the most generous yet: it guaranteed liberty of conscience, allowed Protestant worship in two towns per province and in noble households, restored property and offices, and gave Protestants four places of security (La Rochelle, Montauban, Cognac, and La Charité) for two years. Fiscal exhaustion, not mutual trust, had brought it about, and the Huguenots, who were now holding fortified towns, were seen by many Catholics as a state within the state.

Section 66.7

The Saint Bartholomew's Day Massacres, 1572

After 1570 Admiral Coligny gained influence over the young Charles IX, and he urged the king to intervene against Spain in the Netherlands, where Louis of Nassau, with French support, captured Mons in May 1572. That policy alarmed Catherine, who feared a war with Philip II, and Catholic leaders, including the Guise, who held Coligny responsible for the death of the duke of Guise in 1563. The marriage of the king's sister Marguerite of Valois to Henry of Navarre, celebrated at Notre-Dame on 18 August 1572, brought thousands of Protestant noblemen to Paris, a city heavily Catholic, suffering from economic hardship and already inflamed by preaching. On 22 August, a gunman fired from a house linked to Guise clients at Coligny as he walked from the Louvre; the admiral was wounded but survived. Protestant nobles demanded justice, and many Parisians feared that the Huguenots might take it themselves.

What followed is among the most debated episodes of the century. On 23 August, in a meeting at the Louvre involving the king, Catherine, the duke of Anjou, and other advisers, the crown decided that the Huguenot leaders should be killed. The evidence for who proposed the decision and with what intent is indirect, and historians have long disagreed about whether Catherine, the Guise, Anjou, or the king bore the greatest responsibility. The older view saw a premeditated plot; most modern scholarship sees a decision made quickly, in a context of fear, after the failed attack. In the early hours of 24 August, the feast of Saint Bartholomew, the duke of Guise's men killed Coligny and threw his body from a window; soldiers killed other Huguenot leaders in their lodgings. Henry of Navarre and the prince of Condé were spared after agreeing to attend Mass. The violence then slipped beyond the court's control. Paris militias and crowds killed Protestants of all ranks, men, women, and children, in the streets and houses over several days. Estimates of the dead in Paris range from about two thousand to three thousand.

Over the following weeks, massacres occurred in many provincial cities, including Meaux, Orléans, Lyon, Rouen, Bourges, Toulouse, and Bordeaux, where local authorities and crowds acted on their own initiative or in response to royal letters whose wording was ambiguous. Some governors and officials refused to allow killings, and many Protestants were saved by Catholic neighbors. The death toll across the kingdom is uncertain, with modern estimates ranging from a few thousand to ten thousand or more. The killings have been interpreted in different ways. Natalie Zemon Davis argued that Catholic crowds acted within a culture in which violence against heretics could appear a ritual of religious purification, while Denis Crouzet has emphasized the role of religious anxiety and apocalyptic expectation, and other historians stress the role of local rivalries and factional leadership. The crown first explained that the killings arose from a quarrel between the Guise and Coligny, and later that it had forestalled a Huguenot conspiracy. Pope Gregory XIII marked the event with a celebration, and Protestant Europe reacted with horror. The consequences for France were lasting: the Huguenots lost many of their noble leaders, a number of Protestants converted or conformed, and those who remained concluded that a royal promise of protection was not enough.

Section 66.8

Huguenot Survival, Fortified Cities, and Continued Resistance

After the massacres, Huguenot strength shifted from the court to the fortified towns of the west and south. La Rochelle, a prosperous port with a tradition of civic autonomy, closed its gates, and the crown's army under the duke of Anjou besieged it from February to July 1573. The siege failed, in part because of the city's determined defense and the army's heavy losses, and it ended when Anjou was elected king of Poland, a crown he accepted with little enthusiasm. The Edict of Boulogne of July 1573 gave limited rights of worship to La Rochelle, Montauban, and Nîmes. The survival of these towns, with their garrisons, municipal councils, and artisan and merchant elites, was central to Huguenot survival, and it gave the movement a territorial base that no previous heterodox movement in France had held.

Between late 1573 and 1575 the Huguenots in the south and west built a political organization to coordinate defense, assembling at Millau and Montauban. These assemblies set up regional councils, appointed military commanders, raised taxes, and in effect created a federation of provinces and towns sometimes described as the "Huguenot republic" or the United Provinces of the South. Historians continue to debate how far this organization amounted to a distinct state; it never claimed independence and always acknowledged the king, but it did claim to protect its own members against royal officials. Along with these assemblies, Protestant writers such as François Hotman and Theodore Beza began after 1572 to develop theories of resistance, discussed in Section 66.28. Charles IX died in May 1574, and his brother, who as Henry III left Poland secretly to return to France, took the throne amid a fifth civil war in which Protestants allied with discontented Catholics around the king's youngest brother, the duke of Alençon.

Section 66.9

The Catholic League and the Radicalization of French Politics

The alliance of Protestants and moderate Catholics who opposed the crown's policies, known as the politiques or "malcontents," shaped the Peace of Monsieur, also called the Edict of Beaulieu, of May 1576. It was the most favorable settlement Protestants had yet received: public worship in all places except Paris and its surrounding area and the court; special chambers in the parlements with Protestant members; rehabilitation of the victims of 1572; and eight places of security. For many Catholics it was intolerable. In 1576 local Catholic associations formed in northern provinces, notably around Péronne, among nobles and clergy supporters of the Guise, and they soon took the name Catholic League. Their programs sought to restore the full Catholic faith, protect provincial liberties, and, in some versions, defend the king against evil advisers.

Historical Vocabulary
Politiques

Politiques was a term, at first pejorative, applied by militant Catholics to those who seemed to place the political needs of the state above religious uniformity. It described a range of people, including jurists, royal officials, and some noblemen such as the Montmorency family, who believed that civil peace required a measure of toleration. Politiques were generally Catholic and did not necessarily favor religious pluralism for its own sake; their argument was that the monarchy could not survive if it were identified with one faction. The word is not interchangeable with "moderate" or "secular." It matters for this chapter because the politiques supplied the arguments and, after 1589, many of the officials that allowed Henry IV to rebuild royal authority.

Henry III, who wished to prevent the League from becoming an independent force, attended the Estates General at Blois in 1576 and declared himself the League's head. The Estates refused to fund a war on the Protestants and the crown could not afford to continue the peace; a sixth war followed, and the Peace of Bergerac of 1577 and the Edict of Poitiers narrowed the concessions. The following years brought periodic violence and negotiations, including the Treaty of Nérac in 1579 and a brief seventh war in 1580. Henry III also sought to strengthen the monarchy's religious credentials through penitential confraternities and the founding of the Order of the Holy Spirit in 1578. Critics who saw luxury at court and heavy taxes saw an unworthy king. By the early 1580s the League's early focus on faith had taken on elements of political opposition: the question was no longer only whether Protestants could worship but who could rule.

Section 66.10

The War of the Three Henrys and the Collapse of Valois Authority, 1584–1589

In June 1584 the death of the duke of Anjou (formerly Alençon), the last surviving brother of the childless Henry III, made Henry of Navarre, a Protestant, the heir presumptive under the French law of succession that excluded women and was, by the sixteenth century, increasingly cited as a fundamental law of the kingdom. The Catholic League revived on a much larger scale under the leadership of Henry, duke of Guise, son of the murdered duke. In December 1584, at Joinville, the Guise concluded a treaty with Philip II of Spain, which promised Spanish funds and recognized the aged Cardinal Charles de Bourbon as heir. In July 1585, by the Treaty of Nemours, Henry III, unable to resist, revoked the edicts of toleration and joined the war against the Huguenots. In the same year Pope Sixtus V excommunicated Henry of Navarre. The conflict, in which Henry III, Henry of Navarre, and Henry of Guise each led a party, came to be called the War of the Three Henrys.

Navarre defeated a royal army at Coutras in October 1587, while Guise defeated German Protestant mercenaries later that year. In Paris, the League drew strength from a network of neighborhood committees of the "Sixteen," preachers, and members of confraternities who regarded the king as insufficiently committed. On 12 May 1588, when Henry III sent troops into the city, Parisians raised barricades in the Day of the Barricades, and the king withdrew from his capital. He reached a humiliating accommodation in July, the Edict of Union, and summoned the Estates General to Blois, where the League dominated. On 23 December 1588, with the Estates still sitting, the king had Guise murdered, and the next day his brother, the cardinal of Guise, was killed. The League responded with open revolt. Cities across France repudiated the king, preachers denounced him, and the Sorbonne declared subjects released from their oath of obedience. Henry III turned to the Protestant Navarre, and in April 1589 the two kings allied. While they besieged Paris, a young Dominican friar, Jacques Clément, stabbed the king, who died on 2 August 1589. The Valois line was extinct.

Section 66.11

Henry IV, Conversion, and the Restoration of Royal Power

Dying, Henry III had acknowledged Navarre as his heir, and many royalist Catholics accepted him on condition that he would protect the Catholic religion, but some deserted. The League refused to recognize a heretic and proclaimed Cardinal de Bourbon as Charles X; when the cardinal died in 1590 it had no obvious successor. Henry IV, as Navarre now was, won victories at Arques in September 1589 and Ivry in March 1590, but he could not take Paris, which endured a famine during the siege of the summer of 1590, with deaths that contemporaries and historians estimate in the thousands or tens of thousands. Spanish troops from the Netherlands under Alexander Farnese relieved Paris in 1590 and Rouen in 1592. The League, divided between aristocratic leaders such as the duke of Mayenne, who wanted a negotiated settlement, and radical preachers and the Sixteen, who did not, held an Estates General in Paris in 1593. Spanish representatives proposed that Philip II's daughter, Isabella Clara Eugenia, be queen. The Parlement of Paris rejected the idea in June 1593, reaffirming that no foreign prince could take the French crown.

On 25 July 1593, at Saint-Denis, Henry IV abjured Protestantism and was received into the Catholic Church after consulting bishops. The conversion transformed the political situation. The stated reason for rejecting him had been removed, a truce followed, and the League began to fragment. Henry was crowned at Chartres on 27 February 1594, since Reims was in League hands, and Paris opened its gates to him on 22 March. Over the next years the king purchased the loyalty of former League leaders and cities with offices, pensions, and payments, at great cost. Pope Clement VIII granted absolution in September 1595. Henry declared war on Spain in January 1595, which shifted the conflict into a foreign war, and the Peace of Vervins of May 1598 ended it. Historians debate the sincerity of Henry's conversion; the evidence is ambiguous and the question may be unanswerable. The familiar saying that "Paris is worth a Mass" is not securely documented and should not be treated as authentic. What can be said is that the conversion was politically necessary, that many Protestants accepted it with bitterness, and that Henry continued to protect Protestant interests afterwards.

Section 66.12

The Edict of Nantes, 1598: Religious Coexistence and Its Limits

The Edict of Nantes, signed on 13 April 1598, was a long document, consisting of ninety-two general articles and additional secret or special articles and royal warrants. It declared that the memory of past troubles should be extinguished, and it recognized liberty of conscience: no one should be investigated or compelled in matters of belief. It also restored Catholic worship everywhere in the kingdom, including in places where Protestants had suppressed it, and required Protestants to pay tithes and observe Catholic holidays and marriage laws in public. Public Protestant worship was permitted in places where it had been established in 1596 and 1597, in the households of noble holders of high justice, and in two places in each judicial district, but not in Paris or its immediate surroundings, nor near the court. Protestants could hold offices, attend universities, and enter schools. Special chambers, the chambres de l'édit, were set up in several parlements, with Protestant judges, to hear cases involving Protestants and guarantee equitable justice. The crown also provided some funds for Protestant pastors.

The security provisions were as significant. The special articles permitted Protestants to retain well over a hundred places de sûreté, with garrisons partly paid by the crown, for a period of eight years, later renewed. These towns, including La Rochelle and Montauban, represented a recognition that the promise of a king might not be enough, and in the eyes of its critics a state within the state. The Parlement of Paris, which saw the edict as an infringement on the church and the crown's authority, registered it only in February 1599 after pressure from Henry. The edict was not a treaty between equals but a royal grant, revocable by the king, though it was declared perpetual and irrevocable. It was not modern religious toleration: it protected a minority by exception within a kingdom whose religion was Catholic, it denied Protestants public rights in many places, and it did not recognize a right to religious difference as such. Yet in a period when most European rulers sought confessional unity, it established an institutional framework for coexistence. Chapter 76 will examine how it was eroded and finally revoked by Louis XIV in 1685.

Division III: The Netherlands, Habsburg Rule, Religious Change, and Rebellion, c. 1550–1572
Section 66.13

The Habsburg Netherlands: Seventeen Provinces and Provincial Privileges

The Netherlands that Philip II inherited in 1555 was a creation of dynastic marriage, inheritance, and conquest. As Chapter 54 explained, the Valois dukes of Burgundy had assembled many of its territories in the fifteenth century; the Habsburgs inherited them through the marriage of Mary of Burgundy to Maximilian of Austria, and Charles V, born in Ghent in 1500, added further provinces, including Utrecht, Overijssel, Groningen, and Guelders. By the Pragmatic Sanction of 1549 the provinces were to descend together. In practice this union remained loose. Each province, whether duchy such as Brabant, county such as Flanders or Holland, or lordship such as Friesland, kept its own estates, law, and privileges, which were often confirmed by the prince at his ceremonial entry. Central institutions in Brussels, including the Council of State, the Privy Council, and the Council of Finance, were staffed by councillors and noble courtiers, and a governor general, or regent, acted for the absent ruler. Provincial governors, called stadholders, were great noblemen: William of Orange held Holland, Zeeland, and Utrecht, and Lamoral, count of Egmont, held Flanders and Artois.

The region's economy was among the most dynamic in Europe. The southern provinces, with Antwerp at their center, were rich in textile industries, finance, and international trade, and Antwerp served as an Atlantic and Baltic entrepôt and a center for credit on which the Habsburg monarchy itself depended. Northern provinces, particularly Holland, relied on shipping, fishing, and the Baltic grain trade, with Amsterdam rising as a carrier. Towns were politically powerful, governed by oligarchies and guilds jealous of their rights. The population of roughly three million spoke varieties of Dutch, French (including Walloon dialects), and German. Charles V, who was born among them and spoke French, was accepted as a native prince; Philip, raised in Castile, was not. The Habsburg Netherlands were an important source of revenue for the Spanish monarchy, which Chapter 75 examines, and the Crown's growing demands for taxes and for the maintenance of troops, together with a reorganization of church governance, would soon come into conflict with provincial privileges and noble expectations.

Section 66.14

Religious Reform in the Netherlands

The Netherlands had been a center of Erasmian humanism and lay devotion, and the first Lutheran ideas arrived by the early 1520s through Antwerp's trading and printing networks. Charles V responded with severity. He issued edicts, known as placards, that outlawed the new teaching, and he established inquisitorial machinery under imperial and episcopal authority; the first Lutherans were burned in Brussels in 1523. Anabaptist movements spread in the 1530s, including the radical episode in Münster, which brought the movement into disrepute with authorities, and a more pacifist branch led by Menno Simons, whose followers became the Mennonites. Well over a thousand people were executed for heresy in the Netherlands between the 1520s and the 1560s, according to estimates that vary. Persecution did not eliminate dissent but drove it underground, and the severity of the placards also created reluctance among city magistrates, who feared riot and economic disruption and sometimes refrained from enforcing them.

From the 1540s Calvinism entered from France and through exile churches in London, Emden, and elsewhere. It was strongest in the French speaking south, in towns such as Tournai and Valenciennes, and in the commercial cities of Flanders and Brabant, and gradually in Holland and Zeeland. Reformed ministers built secret congregations organized under consistories, an arrangement called "under the cross," and in 1561 Guido de Brès, a pastor in the south, wrote a confession of faith later known as the Belgic Confession. Calvinists were a minority, concentrated among merchants, craftsmen, and some nobles, and the majority of the population remained Catholic. Many Catholics, including magistrates and nobles, were uncomfortable with persecution without being sympathetic to Protestantism. This large middle group would prove decisive in 1566. Between the 1540s and the 1560s, then, the Netherlands were neither a Protestant nation nor a uniformly Catholic one, but a region in which several religious communities coexisted uneasily in the same towns, under laws that none of them could fully accept.

Section 66.15

Philip II, Centralization, and Opposition to Religious Persecution

When Philip II left for Spain in 1559, he appointed his half sister Margaret of Parma regent and relied on a small council led by Antoine Perrenot de Granvelle, later Cardinal Granvelle, archbishop of Mechelen. Philip's policy rested on his conviction that the Netherlands must remain Catholic and loyal, and on the pressing need for revenue. A major reorganization of the Netherlands church, authorized by the pope in 1559 and carried out from 1561, increased the number of bishoprics from four to eighteen, with the aim of improving pastoral care and enforcing orthodoxy. The new bishops were to be supported by the revenues of wealthy abbeys, which alarmed the nobles who held abbey lands and also the provincial estates, which feared that the bishops would serve as inquisitors. Granvelle was identified with the plan, and with the presence of Spanish troops, withdrawn only in 1561. The leading nobles, Orange, Egmont, and Hoorne, who held seats on the Council of State but felt excluded from real decisions, forced Granvelle's recall in 1564.

The conflict deepened in 1565, when Philip, writing from Spain, ordered strict enforcement of the heresy laws. A confederation of lesser nobles, both Catholic and Protestant, formed the Compromise of the Nobles, which pledged them to resist the introduction of the Inquisition. In April 1566, several hundred nobles marched to Brussels to present a petition to Margaret, requesting that the heresy edicts be suspended and the Estates General be summoned. According to a later and perhaps embellished account, a councillor dismissed them as "beggars," and the petitioners took the name Geuzen, or Beggars, as a badge of defiance. Margaret agreed to a "moderation" that softened the placards, but she lacked authority to go further. The movement, then, began as a political petition against religious persecution and the threat to privileges, made by loyal subjects who professed their devotion to the king, rather than as a rebellion against monarchy.

Historical Vocabulary
Beeldenstorm and Geuzen

Beeldenstorm is the Dutch term for the Iconoclastic Fury, the wave of attacks on religious images, altars, and church furnishings that spread through the Netherlands in August and September 1566. Geuzen (singular geus, French gueux) means "beggars," a name adopted by opposition nobles in 1566 and later by the armed exiles, the Wood Beggars and Sea Beggars. Neither term should be mistaken for a single organization or program. The Beeldenstorm was carried out by many groups, most of them local, with varied motives, while the Geuzen name covered nobles, exiles, privateers, and eventually a symbol of resistance. Both matter because they show how a political protest became a movement with its own identity.

Section 66.16

The Iconoclastic Fury of 1566

In the summer of 1566 the Calvinist congregations, encouraged by the petition and by Margaret's moderation, moved from secret meetings to open-air preaching in fields outside the towns, called hedge sermons. These gatherings, which drew thousands and were often armed, defied the heresy laws without being suppressed. Meanwhile, the Netherlands were suffering from a hard winter and high grain prices, which affected the poor, and from disruptions to the Baltic and English trade that hurt cloth workers in Flanders. On 10 August, a crowd entered churches near Steenvoorde and began destroying images. In the following days, the disorders spread to Antwerp, where much of the cathedral and other churches were stripped on 20 August, to Ghent, Utrecht, Middelburg, Delft, Leiden, and many smaller towns. In some places the iconoclasts were organized bands of artisans and workers who acted with discipline, removing objects but sparing persons; in others, local crowds acted spontaneously or in response to preaching. The destruction was extensive but, as historians have emphasized, generally selective, aimed at images and altars rather than at people.

Why did the movement spread so rapidly? Contemporary Catholics blamed the Calvinist leaders, and Calvinist ministers often denied responsibility. Modern historians have found that it combined religious conviction, in the belief that images were idolatrous and must be removed, with social and economic distress, and with the failure of magistrates to intervene, in part because they feared for their own safety and were unsure of their authority after the petition. The Fury produced a crisis in government. In August, Margaret agreed to permit Reformed preaching where it had already been established, and she depended on Orange and others to restore order in towns such as Antwerp. Her concessions were soon reversed, and by the spring of 1567 she had regained control, besieging and taking Valenciennes. For Philip II, however, the Fury was decisive proof of the failure of the regent's policy of accommodation. Many nobles who had signed the petition were shocked by the destruction and retreated into loyalty, while thousands of Protestants fled abroad. The movement had shown both the strength of Calvinism in the cities and the weakness of the Crown's hold on the Netherlands.

Section 66.17

The Duke of Alba, Repression, and the Council of Troubles

Philip II decided to send an army. Fernando Álvarez de Toledo, duke of Alba, arrived in Brussels in August 1567 with an army of about ten thousand veterans marched from Italy along the "Spanish Road," and Margaret, who objected to Alba's powers, resigned. Alba established a special tribunal, the Council of Troubles, soon called by its victims the Council of Blood, to try those involved in the disorders of 1566. Thousands of persons were summoned or prosecuted, and well over a thousand were executed, although counts vary. Many more fled, with estimates reaching tens of thousands, and their property was confiscated. In June 1568, the counts of Egmont and Hoorne, both Catholic noblemen and knights of the Golden Fleece who had served the Crown, were beheaded in Brussels. Their executions shocked a political class that had believed rank offered protection, and the Crown's seizure of the estates of William of Orange, who had fled to his German lands, further alarmed the nobility.

Alba also tried to restructure the Netherlands' finances and government. He built citadels, including one at Antwerp, to hold the towns, and he proposed taxes, including a hundredth penny on property, a twentieth on land sales, and a tenth penny on sales of goods, that were intended to provide permanent revenue independent of provincial consent. The estates, which controlled taxation by privilege, resisted fiercely, and the tenth penny was implemented only in 1572, with disastrous effects on trade. Meanwhile, exiles and Orange tried to invade. Louis of Nassau defeated a royal force at Heiligerlee in May 1568, but he was crushed at Jemmingen in July, and Orange's campaign in the autumn of 1568 failed when his unpaid mercenaries dispersed. In the short term Alba's repression worked. In the longer term it united Catholics and Protestants against the Crown, alienated the nobility and the cities, and provided the rebels with a cause, a leadership, and a movement of exiles who would form armed bands.

Section 66.18

William of Orange and the Beginning of the Dutch Revolt

William of Orange, born in 1533 into a German Lutheran comital family, had inherited the principality of Orange in southern France at the age of eleven and was educated at the court of Charles V, as a Catholic. A wealthy landholder in the Netherlands, he served as stadholder and councillor, and he emerged as the leading critic of Philip's religious policy, although, as of 1566, he was cautious, opposed to iconoclasm, and personally inclined to religious accommodation. In 1567 he left for Germany to avoid taking a loyalty oath, and in 1568, after his estates were seized, he began to organize armed resistance and published a justification of his conduct. His early campaigns failed. The first successes of the rebellion were not those of his army but of the Sea Beggars, exiled Netherlanders operating as privateers from English ports. In March 1572, Elizabeth I, under pressure from Spain, expelled them from England; on 1 April, a Sea Beggar fleet under William de la Marck seized the small port of Brielle. The Beggars took Flushing days later, and town after town in Holland and Zeeland declared for Orange, driven by hostility to Alba's taxes and soldiers as much as by religion.

In July 1572, the States of Holland, meeting at Dordrecht, recognized Orange as the king's stadholder and authorized taxes to pay for the war, in effect creating a rebel government in the name of the king and privileges. The following months were precarious. In May, Louis of Nassau captured Mons with French Huguenot support, but after the Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres in August the French support collapsed, and Alba recaptured Mons in September. Alba's forces then retook Mechelen, Zutphen, and Naarden, whose inhabitants were massacred in November 1572. The rebellion survived in Holland and Zeeland, where the terrain and the control of water and of ports limited Spanish operations. Orange publicly joined the Reformed church in 1573. The revolt had now become a war in which the parties were bound together by the logic of survival: the rebel towns knew that surrender meant reprisal, and the Crown had committed itself to restoring authority.

Cause and Effect

The Outbreak of the Dutch Revolt

Long-term causes

A composite state of provinces with entrenched privileges, ruled by an increasingly distant Habsburg prince; a wealthy and urbanized society whose elites expected consultation; a tradition of severe heresy laws, unevenly enforced; and the growth of Calvinist congregations among townspeople and some nobles.

Short-term causes

Philip's departure in 1559, the reorganization of the bishoprics, noble exclusion from decision making, the demand for stricter enforcement in 1565, harvest failure and trade disruption in 1565 and 1566, and the collapse of authority during the Iconoclastic Fury.

Event or transformation

Alba's repression and tax demands of 1567 to 1572 turned a protest by loyal subjects into armed rebellion, and the capture of Brielle in 1572 created a territorial base in Holland and Zeeland.

Immediate effects

Exile and displacement of thousands, the formation of a rebel government in the States of Holland, the beginning of siege warfare, and the intervention of foreign powers on the rebels' side.

Long-term consequences

A war lasting until 1648, the division of the provinces into a Catholic south under Habsburg rule and a northern republic, and the emergence of a state founded on provincial sovereignty.

Historical complexity: Religion, taxation, noble status, and provincial privilege were different grievances held by different groups. Many rebels were Catholic, and many Calvinists were initially loyal to the king. The revolt cannot be reduced to a single cause, and its outcome was not determined by its beginning.

Section 66.19

Siege Warfare, Urban Resistance, and the Defense of Holland

The war in Holland was above all a war of sieges. The towns of Holland, ringed by walls and surrounded by water, were difficult to take by assault, and the Spanish army, an experienced force that relied on pikemen, arquebusiers, and cavalry, found its advantages reduced by marsh, canals, and dikes. Haarlem held out from December 1572 until July 1573, when, short of food and with its relief repeatedly defeated, it surrendered on terms that Alba's forces did not entirely honor; the garrison was massacred. Alkmaar, which resisted in the autumn of 1573 and opened its sluices, forced the Spanish to withdraw. A later tradition celebrates the heroism of Haarlem's women, but the evidence for particular stories, such as that of Kenau Simonsdochter Hasselaer, is thin and largely later. What is well documented is that citizens built fortifications, served in militias, and bore the costs of the sieges, and that the rebels' control of the sea and of inland waterways gave them an advantage in supply.

Alba, whose methods had failed to end the revolt, was replaced late in 1573 by Luis de Requesens. The siege of Leiden, which began in earnest in the spring of 1574, became the decisive test. As food ran out and disease spread, the city's population, which suffered many thousands of deaths from famine and plague, hung on while Orange and the States of Holland cut dikes to flood the countryside so that a relief fleet of shallow boats could approach. On 3 October 1574, after a change of wind, the Spanish withdrew, and the city was relieved. The anniversary became an annual festival, and in 1575 Leiden received a university, founded by Orange as a reward and as a Protestant institution. The sieges demonstrated that the Spanish army, despite its reputation, could not take the towns of Holland without enormous cost, and that a population determined to resist could use the landscape as a weapon. They also showed the extraordinary suffering of civilians, since inundation meant the destruction of farmland and livelihoods.

Section 66.20

Spanish Military Crisis, the Spanish Fury, and the Pacification of Ghent, 1576

The Spanish monarchy's power to wage war depended on credit from bankers, and in September 1575 Philip II suspended payments, declaring another bankruptcy. The army in the Netherlands, owed months or years of wages, began to mutiny, a regular response of early modern armies that were not paid. In March 1576 Requesens died suddenly, leaving the government in the hands of the Council of State, which was composed of Netherlanders. Mutineers sacked Aalst in July, and on 4 November 1576 Spanish soldiers stormed Antwerp, killing thousands of inhabitants, burning houses, and plundering the wealthiest commercial city in Europe, in an event known as the Spanish Fury. Contemporary pamphlets and engravings spread the news throughout Europe, and later estimates of the dead, often put at several thousand, remain uncertain. The Fury gave the revolt a moral weight that no argument could have provided.

Four days later, on 8 November 1576, representatives of the provinces, including Catholic Flanders and Brabant as well as Calvinist Holland and Zeeland, concluded the Pacification of Ghent. They agreed to cooperate in expelling the Spanish troops, to suspend the heresy edicts pending a decision by the States General, and to cease hostilities between Holland and Zeeland and the other provinces. For a moment, nearly all the Netherlands was united against the Crown's soldiers, a unity William of Orange worked hard to preserve. The new governor, Don John of Austria, Philip's half brother, accepted the Pacification in the Perpetual Edict of February 1577, but he renewed war in July by seizing Namur. The States General turned to Archduke Matthias of Austria, and in Brussels Orange became the effective leader. The unity proved fragile. The Pacification had bound Catholics and Protestants against a common enemy but had not resolved religious difference, and in several southern cities radical Calvinists took power, alarming Catholic nobles. After the defeat of the States' army at Gembloux in January 1578 and the death of Don John in October, the Crown had a new commander in Alexander Farnese, duke of Parma, who exploited these divisions.

Section 66.21

The Unions of Arras and Utrecht, 1579

Between 1577 and 1579 the question of religion tore apart the provincial alliance. In Ghent, Brussels, and other southern towns, radical Calvinists seized power, expelling Catholic clergy and magistrates; in Flanders and Brabant, a movement for religious peace, supported by Orange, failed to hold. The Catholic nobles of the southern provinces, known as Malcontents, feared social upheaval and Calvinist dominance, and they were open to reconciliation with Philip if their privileges were respected. In January 1579, the representatives of Hainaut, Artois, and the city of Douai formed the Union of Arras, committing themselves to the Catholic religion and to reconciliation with the king; Farnese then negotiated the Treaty of Arras in May, agreeing to withdraw foreign troops. This did not mean that all Catholics supported Spain or that the south was uniformly loyal; it meant that a group of provinces judged that their interests were better protected by reconciliation.

Days later, on 23 January 1579, representatives of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, and the province of Guelders with Zutphen, together with Groningen and the surrounding Ommelanden, concluded the Union of Utrecht. It was intended as a military alliance to continue the war, and it provided for common defense and taxation. It left the regulation of religion to individual provinces, and its thirteenth article asserted that each person should remain free in matters of religion and that no one should be investigated on account of religion. Friesland and Overijssel joined in 1580, and several cities in Flanders and Brabant, including Ghent and Antwerp, joined for a time. The result was not a division along lines of language or religion, as Dutch and Belgian national histories later suggested. Both unions contained Catholics and Protestants, and the line between them shifted over the next decade as Farnese reconquered the south. The Union of Utrecht, which later became the constitutional foundation of the republic, was not intended as the creation of a new state but as a wartime alliance of provinces still nominally loyal to the king.

Section 66.22

The Act of Abjuration, 1581, and the Rejection of Philip II

In March 1580, Philip II issued a ban against William of Orange, declaring him an outlaw and offering a reward for his death. Orange responded with the Apology, a printed defense of his conduct, which was presented to the States General in December 1580 and widely published. The States General, meeting at The Hague, had meanwhile been seeking a new sovereign who could secure foreign support: they negotiated with Francis, duke of Anjou (formerly Alençon), the brother of the French king. On 26 July 1581 they issued the Act of Abjuration, which declared that Philip II had forfeited his sovereignty over the provinces. It justified the decision on the ground that he had violated the privileges and rights of the provinces and had governed as a tyrant rather than as a prince, whose duty was to protect his subjects as a shepherd protects his sheep. It argued that when a prince abandons that duty, subjects are free to choose another.

The Act was a landmark in European political argument, but its limits should be clear. It was issued not by a nation but by the States General, representing the provinces that had joined the Union and other cities, and its authors framed it as a defense of ancient privileges rather than as an assertion of popular sovereignty in the modern sense. The practical problem was that no European monarch was prepared to accept the sovereignty the Dutch offered on terms they could accept. Anjou was inaugurated in 1582 as duke of Brabant, but his attempt in January 1583 to seize Antwerp by force, the "French Fury," failed, and he left. Orange, who had been wounded in an assassination attempt in 1582, held the republic together by his personal authority. In 1584 Anjou died, and in July Orange was assassinated at Delft by Balthasar Gérard, a Catholic acting on Philip's ban. The States then offered sovereignty to Henry III of France and, in 1585, to Elizabeth I of England, both of whom declined. By default, sovereignty came to rest with the provincial states, and this solution to a crisis of legitimacy shaped the republic's constitution.

Section 66.23

Alexander Farnese, the Southern Reconquest, and the Fall of Antwerp, 1585

Alexander Farnese, duke of Parma and son of Margaret of Parma, was a gifted commander and negotiator who combined siege warfare with generous terms. After taking Maastricht in 1579, he reconquered the southern provinces city by city, offering amnesty and the right to return to Catholicism to those who submitted, and allowing Protestants a period of years to settle their affairs and leave. Ypres, Bruges, and Ghent fell in 1584; Brussels followed in March 1585. The great prize was Antwerp. With Orange's death in July 1584 and the divisions in the city, it was besieged for more than a year, during which Farnese built a floating bridge across the Scheldt to cut its supply and Dutch efforts to relieve it failed. The city surrendered on 17 August 1585 on terms that gave Protestants four years to leave.

The fall of Antwerp, together with the closing of the Scheldt by the Zeelanders, which cut the city off from the sea, was a turning point for the economic geography of the Low Countries. Antwerp's population, which may have been around a hundred thousand in the 1560s, declined sharply, with estimates of a fall to about half that by the 1590s. Thousands of merchants, artisans, and skilled workers went north to Amsterdam, Middelburg, Leiden, and Haarlem, or abroad to Germany and England. The consequences were important: the migration brought capital, skills, and commercial networks to the north, which contributed to its rise, though historians continue to debate how large that contribution was relative to the north's existing strengths. The Catholic south, under the control of Farnese and then the Habsburg archdukes, entered a period of religious renewal in the Catholic Reformation, discussed in Chapter 65, while the northern provinces, reduced in territory, became more clearly the center of the rebellion. By 1585 the future division of the Netherlands was starting to take shape, but it was the result of military reconquest, not of any preexisting boundary.

Section 66.24

The Internationalization of the Dutch War

From the outset the revolt had involved foreign powers: German Protestant princes, French Huguenots, English privateers, and the duke of Anjou. After the fall of Antwerp and the assassination of Orange, the Dutch faced collapse and sought English help. By the Treaty of Nonsuch in August 1585, Elizabeth I agreed to send an army of about six thousand foot and one thousand horse, with the Dutch paying for its upkeep, and to receive the towns of Flushing and Brill as security (the cautionary towns). She declined sovereignty, but her favorite, the earl of Leicester, was appointed governor general in December. Leicester's tenure was a failure. He quarreled with the Holland regents, who resented his support for Calvinist radicals and for his attempts at centralization, and he was unable to achieve military success. By late 1587 he had left. Yet English involvement had committed Elizabeth to the war and raised Philip's determination to deal with England, which led to the Armada of 1588, a subject examined elsewhere in the context of English and Habsburg politics.

The war in the Netherlands was inseparable from the French war. Philip's agreement with the Guise in 1584 committed him to supporting the League, and from 1590 Farnese was ordered to take his army into France to relieve Paris and Rouen. Each campaign deprived the Netherlands front of men and money, and the Dutch used the opportunity to recover territory. Henry IV, who needed Dutch and English support, joined Elizabeth and the States General in a triple alliance in 1596. Spain's finances were also under strain, and Philip declared further bankruptcies, including one in 1596. The Dutch cause thus benefited from the wider European conflict, and in particular from the fact that the French civil war and Philip's decision to intervene in it made it impossible for Spain to concentrate its resources on the northern provinces. This connection illustrates the chapter's central comparative point: the French and Dutch conflicts were not isolated national episodes but parts of one European confessional and dynastic struggle.

Section 66.25

The Formation of the Dutch Republic

The republic that took shape in the 1580s and 1590s was not designed in advance. Its governing principle was that sovereignty resided in the provincial estates, each of which was dominated by town governments and, in some provinces, by the nobility. The States General, meeting in The Hague, was a gathering of delegates from the provinces, voting by province, who coordinated defense, finance, and foreign policy but could not command the provinces. The wealthiest, Holland, paid more than half of the common expenses and thus exercised weight beyond its single vote. Town councils, the vroedschappen, were self-perpetuating oligarchies of merchants and officeholders, called regents. The stadholders, a title taken from the days of Habsburg rule, were provincial governors and military commanders: Maurice of Nassau, Orange's son, took the post in Holland and Zeeland in 1585, and others later. The senior official of Holland, the Land's Advocate, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, appointed in 1586, managed much of the republic's politics. The result was a federal and decentralized system in which compromise among provinces and cities was constantly necessary.

Religious arrangements were equally distinctive. The Reformed Church became the "public church": it had exclusive right to use church buildings, its ministers were paid by public funds, and it received the support of officials. Yet it was not a state church in the sense of requiring membership. Many inhabitants, probably a majority at first and still a substantial proportion later, remained Catholic, and Catholic public worship was forbidden, though priests were often tolerated in practice and Catholics met in hidden churches. Mennonites and other groups were permitted to practice, subject to restrictions and sometimes payments, and Jews, including Sephardic families of Portuguese origin who began to settle in Amsterdam in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth century, were allowed to worship, although they were excluded from many privileges. The republic's practice, rooted in the thirteenth article of the Union of Utrecht, was one of limited, locally variable coexistence, not of equality. The tensions within the Reformed Church over predestination, which would erupt in the next decade, and the republic's later political development are discussed in Chapter 77.

Section 66.26

The Twelve Years' Truce of 1609 and the Long Road to Independence

In the 1590s Maurice of Nassau, working with his cousin William Louis, stadholder of Friesland, reorganized the army, drawing on classical models and emphasizing drill, discipline, and siegecraft. Between 1590 and 1594 the republic's forces took Breda, Zutphen, Deventer, Nijmegen, Geertruidenberg, and Groningen, and secured the northern and eastern frontiers. The victory at Nieuwpoort in 1600 showed that the army could defeat Spanish troops in the field, though the long siege of Ostend, which Spain took in 1604 after three years, demonstrated the cost of offensive warfare. In the south, Philip II ceded the Netherlands in 1598 to his daughter Isabella and her husband, Archduke Albert, who ruled as sovereigns with the expectation that the territory would revert to Spain if they had no heirs. Spain, burdened by debts and by war with France, England, and the Dutch, was bankrupt again in 1596 and 1607.

After secret negotiations and a ceasefire in 1607, the Twelve Years' Truce was signed in April 1609. Spain agreed to treat the provinces as free states over which it made no claim during the truce, an arrangement that fell short of formal recognition but amounted to acceptance of de facto independence. The Dutch did not secure Catholic toleration in the south or the lifting of the closure of the Scheldt, and the question of Dutch trade in the East Indies was left unsettled, as Chapter 71 will examine. The truce divided Dutch opinion: Maurice and many Calvinists wanted to continue the war, while Oldenbarnevelt and the merchants of Holland valued peace. War resumed in 1621, in the context of the Thirty Years' War, and Spain formally recognized the independence of the United Provinces at the Peace of Münster in 1648, which Chapter 67 examines. The principal narrative of the revolt closes here, with a republic that existed in fact, was recognized only provisionally, and still faced Spain and its own internal tensions.

Section 66.27

Religious Violence and the Experience of War

The violence of these wars took many forms, and distinguishing them is essential to understanding their effects. There were pitched battles between noble armies, such as Dreux and Coutras, and prolonged sieges, such as La Rochelle, Leiden, and Antwerp. There were massacres of civilians by crowds and by soldiers: Wassy, Toulouse, Paris and the provincial cities in 1572, Naarden, and the Spanish Fury. There was iconoclasm, which attacked objects rather than persons but was understood by Catholics as a violation of the sacred. There were also quieter forms of violence, such as judicial execution, forced conversion, and expulsion. Historians who study these events have emphasized that much of the violence was local, committed by neighbors, confraternity members, militias, and officeholders who knew their victims, and that participants often understood their actions in religious terms, as defense of the community, purification, or retribution. At the same time, violence was shaped by political decisions: royal letters, noble leadership, and the demands of war shaped when and where killing occurred.

The war's material consequences were severe. Armies lived off the land, and soldiers, whether Swiss, German, Spanish, or French, plundered, billeted, and demanded payment from the peasants, whose fields were wrecked and animals seized. Sieges brought famine and disease: the Paris siege of 1590 and the siege of Leiden are well known, but many smaller towns suffered similarly. Taxation increased, and governments borrowed heavily. Commerce suffered: Antwerp's decline is the best known case, but trade in French provinces was also disrupted, and in the Netherlands the inundation of land cost farmers dearly. Refugees moved in large numbers. Protestants left France for Geneva, England, and Germany, though many later returned; thousands left the southern Netherlands for the north, Germany, and England. The exiles carried with them printed texts, commercial contacts, and religious networks that sustained the conflicts. Printing was crucial to the representation of the wars: pamphlets, engravings, martyrologies, and commemorative medals spread accounts of massacres, with the result that memory of Saint Bartholomew's Day, the Spanish Fury, and the relief of Leiden shaped confessional identities for generations. These memories require historians to distinguish carefully between what happened and what later communities remembered.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Women, and Local Communities in a Time of War

Religion was practiced in households as well as in churches. Reformed families read the Bible and sang psalms at home, and Catholic households maintained devotional objects and customs. In towns where Protestants and Catholics lived together, neighbors might share a street, a guild, or a business while worshipping separately, and mixed marriages, though disapproved by clergy, occurred. Women had a prominent place in the story. Noblewomen such as Jeanne d'Albret and Louise de Coligny protected congregations and organized support; Catherine de' Medici acted as regent; and women in towns took part in defense during sieges, in riots, and in the underground networks that sheltered preachers. Women were also among the victims of massacres and among the refugees whose labor supported exile communities.

Rural communities suffered the burden of soldiers and taxes, while artisans in cities faced unemployment when trade was disrupted. Children were baptized, married, and buried according to the rites of the churches to which their families belonged, and a change of religion could affect inheritance, office holding, and community standing. The crises of the 1590s, in particular, combined war with poor harvests and high prices, so that hunger and epidemic disease, as well as violence, shaped daily life. For most people, the wars were experienced not as a series of political events but as interruptions of work and worship, as threats to neighbors, and as episodes of hardship and fear.

Section 66.28

Resistance, Tyranny, Sovereignty, and Political Thought

Before 1572 most Huguenot writers insisted on loyalty to the crown and presented themselves as victims of evil advisers rather than as opponents of monarchy. The massacres changed that. In 1573 the jurist François Hotman, in Francogallia, argued that the French monarchy was historically limited by the consent of an assembly representing the realm and that the kingdom had once been elective. In 1574 Theodore Beza, in Du droit des magistrats, maintained that lesser magistrates, such as nobles and town officials, have a duty to resist a ruler who becomes a tyrant. The most influential text was the Vindiciae contra tyrannos, published in 1579 under the pseudonym Stephanus Junius Brutus; its authorship remains disputed, with Philippe Duplessis-Mornay and Hubert Languet often proposed. Drawing on biblical covenant theory, Roman law, and constitutional history, it argued that kings are bound by a covenant with God and with the people, and that when a king violates it, the representatives of the people may resist. These arguments circulated throughout Protestant Europe and were used by Dutch writers.

The same logic could be used by Catholics. After the Guise murders of 1588 and the repudiation of Henry III by the League, preachers and writers such as Jean Boucher argued that a king who protected heretics or acted as a tyrant could be deposed, and in some League writings, killed. Jacques Clément's murder of Henry III was celebrated by some Leaguers. Royalists, in turn, argued for obedience and the indivisibility of royal authority. Jean Bodin, a jurist and royal official, in his Six livres de la république of 1576, defined sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of making law within a commonwealth, which could not be divided or shared. His response to civil war was a theory of royal sovereignty, though he also accepted that rulers were bound by divine and natural law. Politiques such as L'Hôpital and others argued that peace required toleration of difference for the sake of the state. Dutch arguments, including Orange's Apology and the Act of Abjuration, combined appeals to ancient privileges, the idea of a contract between prince and subjects, and defense of conscience. They did not adopt a theory of popular sovereignty in the modern sense; sovereignty was understood to rest with the provincial estates as the representatives of the people.

These debates did not produce a single doctrine. They contained competing ideas about whether sovereignty was indivisible or limited, whether it resided in the king, in the people, or in representatives, and whether religion was a matter of state or conscience. Their historical importance lies in showing that religious conflict forced people to ask who has the right to command and when obedience may be withdrawn, questions that Chapter 83 will take up through Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau and that Chapter 77 will take up in connection with the English constitutional revolutions and the Dutch Republic.

It reveals the importance of the "lesser magistrate" argument, which gave resistance a legal and constitutional foundation and distinguished it from rebellion by private persons. It does not tell historians how many people accepted such ideas or how they were used in practice, and its learned, Latin audience was small. Its argument was soon turned to other purposes by Catholic writers, which shows that its significance lay in its method more than in its confessional alignment.

Historical Debate

Religious Conviction, Political Conflict, or Both?

The question: To what extent were the French Wars of Religion and the Dutch Revolt caused by religious conviction rather than by dynastic, noble, fiscal, or constitutional conflict?

Interpretation A: Religion as a primary motivating force. Some historians, including Denis Crouzet in studies of French violence, emphasize that participants were moved by deeply held beliefs about sacred purity, idolatry, and salvation, and that these convictions explain the intensity of the violence, the willingness to kill neighbors, and the persistence of the wars. In this view, treating religion as a mask for political interest underestimates what people said and did.
Interpretation B: Political and social conflict as the underlying engine. Others argue that noble faction, dynastic crisis, royal debt, provincial privileges, and competition for office and patronage explain why religious disagreement turned into war. In France the rivalry of the Guise, Bourbons, and Montmorency, and in the Netherlands the defense of privileges and resistance to taxation, supported conflicts in which religion was a way of organizing opposition.

Evidence: Supporters of A point to the language of sermons, pamphlets, and testimony, the ritual character of some violence, and the persistence of divisions after political interests converged. Supporters of B point to the fact that alliances crossed confessional lines (Catholic nobles joined Orange; politique Catholics allied with Huguenots), that grievances over taxes and privileges were important, and that settlements were negotiated in political terms. Many scholars today argue for interaction: religion gave grievances intensity and moral meaning, while political structures determined who could mobilize, who had armies, and what settlements were possible.

Why it matters: If religion was primary, the wars appear to be conflicts about belief that could end only through exhaustion or coexistence; if politics was primary, they appear as struggles over the structure of authority. The answer shapes how historians interpret the Edict of Nantes, the Dutch republic, and the claim that religious pluralism is a product of secular statecraft.

Student task: Select one episode from this chapter, such as Wassy, the Iconoclastic Fury, or the Catholic League, and identify evidence that supports each interpretation. Then explain which combination you find most persuasive and what additional evidence would help.

Section 66.29

Comparing the French and Dutch Settlements

Why did France end the wars with a restored Catholic monarchy and a limited Protestant minority while the northern Netherlands became a republic with a privileged Reformed public church? Several factors help explain the difference, though none is sufficient alone. First, the structure of royal legitimacy differed. In France the dynasty and the principle of succession, defined in the late sixteenth century as a fundamental law, provided a focus that the League could not replace, and the extinction of the Valois line did not end the monarchy but passed it to a legitimate heir whose conversion removed the central objection. In the Netherlands the sovereign was an absentee foreign king who could not be replaced by another prince and whose deposition forced the rebels to locate sovereignty elsewhere. Second, demography and geography mattered. Protestants were a minority in France, roughly a tenth of the population, and could hold regional strongholds but could not hope to dominate the kingdom, while in the northern Netherlands, the maritime and urban provinces of Holland and Zeeland, which were more favorable to the rebels and defended by water, could be held against Spain.

Third, political institutions differed. The Netherlands had a developed network of provincial estates, urban oligarchies, and noble stadholders who controlled taxation and military resources and who could organize an alternative government. France had parlements, estates, and governors, but no comparable body could claim to rule in the king's place, and the League's attempt to do so depended on Spain, whose intervention discredited it. Fourth, foreign resources and strategic constraints mattered: Spain fought on several fronts, was repeatedly bankrupt, and could not suppress both the Dutch and the French wars, while England, German princes, and France itself supplied the Dutch with help. Finally, leadership and contingency played a part, in the assassinations of Guise, Henry III, and Orange, and in the political skills of Henry IV, Orange, Maurice, and Oldenbarnevelt.

The settlements also had much in common. Both recognized that the use of force had failed to restore religious unity, and both institutionalized limited coexistence. In neither was the minority equal to the majority: the Edict of Nantes privileged Catholicism and restricted Protestant worship, while the Dutch Republic privileged the Reformed Church and restricted Catholic worship. Both were pragmatic arrangements, dependent on local officials and on political circumstances, and both would be tested in the following century. Neither should be described as modern religious freedom. What they illustrate is that early modern coexistence emerged from exhaustion, political calculation, and negotiation, not from a principle of toleration accepted by all. The relationship between religion and sovereignty was settled differently: in France, the crown reasserted itself as the single source of law and protection, a development that Chapter 76 examines; in the Netherlands, sovereignty was lodged in provincial estates. Both outcomes would shape the European state system examined in Chapters 74 through 80, and the broader history of religious pluralism that Chapter 140 will synthesize.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Two Settlements: France in 1598 and the Netherlands in 1609

What Changed

  • The French crown, once the unquestioned guardian of a single Catholic kingdom, accepted a legally defined Protestant minority with security towns and special courts
  • The Habsburg Netherlands divided into a Catholic south under the archdukes and a northern union that governed itself without a prince
  • Political language shifted: arguments about resistance, contract, and sovereignty entered public debate and the vocabulary of rulers and subjects
  • Economic geography altered, with Antwerp's decline and the rise of Amsterdam and other northern cities

What Stayed the Same

  • Both societies remained religiously mixed, with Catholics and Protestants living in the same towns and regions
  • Local elites, nobles, officeholders, and urban oligarchies continued to dominate government
  • Confessional hierarchies persisted: one church was privileged in each settlement, and members of other churches were limited in public worship and, often, in office
  • Dynastic and international rivalries, especially between the Bourbons, the Habsburgs, and the Dutch, remained central

Who experienced change most directly? Protestants in France, who gained legal recognition but remained a minority under suspicion; Catholics in the north of the Netherlands, who lost public worship; and refugees and merchants in Antwerp and elsewhere. Who experienced greater continuity? Peasants and artisans whose daily work and obligations changed less than the political order. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that settlements transformed some structures while leaving others in place, and that coexistence was fragile and partial.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two linked conflicts in which religious division, dynastic crisis, and disputes over political authority produced prolonged war. Its central question was why religious conflict in France produced a renewed Catholic monarchy with limited Protestant protections, while rebellion in the Netherlands contributed to the emergence of an independent republic. The answer lies in the interaction of religion with the structures of authority in each society, not in religion alone.

In France, the death of Henry II in 1559 left the Valois monarchy in the hands of young kings and a regent, Catherine de' Medici, while the Guise, Bourbons, and Montmorency competed for influence. The rapid growth of Calvinist congregations, organized by consistories and synods and supported by many noble families, created a Protestant minority that the monarchy could neither suppress nor accommodate. The Edict of January 1562 and the violence at Wassy marked the failure of royal attempts to manage religious difference, and the first civil war began.

The wars of the 1560s were fought largely by noble armies, with foreign mercenaries, and ended in edicts that granted limited worship. After the Peace of Saint-Germain in 1570, Admiral Coligny's influence at court seemed to promise reconciliation, but the Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres of 1572 destroyed that prospect. The killing began as a decision at court to eliminate Huguenot leaders and spread through urban violence to provincial cities, with an uncertain toll of thousands. Its consequences included the loss of Huguenot leaders, the development of Protestant political organization in the south, and new theories of resistance.

From 1576 the Catholic League transformed the conflict. Formed to resist concessions to Protestants, it became after 1584 a movement that challenged the legitimacy of a Protestant heir and, after the Day of the Barricades and the murders of the Guise in 1588, of the king himself. The assassination of Henry III in 1589 ended the Valois line and made Henry of Navarre king in law but not in fact. Henry IV's military victories, the discrediting of the League through its dependence on Spain, his conversion in 1593, and the political value of the French succession principle together restored royal authority. The Edict of Nantes of 1598 granted Protestants liberty of conscience, limited public worship, equal access to office, special courts, and security towns, within a kingdom whose religion remained Catholic.

In the Netherlands, Philip II inherited a composite state of seventeen provinces with strong privileges, wealthy cities, and a politically powerful nobility. His efforts to strengthen the Catholic Church, enforce the heresy laws, and raise revenue provoked a petition by nobles in 1566, hedge preaching, and the Iconoclastic Fury. Alba's army, the Council of Troubles, the executions of Egmont and Hoorne, and the tax demands of 1569 to 1572 turned protest into rebellion. The capture of Brielle in April 1572 and the recognition of Orange by the States of Holland created a territorial base for revolt.

The war that followed was dominated by sieges, in which the control of cities and waterways favored the rebels. The sieges of Haarlem and Leiden demonstrated both Spanish power and its limits. Spain's bankruptcies and unpaid armies brought mutiny and the Spanish Fury of 1576, which briefly united the provinces in the Pacification of Ghent. That unity proved short lived. Religious radicalism in Flemish and Brabant cities, Catholic fears, and Farnese's diplomacy produced the Unions of Arras and Utrecht in 1579. The Act of Abjuration of 1581 deposed Philip II, but the search for a new sovereign failed, and sovereignty came to reside in the provincial estates.

Orange's assassination in 1584 and the fall of Antwerp in 1585 nearly ended the revolt. English intervention under the Treaty of Nonsuch, Spain's diversion of resources to the Armada and to France, and the military reforms of Maurice of Nassau allowed the northern provinces to survive and expand. The republic that emerged had a federal structure, with the States General, provincial estates, town oligarchies, stadholders, and a Land's Advocate. Its public church was Reformed, while Catholics, Mennonites, Jews, and others lived under varied degrees of tolerance and restriction. The Twelve Years' Truce of 1609 recognized in practice what Spain would acknowledge in law only in 1648.

The two conflicts were connected: Huguenots aided the Dutch until 1572, Spanish intervention in France after 1589 relieved pressure on the Netherlands, and Henry IV, Elizabeth I, and the Dutch allied in 1596. Both also produced a body of political argument. Huguenot, League, and Dutch writers developed theories about tyranny, covenant, resistance, and representation, while jurists such as Bodin responded with theories of indivisible sovereignty. These debates shaped later European political thought.

The experience of war also mattered. Violence took many forms, from massacre by neighbors and iconoclastic riot to siege, famine, plunder, and judicial execution. Households, women, refugees, and communities carried the burdens of conflict, and printed propaganda and commemoration shaped memory. Neither settlement should be called modern toleration. Both limited coexistence and preserved a privileged church, and both depended on political circumstances and local enforcement.

Finally, the chapter has stressed contingency. The Valois line might have survived, Orange might have lived, Spain might have been solvent, and the Dutch revolt might have ended in compromise. The French monarchy that emerged in 1598 and the Dutch Republic of 1609 were results of contested events and choices. Chapter 67 will show how these struggles became part of a wider European conflict, culminating in the Thirty Years' War and the settlement of 1648.

Key Terms

Huguenot
A French Reformed Protestant, a term at first used by opponents and later widely adopted by historians.
Politiques
Catholic officials and jurists who held that the survival of the state and civil peace should take priority over religious uniformity.
Catholic League
A movement of Catholic nobles, clergy, and townspeople, formed in 1576 and revived in 1584, that opposed concessions to Protestants and the succession of a Protestant king.
Gallicanism
The tradition that the French church and crown had liberties against papal authority while remaining within the Catholic Church.
Parlement
A sovereign law court in France, the most important being at Paris, which registered royal edicts and could resist them through remonstrance.
Concordat of Bologna
The 1516 agreement between Francis I and the papacy that gave the king the right to nominate bishops and abbots.
Consistory
The governing council of a Reformed congregation, made up of the pastor and lay elders, who enforced discipline.
Edict of Nantes
The royal edict of 13 April 1598 granting French Protestants liberty of conscience, limited public worship, civil rights, and security towns.
Places de sûreté
Fortified towns held by Protestants under the Edict of Nantes as guarantees of the settlement, partly garrisoned at royal expense.
Chambres de l'édit
Special chambers in French parlements with Protestant judges, established to hear cases involving Protestants.
Saint Bartholomew's Day massacres
The killings of Protestants beginning in Paris on 24 August 1572 and spreading to provincial cities.
Salic law (succession principle)
The rule, increasingly cited as a fundamental law of France, that excluded women and their descendants from the throne.
Seventeen Provinces
The collection of duchies, counties, and lordships ruled by Charles V and Philip II in the Low Countries, each with its own privileges.
Privileges
Legal rights and customs of provinces, towns, and corporate bodies that rulers swore to respect.
Stadholder
A provincial governor representing the sovereign, later a leading military and political office in the Dutch Republic.
Placards
Edicts issued by Charles V and Philip II prescribing penalties for heresy in the Netherlands.
Compromise of the Nobles
The 1565 to 1566 confederation of Netherlandish nobles who petitioned against the Inquisition and the heresy laws.
Beeldenstorm
The Iconoclastic Fury of August and September 1566, in which religious images were destroyed in many Netherlandish towns.
Geuzen (Beggars)
A name adopted by the rebellious nobles in 1566 and then by armed groups, including the Sea Beggars.
Council of Troubles
Alba's tribunal, established in 1567, which tried those implicated in the disorders and rebellion.
Spanish Road
The military supply corridor by which Spanish troops moved from Italy to the Netherlands.
Spanish Fury
The sack of Antwerp by mutinous Spanish soldiers on 4 November 1576.
Pacification of Ghent
The agreement of 8 November 1576 uniting most provinces against the Spanish troops and suspending the heresy edicts.
Union of Utrecht
The 1579 military alliance of northern provinces that became the constitutional basis of the Dutch Republic.
Act of Abjuration
The 1581 declaration by which the States General renounced obedience to Philip II.
States General
The assembly of delegates from the provinces, which coordinated defense, finance, and foreign policy.
Regents
The oligarchs who governed Dutch towns and provinces through urban councils and officeholding.
Public church
The Dutch Reformed Church, privileged in worship and funding without requiring membership of all inhabitants.
Cautionary towns
Dutch towns, including Flushing and Brill, held by England as security for repayment under the Treaty of Nonsuch.
Twelve Years' Truce
The 1609 suspension of hostilities between Spain and the United Provinces, treating the republic as free in practice.
Lesser magistrate
In resistance theory, an official such as a noble or town magistrate with authority to oppose a tyrant, unlike a private individual.
Sovereignty
In Bodin's definition, the absolute and perpetual power of making law in a commonwealth.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how religious division, once it entered the political structures of two great societies, shaped the future of the European state system. In France and the Netherlands, the questions of who could worship, who could rule, and by what right were joined, and the answers that emerged differed because the societies' institutions, dynasties, geographies, and international circumstances differed.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Confessional conflict stimulated state building, since wars required armies, taxes, and administrative capacity; it produced new forms of political argument about contract, resistance, and sovereignty; and it forced rulers and communities to search for ways to live with difference without accepting it as a principle. The settlements of 1598 and 1609 were limited, but they showed that coexistence was possible even where unity was not.

Later developments built on these outcomes. The Bourbon monarchy examined in Chapter 76 consolidated the royal authority restored by Henry IV and eventually revoked the Edict of Nantes. The Dutch Republic, examined further in Chapter 77, developed commercial and maritime power, discussed in Chapter 71, and its political arguments reappeared in later debates. The wars also fed into the Thirty Years' War in Chapter 67, in which the same confessional and dynastic rivalries operated on a larger scale.

Within Volume III, Chapter 66 marks the moment when the religious upheaval of the Reformation became inseparable from the problems of the early modern state. It shows that Europe's confessional division was not only a matter of theology but of power, and that its resolution required new arrangements between rulers and ruled. Students should carry forward the habit of treating religion and politics as interacting forces, and of resisting the temptation to read later outcomes back into the sixteenth century.
