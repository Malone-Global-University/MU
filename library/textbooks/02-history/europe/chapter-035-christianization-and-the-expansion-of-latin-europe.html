Christianization and the Expansion of Latin Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A king's baptism can be dated to a day. The Christianization of a kingdom cannot. It required bishops who stayed, churches that were built and maintained, clergy who could read and write, and households that slowly changed how they buried their dead, married, fasted, and named their children.

In the Danish town of Jelling, in central Jutland, two large carved runestones still stand beside a church. The larger of them was raised in the later tenth century by King Harald, later remembered as Bluetooth, in memory of his parents. Its inscription announces that Harald won Denmark for himself and made the Danes Christian, and its carved face shows a figure of Christ, the earliest known image of Christ in Scandinavia. The stone is an extraordinary document, but it is a royal statement, not a census of belief. It tells us what a king wished to be remembered for. It does not tell us what a farmer on Funen, a trader at Hedeby, or a household in the Danish islands believed or practiced.

That gap between royal claim and lived religion defines this chapter. Within roughly a century, from the later tenth to the early eleventh century, rulers in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary accepted Latin Christianity, sponsored bishoprics and, in several regions, monasteries, and entered the diplomatic and dynastic world of Christian kings. Mieszko I of the Piasts accepted baptism in 966. Stephen of Hungary was crowned king around the year 1000 or 1001. The Icelandic assembly, the Althing, is traditionally said to have adopted Christianity around 999 or 1000. These dates are landmarks. They are not the dates on which entire peoples became Christian.

Chapter 34 ended with a warning and a handoff. Latin Christianity carried episcopal organization, monastic landholding, and close ties among church, monarchy, and aristocracy, but it did not carry a ready-made social system. Chapter 35 follows those institutions into regions mostly beyond the former Roman imperial core and, in many cases, only at the edge of the Carolingian world. It asks how rulers, missionaries, clergy, and local communities extended Latin Christianity into northern and central Europe, and how conversion reshaped political authority, institutions, literacy, and social practice without erasing older beliefs overnight.

The chapter's central argument is that Christianization was a long political, institutional, and cultural process, not a sequence of kings converting peoples. Rulers had religious and political reasons for adopting Christianity, and those reasons cannot be cleanly separated. Missionaries and bishops mattered, but so did merchants, captives, migrants, queens, and neighboring dynasties. The non-Christian societies of the north and center of the continent were not waiting passively for Christianity to arrive. Their rulers selected, negotiated, delayed, and sometimes resisted it, and they set the terms on which it was received.

The evidence is uneven, and the chapter treats it as part of the story. Contemporary monuments such as the Jelling stones, coins, cemeteries, church foundations, and a small number of charters and papal letters sit beside narrative sources written by outsiders, such as Widukind of Corvey, Thietmar of Merseburg, and Adam of Bremen, and by much later insiders, such as the authors of Icelandic and Norwegian sagas and of the Polish chronicle of Gallus Anonymus. Saints' lives, written to praise martyrs and founders, add a further layer. Each type of source answers some questions and distorts others.

This chapter also closes Part V. Chapters 29 to 33 traced the post-Roman kingdoms, the Merovingian and Carolingian worlds, the pressures of Vikings and Magyars, and the formation of durable kingdoms in England, France, and the German lands. Chapter 34 examined lordship and rural society. Chapter 35 shows the result of that long formation: a Latin Christian world reaching farther north and east than the Roman Empire ever had. At the same time, Latin Europe was never the whole of Christian Europe. Part VI begins with Byzantium and the Orthodox world, whose own missions to the Slavs belong to Chapter 38, and the account of how Latin and Greek Christendom drifted apart belongs to Chapter 40.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among a ruler's baptism, elite conversion, institutional church building, and the slower Christianization of everyday life.
  2. Explain the mechanisms of mission and conversion, including courts, marriage alliances, trade, captives, monasteries, and diplomacy.
  3. Trace the stages of Christianization in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, and Iceland, and explain why the process was prolonged and regionally uneven.
  4. Analyze how Christianity and monarchy interacted in the Piast realm, Bohemia, and Hungary without treating either as simply an instrument of the other.
  5. Describe the creation of bishoprics, archbishoprics, and monasteries and explain what church organization meant politically in each region.
  6. Evaluate the strengths and limits of chronicles, saints' lives, sagas, inscriptions, coins, and archaeological evidence for reconstructing conversion.
  7. Interpret the Jelling stone as a royal claim and identify what it can and cannot establish about popular belief.
  8. Assess competing explanations of why rulers converted and why some convert-ruling dynasties succeeded in making Christianity durable.
  9. Contextualize Latin missionary expansion alongside Byzantine and other Christian networks without projecting a fixed Catholic and Orthodox division backward.
  10. Compare the regions incorporated into Latin Christendom and explain why shared institutions did not produce uniform societies.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What counts as conversion: a baptism, a law, a bishopric, a cemetery, or a change in household practice? Why does the answer change what historians conclude?
  2. Why did rulers in different parts of northern and central Europe accept Latin Christianity, and why is it misleading to reduce those decisions to a single motive?
  3. How did conversion and the consolidation of royal power influence each other, and where did they pull in different directions?
  4. Why did Christianization proceed so differently in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary?
  5. What did it mean politically for a new realm to possess its own archbishopric, and how should historians avoid describing such churches as modern national churches?
  6. How reliable are later sagas, chronicles, and saints' lives as evidence for events of the tenth century, and how can material evidence correct or complicate them?
  7. How did ordinary communities experience conversion, and what can burial and settlement evidence reveal that royal narratives conceal?
  8. In what sense did Latin Europe expand in this period, and in what sense did it remain religiously and institutionally diverse?

Historical Context

By the middle of the tenth century, Latin Christianity was well established in the former Roman provinces of the west and in the Frankish lands east of the Rhine. Chapter 31 examined the Carolingian effort to join Christian rule, church reform, and education, and Chapters 32 and 33 traced how Viking and Magyar pressure, fragmentation, and political consolidation produced the kingdoms of England, West Francia, and the German lands. In the East Frankish and then Ottonian kingdom, kings after Otto I (r. 936 to 973) relied on bishops and abbots as administrators, landholders, and military contributors. Chapter 34 showed how that relationship between church and lordship worked in local societies. The Ottonian court therefore had both religious and political reasons to support missionary dioceses on its eastern and northern frontiers.

Beyond that frontier lay societies that had their own religious practices, political forms, and long-distance connections. In Scandinavia, communities honored gods and sacred places that later sources associate with Odin, Thor, and Freyr, and local assemblies, called things, gave political and legal weight to chieftains and kings. Among the western and northern Slavs, rulers such as the early Piasts and Přemyslids governed through fortified centers, retinues, and tribute. The Magyar confederation, which settled in the Carpathian Basin from around 895 to 896, as Chapter 32 explained, combined steppe traditions with the agricultural and political realities of its new homeland. Our knowledge of the pre-Christian religions of all these regions is limited and filtered. Most written descriptions come from Christian authors, who had reasons to caricature them, and from much later vernacular writing. Archaeology helps, but it does not recover belief directly.

These societies were not isolated. Scandinavian traders and warriors moved along the Baltic, the North Sea, the Russian rivers, and the Atlantic. Arabic silver coins reached the Baltic region in large numbers in this period, evidence of trade connecting northern and central Europe with the Islamic world through the Volga and Caspian routes. Jewish and Muslim merchants traveled in central Europe, and a Spanish Jewish traveler, Ibrahim ibn Yaʿqub, wrote a description of Slavic realms, including the realm of Mieszko, in the 960s; his original account is lost and survives in excerpts quoted by later Arabic geographers. Christian communities already existed in many trading centers. Anglo-Saxon England, itself Christianized in the seventh century, supplied missionaries and bishops to Norway and Sweden. Byzantine and Latin missionaries had worked in Moravia in the ninth century, an episode whose mission and liturgical consequences belong to Chapter 38. By the time Mieszko or Harald made their decisions, they were choosing among options, not facing a single Latin offer.

Three cautions apply throughout. First, "Latin Christendom" is a convenient description of the communities that used the Latin liturgy and recognized the authority of Rome and its bishops, not a unified institution. Second, these decades precede the Great Schism, a long process examined in Chapter 40. Latin and Byzantine Christians in the tenth century regarded themselves as parts of one Christian world, even when they competed for influence. Third, modern national categories do not fit. The Danes, Poles, Czechs, and Hungarians of the tenth century were political communities in formation, ruled by dynasties whose boundaries and loyalties shifted with each generation.

Section 35.1

What Did Christianization Mean?

The most important distinction in this chapter is between an event and a process. A ruler's baptism is an event, and sources often record it precisely. Christianization is a process, and it happened in layers. At the first layer, a ruler or a small circle of elites accepted baptism. At the second, rulers invited or permitted clergy to settle, and bishoprics were founded with defined territories and revenues. At the third, churches and monasteries were built, endowed with land, and staffed. At the fourth, Christian law and custom began to regulate marriage, burial, Sunday work, fasting, and punishment. At the fifth and slowest layer, rural and urban communities adopted Christian ritual, abandoned or transformed older practices, and began to understand themselves as Christians. Different regions reached these layers at different speeds, and the layers could overlap or move backward.

Using "conversion" for the whole process is therefore misleading. In the sources, a king might be said to have converted a people when he simply required that his court and retinue attend Christian rites. Modern historians often prefer "Christianization" for the long process and reserve "conversion" for individual or collective decisions of belief and adherence. Even then, the terms conceal the fact that belief is the hardest thing to document. A person who accepted baptism may have believed sincerely, followed a ruler's lead, hedged between rituals, or understood Christianity through local concepts. The sources rarely say which.

Reconstructing a Process from Fragmentary Evidence

Historians reconstruct Christianization using several kinds of evidence, each with a different perspective. Chronicles and annals, such as Widukind's history of the Saxons, Thietmar's chronicle, Adam of Bremen's history of the church of Hamburg, and Gallus Anonymus's later chronicle of the Polish dukes, were written by clergy with institutional interests. Saints' lives, or hagiography, celebrate missionaries and martyrs and follow literary conventions, so they must be read for what they reveal about later veneration as well as for events. Papal letters and charters record privileges and foundations but are rare for this period. Sagas, written in Iceland in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, preserve vivid accounts of Scandinavian conversion but were composed two or more centuries after the events and shaped by later concerns.

Material evidence offers a different kind of testimony. Runestones and crosses, coins stamped with Christian symbols, the foundations of early churches, and the contents of cemeteries survive without the interpretive filters of later authors. They are not simple, however. A change in burial orientation or the disappearance of weapons from graves may reflect new belief, changing fashion, social pressure, or a new definition of status. Place names and surviving ritual vocabulary can hint at sacred landscapes but are difficult to date. This chapter therefore treats Christianization as a hypothesis constructed from converging evidence, not as a story told by one reliable narrator.

Section 35.2

Missionaries, Rulers, and Political Conversion

Before examining particular regions, it helps to identify the channels through which Christianity moved. Missions sponsored by Frankish and later Ottonian rulers and bishops were one channel. Ansgar's ninth-century work in Denmark and Sweden, supported by the Carolingian court and the church of Hamburg, is an early example, and it produced chapels and a small number of converts but no durable church. A second channel was commercial. Merchants and seasonal traders from Christian lands lived in trading towns such as Hedeby and Birka, where Christian communities appeared long before any king accepted baptism. A third was captivity and migration. Christian captives, slaves, and settlers lived in non-Christian households, and Scandinavian raiders and traders who spent time in England, Ireland, Normandy, and the Frankish lands encountered Christianity on the ground.

Marriage was a fourth channel. The baptism of Mieszko I followed his marriage to Dobrawa, a daughter of the Bohemian Přemyslid ruler, and later Hungarian and Polish dynasties used marriage to bind themselves to Bavarian, imperial, and other Christian houses. Queens and noblewomen are visible in the sources as patrons of churches, relics, and clergy, although the narratives are often written to praise or blame them. A fifth channel was the monastery, which combined prayer, learning, and landholding, and which could attract aristocratic patrons who saw monasteries as places of burial, memory, and political influence. The relationship of Latin missions to Byzantine missions was often competitive but not necessarily hostile, as Chapter 38 will show.

Why Rulers Accepted Christianity

Rulers had many reasons to accept the religion and to sponsor clergy. Some may have been convinced by Christian teaching or by the experience of Christian courts and communities. Christianity offered access to literate clergy who could write letters, draft charters, administer property, and keep records. It offered a model of kingship, in which a ruler's authority was sanctified by anointing and by the ritual of the church. It offered diplomatic standing among Christian rulers, who were reluctant to treat non-Christian neighbors as full partners and who sometimes justified attacks on them as holy duty. A ruler who accepted baptism on his own terms could reduce the risk of forced conversion by an outside power. Church organization, with its fixed territories and hierarchical offices, was also a useful model for governing.

None of this means that conversion was merely cynical statecraft. Religious commitment and political advantage were intertwined, and sources written by clergy or by hostile critics rarely allow us to separate them. It is equally misleading to suppose that missionaries brought civilization to peoples without law, art, or political order. The Scandinavian, Slavic, and Magyar societies discussed here already had complex political institutions, long-distance trade, craft production, and ritual systems. What Latin Christianity added was a particular set of institutions and a written culture that, over time, transformed how those societies recorded and exercised power.

Finally, the political cost of conversion could be high. A ruler who invited bishops risked antagonizing aristocrats who resented the loss of ritual roles or the growth of royal authority, and he risked becoming dependent on the German king or emperor if his church fell under German archbishops. Rulers therefore worked to secure their own church organization, appointing clergy, endowing sees, and seeking papal or imperial recognition, as the Polish and Hungarian cases below show.

Section 35.3

Denmark and the Beginnings of Scandinavian Christianization

Denmark was the Scandinavian kingdom nearest to the Carolingian and later Ottonian world, and it was the first in which Christian kingship took visible institutional form. Contact began early. In 826, Harald Klak, a Danish ruler who had been expelled and sought Frankish support, came to Louis the Pious's court at Ingelheim and was baptized in the Mainz-Ingelheim setting, with major Frankish sources locating the rite at St Alban's in Mainz. Ansgar accompanied him on his return and began missionary work. A missionary see at Hamburg was subsequently associated with Ansgar, but its traditional foundation in 831 to 834 and its initial archiepiscopal status depend on later and partly contested evidence. These early efforts show how Frankish politics shaped mission, but they did not create a lasting Danish church. Harald Klak's own position was precarious, and Danish kings who followed him were often hostile to or indifferent toward Christianity. Hamburg itself suffered raids and was eventually combined with Bremen.

The more decisive stage occurred in the later tenth century under Harald Bluetooth. Our evidence includes the Jelling monuments, which combine a large mound, a smaller mound, a church site, and two runestones. The excavated chamber in the northern mound has been dated by dendrochronology to the late 950s, and some scholars connect the burial of Harald's father Gorm with that date, while others suggest the chamber was prepared or rebuilt later. Excavations beneath the later stone church revealed several successive wooden buildings. Recent reassessment suggests that the earliest may have been a hall rather than a church, while later wooden phases are more plausibly interpreted as church buildings. The larger runestone carries the inscription discussed in the Primary Source Window below. Written sources outside Denmark, including Widukind of Corvey, describe Harald's acceptance of Christianity in connection with the activities of a missionary named Poppo, and later authors added miracle stories. Adam of Bremen, writing in the 1070s, preserved the traditions of the church of Hamburg-Bremen, which claimed credit for converting Denmark.

What did Harald accomplish? One influential interpretation holds that he sponsored Christianity as part of a program of royal consolidation. The same decades saw the building of large circular fortresses, long bridges, and other works that suggest an unusual degree of royal organization, and the Jelling monuments themselves proclaim a dynasty. By stating that he made the Danes Christian, Harald claimed a role in religion, and the stone presents Christianity as a royal achievement. The claim, however, should not be taken as a literal description. Many Danes, especially outside the royal center, probably remained attached to older practices for generations, and Danish kings of the following decades, including Sweyn Forkbeard and Cnut, used Christian institutions and English connections to reinforce their authority. The Danish church also remained subject to disputes over its relationship with Hamburg-Bremen. Only in the early twelfth century did Denmark acquire its own archbishopric, at Lund, a development that lies beyond this chapter's frame.

Denmark's example shows several features that recur elsewhere. Sponsorship by a king did not require the immediate abandonment of older customs. Christian institutions grew along royal and trade centers first, and the church depended on royal protection. The relationship with the German church and empire was uneasy, since German bishops expected to supervise a church that Danish kings wanted to control. Bishops associated with Hamburg-Bremen appear in Danish sees such as Schleswig, Ribe, and Aarhus, although the evidence for the early date of those sees is debated.

Section 35.4

Norway: Kingship, Coercion, and Conversion

Norway's conversion is closely tied to the creation of a Norwegian kingdom, a process that was itself contested. In the tenth century, regional lords and chieftains held power in coastal districts, and royal claims were intermittent, often depending on foreign support, particularly from Denmark and England. This political fragmentation shaped the course of Christianization. A king who tried to impose Christianity risked losing the support of the assemblies and aristocrats on whom his authority rested.

Later saga tradition, particularly the thirteenth-century Heimskringla, describes Hákon the Good, raised in England at the court of King Æthelstan, as a Christian who tried to introduce his faith but retreated when regional assemblies pressured him to take part in traditional sacrifices. Whether the details are accurate is uncertain, but the story illustrates what later Norwegians believed about the limits of royal power. The kings who followed also faced competing claims, and the Danish kings of Jelling at times asserted overlordship, which linked Christianity to foreign domination in some memories.

The later saga tradition treats Olaf Tryggvason, who ruled for a brief period around 995 to 1000, as a forceful missionary king who brought Christian priests, destroyed sacred sites, and threatened or punished opponents. He died at the battle of Svolder in 1000, fighting a coalition of rivals. Olaf Haraldsson, who ruled from 1015 to 1028, is the king whose reign proved more consequential. He brought English and other clergy to Norway, sponsored churches, and promoted Christian provisions in regional law, working with assemblies and aristocratic allies. He was driven out by rivals who had Danish backing, and he was killed in 1030 at the battle of Stiklestad while trying to regain power. Within a few years, he was venerated as a saint, and his cult became central to Norwegian identity and to the authority of his descendants.

Source criticism is essential. The sagas were composed in Iceland long after these events, by authors who admired Norwegian kings and had their own political and religious aims. They present conversion as a series of confrontations between kings and defiant chieftains, and they depict force more vividly than other sources do. Contemporary skaldic verse, runic inscriptions, coin evidence, and church archaeology are fewer, but they suggest a process in which royal sponsorship, coercion in some cases, and local negotiation all played a part. The Norwegian church acquired its own archbishopric, at Nidaros, only in the twelfth century. The enduring point is that Christian kingship became part of Norwegian political consolidation, but neither process was complete or instantaneous.

Section 35.5

Sweden, Iceland, and the Long Conversion of the North

Sweden shows most clearly how prolonged Christianization could be. Missionaries reached Birka, the trading center in Lake Mälaren, in the ninth century, but their work did not last. By the late tenth century, Christian influence is visible in the coinage struck at Sigtuna under Olof Skötkonung, which carried a cross and an English-inspired design, and in the Christian graves of some elite and trading communities. Later tradition, including Adam of Bremen, remembers Olof as the first Christian king of the Svear. Church organization emerged slowly and unevenly, with sees at places such as Skara in the west, where Christianity seems to have taken hold early, and slower progress around Uppsala. Adam of Bremen describes an important non-Christian cult at Uppsala in the 1070s, though his account is secondhand and written to emphasize the church's missionary mission, so historians treat its details cautiously.

Runestones from the eleventh century in Sweden, with their prayers for the souls of the dead and their crosses, show how Christian language entered aristocratic memory, and coins and cemeteries show growth of Christian practice over generations. Yet older practices, including mound burial and offerings, did not disappear on a single date. The Swedish church acquired an archbishopric at Uppsala only in 1164, long after the first Christian kings. Sweden therefore demolishes the notion that Scandinavia was converted in a single tenth-century moment. It was Christianized by stages, and parts of the region were still being incorporated into church structures in the twelfth century.

Iceland: Conversion by Assembly

Iceland offers a different and unusually well-documented political case. Settled by Scandinavians and Celtic-speaking people in the ninth and tenth centuries, Iceland had no king, and the Althing, a general assembly, made laws and settled disputes. Ari Þorgilsson, writing around 1130 in his Íslendingabók, tells how around 999 or 1000, under pressure from Norwegian king Olaf Tryggvason and from internal division, the assembly agreed that the lawspeaker Þorgeirr, himself a non-Christian priest, should decide the matter. According to this account, Þorgeirr decided that all Icelanders would adopt Christianity, but that certain older practices, such as private sacrifice and the exposure of infants, could continue for a time. Whatever the precise details, the story shows a community deciding collectively and allowing a transition. It also shows that adopting Christianity formally did not require individuals to abandon every older custom at once.

Section 35.6

Poland: Mieszko I and the Piast Realm

In the middle of the tenth century, the Piast dynasty ruled a polity centered on fortified sites such as Gniezno and Poznań in Greater Poland. Archaeology reveals a pattern of large timber-and-earth strongholds, retinues, and tribute. Written sources are scarce. Widukind of Corvey and the Jewish traveler Ibrahim ibn Yaʿqub, whose lost account survives in excerpts preserved by later Arabic geographers, especially al-Bakri, describe a powerful ruler named Mieszko, and Thietmar of Merseburg and Gallus Anonymus preserve later traditions. Mieszko's world was shaped by pressure from the Ottonian kings and margraves on his west, by Bohemian power to the south, and by Slavic and Baltic neighbors.

In 965, Mieszko married Dobrawa, the daughter of the Bohemian ruler, and in 966 he accepted baptism. The date is well established, and later Polish tradition regarded 966 as the foundation of the Christian state. Modern historians note that baptism had immediate political consequences. It changed Mieszko's diplomatic position and facilitated participation in the political and ecclesiastical networks of Christian central Europe. Older interpretations that he converted primarily to deprive German rulers of a pretext for conquest have been substantially challenged, and current scholarship points instead to the Bohemian alliance, conflicts with neighbors such as the Veleti, Christian diplomacy, and mixed religious and political motives. In 968, a missionary bishop, Jordan, was established at Poznań, apparently with Mieszko's support and probably without a fixed territorial diocese in the later sense. The early Polish church was small, linked to the ducal court, and staffed by foreign clergy from Bohemia, Germany, and Italy.

A short document known as Dagome iudex, preserved only in a later summary, appears to record Mieszko's placing of his realm under the protection of the papacy around 991. The text is difficult and its interpretation contested, but one interpretation sees the act as an attempt to secure papal protection or strengthen ecclesiastical autonomy. The ruler's motives remain uncertain, and several competing explanations have been proposed, including protection against neighboring powers, church organization, and dynastic succession. Mieszko's reign thus shows what Christianity gave a ruler: access to literate clergy, a hierarchy that could manage territory, diplomatic legitimacy, and a means of dealing with neighboring rulers. It would be a mistake, however, to reduce the Piast embrace of Christianity to a political device. The dynasty continued to sponsor the church for generations, and its members were buried and remembered in Christian settings.

It would also be wrong to say that Poland became Christian in 966. Archaeologists find that over the tenth and eleventh centuries, burial practices in the Piast heartland shifted toward inhumation, and cemeteries near churches gradually appear. The rate and timing varied across regions, and many communities beyond the core districts were affected much later. The chronicles also record resistance. In the 1030s, after the death of Mieszko II, a period of political crisis saw uprisings that included attacks on clergy and rejection of Christian practice, and the Bohemian invasion of 1038/1039, conventionally dated to 1039, devastated Gniezno. These events show that the church was still vulnerable a lifetime after Mieszko's baptism.

Section 35.7

Bolesław, Gniezno, and the Organization of the Polish Church

Church organization was the central issue of the reign of Mieszko's son, Bolesław Chrobry (r. 992 to 1025). Bolesław expanded Piast power, and his relationship with the Ottonian emperors, the papacy, and Bohemia drew on religious as well as military means. A key figure was Adalbert (Vojtěch), the Bohemian bishop of Prague, who had left his diocese after conflict with the Bohemian ruling house and who, in 997, was killed by Prussians while on a mission along the Baltic coast. Bolesław is said to have ransomed the body and brought it to Gniezno, where Adalbert's tomb became a great pilgrimage site. The possession of a martyr's relics was a source of prestige and sanctity for the Piast realm, and for the emperor Otto III, who had known Adalbert, it was a reason to visit.

In the year 1000, Otto III traveled to Gniezno on pilgrimage. Our knowledge of what happened comes largely from Thietmar of Merseburg and from Gallus Anonymus, writing a century later, and the precise details of ceremony remain debated. What is clear is that around this time Gniezno became the seat of an archbishop, Radim Gaudentius, who was Adalbert's half-brother, and that suffragan bishoprics were created or confirmed at Kołobrzeg on the Baltic, Kraków, and Wrocław, with Poznań retaining a special place. The creation of a metropolitan province meant that the Polish church was not subordinate to a German archbishop such as Magdeburg or Mainz. It could organize its own bishops, hold synods, and be linked directly to Rome.

This had political meaning. A metropolitan church gave the Piast ruler a framework for governing through bishops and a source of prestige in dealings with the emperor. The relationship among monarchy, empire, papacy, and local church was complex, and each had its own aims. Otto III seems to have envisioned a Christian realm of cooperating rulers, with himself as the senior partner. Bolesław took advantage of this vision while pursuing his own ambitions, including, in 1025, a royal coronation shortly before his death. It would be anachronistic to call the Polish church a national church. It was a church province tied to a dynasty and its territories, whose boundaries and personnel were international, and whose clergy included many foreigners.

The fragility of this early structure should not be overlooked. The mid-eleventh-century crisis, the Bohemian sack of Gniezno in 1038/1039, and the restoration under Casimir I show that the church depended on the stability of the dynasty. Monasteries, such as the Benedictine foundations of the eleventh century, and the development of a network of churches and clergy filled out the framework over generations. By the twelfth century, Poland had a dense network of parishes and monasteries, and a hierarchy that played an important part in political life. Its origins, however, lay in the tenth-century conjunction of dynastic initiative, imperial ambition, and the cult of a martyr.

Section 35.8

Bohemia and the Přemyslid Christian Realm

Bohemia's path was tied to its geographic position between the Bavarian and Saxon lands and the Slavic regions to the east. Christian influence entered from several directions. In the ninth century, Bohemian leaders accepted baptism at the Frankish court, and the neighboring realm of Great Moravia, where the Byzantine missionaries Cyril and Methodius and Frankish clergy competed, left a mixed inheritance of liturgical and cultural traditions. That mission belongs to Chapter 38. For the present chapter, what matters is that Bohemia did not acquire a single clear ecclesiastical identity at once, and that by the tenth century its ruling dynasty, the Přemyslids, drew on both Moravian and Frankish Christian traditions while moving gradually toward alignment with the Latin church of the German kingdom.

Later tradition associates the first Christian Přemyslid ruler, Bořivoj, with baptism in the late ninth century, and his wife Ludmila with the conversion of her family and household. After Bořivoj's death, Ludmila acted as guardian of her grandson Wenceslas, and she was killed around 921, probably as part of a conflict in the ruling dynasty. Wenceslas, who ruled in the 920s and 930s, favored church-building and accommodated the Saxon king Henry I. He was killed on 28 September, traditionally dated to either 929 or 935, with 935 often preferred in modern reconstructions, according to tradition by his brother Boleslav, who succeeded him. His cult developed rapidly in the decades after his death. The surviving legends, written in Latin and Old Church Slavonic, are themselves evidence for how Bohemians remembered him. The cult served the dynasty, since it sanctified the ruling house, and it also linked Bohemia to the wider Christian world.

Under Boleslav I and Boleslav II, Bohemia acquired its own bishopric. In 973, the bishopric of Prague was created, within the church province of Mainz, and its first bishop was Thietmar, a Saxon. The second bishop, Adalbert, belonged to the elite group centered at Libice that later historiography called the Slavník dynasty. It has often been portrayed as a rival to the Přemyslids, although recent scholarship questions how coherent and independent that rival dynasty actually was. He sought to reform the church, to enforce Christian marriage law, and to challenge the slave trade. His difficult relationship with the Bohemian elite led to his departure and, after the massacre at Libice in 995, to his missionary work and death. Around 993, Adalbert and Boleslav II founded the Benedictine monastery at Břevnov near Prague, an example of elite and royal patronage of monasteries. The Bohemian church remained part of the German church province, and it was a place where Slavonic liturgical traditions lingered, as in the monastery of Sázava, founded in the 1030s.

Bohemia therefore illustrates that incorporation into Latin Christendom did not equal incorporation into the German kingdom. Bohemian dukes acknowledged the German king's overlordship, with obligations that varied over time, but they governed their own realm, and the church was bound up with their dynasty and its rivalries. Adalbert's cult connected Bohemia, Poland, and Hungary, since all three claimed him. Together with Wenceslas, he became one of the symbols of a Christian Bohemia whose Latin identity coexisted with local traditions.

Section 35.9

Hungary: From Magyar Confederation to Christian Kingdom

As Chapters 32 and 33 explained, Magyar groups settled in the Carpathian Basin around 895 to 896 and conducted raids westward until defeats such as the battle on the Lech in 955. The history of Hungarian Christianization should not be told as a simple story in which the Magyars, defeated, settled and became Christian. The Magyars were already living in the Basin, in a society that included Slavic-speaking populations, remnants of earlier Christian communities, and Turkic-speaking groups, and their political order was built around leading lineages and a ruling dynasty, the Árpáds. Christianization was part of the consolidation of one branch of that dynasty over its rivals across several generations.

Christian contacts predated Géza, who ruled from around 972 to 997. Magyar leaders had visited the Byzantine court, and at least one prominent leader, a gyula, was baptized in Constantinople. Géza invited Latin missionaries from Bavarian and other lands and arranged for his son, Vajk, who took the baptismal name Stephen (István), to marry Gisela, a daughter of the Duke of Bavaria and sister of the future emperor Henry II. The marriage connected the Árpád dynasty to a leading Christian house. Géza seems to have been a pragmatic ruler who sponsored Christianity while continuing to take part in traditional rituals, according to later accounts, and he also relied on Latin clergy and German knights.

After Géza's death, Stephen faced a challenge from a relative named Koppány, who claimed the succession according to older principles and who resisted the new dispensation. Stephen's victory around 997 to 998 secured his position, and he spent the following decades consolidating power, defeating regional rivals, and building a state. He received a crown and royal status around the year 1000 or 1001, according to later tradition with the approval of Pope Sylvester II and Emperor Otto III, although the exact circumstances are uncertain. During Stephen's reign a network of dioceses took shape, headed by Esztergom and including Veszprém, Győr, Pécs, Eger, and Kalocsa, although the precise foundation dates and the early status of several sees, especially Kalocsa, remain debated. He also sponsored monasteries, notably Pannonhalma (Martinsberg), founded around 996. The laws attributed to Stephen regulated church attendance, tithes, marriage, and Christian behavior, and, according to the surviving law, required every ten villages to build and endow a church, with the king supplying vestments and the bishop clergy and books, though the texts need careful analysis.

The church's growth, as in Poland, did not end resistance. Pagan revolts occurred in 1046, after Stephen's death, and the bishop Gellért (Gerard), a Venetian missionary, was killed in the unrest. Byzantine-influenced Christian practices persisted in some regions, and Latin liturgy did not displace them quickly. Jewish and Muslim merchants and residents appear in later sources and law, a reminder that the Hungarian kingdom was a multilingual and multireligious realm. Hungary thus joined Latin Christendom as a monarchy with its own ruling dynasty, a diocesan church, and a distinctive position between the German kingdom and Byzantium.

Section 35.10

Building the Latin Church

Looking across these regions, historians can identify the institutions through which Latin Christianity took root. The first was the diocese, a territory under a bishop who ordained clergy, consecrated churches, confirmed the faithful, and judged certain disputes. New dioceses required property, a seat, and a cathedral community. In several realms, early bishops were missionary bishops without clear territories, and fixed diocesan boundaries emerged over several decades. Archbishoprics, or metropolitan sees, such as Gniezno, Esztergom, and later Lund, Nidaros, and Uppsala, gave bishops a province and a means of governing the church internally. The creation of a metropolitan see was often the moment when a ruler's church ceased to depend on a foreign archbishop.

The second institution was the monastery. Monasteries such as Břevnov, Pannonhalma, and later Benedictine houses in Poland and Scandinavia gave rulers and aristocrats places of prayer, burial, and learning. They held land, produced manuscripts, trained clergy, and sometimes undertook missions. The third was property and revenue. Rulers endowed bishoprics and monasteries with land, and over time a system of tithes, payments from produce of the land, supported clergy. Tithes were slow to be accepted, and kings sometimes had to enforce them by law. Church lands could become sources of conflict, as the Investiture Conflict examined in Chapter 44 would show.

The fourth was a body of people and practices: clergy, who were often foreigners at first and then, over generations, locally trained; saints and relics, which provided objects of pilgrimage and local identity; and ecclesiastical courts and canon law, which touched marriage, inheritance, and discipline. The fifth was a written culture. Latin documentary and liturgical culture expanded rapidly, but it entered different existing written environments. Scandinavia already possessed runic literacy, Bohemia inherited Slavonic Christian traditions, and Hungary had contacts with Greek and Slavonic Christianity. Latin increasingly became the principal written language of the new western church institutions and much royal administration, and charters, liturgical books, saints' lives, annals, and letters multiplied. This expansion affected government, since royal charters and legal records became possible, and it affected memory, since chronicles and legends now shaped how dynasties were remembered. It also shaped our evidence, since much of what we know about these societies was written by clergy.

Parish churches, in the sense of local churches serving defined communities, developed later. In the early period, most worship occurred at royal or aristocratic churches, or in itinerant preaching, and mature parish networks belong largely to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The same was true of other regions incorporated into Latin Christendom. The Slavic lands between the Elbe and the Oder, for instance, saw the creation of bishoprics such as Havelberg and Brandenburg in the tenth century and of the archbishopric of Magdeburg in 968, though the Slavic revolt of 983 destroyed much of this work and the territory was reconquered and converted only over the following two centuries, a process that involved violence and political conflict as well as mission. Other regions on the Baltic, including Pomerania and Prussia, were incorporated later still.

Chapter 34 warned that Latin Christianity did not bring a ready-made social system. This section confirms that warning. The institutions of church and monastery arrived with rulers and clergy, but the societies that received them had their own kinship structures, assemblies, ranks, and economic arrangements. In Scandinavia, free landholders retained significant rights. In Poland, the ducal retinue and fortified centers shaped governance. In Hungary, the ruling dynasty built counties and churches in tandem. None of these societies became a copy of the Carolingian kingdoms, and the manorial and vassalic relationships examined in Chapter 34 did not appear in the same form.

Section 35.11

Conversion from Below: Communities, Customs, and Resistance

A history of royal baptisms and bishoprics is a history of institutions, not of communities. The slower transformation of everyday life is harder to see but equally important. Evidence from cemeteries is among the best. In many regions, the period between the later tenth and the twelfth century saw shifts from cremation to inhumation, from burial with weapons, tools, and food offerings to simpler burial, and from scattered graves to cemeteries near churches, with bodies laid out in an east-west orientation. In Scandinavia, some graves contain both crosses and traditional amulets, and some runestones invoke Christian prayers while depicting scenes from older myth. Such evidence suggests overlap, not a sudden break.

The interpretation of this evidence requires care. A grave without objects may indicate Christian belief, changing social display, poverty, or a ritual practice that left no trace. A Thor's hammer pendant beside a cross may show people hedging between powers, adapting an older symbol to a new one, or simply wearing an ornament without religious meaning. Historians therefore avoid the simplistic vocabulary of "pagan survivals" when the evidence could equally represent adaptation, reinterpretation, or local Christian practice. A better model describes negotiated cultural change, in which people brought existing categories of sacred place, ritual, and obligation into the new religion and reshaped them.

Christian practice also changed the calendar and the household. Sunday rest, fasts, saints' feasts, and baptismal naming altered the rhythm of the year and of family life. Marriage rules, including prohibitions on marriage within certain degrees of kinship, on concubinage, and on divorce, challenged existing aristocratic practices, and enforcement was slow and uneven. Churches were built on or near sites of earlier significance in some cases, and saints' cults, such as those of Wenceslas, Olaf, and Adalbert, offered new focal points of pilgrimage. Women appear as patrons, as transmitters of Christian custom in households, and as targets of church discipline, but the sources seldom record their own views.

Resistance took many forms. In some cases, it was violent, as in the Hungarian revolt of 1046, the Slavic uprising of 983, and the Polish unrest of the 1030s. In others, it was quiet: continued private sacrifice, reluctance to pay tithes, or unwillingness to give up customs of marriage and burial. Churchmen's complaints about such practices are our main evidence, which means they exaggerate or distort, and they reveal the church's ambitions as much as popular behavior. Christianization, in short, was a negotiation between institutions that wished to teach and communities that adapted what they were taught.

Section 35.12

The Expansion of Latin Christendom

By the middle or later eleventh century, all of these regions had experienced substantial Christian institutionalization, though its depth differed sharply. Christian rulers or governing elites, bishops or missionary clergy, churches, and Latin liturgical networks were widespread. Monasteries were already important in Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and parts of Denmark but became firmly established later in much of Norway, Sweden, and Iceland, where durable houses date mainly from around 1100 or the early twelfth century onward. All of these regions participated in a wider Latin Christian world. They did not share identical governments, identical social structures, or identical religious practice. Denmark and Norway were kingdoms with strong assemblies and a free landholding class. Poland was a ducal and later royal polity organized around fortified centers. Bohemia was a duchy under the nominal overlordship of the German king. Hungary was a monarchy led by the Árpád dynasty in a plain between the German kingdom and Byzantium. Iceland had no king at all.

What they increasingly shared was a set of institutional features: Christian rulers, bishops and archbishops, monastic foundations in many regions, the Latin liturgy, links with Rome and western ecclesiastical networks, dynastic marriages with other Christian houses, clerical literacy, saints' cults, and the habit of recording property and rights in written form. This shared framework made it easier for clergy, pilgrims, merchants, and envoys to move across large distances, and it connected the north and center of the continent to the Mediterranean and western regions to a degree not possible before.

The expansion also affected the Latin church itself. Rulers of the new Christian realms participated in the politics of the empire and the papacy, sponsored saints whose cults spread, and sent clergy and pilgrims to Rome and Jerusalem. The new regions became sources of revenue, personnel, and political support. They also complicated the relationship between the church and secular power, as the Investiture Conflict, the subject of Chapter 44, would soon show.

At the same time, Latin Europe was not synonymous with Europe. Byzantine and Orthodox Christianity extended across the Balkans and into the Rus lands, and even in Hungary and Bohemia, traces of eastern liturgical traditions remained. The rivalries and affinities among Latin and Greek missionary networks were real, but they cannot be described as a division between a Catholic West and an Orthodox East in the tenth century. That division took shape gradually, as Chapter 40 will explain. Part VI begins by turning to Byzantium, not as a reaction to the Latin west, but as a Christian Roman state with its own long history and its own missionary achievements.

The conclusion of this chapter, and of Part V, is therefore twofold. Latin Europe expanded, and it did so through rulers, missions, bishoprics, monasteries, literacy, law, and the slow work of communities. But Europe did not become religiously or institutionally uniform, and the societies that joined Latin Christendom shaped it in turn.

Historical Vocabulary
Christianization and Conversion

Christianization is the long historical process through which Christian institutions, law, ritual, and belief became established in a society. Conversion, in the strict sense, is an individual or collective decision to adopt a religion, and it is often used loosely in the sources for a ruler's baptism. The two are not the same. A king could be baptized in a day, but a bishopric, a network of churches, and changes in household practice took generations. The distinction matters in this chapter because it prevents historians from treating a royal baptism as the conversion of a people and from dismissing sincere belief when the evidence is political.

Historical Vocabulary
Diocese, Archbishopric, and Metropolitan Province

A diocese is the territory governed by a bishop. An archbishopric is the see of a bishop who, as metropolitan, presides over a church province of subordinate, or suffragan, bishops. In the tenth century, the creation of a metropolitan see signaled a realm's ecclesiastical independence from neighboring archbishops. Gniezno, with its suffragans in 1000, is a leading example. The term should not be read as implying a modern national church, since these provinces served dynasties and territories, not nations in the modern sense.

Primary Source Window

The Jelling Stone of Harald Bluetooth, later tenth century

Creator: King Harald of Denmark, commissioning the monument · Date: c. 960s to 980s, with dating debated · Place: Jelling, Jutland · Type: A carved runestone with a runic inscription and images, a contemporary royal monument

Context: The larger of the two Jelling stones stands beside a church and two burial mounds. The smaller stone was raised by Harald's father Gorm in memory of his wife Thyra. Harald's stone has three faces, one with an inscription, one with a large animal figure, and one with a figure generally interpreted as Christ, entangled in decorative bands.

Paraphrase of the inscription, in modern English translation The inscription states that King Harald ordered the monument made in memory of his father Gorm and his mother Thyra, and describes Harald as the one who gained Denmark and Norway for himself and who "made the Danes Christian." This is a paraphrase of a runic text, and the sense of some words, including the extent of the claim about Norway, is discussed by specialists.

Analysis: The monument is rare, contemporary, public evidence. It shows that by the later tenth century a Danish king regarded Christianity as an achievement worth proclaiming alongside military and dynastic claims, and it shows the use of runes, a local medium, to convey a Christian message, with a visual image that mixes Christian and Scandinavian artistic forms. It was also a political statement, linking Harald to his parents and presenting him as the founder of a united realm. It cannot, however, tell us how many Danes held Christian beliefs, how clergy were organized, or how the king's program was received outside his centers. A boast is not a survey. The words "made the Danes Christian" should be read as royal ideology, not as a measurement of the religion of ordinary households.

Historical Debate

Why Did Rulers Convert?

The question: Why did the rulers of Denmark, Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and the Scandinavian kingdoms accept Latin Christianity, and how should historians weigh religious conviction against political interest?

Interpretation A: Political and institutional motives predominated. Many historians emphasize that Christianity offered rulers literate clergy, ecclesiastical organization, royal ideology, diplomatic recognition, and protection from attack justified as Christian war. In this view, the timing of royal baptisms, often under pressure from powerful Christian neighbors, shows that political calculation was central.
Interpretation B: Religious conviction and cultural attraction mattered. Others argue that this view underestimates the weight of belief, contact with Christian relatives, queens, merchants, and clergy, and the attractions of Christian rituals of death, kingship, and salvation. In this view, the later devotion of dynasties to saints and monasteries suggests more than strategy.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same narratives, most written by clergy, which describe motives in conventional terms, and on the same actions, such as the founding of churches and monasteries, marriages, and the sponsorship of cults. The sources rarely reveal private belief. Contemporary monuments, such as the Jelling stone, and later dynastic patronage support the argument that conversion had real and lasting meaning, while the tight link to diplomacy supports the argument that it was strategic.

Why it matters: Reading conversion as purely strategic risks treating medieval rulers as modern calculators and ignoring religious worlds. Reading it as purely spiritual ignores the political structures in which decisions were made. Most current scholarship stresses mixed motives that differed from ruler to ruler and cannot be neatly separated.

Student task: Choose one ruler discussed in this chapter and identify the evidence for religious and for political motives. Then explain what additional evidence would help you decide how to weigh them.

Multiple Perspectives

One Conversion, Many Experiences

Note: these are analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians infer from many sources. They are not quotations from any single named person, and the groups did not have equal power in shaping events.

A ruler and his court: Conversion offered literate clergy, links to Christian dynasties, and a new language of kingship. It also risked offending aristocrats and drawing the ruler into dependence on neighboring bishops and emperors.
A missionary or bishop: Clergy aimed to organize churches, baptize, teach, and regulate marriage. Their success depended on royal support and on the willingness of local elites to host them, and many were foreigners working among unfamiliar languages and customs.
A local aristocrat: Elites weighed the benefits of royal favor and church patronage against the loss of ritual roles, the burden of tithes, and challenges to marriage and kinship customs. Some resisted and some used the church to expand their own prestige.
A rural household: For most families, Christianization appeared gradually, as a priest's visit, a new burial place, a new holiday, or a demand for payment. Households could adapt Christian practice to their own routines and keep older customs that did not conflict with the new ones.
A Jewish or Muslim merchant and a long-established Christian trader: Traders already moved among religious communities. Ibrahim ibn Yaʿqub's description of Slavic realms is evidence that Jewish merchants traveled in these regions, and the arrival of Christian kingdoms changed the legal and political setting of commerce without ending its networks.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Before and After Royal Conversion

What Changed

  • Bishops, dioceses, and eventually archbishoprics appeared
  • Churches and monasteries were built and endowed with land
  • Christian burial gradually replaced older forms in many regions
  • Latin writing, charters, and saints' lives entered government and memory
  • Marriage rules and religious calendars changed, and new relations with Christian rulers opened

What Stayed the Same

  • Local elites and kinship groups held power
  • Assemblies and customary law shaped justice, especially in Scandinavia
  • Sacred landscapes and household rituals persisted in adapted forms
  • Trade routes, languages, and agricultural routines continued

Who experienced change most directly, and who continuity? Kings, aristocrats, and urban traders felt change first, since they dealt with clergy and Christian courts. Rural households experienced it later and more unevenly, with greater continuity in daily life.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that Latin Christianity changed institutions faster than it changed everyday practice, and that Christianization and continuity coexisted.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Christianization Become Durable?

Long-term causes

Centuries of contact through trade, raids, marriage, and migration; the missionary and imperial interests of Frankish and Ottonian rulers; and the existence of Christian courts and clergy to imitate.

Short-term causes

Dynastic ambitions of the Jelling, Piast, Přemyslid, Árpád, and Norwegian dynasties; marriages; threats of imperial intervention; and opportunities for papal and imperial recognition.

Event or transformation

Royal baptisms and coronations, the creation of bishoprics and archbishoprics, and the founding of monasteries between the 960s and the early eleventh century.

Immediate effects

Access to clergy and writing, new diplomatic standing, church property, and sometimes aristocratic and popular resistance.

Long-term consequences

Latin Christian institutions took root across northern and central Europe, supporting literacy, law, saints' cults, and a durable relationship between church and monarchy.

Historical complexity

Durability depended on permanent bishoprics, locally trained clergy, church property, succession by Christian heirs, ritual, and saints. A baptism could happen in a day, but institutional Christianization required generations, and the 1030s and 1040s show that it could be reversed in part.
Everyday Life and Social History

Religious Practice in Ordinary Life

For most people, Christianization meant new obligations as much as new beliefs. A household might be asked to observe Sunday, to fast on certain days, to bring children for baptism, to bury its dead in a consecrated place, to pay tithes, and to follow rules about whom its members could marry. These demands competed with agricultural labor and with customs of kinship and hospitality, and the sources record complaints and fines as well as compliance. Rural communities probably met Christianity through itinerant priests, aristocratic chapels, and the occasional visit of a bishop long before they had a local church.

Material culture offers glimpses. Crosses and amulets, runestones that mention prayers for the dead, church foundations, and cemeteries record changing habits. Written law, such as the provisions attributed to Stephen of Hungary or Norwegian regional law, shows what rulers and clergy expected, not necessarily what people did. Women's roles are visible mainly through patronage and through rules about marriage, and the voices of children, slaves, and the poor are almost absent. Slaves, including captives taken in war, were an important element in the economies of Scandinavia, Poland, and Bohemia, and church leaders at times condemned the slave trade while accepting slavery.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 35 has examined how Latin Christianity became rooted in Scandinavia and central Europe between the ninth and the eleventh centuries, and how rulers, missionaries, clergy, and local communities shaped that process. Its central argument is that Christianization was a long political, institutional, and cultural process and not a series of single conversions of peoples.

The chapter began by distinguishing a ruler's baptism from the layered process of church building, legal change, and transformation of daily practice. It explained how historians reconstruct that process from chronicles, saints' lives, sagas, inscriptions, coins, and cemeteries, and why each kind of source requires critical reading.

Missions moved through courts, trade centers, captives, marriage alliances, and monasteries. Rulers accepted Christianity for combined religious and political reasons, including access to literate clergy, kingship ideology, and diplomatic standing, and the chapter rejected both the idea of cynical statecraft and the idea that missionaries brought civilization to peoples without institutions.

In Denmark, early Frankish missions failed to create a lasting church, but Harald Bluetooth's Jelling monuments show a king claiming Christianity as part of a program of consolidation. In Norway, Christian kingship developed through the contested reigns of Hákon, the two Olafs, and local assemblies, with later sagas supplying vivid but difficult evidence. Sweden and Iceland demonstrated that conversion could be prolonged or decided by collective compromise, and that Scandinavia was not converted in a single tenth-century moment.

In Poland, Mieszko I accepted baptism in 966, and his son Bolesław, working with Otto III, secured an archbishopric at Gniezno in 1000 around the relics of Adalbert. This gave the Piast realm its own church province, though not a modern national church, and the unrest of the 1030s showed its fragility. In Bohemia, the Přemyslids drew on mixed Moravian and Frankish traditions, venerated Wenceslas and Ludmila, and entered the Latin church with the bishopric of Prague in 973.

In Hungary, Géza and Stephen built a Christian monarchy in a Carpathian Basin where the Magyars were already settled. They used dynastic marriage, Latin clergy, dioceses, monasteries, and law, and Stephen's coronation around 1000 or 1001 completed a process that had begun generations earlier. Resistance and the persistence of eastern Christian practice show that Latin Christianity did not spread uniformly or uncontested.

Across regions, the Latin church was built from dioceses, archbishoprics, monasteries, property and tithes, clergy, saints, courts, and Latin writing, though mature parish systems came later. These institutions did not bring a ready-made social system, and societies kept their assemblies, kinship structures, and customs. Conversion from below, visible mainly in cemeteries and everyday practice, was a negotiated change that included adaptation and resistance.

The historiographical lessons are that baptism is not conversion, that one date cannot represent a process, that political and religious motives were intertwined, and that local rulers and populations were historical actors, not passive recipients. The Historical Debate on why rulers converted showed that scholarship favors mixed motives that vary by ruler.

By the middle or later eleventh century, the north and center of the continent had joined a wider Latin Christian world through bishops, churches, dynastic marriage, literacy, and saints' cults, with monasteries already important in Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and parts of Denmark and established later in much of Scandinavia. Latin Europe expanded, but Europe did not become uniform, and Byzantine and Orthodox Christianity remained a major part of the continent's religious life.

Part VI now turns to Byzantium, beginning with Chapter 36, to examine the eastern Christian Roman world as a central part of European history. Chapter 38 will return to the missionary work of Cyril and Methodius, and Chapter 40 will explain how Latin and Greek Christendom came to be divided.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Christianization
The long process by which Christian institutions, law, ritual, and belief became established in a society, as distinct from a single act of baptism.
Conversion
An individual or collective adoption of a religion, often used in the sources for a ruler's baptism.
Mission
An organized effort by clergy, often backed by rulers, to establish Christian communities among non-Christians.
Latin Christendom
The communities that used the Latin liturgy and recognized the authority of Rome, not a single unified institution.
Diocese
The territory under a bishop's authority.
Archbishopric
The see of a bishop who presides over a province of other bishops.
Metropolitan
An archbishop with authority over a church province and its suffragan bishops.
Suffragan
A bishop subordinate to a metropolitan within a church province.
Missionary bishop
A bishop appointed to work in a newly Christian region before fixed diocesan boundaries existed.
Tithe
A customary payment, traditionally a tenth of produce, to support clergy and church.
Relic
A saint's body, part of a body, or object associated with a saint, venerated as a source of holy power.
Martyr
A person killed for religious faith and venerated as a saint, such as Wenceslas or Adalbert.
Hagiography
Writing about saints, which combines history with devotional purpose and literary convention.
Saga
An Icelandic prose narrative written in the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, a valuable but late source for Scandinavian conversion.
Runestone
A stone carved with runic inscription, often commemorating the dead and sometimes carrying Christian symbols.
Thing
A Scandinavian assembly of free men that made law and settled disputes.
Lawspeaker
An Icelandic official who recited the law at the Althing.
Monastery
A community of monks or nuns living under a rule, often a center of prayer, learning, and landholding.
Inhumation
Burial of the unburned body, which became more common as Christian cemeteries spread.
Cremation
Burning of the dead, practiced in many pre-Christian societies and discouraged by the church.
Grave goods
Objects buried with the dead, whose decline is one of several possible signs of changing belief.
Royal sacralization
The process of giving kingship religious meaning through anointing, ritual, and saints' cults.
Ordeal
A trial by physical test, such as carrying hot iron, believed to reveal divine judgment; one is attributed to a missionary at Harald's court.
Composite source
A source drawn from earlier traditions and later additions, such as many chronicles and sagas, and therefore requiring critical separation of layers.
Church province
A group of dioceses headed by a metropolitan, as in Gniezno and Esztergom.
Great Moravia
A ninth-century Slavic polity where Frankish and Byzantine missionaries competed, discussed in Chapter 38.
Ottonian
Relating to the East Frankish and German dynasty of Otto I and his successors, who supported missions and bishops.
Árpáds, Piasts, Přemyslids
The ruling dynasties of Hungary, Poland, and Bohemia respectively.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the expansion of Christianity in northern and central Europe was neither a simple diffusion from a western center nor a collection of isolated royal choices. It was a set of negotiated processes in which dynasties, bishops, monks, merchants, queens, and communities each pursued their own aims. Understanding that complexity guards against the teleological assumption that medieval Europe was always destined to be Christian, Latin, and organized into kingdoms.

The chapter also reveals how institutions and writing changed societies. Bishoprics, monasteries, charters, and saints' lives gave rulers new tools and gave later historians most of their evidence. They also reshaped how people remembered the past. The story of a king who made his people Christian is a story told by and for churches and dynasties, and recognizing this allows students to read it critically.

For the argument of Volume II, Chapter 35 completes the formation of Latin Christendom. Part V began with post-Roman kingdoms and ends with a Latin Christian world stretching farther north and east than the Roman Empire ever had. The institutions created here, and the relationships among kings, bishops, and the papacy, would shape the reform movements, the Investiture Conflict, and the growth of medieval monarchies examined in Part VII.

The chapter also prepares a change of viewpoint. Latin Europe was one part of Christian Europe. Part VI turns to Byzantium, the Orthodox world, and the Slavic lands, where Christianity took different institutional and cultural forms. Reading those chapters with the findings of this one in mind helps students see that the religious history of Europe was plural from the beginning.
