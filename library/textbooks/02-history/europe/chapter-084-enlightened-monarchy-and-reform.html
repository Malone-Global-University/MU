Chapter Opening

Could a monarch use Enlightenment ideas to improve government and society while keeping power concentrated in his or her own hands? Between about 1740 and 1790, several rulers tried, and the results were more mixed than their reputations suggest.

In 1752 Frederick II of Prussia composed a political testament describing a state run through careful administration, in which the king alone made the decisive choices and the nobility remained the pillar of army and society. The document is a revealing starting point because it joins rational management with a settled conviction that hierarchy should endure. Chapter 83 examined what made political authority legitimate in theory. This chapter asks what happened when rulers governed through the language and instruments of reform.

The rulers at the center of the chapter are Frederick II of Prussia, Maria Theresa and her son Joseph II in the Habsburg lands, and Catherine II of Russia. Portugal, Spain, Tuscany, and Sweden supply essential comparisons. Historians have long debated whether "enlightened absolutism" names a real program or a label applied afterward to the ordinary strengthening of states, and the chapter treats that question as open.

Reform did improve some things: schooling expanded, legal procedure was rationalized, some religious restrictions eased, and administration became more systematic. Yet reform coexisted with censorship, serfdom, noble privilege, colonial coercion, and imperial expansion. Enlightened monarchy did not mean constitutional government, political equality, or representation. The chapter therefore distinguishes what rulers said, what their laws prescribed, and what subjects experienced.

Evidence includes political testaments, decrees and patents, school ordinances, charters, administrative correspondence, petitions, and the records of resistance. Such sources show official intentions far better than local outcomes, so the chapter reads them alongside evidence of enforcement and response. It connects to Chapter 80 on war and the balance of power, to Chapters 82 and 83 on Enlightenment thought, and looks ahead to Chapter 85 on the economic and social foundations of the age.

The same eighteenth-century argument could have different consequences according to institutional setting. A minister in Lisbon might regard a reorganized university as an instrument for cultivating useful expertise. An official in Prague might treat a school ordinance as a directive to parish teachers. A provincial magistrate in Russia might see a new court as a means of making petitions easier to register, while a privately dependent peasant found no independent political protection in a charter framed for nobles. Reform therefore cannot be judged from the ruler’s intentions alone. Every policy had beneficiaries, intermediaries, costs, and groups who remained excluded.

The chapter’s geography also extends beyond western court society. Prussia comprised separated provinces with diverse legal histories; the Habsburg monarchy encompassed regions whose constitutional relations with Vienna differed; Russia governed a continental imperial frontier; and Portugal and Spain extracted resources and administered subject populations across oceans. The consequences for Indigenous communities, enslaved people, serfs, religious minorities, and urban households belong to the same history as ministerial memoranda and academic praise. Comparative analysis must keep differences of scale and legal position visible.

The evidence used in this chapter includes privately circulated political instructions, official laws, court registers, estate petitions, church and school records, and later evaluations of government. These sources answer different questions. A statute can show an announced right; a lawsuit may show whether that right could be enforced. Rulers’ correspondence can reveal priorities but not prove social outcomes. Students should treat the ten source windows as studies of the historical record, not as substitutes for checking original editions and implementation evidence. The closing exercises bring those forms of evidence into a sustained comparison of government and society.

Historical Context

The mid-eighteenth century was dominated by war. The conflicts examined in Chapter 80, from the War of the Austrian Succession to the Seven Years' War, strained the finances of every major state and exposed administrative weakness. Rulers needed more revenue, more soldiers, and better information about their populations. Cameralist writers in the German lands taught that a prosperous, educated, well-ordered population strengthened the prince, and this practical tradition supplied officials with a vocabulary of reform before many of them read Voltaire.

The Enlightenment (Chapters 82 and 83) added criticism of torture, clerical privilege, and arbitrary law, and the idea that rulers served a public good. But most thinkers who addressed rulers sought to influence them, not to limit them. The resulting reforms varied by state, and so did the institutions through which they had to be carried out: estates, provincial diets, noble corporations, churches, and village communities.

Across the continent, legal and political traditions were not identical. The French and Spanish monarchies had their own histories of royal councils and provincial privilege; Prussia consolidated military and fiscal institutions across disconnected territories; the Habsburg ruler presided over composite jurisdictions; Russia relied on an imperial nobility and regional administrators. Religious institutions remained key to schooling and welfare. Most inhabitants were not members of an equal electorate but subjects whose rights were mediated through estates, communities, churches, and property relations. These conditions shaped which reforms appeared practical to rulers and which demands seemed threatening.

The term Enlightenment refers to overlapping intellectual cultures rather than a single program of legislation. Criticism of torture, arbitrary authority, and restrictions on inquiry could encourage certain projects, yet the established administrative disciplines of fiscal accounting, military supply, and cameralism also shaped ministers’ proposals. Historians should distinguish a thinker’s influence, a ruler’s presentation of influence, and the independent pressures that favored a similar policy. The examples below compare proclamations with their territorial application and with the authority of social groups whose cooperation governments needed.

Chronology matters because codification and implementation rarely occurred in the same year. Frederick II’s legal ambitions preceded the Prussian code that came into force in 1794; Maria Theresa’s school policy preceded Joseph’s accelerated intervention after 1780; Catherine’s Nakaz circulated in 1767 while later provincial statutes and corporate charters answered different problems. These stages should not be fused into one royal decision. The tension between continuity and change is central: new administrative instruments could survive under governments that retained hereditary power and estate privilege.

Section 84.1

Enlightened Monarchy: Definition, Origins, and Historical Debate

Rulers described themselves as first servants of the state or as fathers of their peoples. This language did not require a single program. Frederick stressed duty and law, Joseph II rational uniformity, Catherine consultation and cultural patronage. Contemporaries disagreed about what enlightened government meant, and a ruler's self-presentation, often directed at European intellectuals, must be checked against actual policy.

The conventional term enlightened absolutism describes a family of practices rather than a constitution or a party program. Monarchs and ministers presented themselves as guardians of collective prosperity, interpreters of useful knowledge, and correctors of inherited abuses. Some corresponded with philosophers, supported academies, or read treatises on punishment and administration. Others used similar language without belonging to a recognizably philosophical circle. Their common claim was that the public good could be advanced by a ruler whose authority was not subject to routine representative control. The phrase enlightened despotism was often used polemically or retrospectively; neither label was an official agreement binding Frederick, Joseph, Catherine, and their counterparts to identical policies. It is more informative to ask what each government meant by improvement, what resources it could mobilize, and which subjects could contest the changes. This approach distinguishes constitutional form, the ruler's self-presentation, the intended beneficiaries, and measurable changes in everyday governance.

The older languages of good government remained powerful. Christian kings had long been admonished to provide justice, prevent famine, defend the weak, and care for souls. Princes of the Renaissance had commissioned jurists, surveys, and new fiscal offices. Seventeenth-century ministers had reorganized armies and taxation without waiting for Voltaire. The novelty of eighteenth-century reform therefore lay less in discovering public welfare than in the heightened prestige of rational classification, improvement, administrative comparability, and the claim that customs should be tested against their effects. Cameralist officials spoke about the productivity of subjects and taxable wealth; legal reformers asked whether a penalty deterred crime or simply displayed authority; educational officials linked instruction to more competent administration. Such perspectives could lead to humane measures, yet they could also make populations objects of closer observation and control. The same language of improvement underlay policies of schooling, conscription, resettlement, police regulation, and suppression of independent institutions.

There was no single road from philosophical argument to decree. A proposal might originate with a provincial administrator, an academy prize essay, a military officer, an adviser at court, or a community petition. A monarch could approve it for a mixture of reasons: economy, justice, dynastic prestige, religious conviction, personal curiosity, or apprehension about rivals. Drafting proceeded through chancelleries and councils where officials disagreed. Provincial estates might bargain over finance or refuse to cooperate. Courts could reinterpret regulations, bishops protest interference, and villagers evade rules that threatened established livelihoods. Conversely, local reformers sometimes pushed farther than the ruler intended. A historically adequate explanation must reconstruct these negotiations instead of announcing that a philosopher gave an idea to a king who then transformed society. Even a seemingly unilateral patent depended on interpreters, record keepers, tax collectors, teachers, judges, and the willingness or inability of subjects to comply.

The apparent contradiction between rational reform and unlimited monarchy was not necessarily regarded as a contradiction by reforming rulers. They often argued that independent privileged corporations, hereditary courts, or confessional monopolies obstructed impartial justice. Central authority could therefore appear as the instrument needed to liberate subjects from lesser tyrannies. Yet a reform that took a legal privilege away from a monastery might give a comparable discretion to a minister, and reduced corporate autonomy did not automatically create individual political rights. The effects depended on what rules replaced the old arrangements. Some reforms enlarged options for religious minorities or peasants; others imposed new surveillance or diverted resources to the military. The distinction between state capacity and human welfare is especially important because the two could advance together in the short term while diverging later.

Historical evidence supports a qualified assessment. Court correspondence gives access to intentions, but rulers often tailored statements to their audience. A political testament for a successor differs from a public edict, an academy address, or a letter meant to impress a philosopher abroad. Statutes indicate legal ambition; petitions and litigation reveal where the ambition encountered resistance; school registers, accounts, and local reports sometimes document implementation, although they were produced by officials with incentives to exaggerate success. Retrospective national histories have also used the label enlightened ruler to explain the later emergence of administrative states. We should not judge a reform by its resemblance to nineteenth-century institutions without checking what it meant to contemporaries. Across this chapter, every program will be evaluated in four steps: announced aim, administrative instrument, lived operation, and longer-term survival or reversal.

The comparators in the following divisions also differ in scale. Prussia was a dispersed dynastic kingdom heavily dependent on military institutions. The Habsburg monarchy was a composite collection of territories with distinct laws and languages. Russia was a continental empire governed through nobles, governors, and frontier communities. Portuguese and Spanish reform unfolded across Atlantic and overseas possessions; Tuscany and Sweden faced other constitutional relationships. Calling all these regimes enlightened absolutisms risks flattening their contrasts. Their histories become comparable not because they were identical but because each shows the problem of changing institutions while governing through unequal authority.

One way to evaluate rival interpretations is to compare a celebrated reform with an action that threatens the same ruler's reputation. Frederick II's claim that kings served the state coexisted with acquisition of territory by force. Joseph's proposals for relieving personal dependence coincided with attempts to bypass provincial constitutional safeguards. Catherine's appeal to humane legislation coexisted with confirmation of noble corporate privileges. These are not simply biographical inconsistencies. They reveal that reformers ranked goals, often giving dynastic security, fiscal capacity, or social order precedence over the claims of particular subjects. An analysis focused only on enlightened intentions overlooks the hierarchy of priorities visible when reforms met resistance. Nor should the evidence be forced into a choice between sincere philosophy and cynical disguise. A ruler might genuinely favor better schooling while believing that independent parliaments would endanger stability. The problem of enlightened monarchy arises precisely because a real commitment to specified improvements could coexist with rejection of constitutional accountability. Historical comparison must explain that coexistence rather than use a single approving or disapproving label.

Historical Vocabulary: Enlightened Absolutism and Public Welfare

Enlightened absolutism is a later historical label for forms of autocratic government that invoked reason, improvement, and service to the public good while retaining concentrated sovereign power. Paternal monarchy described authority through the analogy of a ruler caring for dependents. Public welfare could mean better schooling or justice, but rulers also used the term to defend increased taxation and supervision. These concepts are not synonyms for constitutionalism, freedom of criticism, or political equality. Students should investigate a ruler’s policies and outcomes rather than accept the flattering label as a verdict.

Historical Debate: Was Enlightened Absolutism a Distinct Reform Program?

Was Enlightened Absolutism a Distinct Reform Program?

The question: Did the adoption of Enlightenment ideas change how monarchies governed, or does the category simply rebrand older ambitions to enlarge state power?

Interpretation A: Emphasis on systematic education, less arbitrary punishment, religious toleration, and public welfare brought meaningful changes in official priorities and sometimes in subjects’ lives.

Interpretation B: Many reforms continued fiscal-military centralization already underway, and monarchical power, dynastic war, and corporate inequality remained dominant objectives.

Evidence and limits: Private testaments, school statutes, patents, financial records, litigation, and local petitions reveal different aspects. Neither a ruler’s praise of reason nor the presence of coercion alone resolves causation; motives could be mixed and institutions could evolve over long periods.

Why it matters: A historiographical label should clarify historical change rather than confer praise automatically.

Section 84.2

Fiscal-Military States, Bureaucracy, and Administrative Rationalization

War and debt drove reform. Governments counted people, surveyed land, reorganized ministries, and trained specialized officials to raise taxes and recruits more efficiently. Much of this work was done by officials and local intermediaries, not monarchs personally, and administrative competition among ministries often shaped the form reform took. Statistics and oversight grew, but the reach of the state remained limited by distance, custom, and local power.

The fiscal-military state was the most persistent material context for reform. Eighteenth-century warfare demanded trained soldiers, ammunition, transport, fortifications, credit, and provisions on a scale that could not reliably be raised through occasional feudal service. Governments therefore sought predictable taxes, searchable registers, orderly recruitment, and officials who could report supplies and expenditures. Defeat made administrative weaknesses visible, while even victory left substantial debt and pressure to replenish military establishments. The connection between war and reform, however, was not automatic. A new tax office could enrich the treasury without improving public justice; a better census could help distribute famine relief or locate recruits. What mattered was how the bureaucracy used information and who could negotiate its demands. Financial necessity supplied an incentive and often a rhetoric of urgency, but decisions about particular reforms remained political.

Prussia's scattered territories required communication across provinces of different legal and economic histories. The Habsburg rulers faced distinctions between Austrian and Bohemian lands, Hungary, the Austrian Netherlands, and Italian possessions. Russia required governors capable of translating a central command across immense distances. In Iberian kingdoms, colonial revenues and imperial defense shaped calculations made at court. Each government confronted the same basic problem: a decree was not effective merely because it bore the sovereign's seal. It had to pass through offices with competence, local credibility, records, and personnel. Centralization accordingly coexisted with delegation. Provincial governors might gain power as autonomous estates lost it; local notables could become indispensable conduits for rules supposedly designed to curb their privileges.

Administrative science was also a culture of instruction. Cameralism, especially influential in German-speaking lands, combined the study of fiscal affairs, population, agriculture, mining, commerce, and police regulation understood in its older sense of public order and welfare. University chairs and training programs helped teach officials to think of a territory's inhabitants as a source of productive capacity. The terms statistics and political arithmetic referred to descriptive knowledge of a state, not necessarily modern probabilistic techniques. Ministries commissioned land surveys, price reports, crop tables, and registries of taxable households. Information could be partial or manipulated: landlords concealed tenants, communities disputed measurements, and officials produced classifications that matched fiscal rules more neatly than social life. Such limitations mattered especially in multilingual composite monarchies and imperial frontier zones.

The growth of bureaucracy did not eliminate personal monarchy. Frederick often wished to scrutinize matters himself, sometimes distrustful of powerful ministers. Joseph issued extensive instructions and demanded fast compliance, generating workloads that exceeded the ability of local offices to respond. Catherine's provincial reorganization multiplied institutional forms but still depended on the availability of educated nobles and clerks. These differing styles remind us that bureaucratic rationalization and administrative predictability were not identical. A sovereign who could overturn decisions quickly might advance one reform while making consistent local planning difficult. Legal codes and procedural rules sought to make administration less arbitrary, but statutes were not always binding on the highest authority in the same way that they constrained subordinate officers.

The costs were distributed unevenly. Land taxes were resisted by estates that claimed customary exemptions; excise taxes could fall heavily on urban consumers; recruitment and requisitioning affected rural households in ways invisible in fiscal summaries. Soldiers' families lost labor, municipalities financed new schools, and village teachers required salaries. Complaints were not always opposition to change itself. A community might accept the goal of education while rejecting a new charge or the dismissal of a familiar local teacher. Estates might defend privileges, while also articulating locally legitimate concerns about procedure. To understand an administrative reform, historians must ask who paid for it, who acquired authority, which office judged disputes, and what remained beyond the state's reach.

Long-term consequences were substantial but not uniform. Increased record keeping and specialized officials could survive the monarch who introduced them; extraordinary decrees could be withdrawn when dynastic or military pressure changed. A crown that learned to govern through regular provincial offices gained potential capacity for welfare projects and for coercion. These are not interchangeable outcomes. Fiscal-military pressures explain the urgency of the projects described below, but they do not predict whether a particular ruler would favor more schooling, freer worship, harsher censorship, or expanded noble rights. Comparing implementation rather than proclaimed rationality is the discipline of this chapter.

European states also differed in the financial instruments available to them. Governments used domains, customs duties, indirect taxes, public borrowing, and negotiated contributions from estates in varying proportions. Creditors and merchant financiers could constrain policies even where a ruler's formal legislative authority was extensive. A treasury dependent on lenders might hesitate to undermine urban commerce; an army dependent on noble officers might preserve estate privileges despite pressure for taxation. Administrative reform therefore involved more than replacing inefficient clerks with better ones. It meant reallocating authority among crown, creditors, corporations, and regional bodies. Information gathering was politically sensitive because a new survey could expose exemptions or alter which communities bore costs. Officials sometimes had strong reasons to conceal failure or present optimistic numbers. Modern historical assessments must consequently triangulate financial accounts with petitions, audit records, and the history of actual collection. A state's growing capacity to produce statistics should not be confused with the accuracy of every statistic it produced or with a fair distribution of tax burdens.

Historical Vocabulary: Cameralism, Bureaucracy, and Fiscal Administration

Cameralism joined the study of government revenue, agriculture, population, and administration, especially in German-speaking territories. Bureaucracy refers to organized offices, personnel, written procedures, and records through which decisions are made and transmitted. Centralization shifts authority toward the court or central ministries, though officials still depend on local intermediaries. Police in eighteenth-century administrative language often covered broad public-order and welfare regulation rather than only modern law enforcement. Each term requires attention to local legal jurisdictions.

Section 84.3

Religious Toleration, Churches, and the Authority of the State

Toleration meant permission to practice under conditions, not equality. Rulers eased some restrictions on Protestants, Jews, and Orthodox or Catholic minorities while asserting authority over church property, monasteries, courts, and clerical education. In Catholic, Protestant, and Orthodox settings alike, toleration and state control often advanced together, since a state that regulated religion could also decide how much to allow.

Religious toleration was one of the most visible and contested forms of enlightened reform because it touched the everyday authority of churches, the legal standing of families, and the ruler's control of the state. The word did not ordinarily mean a universal right to worship on equal terms. It more often meant that an established church and government permitted specified dissenters to live, work, or practice within carefully defined limits. An edict might allow private worship but prohibit prominent church buildings; it might permit trade but require registration and special taxes; it might grant an exemption in one province while leaving another untouched. The first question is therefore not whether a monarch was tolerant but toward whom, in which territory, under what conditions, and compared with which previous legal regime. Different groups could receive improvements at the same time that political surveillance increased.

Confessional Europe carried institutional legacies of the Reformation and Counter-Reformation. In Catholic territories, bishops, monasteries, and religious orders controlled land, schools, welfare functions, and courts; in Protestant lands, territorial churches could also be deeply integrated into government. A ruler who curtailed ecclesiastical jurisdiction might present the measure as a rational boundary between spiritual duties and civil administration. Yet ecclesiastical institutions were not simply obstacles: they supplied educated personnel, local charity, and teaching networks. Closing a monastery could release property for schools or hospitals, but it could also interrupt services or provoke resentment in communities that valued the institution. The historian must reconstruct the material reallocation rather than equate secular administration with automatic social benefit.

Prussia's government welcomed some religious migrants and permitted varied Christian communities to coexist, while classifying Jewish residents through restrictive regulations and differential fiscal burdens. The Habsburg monarchy under Joseph II offered private worship to certain Protestant and Orthodox groups in territories where such rights had previously been limited, but Catholicism retained a privileged public position. Separate rules for Jewish communities varied by crownland and combined opportunities with pressure toward linguistic and occupational assimilation. Russia's Orthodox monarchy governed Muslims, Catholics, Protestants, Jews in newly acquired lands, and numerous other religious traditions within an expanding empire. Policies there reflected frontier governance and relations with local elites as well as philosophical arguments about toleration. Portugal and Spain, by contrast, pursued strong forms of royal control over Catholic institutions rather than a sweeping system of religious pluralism.

Legal categories had tangible effects. A family's right to marry under the rules of its faith, to register a birth, to educate children, or to hold property could determine whether toleration was meaningful. Permission to worship was less useful if a village lacked sufficient households to form a congregation or could not employ a minister. Communities already practicing discreetly sometimes emerged into view following a new decree, surprising officials. Conversely, registration requirements could make previously inconspicuous dissenters more legible to authorities. A legal status that improved one aspect of life might leave officeholding, municipal membership, residence, and guild access restricted. The state, in this sense, did not simply remove religion from politics; it reorganized the terms on which religious belonging was recognized.

Religious reform also exposed different models of legitimacy. Some rulers defended policies as moral duties to protect conscience; others emphasized useful taxpayers and productive settlers. Joseph II's official language linked tolerance to religion and the state's benefit, not to a complete renunciation of confessional hierarchy. Catholic critics feared the erosion of traditional authority, while dissenters sometimes objected to reforms that dictated their internal organization. Those who supported toleration could still oppose state intrusion into worship. The presence of mutually incompatible motives cautions against assigning every measure to one Enlightenment doctrine. The same ruler could patronize philosophers, regulate sermons, and forbid writing deemed subversive to political order.

A careful assessment distinguishes freedom of conscience, private religious exercise, public worship, communal self-government, and equal civic status. They form no automatic ladder. Because the legal order differed among territories, comparison should proceed through jurisdiction-specific evidence, particularly patents, petitions, local police records, and ecclesiastical correspondence. The case studies to follow show both genuine changes in the lives of minorities and the persistence of inequalities, proving that toleration and a stronger confessional state were not necessarily opposites.

The religious policies of reforming rulers also intersected with charitable and educational institutions. A monastery could maintain a hospital, support a village school, or provide food during hardship; closing it required alternatives if residents were to avoid losing these services. On the other hand, church authorities might defend privileges that hindered minority worship or protected their own wealth. Government inquiries into religious endowments therefore generated real choices about resources and legal authority. Reforming ministers often invoked the welfare of souls and subjects simultaneously, rejecting any simple distinction between secular administrators and religious people. Confessional variety within the Ottoman and Russian empires posed different questions from those arising in Catholic Habsburg territories, where the legal relation between crown and church was particularly close. Comparing these regions requires attention to institutions, not the assumption that one definition of church-state separation applies everywhere. In every case, a decree of tolerance was a beginning of negotiation over buildings, property, marriage, schools, clerical training, and the power to discipline members.

Section 84.4

Law, Education, Agrarian Reform, and the Limits of Central Direction

Common fields of policy included codification, limits on torture, state schooling, professional training, peasant obligations, and taxation. Rulers wanted subjects more productive, literate, and governable. They met local custom, corporate privilege, noble opposition, thin enforcement, and popular resistance. Reform was a process of government with obstacles, not philosophy translated directly into policy.

Law reform often began from a practical problem: justice was slow, expensive, uneven, and sometimes terrifyingly discretionary. Multiple courts exercised overlapping jurisdictions, privileged estates claimed distinct procedures, and magistrates interpreted statutes alongside local custom. Reformers sought clearer rules, reviewable proceedings, professionally trained judges, and more predictable punishments. The influence of Cesare Beccaria and other writers made torture and capital punishment subjects of public debate, but the effects of these arguments depended on judges, police, and rulers, not on philosophical persuasion alone. Codification sought to organize laws into a systematic whole. It did not necessarily abolish estate distinctions, and an orderly code could continue to assign different legal rights to nobles, peasants, and townspeople. The essential distinction is between consistency of law and equality before it.

Educational reform combined different ideals and interests. Philosophers promoted knowledge and criticism; clergy defended moral instruction; ministries valued literacy, numeracy, and obedient officials; rural communities wanted useful teaching without impossible costs. School ordinances could define curricula, teacher training, inspection, and compulsory attendance, yet they required classrooms, funds, and adults willing to spare children from seasonal labor. In multilingual territories, the language of instruction mattered as much as the timetable. Attempts to standardize schooling could widen access while threatening established linguistic or confessional practice. Girls and boys might receive different curricula even when both were legally included. Schooling therefore serves as a good measure of how an abstract reform encountered households, village economies, and local identities.

Agrarian reform posed still greater challenges because rights in land were rarely simple. A village could possess communal grazing rights; peasants held plots subject to rent or labor dues; landlords enjoyed jurisdictional and fiscal privileges; the crown sought predictable food supplies and taxes. A measure abolishing personal subjection was not the same as extinguishing land obligations or transferring ownership. Administrative reform might permit peasants to marry or migrate more freely without freeing them from required work for a lord. Some rulers feared that rapid change would undermine agriculture, noble loyalty, or military recruitment. Others hoped more secure tenure and greater mobility would raise productivity. There was no universal European peasant condition. Eastern Elbian estates, Bohemian manors, Russian serfholding provinces, and Portuguese rural communities operated within distinct legal frameworks.

The category of police, often encountered in eighteenth-century statutes, requires historical care. It referred broadly to the ordering of public life: markets, food safety, sanitation, fire prevention, the regulation of work, and public morals, as well as coercive enforcement. A government could establish street lighting, regulate burial practices, improve hospitals, or control beggary under this heading. Such measures could reduce particular dangers while restricting mobility or stigmatizing the poor. The historian should not project a modern professional police force backward onto every reference to police in a reform document. Similarly, terms such as state, nation, and citizen carried changing meanings and did not automatically imply equal membership.

Uniformity was especially difficult in composite governments. An ordinance drafted for Vienna might need adjustment in Hungary or the Austrian Netherlands, where representative estates defended constitutional privileges. Frederick's officials managed separate provinces with varied legal legacies. Catherine's governors worked among different languages, religions, and settlement patterns. Ministers often used exceptions, transitional periods, or local compromises to secure implementation. That unevenness was not always simple administrative failure: old arrangements could protect meaningful local rights even when they sheltered privilege. Reform outcomes should therefore be compared through the interactions of central decrees, intermediate authorities, and local practice rather than by reading the preambles of statutes as completed social change.

The four major policy areas illuminate a shared paradox. Rulers invoked order, utility, and improvement to justify new regulations. Subjects might gain a more accessible school, fewer arbitrary punishments, or legal permission to move; the same policies could expand the number of officials who inspected, registered, and disciplined them. A reform was neither automatically emancipatory nor automatically oppressive. Its significance depended on enforceable entitlements, distribution of costs, and the possibilities available to people outside the ruling elite. This is the analytical framework through which the Prussian, Habsburg, Russian, and southern European cases must be examined.

The language of improvement also became a way to decide which forms of knowledge counted. Governments sometimes favored engineers, physicians, surveyors, agronomists, and university specialists over customary authorities, but experts were not a unified group. A physician could support regulation in the name of public health while disagreeing with a parish priest about burial; an agricultural adviser could seek enclosure or drainage while villagers defended common resources. Nor did central officials necessarily possess more accurate local information than the people who cultivated land or managed water. A successful policy could combine measured technical knowledge with longstanding practice; an unsuccessful one could dismiss the latter as superstition. The distinction matters when evaluating claims that eighteenth-century reforms applied reason to society. Reason was invoked in competing ways, and evidence about productivity or welfare was incomplete. A school statute, legal code, or agricultural instruction should therefore be read as a proposal to reorder authority over knowledge as well as an attempt to produce a particular material result.

Section 84.5

Frederick II, Royal Duty, and the Idea of the Enlightened Sovereign

Frederick acceded in 1740, had corresponded with Voltaire, and wrote on kingship as service to the state. Yet he seized Silesia that same year, and dynastic ambition and Prussia's great-power position remained constant (the wars themselves are treated in Chapter 80). His understanding of duty was personal and military: the king supervised government directly.

Frederick II was already a public literary figure when he inherited the Prussian throne in 1740. His early experiences included sharp conflict with his father, Frederick William I, whose military and administrative priorities shaped the kingdom's resources. Frederick wrote poetry and political criticism in French, corresponded with leading intellectuals, and composed Anti-Machiavel, a critique of cynical power politics published around the moment of his accession with Voltaire's assistance. That book should not be mistaken for a binding program. Within months the new king invaded Habsburg Silesia, initiating a sequence of conflicts that made Prussia a major European power and imposed exceptional strains on government. The contrast was obvious to contemporaries. Frederick could sincerely believe that a ruler should work for public welfare while also pursuing territorial security and advantage through war. His conception of political duty permitted both ends; a retrospective portrait of the philosopher-king often separates them too sharply.

Prussia was not a compact, uniform national state. Its territories lay across distinct parts of northern and central Europe, from the Brandenburg heartland to East Prussia and newly acquired Silesia. Local diets, estates, towns, and noble jurisdictions constrained how royal decisions reached subjects. A monarch attentive to administrative detail needed advisers, fiscal offices, provincial officials, military commanders, and reliable correspondence. Frederick cultivated the image of personal supervision, but the volume of business made complete direct control impossible. His interventions sometimes expedited decisions and sometimes unsettled procedures. The king's preference for working from memoranda and reports therefore reveals not pure bureaucratic impersonality but a distinctive mixture of personal judgment and organized state capacity.

The 1752 Political Testament is especially valuable because it was addressed to a successor rather than published as a celebration of the ruler. It discusses the nobility as a strategic resource, the army as a central institution, and the king's obligation to direct the government. Frederick argues that noble families should retain the landed position from which officers could be recruited. He worries about the service of nobles abroad and about the political dangers of independent ministers. He also describes the sovereign as responsible for justice, careful expenditure, and the well-being of subjects. The combination is not accidental. In his vision the king protects hierarchy because he considers it functional for the state. What a peasant might regard as an inequitable obligation could appear to the sovereign as part of a necessary order.

This source should also be read against the king's discussion of peasant protection. Frederick recognized that landlords might destroy agricultural capacity through excessive demands, and in some crown domains labor services could be moderated. Yet his distinction between noble estates and peasant holdings was designed partly to maintain the social and military structure. The document's language of balance did not create a general, enforceable equality between landlord and cultivator. The king could correct what he regarded as abuses while leaving intact the structure that enabled them. His priorities also shifted with war, peace, and provincial circumstances. Neither his own statements nor later anecdotes can substitute for a study of courts, manorial relations, and fiscal practice.

Frederick's association with Voltaire was both intellectually meaningful and politically useful. The philosopher spent time at the Prussian court, but their relationship deteriorated, illustrating the fragility of patronage when criticism collided with royal authority. The Berlin Academy offered prestige and a forum for scholars; French remained influential in elite correspondence. Yet the court's cosmopolitan culture was not equivalent to a broad public right to criticize the government. Frederick could champion freedom of theological discussion more readily than public opposition to fiscal or military policy. The institutions in which writers worked remained dependent on patronage and political protection, themes already developed in Chapter 82.

The test of Frederick's enlightenment is consequently comparative and institutional. Did courts become more predictable? Were schools more available? Did certain subjects gain religious security? Did crown-domain cultivators experience reduced obligations? Which groups retained exemptions from tax or political participation? War and territorial acquisition shaped the same regime that undertook reform. A historian can find practical innovations without treating Frederick's literary reputation as sufficient evidence of humane government. His writings reveal an influential model of royal duty, but that model remained compatible with centralized power, dynastic warfare, militarized administration, and a privileged nobility.

Frederick's dealings with petitions demonstrate the difference between royal attentiveness and institutional accountability. A ruler might personally respond to a subject's complaint and appear more accessible than an entrenched local authority. Yet the relief depended on the sovereign's judgment and the petitioner's ability to secure notice. It did not necessarily create a predictable legal right for everyone similarly situated. The political testament itself urges the monarch to hear complaints and deliver justice, while defending the central place of the nobility. This combination helps explain why a king could be celebrated as energetic and conscientious by many subjects without governing through representative institutions. Anecdotes of direct royal intervention should be checked against correspondence and court records before being treated as typical. The crucial question is whether subjects could expect remedies when the king was absent or uninterested, not merely whether one petitioner obtained a favorable response. Frederick's centralization made the crown a powerful arbiter, while leaving the institutional problem of unchecked sovereign authority unresolved.

Section 84.6

Prussian Administration, Justice, and the Reform of Law

Frederick oversaw taxation, provincial administration, and courts. Samuel von Cocceji and later jurists reformed judicial procedure, and torture was largely curtailed. Codification proceeded slowly. The Prussian General Law Code took effect only in 1794, after Frederick's death, and should be treated as a later outcome of earlier initiatives, not as his own enactment.

Prussian legal reform unfolded across decades and should not be attributed to a single statute or ruler. Under Frederick II, officials and jurists pursued faster procedures, professional judicial oversight, and the reduction of opportunities for arbitrary treatment. Samuel von Cocceji, prominent in the earlier reforms, reorganized aspects of the judiciary and attempted to make proceedings less dependent on entrenched local networks. Frederick's public interventions in conspicuous cases could convey a desire to discipline judges, yet they also raised a question: could courts become reliably lawful if the sovereign personally corrected decisions whenever he considered them unjust? The aspiration to consistent justice existed alongside the possibility of discretionary royal intervention. That tension lies near the center of enlightened monarchy.

Torture had been used in parts of European criminal procedure as an instrument for obtaining confessions, under legal conditions that varied by court and crime. Frederick sharply curtailed its use early in his reign, with exceptions and uneven enforcement that should not be erased by a simple announcement that torture had disappeared. Punishment remained harsh in many contexts; military discipline, forced labor, and corporal penalties were not swept away by a new theory of humanity. Beccaria's later criticisms of penal violence circulated in Prussia as elsewhere, but individual legal changes depended on local officers and on institutions that predated his work. It is misleading to make the chronology of penal reform a sequence of philosophical inventions followed mechanically by royal decrees.

A related project was the production of a comprehensive and intelligible legal code. Prussia inherited distinct provincial systems, social jurisdictions, and corporate rights. Integrating them required decisions about property, inheritance, family relations, criminal law, public administration, and the authority of the crown. A code could restrain inconsistent local discretion by placing rules in a common printed framework; it could equally preserve distinctions among noble estate, urban corporate membership, and rural dependence. Reform jurists worked under Frederick, and the project continued after his death under Frederick William II. The General Law Code for the Prussian States was first issued in 1791, withdrawn amid political concern, then promulgated in a revised form in 1794, entering into force on June 1. Its effective date must never be given as an act of Frederick II, who died in 1786.

The Code's long-term importance is not that it suddenly created equality. It contained an enormous body of detailed regulations and attempted to give legal relationships a common structure, yet continued to recognize the estates and the distinctive rights and duties of social orders. It could protect a subject against certain forms of arbitrary treatment while defining subjects unequally. The political context of the French Revolution sharpened official anxiety about provisions thought to limit sovereign discretion. Revision revealed how codification could serve the rule of law but also be contested as a potential curb on the ruler. Students should distinguish the program of legal rationalization from constitutional government: a code is not necessarily an instrument through which subjects can compel the monarch to honor the same rules.

Access to justice was another problem beyond a statute's wording. A rural cultivator faced travel expenses, documentary requirements, language barriers, and the need to challenge an authority with substantial social power. Urban merchants might use courts more frequently or possess legal expertise unavailable to poorer people. Military justice operated under distinctive rules and expectations. Translation of royal decisions into provincial practice depended on judges, clerks, notaries, and supervisors whose training and incentives differed. Even where new procedures existed, their availability could be compromised by the costs and duration of litigation. Historians reconstruct such patterns through surviving case files and complaint registers, not only through legal proclamations.

The Prussian example thus shows two different meanings of improvement. For administrators, a clear code and quicker courts increased predictability, reduced transaction costs, and made the state more governable. For subjects, improvement depended on whether the law afforded practical remedies against powerful neighbors, landlords, officials, or even government itself. The 1794 Code is therefore an appropriate closing document for the Prussian legal story, provided its chronological placement and conservative compromises are made explicit. It marks an important culmination of eighteenth-century reform while revealing the continuing strength of the social hierarchy that reformers largely declined to dismantle.

Codification was also an intellectual undertaking with a social constituency. Lawyers trained in different jurisdictions disagreed about the appropriate order of law, the role of local custom, and how much discretion judges needed in unforeseen circumstances. A comprehensive code promised accessibility, but its immense length could itself make legal expertise indispensable. The Prussian General Law Code's thousands of provisions demonstrate the administrative ambition and the practical challenge of exhaustiveness. Rural subjects could scarcely be expected to navigate such a system without intermediaries. Nor did the mere publication of rules abolish private influence over the selection of officials or the financing of litigation. An important source exercise is to compare the 1794 code's claims to general legal order with clauses preserving the distinctions of estates. Students then see how a text can simultaneously point toward more regular administration and reinforce an unequal social structure. This approach avoids reading the code either as an early democratic constitution or as a worthless display of royal power.

Section 84.7

Religious Toleration, Education, Economy, and Intellectual Patronage in Prussia

Prussia admitted Catholics, Protestants, and Jews under unequal conditions, and Jewish residents faced heavy restrictions despite the rhetoric of toleration. Elementary schooling, migration and settlement policy, and agricultural improvement all expanded, carried out by officials, teachers, landowners, military institutions, and communities, not by the king alone.

Frederick's religious policy is often described as tolerant because Prussia's ruler accommodated several Christian communities in a territory that had experienced confessional change and expansion. Protestant traditions remained central to the monarchy's institutional history, but Catholics in Silesia, French-speaking Reformed migrants, and other communities could be valuable subjects. Religious plurality was in part a demographic reality: annexation made it impossible to govern Prussia as though every inhabitant shared the same confession. Frederick generally regarded productive settlement and political loyalty as weightier reasons for favoring a community than strict theological agreement. Yet the monarchy did not abolish distinctions of legal status. Jewish subjects encountered restrictions on residence, occupation, family formation, and taxation, and different categories of protected Jewish households possessed unequal privileges. The fact that a sovereign expressed indifference to doctrinal differences did not entail a universal right of equal citizenship.

The practical calculation of toleration is visible in migration and settlement policy. Prussia sought artisans, cultivators, merchants, and specialists who could strengthen the economy and increase taxable population. Migrants might receive assistance in settlement or limited religious arrangements, but such incentives were selective, designed for populations considered useful to the state. The experience of established residents, poorer migrants, and those excluded from favored categories could differ profoundly. Authorities sometimes viewed an immigrant family first as a potential contributor to production and military supply rather than as the holder of unconditional rights. Policies of colonization and land improvement must also be examined in terms of property relations and the existing communities whose rights they affected.

Education occupied a similar intersection between welfare and utility. The Prussian General School Regulation of 1763, associated with Johann Julius Hecker and Frederick's administration, expressed an ambition for more regular elementary instruction, religious education, and the training of useful subjects. It did not instantly generate an adequately staffed, fully attended school system throughout the kingdom. Parish institutions, schoolmasters, village finances, local supervision, and seasonal labor demands shaped attendance. Formal obligations might be ignored or only partly realized in poorer communities. Pupils learned reading and religious instruction alongside elementary practical skills, and the quality of teaching depended heavily on material circumstances. Comparisons with Maria Theresa's 1774 school ordinance should therefore examine organizational similarities and the sharply different provincial contexts.

Frederick supported forms of economic improvement associated with agrarian drainage, reclamation, crop cultivation, state factories, and infrastructure. Projects in marshlands and other territories were celebrated by later biographers as evidence of the sovereign transforming nature for the public good. Their results were mixed and often depended on settlers, engineers, local capital, and compulsory or subsidized labor. State enterprises could lose money or serve military supply more directly than consumer welfare. Protection of particular industries might benefit manufacturers while limiting competition; land improvement might encourage food production but threaten inherited grazing or fishing arrangements. The language of improvement must be tested by examining who obtained land, who performed work, and how economic gains were divided.

Intellectual patronage reinforced Prussia's reputation as a center of Enlightenment culture. Frederick supported the Berlin Academy and promoted French-language literary exchange. His court attracted writers and musicians and represented itself to other European elites as cultivated. Such patronage enhanced diplomatic prestige, but it did not represent the whole of Prussian society. University scholars, clergy, local teachers, Jewish intellectuals, and municipal readers engaged with wider learned networks on different terms. The flourishing German public culture of the later eighteenth century cannot be reduced to royal sponsorship. Indeed, intellectual autonomy might develop in tension with the preferences of an ostensibly enlightened sovereign, as disagreements over doctrine and criticism showed.

In assessing these policies, distinctions among admission, tolerance, patronage, and equality are essential. A Catholic community might worship legally but not enjoy all the advantages of the established church; a Jewish merchant might receive a residence permit without equal civic rights; a village might be required to maintain a school without sufficient funds for a competent teacher. Administrative improvement and philosophical prestige were real aspects of Frederick's rule, yet their reach was selective and contingent. The differences between proclaimed rules and local opportunities supply the most reliable historical measure of reform.

The legal framework for Jews in Prussia demonstrates why a single adjective such as tolerant is insufficient. Residence privileges could be attached to particular households, financial resources, or occupations, and families might need renewed permission as circumstances changed. Such selective recognition could encourage commerce without offering security to every member of a religious minority. The most celebrated intellectual exchanges in Berlin involved small circles whose visibility must not be taken as representative of the wider population. Similar care is required when discussing schools: the promulgation of a regulation produced an administrative expectation, but inspectors, pastors, parents, and landlords made consequential decisions over attendance and curriculum. Historians of education distinguish compulsory provisions in legislation from measured attendance and literacy. A ruler could gain credit abroad for ambitious school policy before it had transformed the daily lives of children in poorer rural areas. This contrast between official aspiration and social coverage is a central measure of enlightened reform.

Section 84.8

The Junkers, Peasantry, Army, and Contradictions of Frederick's Rule

The officer corps drew heavily on noble landowners, whose manorial authority over peasants varied by region. Policies to protect agriculture stopped short of major social restructuring because the army and the estates depended on the existing order. Administrative innovation, estate hierarchy, military discipline, and dynastic expansion coexisted. Frederick is best judged by his institutions, not his reputation as a philosopher-king.

The Prussian nobility, often described collectively as Junkers in the territories east of the Elbe, formed an indispensable part of Frederick's military and administrative order. Noble landownership was linked to officer recruitment, local jurisdiction, and claims to political influence. The category nevertheless concealed variation among wealthy proprietors, less prosperous noble families, regional landholding practices, and the different historical arrangements of Brandenburg, East Prussia, Pomerania, and Silesia. Frederick believed that the nobility supplied the officer corps with loyalty and social cohesion, and his 1752 testament explicitly discussed measures to preserve noble estates. This was not an accidental survival neglected by a reforming monarch: it was one of the conditions under which he envisioned reform itself. A conception of the public interest could include the deliberate maintenance of hereditary privilege.

Peasant conditions varied with legal status, land tenure, and local manorial practice. In some regions cultivators were bound by strong personal and labor obligations; in others they possessed forms of tenure or freedom that altered bargaining power. Crown domains and noble estates were not governed in identical ways, and officials sometimes sought to prevent landlords from exhausting the labor and resources of their peasants. Frederick's own statements reveal awareness of the damage caused by excessive services and the need to keep farms occupied and productive. Yet the crown also needed stable grain supplies and noble cooperation. Restricting certain abuses was easier than dismantling a relationship of dependence embedded in tax collection, jurisdiction, and recruitment.

A peasant household's experience cannot be summarized by a legal label alone. Labor services were normally timed around agricultural seasons and could threaten a family's ability to cultivate its own plot. Cash rents mattered differently where markets were accessible than in regions dominated by obligations in labor or kind. Marriage, migration, inheritance, and the movement of children into domestic service varied according to customary rights and lordly claims. Women participated in household and field labor and bore the effects of new obligations even when official records listed a male household head. Community institutions could defend common rights, negotiate disputes, or reproduce inequalities among villagers. Such details show why protection from a landlord did not necessarily translate into equal standing in court or freedom to choose one's occupation.

Military organization integrated noble power with royal authority. A great-power army demanded officers who could command and populations from which soldiers could be raised. Recruitment arrangements affected households, labor markets, and settlement. The crown could reward aristocratic service while restricting noble autonomy in foreign policy or central administration; the result was neither simple noble independence nor simple destruction of noble privilege. Urban merchants and professionals had different opportunities, and some resented restrictions on the ownership of noble land. The social divisions that strengthened the state from the monarch's perspective could narrow political and economic options for people outside the favored estate.

Frederick also governed lands acquired by conquest. Silesia had religious, legal, and economic traditions that differed from Brandenburg; administrators sought information and loyal personnel while watching established institutions for potential Habsburg sympathies. Policies applied there could diverge from older provinces. The acquisition of territory thus increased the complexity of a state whose official ideology emphasized central direction. It is misleading to describe Prussian society as uniform or to presume every reform was enforced with equal vigor. The geography of power matters: a rural manor, a garrison town, and the royal court each presented a different face of the same monarchy.

Judging Frederick II requires holding together several realities. He valued disciplined government, backed scientific and cultural institutions, supported some changes in legal procedure, and sought to improve the material productivity of the kingdom. He also protected hereditary landed privilege, relied on coercive military structures, and expanded Prussian power through war. His legacy illustrates the central limit of enlightened absolutism: a ruler could define the public good as an efficient hierarchical state and genuinely pursue it without committing himself to social equality. The political question was who possessed the authority to define improvement and who could contest the price of achieving it.

The status of the nobility also highlights limits to a modern concept of merit. Frederick praised service, competence, and disciplined labor, but he often regarded noble birth as a qualification for military leadership and landed authority. A talented non-noble subject might be encouraged toward commerce or state employment while remaining excluded from the social role assigned to noble officers. This was not the elimination of hereditary distinctions but an attempt to make them serve the monarchy more effectively. In rural districts, noble families were not merely private proprietors; they exercised parts of the administrative and judicial authority through which government was experienced. Efforts to monitor them therefore involved tension between crown oversight and local power. The king's reluctance to transfer noble land into non-noble ownership shows the importance of estate structure to his governing vision. Better administration could restrict certain abuses without challenging the distribution of political and economic influence on which the state relied.

Division III · Maria Theresa, Joseph II, and Reform in the Habsburg Monarchy
Section 84.9

Maria Theresa and the Foundations of Habsburg Administrative Reform

After 1740 war pressure led Maria Theresa to centralize finance and provincial government and to curb some noble privileges. The General School Ordinance of 1774 established elementary schooling, though implementation was uneven, especially in rural areas. Her program stood on its own and was not merely preparation for Joseph.

Maria Theresa inherited the Habsburg hereditary monarchy in 1740 amid a succession crisis and challenges to the territorial inheritance of the Pragmatic Sanction. The loss of much of Silesia to Prussia and the expense of successive wars made administrative reform urgent. But the territories under Habsburg rule formed a composite monarchy, not a unified administrative nation. Austria, Bohemia, Hungary, the Austrian Netherlands, and Italian lands possessed different diets, privileges, courts, and fiscal arrangements. Maria Theresa could not simply impose a single model everywhere. Her administration worked through particular bargains, selective centralization, and efforts to make provincial resources more reliably available to the dynasty. These measures are often characterized as preparations for Joseph II, but her reign had its own reforming logic, shaped by war, dynastic survival, confessional commitment, and household government.

The administrative restructuring associated with Friedrich Wilhelm von Haugwitz sought more consistent military and fiscal resources from the Austrian and Bohemian lands. Central offices and provincial bodies were reorganized, and estates' traditional roles in collecting and authorizing taxes came under pressure. Wenzel Anton von Kaunitz later played a significant role in foreign and administrative policy. Yet the monarchy continued to depend on negotiated financial arrangements, landowners, trained officials, and distinct provincial institutions. Hungary's relationship with the dynasty could not be assimilated to that of Austrian or Bohemian territories without jeopardizing its constitutional privileges. The same reform decree might therefore have sharply different reach depending on its legal geography.

Maria Theresa's changes in justice and social administration also deserve independent attention. She supported legal codification projects and revised criminal procedure, but reform was gradual and internally inconsistent. The Constitutio Criminalis Theresiana of 1768 attempted systematization while retaining severe punishments and the regulated use of torture; a decisive move against judicial torture followed in 1776. This sequence shows that procedural rationalization did not immediately entail more humane penalties. Officials disagreed about whether deterrence, customary expectations, and the need to defend order justified violent practices. The state could proclaim predictability and still authorize punishments later critics would condemn. Those distinctions are essential when describing any reign as enlightened.

The General School Ordinance of December 6, 1774, developed with major assistance from Johann Ignaz von Felbiger, was a particularly ambitious institutional measure. It organized normal, main, and elementary or trivial schools with differentiated curricula and supervision. Religious instruction remained central, alongside reading, writing, and arithmetic. Its German-language administrative title should not be read as evidence that all the monarchy's children were taught identically or that the multilingual problem had been solved. Schools needed trained teachers, buildings, money, and parental cooperation. Rural families often relied on children's labor, and attendance could be seasonal or intermittent. Officials might describe educational obligations as general while implementing them unevenly across districts and social groups.

Maria Theresa's religious policy was more conservative than the reputation of later Josephine reforms sometimes suggests. She remained deeply attached to Catholic monarchy and could be hostile to religious dissent and Jewish communities. At the same time, administrative advisers recognized the economic costs of exclusion and the difficulties of governing religiously varied populations. Pragmatic adjustments did not necessarily become principled toleration. Reform of church oversight and schooling could increase the crown's influence without diminishing Catholicism's privileged standing. The case is important because it disrupts the assumption that better administration and more extensive education must accompany broader freedom of conscience.

The experience of subjects differed by estate, confession, place, and livelihood. A provincial official might welcome regular salaries and clearer instructions; a noble estate might resent new fiscal assessment; a village might gain a school but struggle to finance it; a peasant household might confront obligations unchanged by laws directed chiefly at central institutions. The history of Maria Theresa's reign is therefore one of durable organizational change within a monarchy that remained aristocratic, Catholic, and territorially diverse. Joseph II inherited both its stronger instruments of government and the resistance those instruments could generate. His reforms cannot be explained without this institutional foundation, nor can they be substituted for the reforms his mother enacted herself.

Maria Theresa's reliance on advisers also complicates monarch-centered narratives. Felbiger's schooling program, Haugwitz's administrative initiatives, and Kaunitz's policies emerged through networks of expertise and rivalry, not simply commands invented by a ruler in solitude. The monarchy drew knowledge from older ecclesiastical and provincial institutions even while limiting their independence. Its school reform is a useful case of continuity within innovation: confessional education remained important, parish communities supplied local infrastructure, and pedagogical regulation sought to impose common standards across unequal districts. Inspectors could report the number of schools or teachers, but outcomes also depended on the quality of instruction and children's attendance. Regional differences in language and funding made enforcement uncertain. A sound chapter should therefore distinguish the ordinance's legal scope, intended curriculum, and eventual influence from any assertion that every child in the Habsburg lands suddenly received standardized public education in 1774.

Section 84.10

Joseph II: Religious Toleration, Josephinism, and Church Reform

Joseph was co-ruler from 1765 but ruled the hereditary lands alone only from 1780. The Patent of Toleration (1781) permitted non-Catholic Christian worship under limits, and further measures eased some restrictions on Jewish communities. Monasteries were closed and church organization brought under state control. Toleration remained far from equality.

Joseph II became Holy Roman emperor in 1765 and shared the Habsburg governing partnership with Maria Theresa, but he did not enjoy unrestricted direction of her hereditary lands while she lived. His reforming preferences and impatience frequently conflicted with her more cautious judgment. After her death in 1780 he could pursue an accelerated program in which the sovereign's direct supervision and the reduction of provincial and corporate exceptions played major roles. The distinction between his imperial title and his authority over Habsburg territories is essential. The Holy Roman Empire encompassed numerous states outside his direct control; a reform applicable to his hereditary lands was not automatically a law for every imperial principality. Contemporary descriptions of an Austrian empire can also obscure the constitutional geography of this eighteenth-century monarchy.

The Patent of Toleration issued in October 1781 for specified Habsburg lands permitted adherents of Lutheran, Reformed, and Greek Orthodox Christianity a form of private religious exercise where public worship had been restricted. Catholicism remained the established religion with a privileged public status. Conditions on the design of worship buildings, organization of congregations, and requirements of numbers or resources could limit the practical exercise of newly granted rights. The patent did not grant universal religious equality and did not apply identically across every Habsburg territory. In some places earlier law had already secured forms of Protestant worship; elsewhere the change allowed communities that had practiced quietly to become more visible. The precise territory and legal category must accompany any account of its effects.

Joseph's policies toward Jewish subjects followed separate edicts and timetables, with significant variations between Vienna and Lower Austria, Bohemia, Moravia, Hungary, Galicia, and other lands. The January 1782 Lower Austrian edict, for example, offered regulated economic and educational opportunities while preserving constraints on Jewish residence and public communal institutions. Reforming authorities emphasized making Jewish residents economically useful, assimilating selected practices, and subjecting community life to state supervision. Jewish families could gain new avenues of livelihood without obtaining equal citizenship or freedom to maintain institutions on their own terms. Historians should reject the shorthand that a single 1781 patent emancipated all religious minorities. The distinction between legal toleration and equal civic membership is especially stark in this case.

The program known as Josephinism also reorganized the Catholic Church's place in society. Monasteries deemed insufficiently engaged in teaching, pastoral care, or social utility were suppressed, and some resources were redirected toward parishes and state-supervised functions. Seminaries and clerical training came under closer royal oversight; episcopal and papal authority encountered expanded administrative claims. Joseph and advisers presented these changes as ways of making religion serve the population more directly. Yet monastery closures affected livelihoods, local charity, pilgrimage, cultural life, and established patterns of devotion. Religious resistance arose not only from conservative elites but also from worshippers for whom familiar institutions mattered. A policy whose fiscal purpose was clear at court could carry different meanings in a town or parish.

Toleration and ecclesiastical regulation therefore advanced together. Joseph could loosen restrictions on a Protestant congregation and at the same time impose new rules on Catholic worship, charitable institutions, and clergy. Both reflected the premise that the state had authority to decide the external organization of religion in the public interest. This is not the same as the liberal idea that the state should withdraw from matters of conscience. In Joseph's system the crown acted as a judge of useful and unnecessary practices. Some reforms were welcomed by groups previously constrained; others created anxiety among communities that feared losing control of their own affairs.

Opposition also arose from the form and pace of legislation. Provincial estates feared the erosion of customary guarantees; bishops and monks disputed legal competence; village clergy encountered demands that seemed to conflict with pastoral realities. Orders issued in Vienna had to be translated, circulated, interpreted, and enforced in territories with different legal memories. Joseph's personal energy magnified the breadth of the program but made adjustment difficult. The history of his religious reforms should therefore evaluate both the genuine widening of permissions for some minorities and the political fragility produced by central direction, rapid change, and unequal participation in decision-making.

Joseph's monastery policy is sometimes portrayed as an unambiguous transfer of resources from contemplation to social usefulness. In practice, identifying which houses performed valuable work required controversial judgments. A religious foundation that seemed insufficiently productive to officials might support local memory, care, or informal education. Conversely, wealth concentrated in institutions beyond effective oversight raised concerns about public accountability. Local residents could disagree with their priests or bishops, and different monastic houses had differing functions. The same complexity applies to toleration: a non-Catholic worshipper might gain a lawful meeting place but encounter new limits on public display, or a Jewish merchant might obtain a license while the community's institutional autonomy contracted. Joseph's policies were not merely symbols of progress or persecution. They redistributed recognized forms of authority, and their actual effects are best reconstructed by comparing petitions from beneficiaries and opponents with the wording of the patents. In surviving official notices, commands could be phrased as universally binding while practice remained contingent on who lived close to an administrative center. A Protestant family in a town with sufficient co-religionists could organize worship more readily than isolated villagers; a Jewish family in Vienna faced rules unlike those governing families elsewhere in the monarchy. A government that recognized a religious community often also required lists of its members, officials, and properties. That registration served both practical protection and official surveillance. Joseph's claim to improve religion by making it socially useful created new channels for some worshippers and reduced older forms of institutional autonomy for others. The documentary test is whether one can identify the exact local order, the groups covered, the officers charged with implementation, and the records of actual compliance or complaint.

Section 84.11

Joseph II's Agrarian, Judicial, Fiscal, and Administrative Reforms

The Patent on Serfdom (1781) ended personal servile dependence in parts of the monarchy, including limits on marriage and movement restrictions, but did not abolish all feudal obligations such as labor dues. Joseph pursued tax and judicial change and administrative uniformity, including greater use of German in central administration. Effects differed among Bohemia, Austria, Hungary, Galicia, and other lands.

Joseph's measures concerning personal dependence illustrate why legal emancipation must be defined precisely. The Patent on Serfdom of November 1, 1781, in Bohemia, Moravia, and related jurisdictions curtailed forms of personal subjection that had restricted marriage, movement, occupational choice, and service. The exact provisions varied with territory and administrative application. The famous reform did not abolish all land dues, rents, or compulsory labor services owed through manorial tenure. The patent itself preserved the robot, or labor obligation, and payments connected with holdings. A tenant could acquire greater freedom to seek work or marry while continuing to owe substantial labor to a lord. Describing 1781 as the abolition of feudalism throughout the monarchy confuses personal legal status with agrarian property relations and overlooks the difference between decree and enforcement.

For rural families, the difference nonetheless could be significant. A marriage previously dependent on a lord's permission might become possible with notification; a young person could pursue a craft or leave certain domestic service obligations. These changes modified the relations of bargaining and household planning. But a family's farm remained subject to the rhythms of compulsory services, local fees, and estate jurisdiction. A poor household without land might gain legal mobility without the resources to move, while a well-situated cultivator could use new rights more effectively. Women and children experienced these reforms through marriage, inheritance, work, and domestic arrangements, not simply through the formal status of a male head of household. Regional and social distinctions are indispensable to a credible assessment.

Joseph also sought changes in taxation, land registration, and relations between landlords and peasant cultivators. Attempts to assess land more uniformly threatened noble exemptions and complicated systems of dues. Fiscal reform could be defended as fairer if burdens shifted away from privileged groups, yet it generated resistance where estates feared loss of income or authority. Local measurements and classifications were contested; village representatives and landowners could disagree about the productivity assigned to particular plots. An ambitious proposal did not necessarily become a stable fiscal system. Some land and tax measures were weakened or reversed as the monarchy approached political crisis near the end of Joseph's reign.

Administrative uniformity extended to language, office procedure, and the structure of provincial government. Joseph's promotion of German in important functions of central administration was intended to ease coordination but could be perceived in Hungary and other lands as an assault on political identity and customary privileges. Latin continued to have institutional significance in parts of the monarchy, and multiple vernacular languages shaped everyday life. The use of German thus was more than a technical convenience. It raised the question of whether administrative efficiency justified overriding institutions that represented historical communities, even when those institutions were themselves exclusive or dominated by elites.

Criminal justice and civil administration received attention as well. Joseph sought to rationalize penal procedure and to reduce reliance on certain cruel punishments, although detailed rules and their dates require careful distinction from the later histories of neighboring states. Central authorities demanded more regular reporting from officials and sought to reduce the gap between the monarch's will and provincial action. Yet the accumulation of mandates created its own bottlenecks. Judges and district officers needed translations, instructions, personnel, and resources; litigants had to navigate new procedures while older relationships persisted. For subjects, predictable justice depended on practical access, not only a statute's declared ambition.

The most revealing comparison is between the scale of Joseph's intentions and the social structure through which they operated. Estates collected revenues and guarded privileges, nobles controlled manorial resources, clergy served communities, and local officials interpreted law. A ruler could abolish a category of personal subjection more readily than he could reorganize landownership and agricultural labor overnight. The effects of the 1781 measures were therefore real but bounded. Their partial survival after Joseph's death further complicates judgments of success or failure: political reversal did not extinguish every reform. The history of Joseph's social policy is a history of changing rights within institutions that retained unequal economic power.

Questions about robot obligations were not only economic but legal and social. Different manors calculated required days or services in different ways, and local practices could determine the actual burden. A rule granting freedom of marriage, migration, or occupational choice was difficult to enforce where the person seeking to exercise it depended on a landlord for land, credit, or protection. Historical inquiry must therefore examine how households used new permissions and how local authorities responded when customary power was challenged. Complaints and administrative appeals can offer evidence, but surviving petitions privilege people who knew a procedure existed and could use it. Some peasants may have experienced improved mobility; others continued within the same labor regime with little practical leverage. Joseph's efforts to restructure taxation and labor obligations were contested partly because they threatened the revenues and expectations of landholders. Reform did not occur on an empty legal field. It entered a network of contracts, customs, and coercive dependencies whose alteration required more than official commitment.

Section 84.12

Reform, Resistance, and Reversal in the Habsburg Lands

Nobles, clergy, estates, officials, and some peasants resisted. Hungary resisted central uniformity, and in the Austrian Netherlands the Brabant Revolution (1789 to 1790) overthrew Habsburg rule for a time. Even measures that offered material gains could provoke opposition when imposed from the center. Facing crisis, Joseph reversed many measures shortly before his death in 1790, though the toleration and serfdom patents survived in important respects.

Resistance to Joseph II did not arise from a single conservative conspiracy. It included aristocrats defending tax and jurisdictional privileges, clergy protecting religious institutions, provincial estates asserting constitutional procedure, urban groups concerned about local autonomy, and ordinary communities unsettled by changes in worship or obligation. Their interests could conflict with one another. A noble diet might invoke the language of ancient liberty while excluding peasants from deliberation; villagers might welcome relief from a landlord and simultaneously oppose a policy that altered customary festivals or parish life. Describing all opposition as irrational obscures the political problem: the sovereign claimed a public benefit, but those affected often lacked accepted channels for negotiating how it would be delivered.

Hungary was a particularly difficult setting for administrative uniformity. Its constitutional traditions, political language, noble privileges, and relationships with the Habsburg ruler differed from those of the Austrian hereditary lands. Joseph's decisions on language, tax assessment, and provincial governance raised questions about the authority of the Hungarian Diet and the kingdom's laws. Opposition sharpened toward the end of his reign and approached open revolt, but it should not be casually equated with the revolution in the Austrian Netherlands. Hungary's crisis produced petitions, estate mobilization, and negotiations in a distinct political setting. Joseph withdrew important measures in an effort to stabilize the situation, while some reforms concerning toleration and personal status were retained.

In the Austrian Netherlands, resistance escalated into the Brabant Revolution of 1789–1790. The provinces had their own historic constitutional guarantees and political institutions, and the imperial government's interventions threatened rights defended by corporate and religious leaders. Opposition brought together groups that did not necessarily share a modern liberal program. Conservatives and reform-minded participants could cooperate in defending provincial autonomy against Vienna, though they envisioned different futures. Habsburg authority was overthrown for a time, and a short-lived political union emerged. These events demonstrate why reform cannot be evaluated solely by its content: methods of imposition, historical expectations, and who held legitimate decision-making power mattered profoundly.

Joseph's broader program also met practical limits. Closing monasteries could provoke local religious anger; school reform required a sustainable teaching workforce; attempts to reorder serf obligations affected noble revenue; and central decrees created administrative congestion. The emperor's habit of moving rapidly across domains left little time for regional consultation. Officials might respond by issuing contradictory clarifications or seeking informal exceptions. Disputes over church bells, sermons, village rites, or the language of records could become symbols of larger fears about the transformation of society. These details are not peripheral to the history of reform: they explain why a measure praised by advisers in Vienna could be despised by the people expected to implement it.

In the last months of his life Joseph rescinded or suspended many controversial measures. He died in February 1790, leaving a monarchy still facing territorial tensions and wars. His successor Leopold II pursued stabilization through selective compromise. It would nevertheless be misleading to describe the reign as a complete failure. Religious toleration and alterations to personal serfdom survived in important jurisdictions, and administrative practices introduced under Joseph and Maria Theresa influenced later government. Other initiatives were reversed or modified. The survival pattern requires attention to each jurisdiction and policy, rather than one verdict delivered upon the ruler's personality.

The Habsburg experience highlights a general historical problem. Reform imposed from above could expand the rights of individual subjects while reducing the recognized authority of bodies that claimed to protect a province's constitution. Sometimes those bodies represented privileged elites; sometimes they also embodied meaningful limits on arbitrary government. A court attempting to remove manorial abuses could be accused of attacking ancient law, while its critics might have genuine complaints about procedural legality. Historians should avoid treating centralization as inherently progressive or resistance as necessarily reactionary. What happened depended on whose rights were at stake, which provisions were enforced, and what political arrangements replaced the old ones. Joseph's legacy lies in this unresolved mixture of innovation, coercion, resistance, and partial endurance.

A second problem was political language. Provincial estates could defend liberty while meaning freedom from central interference for a narrowly privileged community. Ministers in Vienna could defend equality while meaning uniform treatment of provinces for purposes of taxation or administration, rather than equal participation by individuals. These different meanings explain why reform disputes cannot be organized into a simple opposition between liberal rulers and conservative subjects. In the Austrian Netherlands, arguments about ancient constitutional guarantees became vehicles for broad mobilization, although participants disagreed over religious and social reform. In Hungary, the defense of customary rights operated through a different institutional framework. The historian must locate the speaker, audience, and legal tradition of each claim. Joseph's setbacks exposed the absence of mechanisms for translating ambitious centralized legislation into accepted agreements across a composite monarchy. His failure to secure durable consent for parts of the program was itself a political consequence, separate from the moral value of particular reforms.

Division IV · Catherine II and Enlightened Government in the Russian Empire
Section 84.13

Catherine II, Philosophical Correspondence, and the Nakaz of 1767

Catherine took the throne in 1762 and cultivated European intellectuals. Her Nakaz, addressed to the Legislative Commission of 1767, drew on Montesquieu and Beccaria. The Commission offered consultation, not parliamentary representation, and produced no comprehensive code.

Catherine II came to the Russian throne in 1762 after the overthrow of Peter III. Her accession depended on support from influential court and military circles, and her subsequent legitimacy was never entirely separable from that political origin. She cultivated a European image of enlightened sovereignty through correspondence, literary patronage, collecting, and the language of rational law. Diderot and Voltaire were among the thinkers with whom she associated herself. These exchanges mattered because they helped present Russia as an active participant in European intellectual culture, but they do not establish that philosophical writers controlled Russian policy. Catherine governed a vast imperial society marked by different legal orders, religious communities, and forms of servile labor. The practical needs of the crown and its elite allies often pointed away from the universal claims found in reforming prose.

The Nakaz, or Instruction, of 1767 is her most celebrated attempt to articulate a legal philosophy. Written substantially in French and drawing selectively on Montesquieu, Beccaria, and other writers, it instructed a commission summoned to consider a new law code. It criticized aspects of cruelty and arbitrariness and proposed principles concerning lawful punishment, public order, and the organization of justice. Yet the document also presented autocratic authority as appropriate to Russia's size and circumstances. Its authorship was royal, and the selection of intellectual sources was deliberate. Catherine could draw on a thinker who analyzed restraints on monarchy without accepting every implication of constitutional limitation. That selective borrowing is an important example of how Enlightenment argument was adapted to the needs of government.

The Legislative Commission assembled deputies from several estates, territories, and institutions and produced evidence of the grievances and expectations of parts of imperial society. It was not a modern parliament with authority to legislate independently of the sovereign; significant populations, especially privately owned serfs, had no equal political voice. Deputies represented legally defined constituencies under prescribed rules rather than individual citizens under a universal franchise. Their instructions and debates nonetheless reveal a variety of interests, from noble claims to merchant concerns and regional demands. Catherine learned about the empire through an institution that also helped advertise her commitment to orderly government. The Commission did not produce the comprehensive code imagined in its assignment, and its operations were disrupted as international and domestic priorities changed.

The Nakaz's European circulation added another dimension. Translations made its principles available to readers who would never see Russian administrative records. Intellectual observers could praise the empress for adopting rational legislation even when Russian subjects had little opportunity to enforce those principles against powerful officials or landlords. The distinction between external reputation and internal practice matters especially for a ruler whose political authority depended on a court elite and a military establishment. A printed instruction could become evidence of intellectual ambition without functioning as a constitution or a code. Historians compare it with judicial records, provincial administration, the status of serfs, and the crown's responses to dissent in order to assess its practical significance.

Catherine's interest in education and culture was also genuine but selective. She patronized institutions and proposed changes that might cultivate more useful and refined subjects. Court culture displayed the language and forms of European learning, while schools and academies developed within an empire where opportunities differed sharply by estate, city, religion, and gender. The intellectual ambition of the court did not mean that the rural population could participate in the same learned networks. It was easier for a monarch to sponsor a library or correspond with philosophers than to alter the fiscal and legal foundations of a society dependent on bonded labor.

The central historical problem is therefore not whether Catherine really read or admired Enlightenment writers. The evidence shows that she engaged with their ideas. The more important question is what happened when an autocrat selected some of those ideas while rejecting demands incompatible with her understanding of sovereignty and her dependence on elite support. The Nakaz is best read as an aspirational legislative text, a political statement to domestic elites, and an international instrument of prestige. Its failure to become a complete code was not evidence that it was meaningless; nor does its existence demonstrate that the Russian Empire had acquired a rule-bound, representative constitutional order.

The Commission's proceedings also expose how representation differs from consultation. Deputies who carried instructions from corporate constituencies could describe local conditions and propose improvements without being authorized to challenge the sovereign source of legislation. Their presence made a government better informed while preserving a hierarchy of political authority. Nobles had opportunities to present grievances that privately owned serfs did not possess on equal terms. This imbalance shaped the kind of information the crown could gather and the solutions the Commission considered plausible. Reading the Nakaz alongside the deputies' instructions permits students to ask whether a highly articulate legislative philosophy changed the categories of people who counted as political participants. The answer is mixed: the empire acquired a notable forum for gathering and presenting petitions, but that forum did not transform sovereignty or eliminate estate distinctions. This makes it a model case for distinguishing a reformer's intellectual ambition from an institutional transfer of power.

Section 84.14

Provincial Government, Courts, Education, and Administrative Reorganization

The provincial statute of 1775 multiplied local offices, courts, and policing in a vast, diverse empire. Education and municipal institutions also expanded. Implementation depended on provincial elites, officials, and corporate bodies, who supplied much of the personnel.

The scale of Catherine's empire made provincial government a central problem. The court in Saint Petersburg faced substantial distances, uneven roads, seasonal travel, widely different settlement patterns, and communities governed through distinct religious and customary arrangements. Orders from the center depended on provincial officers and local elites who might lack trained staff or reliable information. A governor could be given responsibility without adequate resources; judges and police officials might operate across enormous territories; tax collection and enforcement required cooperation from landlords and towns. Reform aimed to make the empire more intelligible and manageable, but standard administrative maps could not abolish its geographical and social complexity. The task was as much one of assigning responsibility and creating durable channels of reporting as of issuing new substantive laws.

The provincial reform statute of 1775 was adopted after the Pugachev rebellion had revealed weaknesses in local oversight and public security. It reorganized governmental divisions and multiplied offices responsible for justice, administration, policing, and welfare functions. The reform sought a denser framework through which subjects might petition and officials might report. More offices did not necessarily mean more impartiality. Personnel had to be recruited and paid, and landowners retained significant influence in the social world in which offices operated. In some districts the new institutional architecture could improve the crown's ability to receive information and respond to disorder; in others old connections continued to determine access to protection and the outcome of disputes.

Courts illustrate the ambivalence of classification by estate. Institutions could be organized to reflect distinctions among nobles, townspeople, and other groups, producing more regular procedures within a hierarchy rather than removing the hierarchy. Officials needed knowledge of applicable rules, records of property, and the authority to enforce decisions. A serf challenging an estate manager was not situated like a noble litigating with another landowner. The existence of a courthouse on a provincial administrative map does not establish the availability of remedies to those without economic independence. The question is not merely how many offices Catherine created but what jurisdiction they possessed, which groups could use them, and whether the crown could supervise local power.

Catherine also supported education and the institutionalization of urban life. The 1780s saw initiatives in public schooling and new arrangements for municipal corporate groups. These projects drew on ideas of state utility, moral education, and orderly civic administration. Practical results depended on teachers, funds, books, and the willingness of local communities to participate. A central urban school in a provincial capital served a different population from children in a remote peasant village. Education was therefore not automatically empire-wide in reach, and promotion of instruction should not be mistaken for universal access. Similar differences characterized health provision, poor relief, and the government's efforts to regulate trade and markets.

The 1785 Charter to the Towns granted defined corporate arrangements to urban populations classified by occupation and status. It was an attempt to give municipal life a more legible institutional order, though it did not create equal political citizenship. Corporate categories could strengthen some merchants' and proprietors' influence while excluding poorer residents from effective decision-making. Urban reform was connected to fiscal administration because towns generated taxes, customs, trade, and services to the state. It also reflected the monarchy's interest in classifying people for governmental purposes. The language of civic rights thus needs to be read alongside the legal conditions attached to status and registration.

The contrast between institutional design and operation was especially marked in frontier territories. Imperial expansion incorporated communities with their own elites, religions, languages, and property relations. Uniform offices could coexist with negotiated local arrangements, and governors exercised discretion far from the capital. A provincial reform might strengthen the center's knowledge without displacing the coercive power of a landed proprietor. Catherine's administrative history is best understood as a sustained effort to govern through more structured provincial channels while remaining dependent on hierarchical relationships that reform did not fundamentally abolish.

A governor's power was also constrained by incentives. Officials might seek additional staff or money by exaggerating local disorder; noble landowners might demand stronger policing but resist inquiries into their own management of peasants. The central government tried to coordinate these conflicting perspectives with more regular divisions and reporting. Yet many forms of conflict were resolved informally, and neither official maps nor printed instructions could reveal every settlement between landlord, village, and provincial authority. The historian should use the 1775 statute as evidence of designed government, then compare it with records of appointments, lawsuits, town administration, and public security. Catherine's new provincial structure could endure as an administrative framework even when some of its promised services remained limited. Institutional persistence is not the same as universal effectiveness, but it can explain why later Russian governments operated within patterns established in her reign.

Section 84.15

Serfdom, Pugachev's Rebellion, and the Privileges of the Nobility

The Pugachev rebellion (1773 to 1775) revealed the fragility of provincial order. The Charters to the Nobility and to the Towns (1785) confirmed corporate rights of different beneficiaries. Catherine did not emancipate serfs, since noble cooperation underpinned imperial government.

Serfdom was the great contradiction of Catherine's enlightened reputation because it placed large numbers of people under obligations enforced through landed property and noble power. The precise conditions of servitude differed between privately owned serfs, state peasants, and other groups, and even among estates. Some cultivators performed labor services, others paid rents or cash dues, and many encountered a combination of burdens. The legal dependence of privately held serfs could limit movement, marriage, and recourse against landlords. In such a society an argument about improving legislation did not automatically translate into an equal right to contest a landlord's demands. The crown's reliance on the nobility gave this inequality institutional resilience. Officials could regard the orderly administration of estates as part of public welfare while leaving the basic relation of domination unchallenged.

The rebellion associated with Yemelyan Pugachev from 1773 to 1775 exposed strains in the social and imperial order. Its participants were not an undifferentiated body of Russian peasants. Cossacks, peasants, workers, and members of several communities in the Volga and Ural regions joined at different moments for different reasons. The revolt drew upon grievances concerning service, labor, property, local government, and cultural or religious pressures; Pugachev's claim to be Peter III provided a dynastic language for opposition. The movement's breadth frightened the government and revealed that the imperial center could not reliably control distant regions through existing intermediaries. Its suppression involved extensive coercion and was followed by efforts to tighten provincial government and improve the crown's capacity to identify and preempt unrest.

Catherine's response did not lead to general serf emancipation. Noble cooperation was critical to the operation of the empire, from provincial administration to military service and court politics. Far-reaching interference with noble estate relations could alienate the people through whom the monarchy governed much of the countryside. The 1785 Charter to the Nobility formalized corporate privileges, personal status advantages, and protection of noble property, reinforcing the social framework of imperial rule. It is important to distinguish privileges granted or confirmed to nobles from changes affecting the peasantry. Calling the charter a reform is not inaccurate, since it altered and clarified corporate rights, but it was not egalitarian reform. It demonstrated how clearer law could strengthen unequal institutions.

The Charter to the Towns, adopted the same year, concerned different corporate populations and should not be merged with the noble charter as though they created a common civic status. Urban merchants and other legally defined groups gained forms of organization according to rank and property. The project expressed an ideal of ordered estates within an administratively legible empire. For an individual who gained access to a municipal body, this could matter. For someone outside the privileged categories, the same institutional reform could reinforce exclusion. The distinction between codifying a recognized corporate identity and granting equal personal rights must remain clear.

Catherine did at times address abuses and contemplate improvements in the administration of peasant life. Yet a ruler's displeasure with a cruel landlord was not the same as a system in which all cultivators could legally challenge their dependence. The capacity of officials to discipline estate owners remained limited by patronage and local power. Claims made in the Nakaz about justice and humanity therefore require comparison with legal status, petitions, and provincial enforcement. Moral language could coexist with coercive institutions not because ideas never mattered but because reforming monarchs selected measures within political arrangements they intended to preserve.

The historical lesson is sharper than a simple verdict of hypocrisy. Catherine's government changed important aspects of administration and law and participated in European debate about enlightened rule. It also confirmed noble corporate interests and declined to transform the structure of serfholding society. These were parts of the same political settlement. The rebellion and the charters illuminate why the crown preferred stability through privileged intermediaries to a general transfer of rights to dependent peasants. Any assessment of Catherine's reform must therefore ask whose security and property were protected, who enforced the relevant laws, and who remained without the ability to hold rulers and landlords accountable.

The legal language of a charter deserves particular scrutiny. Catherine's nobility charter was framed as a statement of rights and freedoms, yet its beneficiaries formed a hereditary estate possessing resources and authority denied to most people. Historians should not assume that the term rights necessarily indicated rights held by all subjects. In eighteenth-century corporate society, an estate could possess privileges that a subordinate group could not claim. The charter also underscores the reciprocal political arrangement between crown and nobility: confirmed protections helped secure elite cooperation with administration and the maintenance of order. A source window should ask how a noble understood the document and what a privately owned serf would find absent from it. Such questions bring the connection between Enlightenment rhetoric, legal classification, and coercive social relations into view without forcing the text into a modern democratic vocabulary.

Empire, Religious Diversity, Frontier Government, and Catherine's Contradictions

Catherine governed Orthodox Christians, Muslims, and others, and incorporated new territories including lands from the partitions of Poland. Policy mixed accommodation, settlement, coercion, and cultural patronage, with limits on dissent. Details of diplomacy and war belong to other chapters and to the Russian History textbook.

The Russian Empire's diversity created a set of political questions that cannot be answered by describing the policies of an Orthodox court alone. Catherine ruled territories inhabited by Orthodox Christians, Muslims, Catholics, Protestants, and other communities, and by peoples whose customary arrangements differed in land tenure, law, and political authority. Expansion toward the Black Sea and territorial gains connected to the partitions of Poland brought new populations under imperial administration. The incorporation of Crimea in 1783, for example, altered the sovereignty and circumstances of the Crimean Tatars and neighboring societies. State planners presented settlement, surveys, and the establishment of ports as improvements, but annexation also involved loss of political independence and reordering of property relations. The imperial setting therefore makes the question of who defined public welfare unavoidable.

Governance in Muslim regions illustrates the interaction between accommodation and supervision. The crown sometimes recognized local religious leaders and sought stable arrangements for Islamic institutions while also using administrative bodies to regulate religious life. Measures associated with recognized Muslim clerical structures near the end of the eighteenth century should not be simplified into modern religious equality. State-sponsored organization could give communities channels for worship or adjudication while increasing official oversight and limiting independent authority. Officials balanced concerns about frontier stability, taxation, recruitment, migration, and the loyalty of local elites. The resulting policies differed over time and territory, reflecting pragmatic government as much as philosophical commitments.

Newly incorporated lands from the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth created other challenges. Population groups brought with them existing noble privileges, Jewish communal structures, confessional institutions, and different legal expectations. The Russian state had to determine which arrangements would be preserved, revised, or restricted. Policies affecting Jewish settlement and occupation changed in relation to annexation and administration, and later measures should not be projected indiscriminately into the 1760s. A government that categorized subjects by religion and estate could provide limited protection to groups while also constraining migration and economic life. It is historically misleading to treat every group within the empire as experiencing Catherine's reign through the same institutions.

Imperial expansion required soldiers, transport, state finance, and settler populations. Plans for roads, ports, agriculture, and urban development were linked to military security and imperial competition. People living in frontier regions often experienced reform as a change in authority rather than an improvement of an existing institution. Officials might see an ordered provincial grid where inhabitants saw confiscated grazing areas or unfamiliar courts. Indigenous and locally rooted communities exercised agency through negotiation, petition, migration, evasion, service, or resistance, depending on their options. The assumption that populations were passive recipients of improvement would reproduce the royal administrative viewpoint rather than a historical account of imperial society.

Catherine's cultural patronage also projected an image of a European sovereign commanding useful knowledge. Cartography, collecting, academies, and correspondence helped give the court a place in international intellectual networks. Scientific and geographical inquiries could support government and commerce, but they were not neutral in their relationship to empire. Maps could clarify routes and resources while also supporting claims of possession. Translators and local informants contributed knowledge that later appeared under official names. The historian must attend to the several participants in knowledge production rather than crediting the center alone.

The comparison with Prussia and the Habsburg monarchy reveals a common feature and an important difference. All relied on intermediary elites, administrative reports, and military power. Russia's continental diversity and frontier expansion made the coexistence of differentiated legal statuses especially conspicuous. Catherine's government advanced selected educational and administrative reforms while defending autocracy, noble privilege, and imperial enlargement. Its treatment of religious and social difference was both pragmatic and unequal. This does not erase the changes that occurred, but it prevents the title enlightened monarch from becoming a substitute for analyzing conquest, hierarchy, and the lived experience of imperial subjects.

Knowledge itself was part of imperial government. Surveyors, military engineers, scholars, and administrators gathered information about climate, language, commerce, and population, often relying on translators and local informants. A report that entered a court archive could influence a settlement order or taxation plan, but the perspective of the community being described might disappear from the final record. This problem is especially important when studying newly annexed territories. What appeared to a governor as the rational allocation of land could be experienced locally as the loss of customary access. Describing Catherine's empire through central statutes alone risks reproducing the administrative gaze of the monarchy. Balanced historical analysis asks which knowledge was collected, whose interests it served, and whether local communities could contest decisions. In this respect the imperial setting magnifies the broader contradiction between useful information and the unequal distribution of decision-making power.

Portugal: Pombal, Royal Authority, Education, and Reconstruction

Pombal served King Joseph I. After the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 he directed reconstruction, commercial and fiscal reform, expelled the Jesuits (1759), reorganized censorship and schooling, and reformed the University of Coimbra (1772). Improvement and ministerial coercion went together, within a colonial empire.

Portugal offers a distinctive example because reform operated primarily through the powerful minister Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later Marquis of Pombal, under King Joseph I. The Lisbon earthquake of November 1755 destroyed large parts of the capital and created an immediate crisis of shelter, provisioning, policing, and commercial recovery. The subsequent reconstruction demonstrated the government's ability to mobilize engineers, administrators, money, and labor for a highly visible project. It also strengthened the minister's political position. The rebuilt urban plan is sometimes praised as a triumph of rational design, but it must be understood as the outcome of disaster, emergency authority, property disputes, and a distinctive court political environment. For survivors, rebuilding involved lost homes and businesses, forced choices about property, and years of changing urban space, not simply the fulfillment of a geometric ideal.

Pombal pursued fiscal and commercial initiatives intended to increase royal revenue, strengthen domestic production, and make economic activity less dependent on external interests. State-backed trading companies, regulation of commerce, and efforts to improve manufacturing involved merchants and colonial producers whose interests were not identical. Some initiatives conferred advantages on particular firms or regions while imposing restrictions on others. Portuguese reform was inseparable from Brazil and the wider empire, including systems of coerced labor and the production of commodities for Atlantic markets. A narrative that celebrates metropolitan improvement without examining colonial extraction would misidentify both the resources and the costs of policy. The government treated empire as a source of revenue, strategic advantage, and administrative problems requiring new controls.

Relations with the church were among the central political conflicts. Pombal's government expelled the Jesuits from Portuguese territories in 1759 and reorganized functions previously carried out by the order. The expulsion was connected to ministerial struggles over political authority, missions, education, and accusations concerning loyalty to the crown. It cannot be reduced to a modern preference for secular schooling. The Jesuits had operated educational and missionary institutions whose closure created practical problems of staffing and curriculum. Measures against the order also affected colonial missions, Indigenous communities, and local relationships of power. New government oversight did not by itself guarantee that educational services improved for everyone who had used them.

The Real Mesa Censória, created in 1768, illustrates the paradox of a reforming ministry that sought intellectual modernization while tightening centralized control over publication. Authority over the examination and licensing of books was reorganized in a royal framework. The government could promote scientific and administrative texts and restrict writing judged dangerous to religion, morality, or the state. Thus censorship did not simply vanish under the influence of the Enlightenment; it could become more centralized and more closely attached to the ministerial project. The contradiction is apparent only if one assumes that improving knowledge necessarily meant permitting free discussion of governmental power. Pombal's reforms operated according to a narrower concept of useful knowledge.

The University of Coimbra reforms of 1772 were particularly ambitious. New statutes, prepared through royal and academic commissions, reorganized the curriculum with greater attention to mathematics, natural philosophy, medicine, and practical study. Reform was not the work of Pombal alone: João Pereira Ramos de Azeredo Coutinho and Francisco de Lemos were among important contributors to the preparation and implementation. New courses and facilities required staff, texts, instruments, and institutional changes. The transformation of higher learning is significant, but a change at Coimbra did not automatically create widespread schooling in the countryside or equal educational access across social classes. The history of implementation is essential to assessing what the statutes achieved.

Pombal's state-building combined administrative skill with coercion. His power depended on royal favor, and opposition could be met through prosecution, censorship, and exclusion from office. The accession of Maria I in 1777 changed the court environment and led to his dismissal and a reconsideration of aspects of his administration. Some initiatives persisted, while other policies and reputations were revised. Portugal therefore demonstrates both the durability of certain educational and administrative changes and the fragility of a program attached to a powerful minister. Its enlightened dimensions cannot be separated from the centralized and often coercive methods by which reforms were undertaken.

The reforms at Coimbra show the difference between curricular design and social access. A university capable of teaching experimental science or revised medicine required qualified professors, collections, laboratory equipment, and students prepared by earlier schooling. A royal statute could mandate departments, but changes in secondary education and the supply of books determined how widely the reform could reach. The disruption of Jesuit teaching also created an institutional transition that cannot be described as a simple replacement of ignorance by knowledge. Scholars and instructors operated on both sides of the political dispute. By comparing the surviving 1772 statutes with appointments, finances, and later student records, historians can separate the reformers' aspirations from the actual experience of higher education. The Portuguese case is particularly useful because the same ministry that promoted specialized learning centralized censorship, demonstrating that a government could encourage selected forms of knowledge while circumscribing criticism of its own authority.

Spain: Charles III, Bourbon Reform, the Church, and Imperial Administration

Charles III's ministers, among them Campomanes, Floridablanca, Aranda, and Esquilache, pursued fiscal reform, economic societies, and municipal improvement. The Esquilache riots (1766) preceded the Jesuits' expulsion (1767). Reform extended to the American empire, where colonial labor, Indigenous communities, and resistance were part of the system.

Under Charles III, Spain experienced a broad program of Bourbon reform that involved royal ministers, provincial officials, military planners, church leaders, municipal authorities, and imperial administrators. The king had previously ruled Naples and Sicily and brought administrative experience to Madrid when he took the Spanish throne in 1759. His ministers included Leopoldo de Gregorio, Marquis of Esquilache, as well as Campomanes, Aranda, Floridablanca, and others whose responsibilities and influence changed over time. Treating their policies as a single coherent blueprint would be misleading. Reforms followed crises, court struggles, war, and shifts in priorities, while the Crown sought to strengthen revenue, royal jurisdiction, urban services, and imperial defense. The political aim was usually more effective Bourbon monarchy rather than the creation of a government answerable to a representative citizen body.

Urban reform provides a useful entry point because its consequences were visible in everyday life. Measures concerning street lighting, sanitation, clothing, policing, and public order could be justified as improvements to health and safety. The Esquilache disturbances of 1766, however, revealed the risk of imposing unfamiliar regulations amid anxieties over food prices, ministerial power, and local custom. An edict restricting long cloaks and broad hats became a symbol of wider grievances. The unrest was not merely a comic resistance to fashionable dress, nor was it a universal revolt against improvement. Bread prices, perceptions of foreign advisers, and the manner of royal intervention helped shape the crisis. The removal of Esquilache demonstrated that even a powerful monarchy had to reckon with subjects who could act collectively in the streets.

Relations with the Catholic Church were central. In 1767 the Spanish crown ordered the expulsion of the Jesuits from Spain and its overseas territories and the seizure of their property. The decision followed complex conflicts involving political loyalty, educational control, and the aftermath of disorder. The decree was an assertion of royal power with a vast practical reach. It affected colleges, missions, local economies, and religious communities on several continents. Royal officials had to coordinate deportation, property inventories, and replacements for displaced personnel. Students should distinguish the crown's stated reasons from historians' explanations of the decision and from the experiences of those subjected to it. A government could rationalize authority over the church while intensifying punishment and censorship.

Bourbon reform also included attention to agriculture, manufactures, the circulation of goods, and provincial government. Economic societies encouraged discussion of techniques, schooling, and useful knowledge, although participation was socially selective. Fiscal measures generated disputes about who should pay, and the monarchy's ability to collect revenue varied among regions. Spain was not a uniform country administered from a single template. Historic privileges and local institutions remained important, and programs advanced through negotiations that could alter their original designs. Some ministers favored wider commercial exchange; others protected particular industries or sought more dependable fiscal returns. Administrative improvement therefore needs to be measured through implementation and distribution, not through the existence of reformist correspondence or royal patronage.

The American empire was central, not peripheral, to these policies. Following the Seven Years' War and the temporary British seizure of Havana, the Bourbon monarchy strengthened military defenses and reorganized parts of colonial administration. New intendancies and fiscal measures sought more efficient government and greater revenue from American possessions. Different initiatives developed at different dates and under different ministers, especially José de Gálvez. Colonial merchants, Indigenous communities, enslaved Africans, settlers, and local elites experienced these programs differently. New inspections or tax demands could intensify extraction, while administrative improvements might create opportunities for particular urban or commercial groups. Imperial reforms should not be presented as a gift of modernization made by Spain to passive colonies; they were negotiated, resisted, and reshaped by people across the Atlantic.

The Spanish case resembles Portugal in the effort to control church institutions and imperial revenue, but differences in political structure, scale, and colonial governance mattered. It also resembles Prussia and Austria in the tension between a ruler's public-good claims and entrenched corporate privilege. The unrest of 1766 and the expulsion of the Jesuits show that reform did not eliminate conflict; it could reorganize conflict around new assertions of sovereignty. A credible assessment of Charles III's reign recognizes significant investment in administration, defense, urban improvements, and education while also examining coercion, unequal political access, and the fiscal burden borne by imperial subjects.

The timing of imperial reform is crucial. Military setbacks in the Seven Years' War stimulated attention to Atlantic defense, revenue, and the staffing of colonial offices, but measures were rolled out unevenly. An intendancy established in one province did not mean all overseas subjects suddenly encountered the same administrative system. Provincial elites could bargain, resist, or cooperate, and the effects of new fiscal assessments differed between merchants, cultivators, mine owners, and communities subject to tribute. Spanish reform also interacted with the labor and legal institutions of Indigenous societies in the Americas, making the relationship between metropolitan efficiency and colonial justice deeply contested. Students should track one policy from proposal through royal approval and local implementation rather than assume it was effective everywhere at its nominal date. Such a case study shows why the history of enlightened monarchy cannot stop at the political borders of Europe when overseas revenues and coerced labor helped sustain reforming states. The tension between central government and colonial practice was especially sharp because orders crossed an ocean and passed through viceroys, audiencias, municipal councils, and networks of merchants. Distance did not merely delay enforcement. It created opportunities to reinterpret instructions, negotiate exceptions, or concentrate costs on groups with little leverage at court. In some places imperial officers sought revenue from communities whose members disputed the justice of assessments; in others merchants used reforms to challenge established monopolies. The social and political consequences of Bourbon reform were therefore not contained within Madrid. A proper comparison with Prussia or Austria must ask where tax revenue was raised, who controlled its expenditure, and what forms of consent or petition were available to those who bore new burdens. This broadens the analysis of reforming monarchy without turning Chapter 84 into a complete history of Spanish America.

Section 84.19

Tuscany, Scandinavia, and the Comparative Limits of Enlightened Reform

Peter Leopold's 1786 criminal reform in Tuscany abolished the death penalty, but capital punishment returned later. Gustav III of Sweden, after the 1772 coup, pursued reform within a different balance of crown and estates. Some reforms endured, others were reversed, and "enlightened absolutism" fits these governments unevenly.

Tuscany provides a conspicuous case of legal reform under Peter Leopold, grand duke from 1765 to 1790 and later emperor Leopold II. Working with ministers and jurists, he sought to rationalize administration, taxation, church relations, and criminal justice in a relatively small Italian state. Tuscany's scale made some reforms easier to implement than policies imposed across the widely varied Habsburg lands, but local elites, religious institutions, and municipalities still mattered. The criminal code of November 30, 1786, commonly called the Leopoldina, abolished the death penalty and judicial torture, making Tuscany an influential example in debates over the purpose of punishment. These measures were shaped by eighteenth-century penal criticism, including Beccaria's arguments, but their importance should be evaluated through legal administration and subsequent developments rather than celebrated as a timeless achievement beyond the politics of its day.

The Tuscan reform did not end all coercive punishment. Imprisonment, labor penalties, policing, and other state powers continued, and capital punishment was later restored under changed circumstances. The reversal matters because it reveals the relationship between criminal legislation, public fears, regime security, and the contingent nature of reforms. A declaration that a ruler abolished the death penalty must specify the territory, date, and subsequent history; otherwise it falsely suggests a permanent transformation of Italian law. Students should compare the Tuscan code with Prussian codification and Joseph's penal reforms, asking how far each government protected subjects from arbitrary judicial power and which distinctions of social status remained. The scale of an innovation and the permanence of that innovation are separate questions.

Sweden demonstrates a different interaction of monarch, estates, and constitutional forms. Gustav III took power in a coup in 1772 and reshaped the political settlement after the Age of Liberty, during which the estates had exercised substantial influence. He promoted cultural patronage and selected reforms, but his authority remained contested. The Act of Union and Security in 1789 substantially enlarged the crown's powers and diminished the role of established institutions. To call Gustav simply an enlightened ruler because he supported arts or revised administration would overlook the constitutional conflict at the center of his reign. Moreover, Sweden's estate system and the status of peasants differed from Prussian noble estates or Russian serfholding. Comparisons must avoid treating all social hierarchies as variations of a single legal form.

Other German and Italian states supply further evidence that reform was not restricted to the famous three monarchs. Territorial princes experimented with bureaucratic training, schools, poor relief, penal law, and church administration in institutional settings that varied greatly. The extent of local consent and the ability of estates to bargain differed among small principalities, free cities, ecclesiastical territories, and larger dynastic states. Some initiatives survived as administrative routines; others remained proposals or were undone with changes of ruler. This variety cautions against imagining a single continental reform program advancing evenly outward from royal capitals.

Comparative judgment requires a common set of questions. What did the ruler or minister identify as a problem? Which statute or office addressed it? Whose established power was reduced or protected? What evidence survives for enforcement, compliance, avoidance, and resistance? Did the policy persist after its sponsor's death or dismissal? These tests reveal that some reforms had genuine social benefits and continued to influence nineteenth-century administration, while others functioned mainly to concentrate royal power. The same measure could do both. Centralized schooling expanded state reach and sometimes literacy; reform of penal procedure reduced certain abuses while retaining estate inequality; religious toleration improved lives while narrowing communal autonomy.

The chapter's central conclusion is therefore neither an endorsement of philosopher-kings nor a dismissal of the reforms as mere propaganda. Eighteenth-century rulers and ministers developed more systematic means of governing, shaped in part by Enlightenment argument and in part by war, revenue needs, and institutional competition. They sometimes loosened restrictions that had burdened specific populations. They also governed without equal political participation, preserved coercive structures, and sometimes intensified the state's demands on subjects. The contradiction between public welfare and concentrated authority was a real historical tension, but it was not resolved through one common European trajectory. Chapter 85 will examine the demographic, agrarian, commercial, and consumer conditions in which these governments operated, further complicating the relationship between reform, material prosperity, and power.

Comparing the three major northern and eastern monarchies with Portugal, Spain, Tuscany, and Sweden also exposes the danger of treating later influence as evidence of original intent. Reform jurists might formulate legal protections that became important in nineteenth-century government, yet they worked within eighteenth-century hierarchies and assumptions. A modern constitutional tradition may look back to these initiatives as beginnings, but that retrospective significance does not make the eighteenth-century rulers constitutionalists. Likewise, the withdrawal of a particular decree does not prove that every associated administrative change vanished. Personnel, record systems, professional training, and altered expectations could survive formal reversal. A disciplined conclusion should distinguish changes of law, changes of administration, changes in subjects' opportunities, and changes of historical reputation. Those measures may point in different directions, which is why the category enlightened absolutism remains debated rather than simply accepted or discarded. Students should also distinguish the chronology of claims about enlightenment from the chronology of political reaction. A reform introduced in the 1760s could be evaluated differently after 1789, when revolutionary events changed rulers' fears about law, public debate, and organized resistance. The withdrawal of a measure during a crisis may reveal concern about state security rather than conclusive proof that the original experiment failed on its own terms. Conversely, a law that survived did not necessarily achieve equal implementation or stable social legitimacy. The comparative evidence is therefore most convincing when presented as a sequence of partial adjustments, local adaptations, and reversals rather than a parade of heroic royal acts. The conclusions should remain open to contrary documentary findings and should make explicit the limits imposed by uneven survival of records.

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has assessed reforms through four distinct questions: what governments said they intended; which laws and institutions they created; how measures worked in particular jurisdictions and households; and which changes endured after their sponsors lost power. Those questions yield no universal verdict that enlightened monarchy was either a fiction or an unqualified achievement.

Frederick II combined cultivated royal duty with militarized government and protection of nobility; Maria Theresa and Joseph II made administrative and social changes within a composite monarchy whose constitutional divisions could not be dissolved by command. Catherine II adopted the language of rational law while governing through estate privilege and imperial expansion. Portuguese and Spanish ministers linked reform to church control, education, revenue, and colonial authority; Tuscany and Sweden reveal further variations in penal law and constitutional conflict.

The enduring issue is accountability. A ruler could improve justice, schools, or religious permissions while rejecting any principle that subjects should collectively direct political decisions. This tension was not resolved in one step and did not follow a single European chronology. Chapter 85 examines demographic, agrarian, commercial, and consumer transformations that reshaped the resources, opportunities, and constraints of these states. The chapter’s review activities ask students to test reformist claims against historical evidence, not to repeat the vocabulary of royal self-praise.

Chapter Summary

Eighteenth-century programs of enlightened monarchy grew from a conjunction of military pressure, fiscal competition, inherited ideas of royal duty, and criticism of arbitrary practice. Reforming rulers and ministers sought more regular government, useful education, accessible courts, religious accommodation, and productive populations. They did not agree on a common philosophy, and they did not generally intend to surrender central political authority to subjects. The label enlightened absolutism remains contested because it can describe both genuine reforms and strategies that strengthened monarchies.

Administrative centralization operated through distinct territorial settings. Ministers, jurists, governors, estate representatives, clergy, and municipal authorities interpreted and sometimes resisted central laws. Better surveys and registers did not guarantee fairer taxation; a new office could improve public access while also increasing surveillance. The effectiveness of any measure depended on resources, local cooperation, and the ability of subjects to obtain remedies.

Frederick II promoted an image of disciplined kingship and personal service to the state. His 1752 testament linked conscientious government with protection of a landed nobility essential to the army. Prussian legal and educational initiatives developed over several decades, while royal power and manorial inequalities endured. The General Law Code that entered into force in 1794 represented a later stage of codification after Frederick’s death.

In Prussia, religious plurality was accommodated selectively, and education regulations raised ambitions that could exceed village resources. Jewish communities did not receive uniform civic equality. Noble landholding, military recruitment, and peasant obligations continued to shape social life. These institutions were not simply forgotten by a philosopher-king; they were central to his conception of the political order.

Maria Theresa pursued substantial administrative and educational change within a composite monarchy challenged by war and territorial loss. Her 1774 school ordinance was an ambitious assertion of state-supervised instruction, yet its reach depended on teachers, funding, language, and attendance. Her religious commitments remained more restrictive than those associated with her son. Her policies established institutions that Joseph II inherited without resolving the monarchy’s regional differences.

Joseph II accelerated church, legal, fiscal, and social reforms after 1780. The 1781 toleration arrangements expanded permitted private worship for specified Christian groups in relevant territories, while different rules applied to Jewish communities. His serfdom measures changed aspects of personal legal dependence without removing all manorial dues. Central intervention threatened estates and religious institutions as well as particular burdens on subjects.

Resistance to Joseph II emerged from conflicting social interests and constitutional claims. Hungary and the Austrian Netherlands did not follow identical courses; the Brabant Revolution temporarily overturned Habsburg authority, while Hungary’s crisis brought intense political opposition. Important reforms survived despite major reversals around 1790, demonstrating that policy durability varies and cannot be reduced to a ruler’s success or failure.

Catherine II’s Nakaz used European reforming arguments to articulate legal improvement under autocratic authority. The Legislative Commission was a forum for some estates and constituencies, not an independent representative parliament. Provincial reform created new offices and reporting arrangements, but the empire depended on noble cooperation, and the 1785 charters confirmed unequal corporate rights. Privately owned serfs did not receive the freedoms accorded to the noble estate.

Portugal under Pombal and Spain under Charles III joined governmental reorganization to church control, education, revenue, and colonial governance. Their Atlantic empires shaped the resources and costs of reform. Tuscany’s abolition of the death penalty in 1786 demonstrated the possibility of penal innovation but later suffered reversal. Sweden under Gustav III showed that cultural and administrative reform could accompany stronger monarchical constitutional power.

The chapter demonstrates that public welfare and concentrated power could advance together without creating political equality. Historical evaluation requires distinguishing proclamations from enforcement, law from social status, and administrative survival from popular consent. The following questions ask students to evaluate reform through documents, regional comparisons, and the experiences of those governed rather than through royal reputations alone.

Chapter Reflection

Enlightened monarchy reveals a recurring tension in European history: states could become more capable of organizing welfare, education, justice and economic activity without becoming accountable to the people they governed. Improvement from above did not have one meaning. For a ruler it could mean more dependable revenue or trained administrators; for a peasant it might mean relief from a personal restriction; for a privileged estate it could appear as an attack on long-held legal guarantees.

The distinctive contribution of the eighteenth century lay not in the invention of benevolent kingship but in the expanding authority of specialized institutions, codified law, statistics, organized education and public criticism. Yet these tools were not politically neutral. They could help restrain arbitrary action while simultaneously enabling closer surveillance and more efficient extraction. The historical value of reform rests in what changed for particular people in particular jurisdictions, not the claims printed in royal preambles.

Comparison also exposes the unequal geography of reform. Prussia’s army and nobles, the Habsburg monarchy’s diverse crownlands, Russia’s serfholding and frontier societies, Iberian colonial empires, and the smaller Tuscan and Swedish states did not share one institutional starting point. Their trajectories varied, and not all changes survived. Recognizing these differences prevents the history of Europe from becoming a parade of rulers evaluated by a single modern standard.

Chapter 85 moves from government policy to the conditions of population growth, agrarian work, trade, markets and consumption. Those developments supplied resources for reform and imposed limitations that rulers could not escape through decree. Chapter 84 thus joins the preceding studies of Enlightenment knowledge and political ideas to the social and material history that follows. Its central lesson is methodological as well as historical: judge claims to good government by institutions, experience and evidence.
