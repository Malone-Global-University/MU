Lords, Peasants, Manors, and Medieval Power

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Kings could found kingdoms, but most people met power in the form of a landlord, a steward, a church court, a mill, a due to be paid, or an armed man who lived nearby. This chapter asks how land, labor, protection, justice, and force were organized below the level of kings, and why a single word, "feudalism," has so often hidden the variety of what historians find there.

In the early ninth century, an administrator working for the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, near Paris, compiled a long written survey of the abbey's estates. The survey listed, household by household, who held which plots of land, who owed which rents and services, how many plows, vines, and meadows belonged to the abbey's own fields, and what the people on each estate were required to deliver. The document is not a story, and it tells us nothing of the feelings of those it counted. Yet it opens a window onto a world in which an institution of prayer was also a landowner, an employer, a collector of dues, and a source of command over hundreds of families. That single survey, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, illustrates the central subject of Chapter 34: how medieval power worked on the ground.

Chapters 31 to 33 traced the great political story of the Carolingian empire, its fragmentation under internal division and external pressure, and the consolidation of durable kingdoms in England, France, and the German lands. That story was told largely from the perspective of rulers. But kings did not govern each village directly. They depended on counts, bishops, abbots, magnates, and local landholders, who controlled the estates that fed armies, housed courts, and sustained churches. Chapter 34 therefore changes scale. It asks who controlled land and labor, who judged disputes, who commanded armed men, and what ordinary rural people experienced as they lived within these relationships.

The chapter's central argument is that medieval power was layered rather than organized through one universal system. Kings, counts, bishops, monasteries, castellans, warriors, free peasants, dependent tenants, and enslaved people participated in overlapping relationships involving landholding, rents, labor, protection, jurisdiction, military service, kinship, oath, and patronage. Some of those relationships became more localized and more coercive between the ninth and eleventh centuries. But there was no single "feudal system" imposed evenly across western and central Europe, and the chapter's later sections show why the term has been so contested.

The historical problem is also one of vocabulary. Older textbooks often taught a neat diagram, with a king at the top, nobles beneath him, knights beneath them, and peasants at the bottom, all bound by fiefs. Modern scholarship has largely dismantled that picture. Contemporary historians usually separate the components that earlier writers combined: vassalage and fiefs, manorial or seigneurial rural relationships, and political decentralization with local lordship. They then ask whether the umbrella word is useful at all. This chapter follows that method. It explains the real relationships clearly before evaluating the label.

The evidence is unevenly distributed. Estate surveys, charters, capitularies, law codes, chronicles, saints' lives, and the physical remains of fortifications and settlements survive, but they favor institutions that kept records, above all monasteries and cathedral chapters. Peasants almost never wrote about themselves, and lay aristocratic archives are far rarer than ecclesiastical ones. Historians must therefore ask of every source what its creators wished to record and whose experience it leaves out.

Within Part V, Chapter 34 is the social and structural chapter. It lies between the consolidation of kingdoms in Chapter 33 and the expansion of Latin Christian institutions into Scandinavia and central Europe in Chapter 35. Within Volume II, it provides the rural and aristocratic foundations on which the commercial, religious, and intellectual developments of the High Middle Ages, beginning in Part VII, would build. It does not narrate the later agricultural expansion, which belongs to Chapter 42, nor the broad portrait of daily life that Chapter 49 will provide.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why kingdoms depended on local lords, churches, and landholders, and distinguish political lordship, economic dependency, military relationships, and landholding.
  2. Describe the landed foundations of lordship, including the role of monasteries and bishoprics as major landholders.
  3. Analyze the manorial model of estate organization using Carolingian estate surveys, and explain why manorialism was never one uniform institution.
  4. Distinguish among free landholders, tenants, dependent peasants, and enslaved people, and explain why "peasant" describes a socioeconomic position more reliably than a legal status.
  5. Trace the persistence of slavery alongside changing forms of dependency, and evaluate why the story of slavery becoming serfdom is misleadingly simple.
  6. Assess jurisdictional or banal lordship and explain why lordship cannot be reduced to rent collection.
  7. Contextualize castle building within regional chronologies and the dispute over localization of authority.
  8. Compare vassalage, benefice, and fief as separate phenomena, and critique the feudal pyramid.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of "feudalism" and of the "feudal revolution" around 1000.
  10. Interpret a Carolingian polyptych as evidence, identifying its purposes, silences, and limits.
  11. Connect layered local power to the kingdoms of Chapter 33 and the expansion of Latin Christendom in Chapter 35.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How were land, labor, protection, justice, and military power organized locally in early medieval western and central Europe?
  2. Why is it misleading to describe all of these relationships simply as "feudalism," and what relationships has the term been used to cover?
  3. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct rural society, and whose experiences does that evidence conceal?
  4. How did the legal and economic positions of peasants vary, and why does a simple free versus serf binary fail?
  5. In what sense did slavery persist after antiquity, and why should medieval serfdom not be treated as its direct replacement?
  6. What kinds of power did a lord possess, and how did landowning, judging, commanding, and protecting combine?
  7. Did political authority become dramatically more localized around the year 1000, or does that depend on the region and the evidence?
  8. How did churches, kings, and warrior elites participate in the same networks of land and obligation rather than standing outside them?

Historical Context

The rural society examined here grew out of the late Roman and post-Roman West. As Chapters 18 and 27 explained, the Roman countryside had combined large estates worked by tenants, dependent laborers, and enslaved people with communities of smaller free landholders. The fifth and sixth centuries brought political fragmentation, migration, and the creation of successor kingdoms, examined in Chapters 29 and 30, but they did not erase that agrarian base. Great landowners, including the church, persisted, and new Frankish, Gothic, Lombard, and Anglo-Saxon elites acquired land and adapted older structures of patronage, tenancy, and rural labor.

The Merovingian and Carolingian kings, discussed in Chapters 30 and 31, ruled through counts, bishops, abbots, and royal estates. They drew military service from landholders, granted lands and immunities to churches, and relied on oaths of fidelity. Carolingian rulers made unusual efforts to record and regulate, issuing capitularies and commissioning estate inventories, and this administrative energy is the reason that the ninth century is comparatively well documented. Yet the same century saw the partition of the empire, Viking and Magyar raiding, and regional reordering, as Chapter 32 showed. Chapter 33 then traced how durable kingdoms emerged in England, France, and the German lands.

Those kingdoms were composites of local powers. A count administered a county, but he also held private estates and commanded followers. A bishop was a spiritual leader who might also control extensive lands, judicial immunities, and armed contingents. A monastery was a community of prayer and a landlord. A village might contain free owners, tenants of several lords, and families of dependent or enslaved status. This chapter sits at precisely that level of social organization.

Students should keep in mind the geographic limits of the chapter. Its principal evidence comes from the former Carolingian lands, from England, and from Italy and Catalonia, and its conclusions should not be assumed to apply automatically to the Byzantine countryside, treated in Chapter 37, to the Slavic and Scandinavian societies that Chapters 35 and 39 discuss, or to al-Andalus. Even within the Latin West, the chapter emphasizes variation. It draws on several kinds of evidence: estate surveys and polyptychs, charters recording gifts and sales, capitularies and law codes, narrative chronicles, archaeological evidence for villages, mills, and fortifications, and later legal compilations that must be read with caution as retrospective systematizations.

Section 34.1

From Kings to Local Power

Chapter 33 ended with durable kingdoms, yet a durable kingdom was not a centralized administration reaching into every village. Medieval kings had courts, household officers, itinerant habits, and considerable symbolic authority, but they lacked professional bureaucracies, standing armies, and regular taxation of the kind later states would develop. They governed through intermediaries. Most people encountered authority not as a royal command but as a landowner, a local court, a bishop or monastery, a count or lesser lord, an estate manager, an armed retainer, or a set of customary obligations that had long been attached to their land.

This chapter's most important distinction follows from that observation. Political lordship, economic dependency, military relationships, and landholding were connected, but they were not the same thing. A person could owe rent to one landowner, attend the court of a second lord, pay a tithe to a church, and be summoned to military service by a king or count. A warrior might hold land from several lords or from none. A bishop might be simultaneously a landlord, a judge, a royal adviser, and a provider of armed men. Treating these relationships as aspects of a single thing called "feudalism" obscures the variety, and the next sections therefore take them apart.

Older textbooks, following influential syntheses of the twentieth century, often bundled all of these relationships together and described the result as a feudal system. Contemporary historians normally separate the components before deciding whether the word is useful. That approach is not merely a fashion in scholarship. It reflects the evidence. The surviving sources of the ninth century speak of estates, counts, benefices, vassals, immunities, and labor services, but they do not speak of a system that joined them. The system is a modern construction, and Section 34.10 examines how and why it was built.

The chapter's geographic focus is western and central Europe, principally the Carolingian successor lands, England, northern and central Italy, and Catalonia. This focus is determined by the evidence and by the chapter outline, not by any claim that these regions were the norm for Europe. Byzantium developed very different relations between the state, great estates, and dependent cultivators, examined in Chapter 37, and the societies of Scandinavia and the Slavic lands entered Latin Christendom on their own terms, as Chapter 35 will explain.

Section 34.2

Land, Wealth, and the Foundations of Lordship

Land mattered because agriculture and pastoral production were the largest sources of wealth across most of early medieval Europe. Yet it is misleading to call the early medieval economy purely land-based. Trade, exchange, craft work, tolls, coinage, and the circulation of precious goods all existed, and Chapter 43 will show how commerce revived and expanded. The accurate statement is that control of productive land, and of the labor and rights attached to it, was the most widespread foundation of elite wealth and of social power.

Land was held in many forms. Kings owned estates (fiscal lands) from which they supplied their courts and rewarded followers. Aristocratic families held inherited property, often scattered across several districts. Some holdings were allodial, meaning they were not held tenurially from another lord, although their holders could still owe public duties to king or count, and the term varied by region. Others were held as tenancies, benefices, leases, or conditional grants. Churches received extensive gifts from the pious and from families seeking prayers for their dead, and they claimed perpetual ownership of donated property in principle. In practice, church estates could be leased, exchanged, granted in precaria, or lost through political pressure. Because inheritance divided property among heirs in many regions, and because marriage, purchase, gift, and confiscation constantly moved land, estates were rarely tidy. A lord's possessions might include fields in several villages, a vineyard far away, shares in a mill, and rights to pasture in a forest, without forming one compact territory.

Lordship also attached to things other than fields. Woods supplied timber, fuel, and pasture for swine. Mills ground grain and could be powerful sources of dues. Fisheries, salt works, roads, bridges, ferries, and markets produced revenue through tolls and fees. Control of such resources gave lords leverage over neighbors even where those neighbors owned their own land. The wealth of an elite family therefore lay in a bundle of rights as much as in acres.

Church institutions remain central here. Monasteries and bishoprics were not merely religious organizations. They accumulated enormous landed resources through royal grants, private donations, and purchase, and they were often among the largest landowners in a region. Royal grants of immunity could exclude counts and other royal officials from entering a church's lands, and so could transfer or strengthen judicial and fiscal authority within the immunized lands, although the scope varied by grant, region, and period. A common tool of the period was the precaria, a temporary or conditional grant of the use of property, especially ecclesiastical land, whose precise terms varied widely. Some carried military service or other dues, and some involved property first donated to a church and then granted back to the donor for use. Through such arrangements, churches and families managed property, security, and patronage at once. Abbots and bishops, who were typically drawn from aristocratic families, managed estates in ways that mirrored and interacted with lay lordship. In Ottonian Saxony, female religious houses such as Quedlinburg and Essen, governed by abbesses of noble and royal families, likewise controlled substantial lands and dependents.

Section 34.3

The Manor and the Organization of Rural Production

Historians use the word manor, and the related term manorialism, to describe a widely discussed model of estate organization. In its classic analytical form, an estate was divided into two parts. The lord's own portion, the demesne, was cultivated for the lord's direct benefit. The rest of the land was divided into holdings, often called mansi (singular mansus), each occupied by a peasant household that supported itself from the holding while owing the lord rents, renders, and labor. Because the estate combined the lord's land with tenant holdings, scholars have called it a bipartite estate. The obligations took several forms: labor services (corvées) such as plowing, harvesting, carting, and fencing on the demesne; food renders such as grain, wine, poultry, and eggs; money payments; and payments for use of mills, ovens, woods, and pasture. Stewards, reeves, and other estate officials supervised the work and collected what was owed.

The best-known evidence for this model comes from Carolingian estate surveys called polyptychs, especially the one compiled for the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, which this chapter examines in its Primary Source Window. These documents show that some great estates were indeed organized along bipartite lines and that they recorded precise categories of holdings, households, and obligations. They are invaluable, and historians have used them for generations to reconstruct rural life.

But the model must be handled cautiously, and this caution is one of the chapter's principal lessons. Not every village was a manor, not every estate was organized identically, and manorialism was never one uniform European institution. The polyptychs come from large ecclesiastical estates in particular regions, especially in northern Francia between the Seine and the Rhine, where such organization was most developed. They reflect the concerns of administrators who wished to record resources and obligations, and they may present an orderly ideal more clearly than the lived reality. Recent scholarship has emphasized that the proportion of demesne to tenant land varied widely, that labor services could be light or heavy, and that many peasants were wholly or partly outside such arrangements. In many places, tenants might owe chiefly rents in kind or in coin, with limited work on a lord's own fields.

Regional variation is essential. In England, lordship and dependency took forms shaped by Anglo-Saxon custom, including the obligations of thegns and ceorls, and by regional differences such as the prominence of free tenants, often called sokemen, in areas of Scandinavian settlement. Domesday Book, compiled in 1086, recorded English estates in a formulaic manner that can give a misleading impression of uniformity. In the Rhineland and the German lands, large ecclesiastical and aristocratic estates, often called Grundherrschaften in modern German scholarship, combined demesne farms with dependent holdings, though their organization differed from that of the Paris region. Northern and central Italy had large estates, but also leasehold tenancies and a significant role for towns and for rural communities. In Catalonia and other northern Iberian frontier zones, political expansion and colonization produced distinctive patterns of smallholding, local communities, and lordship; older claims that these were simply depopulated lands later repopulated by settlers have been substantially revised, though free smallholders remained important. In none of these regions did one model exhaust the variety of arrangements.

The chapter also observes a boundary. The discussion here is about how production was organized, who controlled it, and what dependents owed. It does not treat the tools, crop rotations, forest clearance, or population growth that transformed the countryside in the eleventh to thirteenth centuries. Those developments belong to Chapter 42, and the commercial forces that altered rents and markets belong to Chapter 43.

Historical Vocabulary
Demesne, Manse, and Manor

The demesne is the portion of an estate kept under the lord's direct control and cultivated for the lord's benefit, typically with labor owed by tenants or performed by dependent workers. A manse (Latin mansus) was a unit of landholding and obligation, often the holding supporting a peasant household, used in Carolingian estate surveys. A manor is a modern and later medieval term for an estate unit combining a lord's demesne with dependent holdings, and manorialism is the scholarly label for the system of estate organization just described.

The vocabulary matters because these terms are not neutral equivalents across time and space. The word "manor" becomes standard in England after the Norman Conquest, and historians apply it to earlier periods as an analytical convenience. Students should not assume that a ninth-century Frankish estate and a thirteenth-century English manor were identical institutions, nor that all rural Europe was organized in this way.

Section 34.4

Peasants: Free, Unfree, and Everything Between

Early medieval rural people cannot be sorted into two tidy categories. At one end were free landholders who held property without paying private rent to a landlord, although they could still owe public military, judicial, transport, fiscal, or fortification obligations depending on region and period. At the other were enslaved people, who were legally the property of others. Between them stretched a wide spectrum. There were free tenants who rented land from a lord. There were people who were personally free but owed labor on the lord's fields. There were dependent peasants who could not leave the estate or marry freely without consent. There were freed people, who had been manumitted but who remained bound by obligations of service or respect to their former owners. There were people whose status was tied to the land they held rather than to their persons. And there were locally specific categories, with regional terms that historians continue to struggle to translate precisely.

Rights and obligations could differ according to land, person, locality, inheritance, marriage, custom, and lord. A holding might be classified as servile or free regardless of the status of the household occupying it, which is why Carolingian estate surveys sometimes record people of one legal status holding land of another category. A person might possess significant property while owing particular services, or be legally free and still economically dependent on a powerful neighbor. Marriage between people of different statuses, or between dependents of different lords, produced disputes that appear in charters and in the decisions of church councils. Custom, which varied from estate to estate, often mattered more than general law.

For these reasons the chapter adopts a simple principle: "peasant" describes a socioeconomic position more reliably than a single legal status. Peasants were rural cultivators who worked land mainly with household labor and lived largely from what the household produced. Among them there were relatively prosperous families with extensive holdings and families barely supporting themselves on small plots. Some owned oxen and plows, and others depended on hand labor. Some had servants, and some were servants. The word describes a way of life and a position in the agrarian economy, not a legal class.

Peasants also had collective lives. Villages possessed customs, shared pasture, cooperative work routines, and informal mechanisms for settling disputes. Families negotiated inheritance and marriage, and they bargained, resisted, and adapted within the constraints of lordship. Peasants sometimes appear in the sources as litigants, in disputes recorded in court proceedings and charters, which shows that they were not passive. The evidence does not record their own words, but it does record their presence as people who argued about boundaries, services, and status. Even so, the evidence is thin, and historians must reconstruct peasant experience through inference from sources created by and for elites.

Section 34.5

Slavery, Servitude, and the Development of Serfdom

Textbooks often tell a tidy story in which Roman slavery gradually became medieval serfdom. Current scholarship rejects that simplification. Slavery did not end with the western Roman empire, as Chapter 28 noted in discussing continuity. Enslaved people remained present throughout the early Middle Ages, working in households, on estates, in craft production, and in other settings. Legal codes of the post-Roman kingdoms regulated slave status, penalties, and ownership, and charters record gifts and sales of enslaved people.

Slave trading also continued and had a wide geography. Captives taken in warfare and raiding, including many from the Slavic lands, were sold within Latin Europe and traded to the Islamic and Byzantine worlds. The Latin word sclavus, from which the English word "slave" derives, is usually connected to the prominence of Slavic captives in this trade. Venice, Verdun, and other centers were involved, and demand in al-Andalus and the Mediterranean shaped routes that crossed Europe. In England, the trade included the export of captives across the Irish Sea from ports such as Bristol, and Domesday Book classifies roughly 10 percent of its recorded households as servi, or slaves, in 1086, a figure that should not be converted mechanically into a percentage of the total population.

Christian institutions were not outside this system. Bishoprics and monasteries owned enslaved people and received them as gifts. Church teaching encouraged manumission as a pious act and sought to restrict the sale of Christians to non-Christians, but it did not condemn slavery as an institution. Manumission took several forms, and freed people often remained tied to their former owners or to a church by continuing obligations. This is one reason the categories of free and unfree blur in the sources.

Alongside persistent slavery, other forms of dependency expanded. Some free tenants, under economic pressure, insecurity, or the demands of powerful neighbors, took on obligations that resembled those of the unfree. Some enslaved people were settled on holdings of their own, which gave them a degree of household autonomy and moved them closer to the position of dependent tenants. The result was a convergence of some free and unfree cultivators into groups owing comparable rents and services to their lords. In some regions, and especially in France after about 1000, lords came to claim distinctive dues from dependents, such as restrictions on marriage outside the estate or on leaving it, and a more clearly defined servile status, often called serfdom, took shape in law and custom.

The timing and extent of this change are contested. Some historians, notably in studies of Catalonia and southern France, have argued that around the year 1000 a real transformation replaced slavery with serfdom as lords imposed new forms of coercion on formerly free peasants. Others emphasize gradual change, regional diversity, and the persistence of slavery in some places long after. The argument of this chapter is that slavery persisted while other forms of dependency expanded, overlapped, and changed; medieval serfdom emerged unevenly rather than replacing slavery on a single timetable. Serfdom was not a Europe-wide event. In England, for example, a status of unfree tenant later called villeinage was defined by royal law only after the period covered here, and Chapters 45 and 52 return to that question. Serf, moreover, is not a synonym for slave: serfs had families, held land, and possessed some rights, even though they were constrained by obligations and by their lord's power.

Section 34.6

Lords, Courts, and the Power to Command

Lordship was more than rent collection. A lord was frequently a judge, a protector, a creditor, and a commander, and these powers reinforced one another. The basic distinction is between economic lordship, the ability to draw rents and labor from land and dependents, and jurisdictional lordship, the ability to hold courts, impose fines, and compel obedience. The two often coincided, but they could also be held by different people.

Jurisdiction came in several forms. Royal immunities, described in Section 34.2, gave churches and some lay magnates the right to judge and discipline their dependents without interference by royal officials. Counts and other officeholders, acting with royal authority, presided over courts that resolved disputes, enforced law, and collected fines. Lords also exercised informal authority as arbitrators, settling quarrels among neighbors and dependents. Revenue from such judicial powers, including fines, confiscations, and fees, was part of a lord's income. Other powers included tolls on roads, bridges, and markets, rights over mills and ovens, policing of disorder, collection of customary dues, and, in some cases, the right to summon men for armed service.

Historians have developed the concept of banal lordship, from the Latin and Old French ban, meaning a power of command, to describe these prerogatives. The argument is that powers of command once associated particularly with public authority, exercised by kings and their officials, could come to be exercised by local lords over people living in their territories, regardless of whether those people held land from them. Under banal lordship a lord might oblige nearby residents to use his mill and pay for it, to bring disputes to his court, and to provide services or payments. The idea has been influential, especially in studies of France, but it is also debated. Critics caution that the transfer of public authority to private hands was gradual, that royal and comital courts continued to function, and that the evidence differs widely by region.

The key point for students is that a lord who could judge disputes, compel labor, monopolize a mill, impose tolls, summon armed men, and control fortifications possessed several kinds of power simultaneously. Those who lived within reach of such a lord faced not a single obligation but a network of claims. Yet lords competed with other lords, with kings, and with the communities themselves, and power was always negotiated.

Historical Vocabulary
Ban and Banal Lordship

The ban was the power to command, compel, and punish. In the early Middle Ages it was associated especially with kings and their officials. Banal lordship (French seigneurie banale) is the modern historiographical term for the exercise of such commanding powers by local lords, including the right to impose obligations to use lordly mills and ovens, to attend courts, and to pay certain dues. The term is most developed in scholarship on France, and historians disagree about how widespread and how new such powers were. It matters here because it shows why lordship cannot be reduced to landowning.

Section 34.7

Castles and the Localization of Power

Castles belong at the center of any discussion of medieval lordship, but chronology matters. Fortifications existed throughout the early Middle Ages: Roman fortresses and walled cities, royal strongholds, refuges, fortified monasteries, and, in Anglo-Saxon England, the planned fortified centers called burhs that Chapter 33 distinguished from castles. These structures were often public or royal in character, part of defensive systems organized by rulers. They should not be confused with the private aristocratic castle that became more prominent later.

From the tenth century, and especially in the eleventh, evidence grows in several regions for private or semi-private fortifications controlled by aristocrats and their followers. The pattern varied widely. In central Italy, historians have described a process of incastellamento in which populations were gathered into fortified settlements organized by local lords, beginning in the tenth century. In Catalonia, networks of castles were central to the organization of frontier territory, local lordship, and warfare. In western France, including Normandy and Anjou, earthwork fortifications of the type known as the motte and bailey (a raised mound with an enclosure) appear in the eleventh century. In England, such castles spread rapidly after the Norman Conquest of 1066, although a small number had been built earlier. In the German lands, fortifications, including royal and Ottonian strongholds, developed along regional lines, and the nobility's private castles increased in number later. Students should avoid calling every fortification a motte and bailey, and should not assume that a ninth-century noble was a castle lord.

Castles could do many things. They protected people and property in times of danger. They dominated roads, river crossings, and communications. They housed armed followers and stored supplies. They served as administrative centers from which a lord could control surrounding territory and as places where courts met and dues were collected. They symbolized status. They could also be instruments of coercion, as places from which lords threatened neighboring communities, imposed demands, and held prisoners. Chroniclers who disliked castle-building lords emphasized this last function, and their testimony must be weighed against the fact that castles also offered genuine security.

Castle building is at the heart of a historiographical dispute. Some historians see a dramatic localization of authority around the year 1000, with castle lords taking over powers once held by public officials. Others argue that castles multiplied more gradually, that kings and counts built and controlled many of them, and that their distribution varied sharply. The next sections return to this question, and Section 34.11 addresses it directly. For now it is enough to note that castles are evidence of localized power, but the evidence must be interpreted region by region.

Section 34.8

Warriors, Retinues, and Aristocratic Society

Armed power was central to lordship. Early medieval elites were warrior aristocracies in the sense that military leadership, service, and display were fundamental to their status, although aristocrats were also landowners, judges, patrons of churches, and participants in diplomacy. Armed service took several forms. Kings summoned free men to campaign, and Carolingian capitularies tied the obligation to serve, and the quality of equipment, to landholding, with wealthier holders expected to provide heavier armor. Kings and magnates also maintained household retinues of followers who lived with or near them, were supported by them, and fought for them. In England, thegns owed service and payments to their lords or to the king, including a payment of weapons and horses to the lord at a warrior's death called the heriot.

Mounted warfare mattered, but the equipment was expensive. A warhorse, armor, weapons, and the training to use them required resources, which is why military elites were typically those who held land or received support from lords. Patronage, gift exchange, and feasting bound warriors to leaders, and honor, reputation, and kinship shaped aristocratic politics. Family networks, extended through marriage and alliance, were as important as any formal bond. Aristocratic women also participated in these networks. They inherited and managed property, acted as widows and guardians for heirs, founded and patronized religious houses, and in some cases exercised local authority, although the sources often obscure their roles.

Students should be careful not to imagine a fully formed knightly class appearing suddenly. The Latin word miles, which classical writers used for a soldier, changed meaning over time and from place to place. In the ninth and tenth centuries it could mean simply a warrior or armed follower. By the eleventh century, in some regions, it increasingly designated a distinct class of armed men of high or middling status, and only later, as the twelfth century progressed, did ideals of knighthood, ceremonies of dubbing, and a hereditary knightly status become widespread. Unfree and dependent retainers had long formed part of aristocratic households. From the later eleventh century, the more clearly documented group known as ministeriales became especially important in the German lands: dependent and often legally unfree agents of lords who served as warriors and administrators and rose over time into the lower nobility. This later case is a good reminder that military service and legal status were not always aligned.

A warrior could serve a king, a duke, a count, a bishop, an abbot, or a local lord. Clerical norms generally restricted personal armsbearing and bloodshed, but bishops and abbots in the Frankish and German worlds could be responsible for military contingents and sometimes accompanied or directed military activity, and they relied on lay advocates and warriors to defend their lands; practice did not always match canonical ideals. Military service was embedded in political and social relationships, including kinship, patronage, and oath, and was not governed everywhere by a uniform pyramid.

Historical Vocabulary
Miles

Miles (plural milites) is a Latin word for soldier. Medieval writers used it with shifting meaning. In the early period it often simply meant an armed man or follower. In the tenth and eleventh centuries, in some regions, it came to designate specialized mounted warriors who might be supported by a lord's household or by land. Later it was associated with knighthood as a distinct status. Historians translate it as "soldier," "warrior," or "knight," depending on context, and the choice of translation is itself an argument. It matters in this chapter because the apparent rise of the miles is part of the evidence debated in discussions of social transformation around the year 1000.

Section 34.9

Vassalage, Benefices, and Fiefs

This section treats the relationships that older textbooks made the starting point of their accounts and then used to explain all of medieval society. Three terms must be kept distinct. Vassalage was a personal relationship of fidelity, service, and patronage between a man and a lord. Benefice (Latin beneficium) was a grant, often of land or of the revenues from land, that provided support in return for service or as a favor, and the word appears in several early medieval contexts, including church lands. Fief (Latin feodum or feudum) is a term that appears in documents from about the tenth century, notably in southern France, with usage and legal meaning that changed over time and should not be treated as a timeless institution.

Carolingian sources use terms such as vassus or vassallus for dependents and followers, and beneficium for several kinds of grant or use-right. These relationships sometimes intersected: royal vassi could perform military or temporary political functions, oaths of fidelity were significant in Carolingian politics, and some benefices supported service. Charlemagne and his successors legislated about lords and followers and used such ties to bind aristocrats to the crown. But the surviving evidence does not establish one uniform institution in which every vassal entered by commendation, received a benefice, and owed a standardized package of military, court, and counsel services. Many followers were supported in a lord's household, not every benefice was granted for military service, and older historians too readily assumed that vassus plus beneficium already equaled the later fief-vassal relationship.

Over the ninth to eleventh centuries, practices shifted. In many regions benefices and other grants increasingly passed to heirs, and some holders came to treat their offices and lands as hereditary family property. Traditional accounts emphasize the Capitulary of Quierzy of 877, but historians disagree about its significance, and hereditary tendencies developed unevenly. By the eleventh century in parts of France, Germany, and Italy, the term fief and its cognates was used more regularly for grants held by vassals in return for service, and legal writers began to define the rights and duties involved. In northern Italy, an edict issued by the emperor Conrad II in 1037 addressed the security of the holdings of lesser vassals, showing how disputes over hereditary tenure had become matters for imperial legislation. Later in the twelfth century, Lombard legal scholars compiled texts on fiefs that shaped how subsequent writers imagined the whole system, and this scholarly tradition strongly influenced early modern and modern interpretations.

The classic diagram, king, nobles, knights, peasants, as though it described medieval society, should appear, if at all, only as something to critique. It falsely suggests a single chain of command in which each lord granted a fief to each lesser lord in return for military service and in which peasants sat at the bottom by virtue of the same relationship. In reality, peasants' obligations to landlords did not flow from vassalage. Many warriors held land from more than one lord, creating overlapping loyalties. Some held land outright, and some were supported without land. Kings were themselves part of the web, sometimes as lords and sometimes as holders of lands from churches. And the evidence for oaths, benefices, and fiefs is distributed very unevenly across regions and periods. Vassalage and fiefs were real and important, but they describe one set of relationships among elites, not the whole of medieval society.

Section 34.10

Was There a "Feudal System"?

The word feudalism is a modern historiographical category. It was coined and popularized by early modern jurists and Enlightenment writers, who used it to describe what they saw as the outdated legal and social order inherited from the Middle Ages. Medieval Europeans did not have a term for one integrated social system of this kind. Much of the modern scholarly construction owes to the legal historians of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, to F. L. Ganshof's influential account of feudal institutions as a system of vassalage and fiefs, and to Marc Bloch's broader vision of a feudal society that included dependent peasants, warrior elites, and fragmented political authority.

It is useful to separate at least three things that textbooks historically called feudalism. The first is vassalage and fiefs: the lord-vassal relationships and grants discussed in Section 34.9. The second is manorial or seigneurial rural relationships: the organization of estates and the obligations of dependent peasants discussed in Sections 34.3 to 34.5. The third is political decentralization and local lordship: the wider transfer or localization of powers of command discussed in Sections 34.6 and 34.7. Many historians object to combining these, because they do not necessarily coincide. Manorial estates existed without vassalage, and vassalage existed without manors. Strong kings could coexist with vassals and manors. The combination of all three in a single coherent system has been difficult to demonstrate from the evidence.

The critique was sharpened from the 1970s. Elizabeth A. R. Brown argued that the concept of feudalism was a construct that distorted the diversity of medieval evidence. Susan Reynolds, in a major study of fiefs and vassals, argued that the legal category of the fief, as a conditional grant of land in return for military service, was largely the product of twelfth-century and later lawyers, and that earlier evidence was often read backward through later concepts. Reynolds emphasized the importance of communities, assemblies, and collective action in medieval politics, which the image of a feudal pyramid obscures.

The chapter does not prohibit the word. A reasonable conclusion is that "feudalism" can be useful only when the author defines exactly what phenomenon the term is describing. It may be a legitimate shorthand for certain patterns of lord-vassal relations, or for localized aristocratic power in particular regions and periods. But it is misleading when used to name a single medieval social system. The better question is not "How did the feudal system work?" as if its existence were established, but rather "What relationships have historians grouped under the label feudalism, and how useful is that grouping?"

Historical Debate

Was Medieval Europe "Feudal"?

The question: Does the concept of feudalism capture a real and coherent structure of medieval society, or does it impose later legal categories and artificial uniformity on highly varied evidence?

Interpretation A: The term captures meaningful relationships. Historians in this tradition, building on the work of Bloch, Ganshof, and Duby, argue that across much of western Europe, from roughly the ninth to the thirteenth centuries, there were recognizable patterns: aristocratic warriors linked by oaths to lords, grants of land and revenue in return for service, dependent peasant populations, and authority that was exercised locally rather than centrally. Whatever the variations, the term identifies real social relationships, and abandoning it entirely would lose a useful tool for comparison.
Interpretation B: The term combines institutions and imposes later categories. Critics such as Brown and Reynolds argue that the term merges vassalage, fiefs, manorial dependence, and political fragmentation, which contemporaries did not regard as one system. They argue that the legal concept of the fief as the basis of a hierarchy was largely created by later jurists, and that earlier sources reveal a more varied world in which property, kinship, community, and royal authority all mattered. On this view, the word misleads more than it clarifies.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same documents, including capitularies, charters, estate surveys, chronicles, and later legal compilations. They differ in how they treat the later legal texts, how much weight they give regional variation, and how they define the phenomena that the term is meant to describe. Much depends on whether "feudalism" is used as a strict legal model, a broad social label, or a limited descriptive tool.

Why it matters: How historians resolve the issue affects how they describe medieval political order: as a pyramid, as a network of overlapping obligations, or as a set of regionally varied arrangements. It also affects how they compare medieval Europe with other societies, since an inflated concept of feudalism can encourage false analogies.

Student task: Choose one relationship discussed in this chapter, such as a monastic tenant's obligations, a vassal's oath, or a castellan's jurisdiction. Explain which of the three components of "feudalism" it belongs to, and whether calling it "feudal" helps or hinders understanding.

Section 34.11

The "Feudal Revolution" and the Transformation around 1000

A separate question from the general debate over feudalism concerns chronology. Beginning in the 1950s, French historians of the Mâconnais region, notably Georges Duby, and later scholars working on Catalonia, Languedoc, and other regions argued that around the period c. 950 to 1050 western Europe, and especially France, underwent a profound social and political transformation. Studies associated with Pierre Bonnassie, Jean-Pierre Poly, Éric Bournazel, and, in the English-speaking world, Thomas Bisson gave this argument the label "feudal revolution" or "feudal transformation."

The thesis emphasizes several changes: the proliferation of castles controlled by local lords; the privatization or localization of justice, with local lords exercising powers of command once associated with public courts; an increase in aristocratic violence and the exploitation of peasants; new forms of coercive lordship; the emergence of a distinct knightly elite; and the weakening of older public institutions such as royal and comital courts. On this account, the ninth-century order of kings, counts, and public assemblies gave way to a world of castellans and their dependents.

Critics have offered a substantial counterargument. They suggest that the changes may have been more gradual, extending over a longer period. They observe that the surviving documents themselves change in form around this time, with new kinds of charters and new vocabulary, and that the apparent transformation may partly reflect changes in record-keeping and terminology rather than in society. They argue that royal and public authority did not simply vanish, and that kings, counts, and courts continued to function in many places. Above all they stress regional variation. England, Germany, Italy, Catalonia, and Francia followed different trajectories. In England, strong royal government under Anglo-Saxon and then Norman kings developed along quite different lines. In the German lands, royal and ecclesiastical power remained important under the Ottonians, as Chapter 46 will show. In Italy, towns and bishops played major roles.

This debate is an excellent example of how a major historiographical thesis can organize evidence without being accepted as a literal Europe-wide event. The "feudal revolution" is best treated as an interpretation that has illuminated real changes in some regions, especially parts of France and Catalonia, while prompting valuable objections. Students should not treat the year 1000 as a magical dividing line, nor assume that the thesis describes all of Europe. It is a hypothesis about a particular set of regions, tested against evidence that remains open to interpretation.

Section 34.12

Medieval Power from the Village to the Kingdom

This final section puts the layers back together. A single peasant household might owe rent to one landowner, labor services attached to its holding, a tithe to a church, attendance at a court, military or transport obligations, fees for using a mill, and customary payments on particular occasions. The household might also have neighbors who were free owners, dependents of another lord, or enslaved workers in a nearby house. A warrior might hold land from one lord, swear fidelity to another magnate, possess judicial authority over his own dependents, and maintain an armed following. A bishop could be a spiritual leader, landowner, royal counselor, judge, and organizer of military contingents, all at once. And the king remained part of this system rather than standing outside it, as a great landowner, a judge, a lord of vassals, and the source of legitimating authority.

Contemporary writers sometimes tried to impose order on this complexity. In the early eleventh century, bishops in northern France articulated the idea of three orders, those who pray, those who fight, and those who work, as a divinely ordained social arrangement. The formulation is ideologically important and would become widely influential, but it should not be taken as a description of actual social organization. It reflects the concerns of clerical authors and omits the variety of rural and aristocratic statuses, the role of women, townspeople, Jews, and other groups, and the many who belonged to more than one category.

Non-Christian and minority communities also appear in this landscape. Jewish communities, for instance, are documented in Narbonne and elsewhere in southern Francia, although evidence for their property holding and economic roles in the early medieval period is uneven and becomes much fuller in later centuries, and Muslim powers in al-Andalus shaped the northern Iberian frontier both as adversaries and as trading and diplomatic partners. Their evidence is sparser, but it reminds us that rural and urban society was more varied than the triad suggests.

The chapter concludes that medieval society was structured through overlapping hierarchies and dependencies, not one neat feudal pyramid. Local lordship was real and powerful, and in some regions it grew more coercive between the ninth and eleventh centuries. But it operated within kingdoms, churches, communities, and customs that limited, challenged, and sometimes reinforced it. Chapter 35 will show how Latin Christianity, with its episcopal organization, monastic landholding, and ties to royal and aristocratic power, expanded into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and other regions, carrying forms of political and ecclesiastical organization, though not a ready-made social system, into societies with their own structures of lordship and dependency.

Primary Source Window

The Polyptych Associated with Saint-Germain-des-Prés, Early Ninth Century

Creator: Administrators of the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, traditionally associated with Abbot Irmino · Date: early ninth century, conventionally c. 825 to 829 · Type: An estate survey (polyptych) in Latin, recording the holdings, households, rents, and services of the abbey's estates

Context: Carolingian churches and rulers produced inventories to manage and defend their property. This survey covers numerous estates, principally in the Paris region and beyond, and lists them one by one. For each it records the lord's demesne, including arable land, vines, meadows, and woods, and then the tenant holdings, naming the heads of households and often their wives and children, together with the status attributed to each holding and the services, renders, and payments owed.

Paraphrase of the structure and content of the polyptych For each estate the survey first describes the lord's own land and its produce, then lists the holdings of tenants by category, such as those classed as free, servile, or held by freed people. Tenants are recorded with their households. Obligations include plowing set areas on the demesne, carting and carrying services, payments of grain, wine, poultry, and eggs, money payments, and contributions connected with military service. The survey is not a translation of any single passage but a summary of recurring features across entries.

Analysis: The survey is valuable because it shows, in concrete detail, how a great church estate was organized and what dependents owed. It demonstrates the bipartite estate in practice on this and similar properties, the variety of tenant holdings, and the ways in which a monastery functioned as a landlord. It also reveals a puzzle: the status assigned to a holding did not always match the apparent status of the family holding it, which warns against assuming neat legal categories.

But the source must be read critically. It was produced by and for administrators, to record what the abbey could claim. It reflects what they wished to record, not the whole life of the peasants it names. It says little about what tenants thought, how rigorously services were enforced, what negotiations occurred, or how families survived hardship. It covers one abbey in one region, and does not represent all of Europe. The names and numbers it gives are valuable, but they should not be mistaken for a complete census.

Multiple Perspectives

One Estate, Many Views: Perspectives on Rural Lordship

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on documented circumstances, such as those visible in estate surveys, charters, and law codes. They are not quotations from named individuals, and they do not attribute thoughts that the sources cannot support.

A dependent tenant on a monastery estate: The documented position is that of a household holding land in return for rents, renders, and labor, recorded by name in a survey. Security of tenure, access to the estate's resources, and the lord's protection were possible benefits, while obligations and the lord's jurisdiction limited autonomy.
A free peasant landholder: Owning land outright could mean freedom from rent, but it also meant exposure to powerful neighbors who might seek to draw the owner into dependence through gifts, protection, or pressure. Free owners appear in charters selling or donating land, sometimes to churches.
An enslaved agricultural or household worker: Enslaved people were legally property, could be sold, and appear in gifts and inventories. Some were settled on holdings of their own, and manumission was possible, but it frequently left continuing obligations. Their own experience is almost entirely hidden in sources written by owners.
An estate manager or reeve: Managers supervised work, collected dues, and mediated between lord and tenants. They could be dependents themselves, and their position might give them authority and opportunities, but also made them accountable to the lord for shortfalls.
A monastery as landlord: A monastic community needed food, revenue, and security to sustain prayer and charity. The survey records what it could claim. The institution also defended its property with documents, advocates, and sometimes armed men.
An armed retainer or miles: Service to a lord could bring support, horses and equipment, status, and opportunity, but also risk, dependence, and competing loyalties.
A count or castellan exercising justice: Judicial power yielded fines, status, and control over dependents, but had to be maintained against rivals, the king, and communities that could resist.
A widow managing inherited property: Law and custom gave widows, in varying degrees, rights to dower and to manage estates and the interests of their children. Some became effective local lords, although they faced pressure from relatives and rivals. Sources record their property transactions, not their feelings.

Important caution: These groups did not have equal power, freedom of action, or access to courts. The purpose is to show complexity, not to suggest that the obligations of a tenant and the privileges of a lord were balanced.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Rural Society from the Carolingians to c. 1050

What Changed?

  • Greater regionalization of authority in some areas after the ninth century
  • Expanding local jurisdiction and, in some regions, banal lordship
  • Proliferation of castles, especially in the tenth and eleventh centuries
  • Changing servile statuses and, in some regions, the emergence of serfdom
  • Growing use of new vocabulary for landholding and service, such as fief and miles

What Remained Continuous?

  • Great estates, including those of churches
  • Peasant household farming
  • Church landholding and ecclesiastical lordship
  • Dues and labor owed by dependents
  • Aristocratic military power
  • Royal justice and public assemblies, which did not vanish

Who experienced change most directly? People living in regions and on estates where castles multiplied and local lords expanded their claims, and dependents whose status was redefined, experienced change most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Households in areas of stronger royal government or long-established estate organization, and peasants whose daily rhythm of farming persisted, experienced greater continuity.

Why the pattern matters: It prevents two errors: imagining a static medieval countryside and imagining that an abrupt revolution remade all of Europe.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Local Lordship Become More Powerful?

Long-term causes

Possession of landed resources, inherited family property, the wealth of church institutions, long traditions of patronage and armed followings, and royal reliance on local intermediaries.

Short-term causes

Political fragmentation after the ninth century, the need for defense against raiding, royal grants and immunities, control of local courts, and the building of fortifications in particular regions.

The transformation

In some regions, especially parts of France and Catalonia, lords came to exercise wider powers of command and to extract new or heavier dues, while elsewhere change was slower or followed different paths.

Immediate effects

Greater local security in some places, greater coercion in others, more competition among lords, and new claims over dependents.

Long-term consequences

Regional lordships became enduring features of European political life, shaping relations between kings and aristocrats, the status of rural dependents, and later debates about privilege, as later chapters will show.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the growth of local lordship. Inheritance strategies, royal policy, economic resources, religion, and local conflict interacted, and outcomes varied by region.
Everyday Life and Social History

Living within Dependency: Land, Labor, and Obligation

For most rural people, power was experienced through routine demands. The agricultural year organized work on the household's own holding and, for many, on the lord's demesne. Labor services could fall at the busiest times, such as plowing and harvest, and compete with the household's own needs. Payments in grain, wine, poultry, or coin had to be found even after poor harvests. Use of a lord's mill or oven might be compulsory in places where banal rights were claimed, and the cost and inconvenience were part of daily life. The tithe supported the church, and church courts and lay courts might both claim authority over disputes.

Households negotiated these burdens through custom. Families arranged marriages with attention to land, status, and the consent of lords. Children inherited obligations along with holdings, and widows and orphans depended on local custom and on the goodwill or pressure of lords and neighbors. This chapter has focused on relationships of power, status, land, labor, and dependency, and Chapter 49 will provide a broader portrait of food, housing, household life, disease, leisure, and popular religion. The key point here is that ordinary people were not merely subjects of rulers but participants in a dense web of local obligations that shaped their lives more immediately than any kingdom.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how land, labor, protection, justice, and military power were organized below the level of kings in early medieval western and central Europe. Its central argument is that medieval power was layered rather than organized through one universal system. Kings governed through counts, bishops, abbots, and landowners, who controlled estates, courts, and armed followers. Most people encountered authority locally, through landlords, courts, churches, and customary obligations.

The chapter distinguished political lordship, economic dependency, military relationships, and landholding. They were connected but not identical, and older accounts that bundled them together under the label feudalism obscured variety. Modern historians normally separate the components before evaluating the term.

Land was the most widespread foundation of elite wealth, but lordship also attached to mills, woods, fisheries, roads, markets, and other rights. Holdings were often scattered rather than compact. Monasteries and bishoprics were major landholders, exercising economic and judicial authority, and they remained central to rural society.

The manor, or bipartite estate, combined a lord's demesne with tenant holdings owing rents, renders, and labor, and Carolingian polyptychs provide vivid evidence for this model on great church estates. But not every village was a manor, and manorialism was never one uniform European institution. England, northern Francia, the German lands, Italy, Catalonia, and frontier regions differed considerably.

Peasants occupied a spectrum of statuses, from free landholders to dependent tenants, freed people, and enslaved workers. Rights and obligations varied by land, person, locality, marriage, inheritance, custom, and lord. "Peasant" describes a socioeconomic position more reliably than a single legal status.

Slavery persisted into and through the early Middle Ages, supported by warfare, trade, and church ownership, even as other forms of dependency expanded and converged. Serfdom emerged unevenly, rather than replacing slavery on a single timetable, and it was not a Europe-wide event. Serfs were not slaves, and peasants were not all serfs.

Lordship included jurisdictional powers, such as courts, fines, tolls, and rights of command, often discussed as banal lordship. Castles, which developed on regional timetables mainly from the tenth and eleventh centuries, were instruments of protection, control, and coercion, and they figure in the debate over the localization of authority. Warriors served kings, magnates, bishops, abbots, and local lords within networks of patronage, kinship, and oath, and the meaning of miles changed over time.

Vassalage, benefice, and fief were distinct phenomena that became important for elite relations, but they did not describe all of medieval society, and the classic feudal pyramid should be critiqued rather than taught as reality. "Feudalism" is a modern historiographical category. It can be useful only when its user defines exactly what the term describes, and the debate between its defenders and critics, including Brown and Reynolds, illustrates how historical categories are constructed.

The proposed "feudal revolution" around 1000 is an interpretation that illuminates changes in some regions, especially in parts of France and Catalonia, while its critics emphasize gradual change, changes in documentation, continuing public authority, and regional diversity. The chapter concluded that medieval society was structured through overlapping hierarchies and dependencies, in which kings, churches, warriors, and peasants all participated. Chapter 35 now turns to the expansion of Latin Christianity into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and other regions.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Lordship
Power over land, people, or rights, which could include economic claims, judicial authority, and military command.
Demesne
The part of an estate cultivated for the lord's direct benefit.
Manse (mansus)
A unit of landholding and obligation, typically supporting a peasant household in Carolingian estate surveys.
Manor
A later and modern term for an estate unit combining demesne and dependent holdings, strongly associated with England.
Manorialism
Scholarly label for the organization of estates through demesne and dependent holdings; never a uniform European institution.
Bipartite estate
An estate divided between the lord's demesne and tenant holdings owing rents and labor.
Polyptych
A Carolingian estate survey recording holdings, households, and obligations.
Corvée
Labor service owed to a lord, such as plowing, harvesting, or carting.
Render
A payment in kind, such as grain, wine, or poultry, owed to a lord.
Allod
Land not held tenurially from a lord, though its holders could still owe public duties; meaning varied by region.
Precaria
A temporary or conditional grant of the use of property, especially church land, with widely varying terms, used by churches and families to manage property and patronage.
Immunity
A grant excluding royal officials from an estate, which could transfer or strengthen judicial and fiscal authority there; scope varied.
Tithe
A customary payment, nominally a tenth of produce, owed to the church.
Peasant
A rural cultivator working land mainly with household labor; a socioeconomic rather than legal term.
Serf
A dependent cultivator bound by obligations and restrictions but not legally identical to a slave; status varied by region and period.
Slavery
Legal ownership of persons, which persisted through the early Middle Ages and was supported by trade and warfare.
Manumission
The freeing of an enslaved person, often leaving continuing obligations to a former owner.
Ban
Power of command and compulsion, originally associated with public authority.
Banal lordship
Historiographical term for local lords' exercise of powers of command over people in their territories.
Castellan
A commander or holder of a castle who often exercised local authority.
Incastellamento
Modern term for the development of fortified settlements organized by local lords, especially in central Italy from the tenth century.
Burh
A fortified center in Anglo-Saxon England; not a castle in the later sense.
Motte and bailey
A type of earthwork castle with a raised mound and an enclosure, not the form of every fortification.
Miles
Latin term for warrior or soldier whose meaning shifted toward knight over time.
Ministeriales
Dependent administrators and warriors, clearly documented from the later eleventh century, mainly in the German lands, who could rise into the lower nobility.
Heriot
In Anglo-Saxon England, a payment of weapons and horses to a lord at a warrior's death.
Vassalage
A personal relationship of fidelity, service, and patronage between a man and a lord.
Commendation
The act of placing oneself under a lord's protection, often accompanied by an oath of fidelity.
Benefice
Beneficium, a term with varied meanings for grants or use-rights of land or revenue, sometimes connected with service; not an early synonym for fief.
Fief
A medieval term, appearing in forms such as fevum and feodum, for property or revenues held under varied conditions; later legal writers increasingly associated it with vassalage and service.
Feudalism
A modern historiographical category, variously used for vassalage and fiefs, seigneurial relations, or political decentralization; contested.
Feudal revolution
A thesis that c. 950 to 1050 brought a radical transformation of social and political power, especially in parts of France; debated.
Three orders
An early eleventh-century ideological formulation dividing society into those who pray, fight, and work; not a literal description.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reveals the social foundations beneath the political narratives of Part V. Kings, empires, and kingdoms depended on the estates, courts, fortifications, and armed followings of local lords, and on the labor of peasants and enslaved people who produced the wealth those lords commanded. Understanding that layered structure explains why medieval rulers governed through negotiation, patronage, and delegation, and why political authority could be strong at the top while power was exercised locally.

The chapter also demonstrates how historical categories are made. "Feudalism" shaped generations of textbooks and continues to appear in popular speech, yet it is a modern construct that combined several distinct phenomena. Learning to separate vassalage, manorial dependency, and local lordship, and to ask whether a label helps or distorts, is a skill students will need in every later chapter that encounters similar labels, from absolutism to the Enlightenment.

Its themes will return. Chapter 35 traces how Latin Christianity and its institutions expanded into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary. Chapter 42 examines the agricultural expansion that transformed the countryside, Chapter 43 the commercial revival that altered rents and markets, and Chapters 50 to 52 the crises that reshaped labor and dependency in the later Middle Ages. The relationships of land, status, and obligation introduced here provide the baseline against which those changes can be measured.

Within Volume II, the chapter contributes the argument that Christendom was not only a community of belief and church governance but also a society of estates, courts, castles, and households, in which bishops, abbots, warriors, and peasants all had roles. It asks students to hold together two truths: local lordship was powerful and sometimes oppressive, and it operated within a world of kingdoms, churches, communities, and customs that constrained it. That tension runs through the rest of medieval history.
